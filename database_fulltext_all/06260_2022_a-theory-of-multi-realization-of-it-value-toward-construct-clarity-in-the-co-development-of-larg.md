# A THEORY OF MULTI-REALIZATION OF IT VALUE: TOWARD CONSTRUCT CLARITY IN THE CO-DEVELOPMENT OF LARGE PLATFORMS<sup>1</sup>

Leif Skiftenes Flak Department of Information Systems, University of Agder, Kristiansand, NORWAY {leif.flak@uia.no}

Hans Solli-Sæther Department of International Business, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Trondheim, NORWAY {haso@ntnu.no}

Detmar William Straub MIS Department, Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {straubdetmar@gmail.com}

Realizing or appropriating value from individual firm-level IT investments has been a recurring theme in the IS literature for decades. Further, failure rates related to IT investments have been high for many years. However, realizing value from IT in settings where numerous organizations need to work together to co-develop sizable digital platforms has been undertheorized and/or hobbled with conflated theoretical constructs. To address this dearth of novel theory and accompanying empiricism, high-quality processes such as benefits management have been suggested in the scientific literature and have gained substantial interest in practice as a means for organizations to structure their routines and processes in ways that actually capture value. The complexity of this challenge suggests that there should be a renewed theoretical effort in IS to reconceptualize several extant scholarly streams. We address this challenge first by deconstructing traditional scholarly frameworks into a moderated theoretical model of multi-creation and multi-realization of IT value termed “MUIT.” The first construct in the model is based on the resource-based view; then, the model is further informed by appropriation theory, adaptive structuration theory, network governance theory, and, finally, the benefits management literature. Our variance model is a Type IV theory according to Gregor’s typology—a theory for explaining and predicting how largescale strategic consortia can collaborate to realize greater value from IT than they could in isolation. Regarding praxis, we contribute to the understanding of how interorganizational resources and capabilities, relationships, and value realization can be spring-boarded into both successful large-scale platforms and superior realization of benefits.

Keywords: Digital platforms, co-creation, co-realization, resource-based view of the firm, adaptive structuration theory, appropriation theory, the DeLone-McLean model, benefits management, network governance theory, theory development

## Introduction

Achieving benefits from individual firm-level IT investments has been a recurring theme in the IS literature for decades. Failure rates related to IT investments are high and have been for many years. Standish Group Chaos Reports from 1994 to 2018 cite failure statistics that indicate that roughly 25% of IT projects are outright failures, more than a third are only marginally successful, and only a third can be deemed successful.<sup>2</sup> The situation is even more dire in the context of emerging systems like artificial intelligence (AI). An IDC global survey of 2,473 organizations (2019) reports that numerous organizations worldwide that are already using AI solutions report up to a 50% failure rate.<sup>3</sup>

While there is a wealth of literature on IT projects at the level of an individual organization, there is a dearth of studies on realizing value from IT in settings where sizeable numbers of organizations join together in co-creating large-scale digital platforms.<sup>4</sup> Indeed, recent developments in the IS literature suggest that value creation is shifting from single organizations to clusters of organizations, value chains, strategic alliances, networks, or consortia of organizations, a change that is especially poignant in an era of disruptive and transformational digital technologies like AI, blockchain, cloud, and big data analytics. While digital platforms offer massive value-added potential, recent studies point out that current practices may have significant weaknesses that, over time, may challenge the sustainability of such platforms. For instance, a study of Scandinavian hotels regarding international online travel agencies such as Hotels.com shows that while the online travel agencies offer effective marketing, they also erode revenue for the hotels (Bygstad & Iden, 2017). This parallels the wellknown examples of how Amazon outcompeted bookstores and, in a similar vein, how Uber outcompeted taxi companies—leading to a winner takes all scenario. While such situations can lead to massive profits for winners, the rest of the value chain may struggle. Consequently, the need for theory development related to digital platforms has been repeatedly mentioned in the IS literature.

To date, an all-encompassing, holistic IT theory to study the entire chain of events from idea inception to interorganizational (IO) value capture is missing. From the broader perspective of organizational theory and strategic management, the value creation-to-capture conceptualization across multiple organizations is certainly not new. It has been studied for quite some time through relevant concepts like co-opetition (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1996), complementary advantage, and interorganizational strategy and cooperation. In our MUIT model, though, the critical shift from individual organizations to multiple entities reflects both scholarship and praxis over at least the past thirty years.

In IS and elsewhere, there has been a serious, long-standing interest in collaborating partnerships—variously named using the “co-” prefix. In marketing, co-creation has been studied as a set of activities that involves both the firm and its customers (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004; Ramaswamy & Gouillart, 2010). New product development is often the output of such interactions (Bendapudi & Leone, 2003; Kelly et al., 1990). Interorganizational, co-created IT value for firms such as Motorola and its retailers (Cederlund et al., 2007) and within the Rosettanet consortium (Kohli & Grover, 2008) have also been investigated by IS scholars in special issues (e.g., in MIS Quarterly, 36(1), 2012).

There is little doubt that many scholars are interested in cocreation in large-scale projects such as digital platforms, but there have been no comprehensive studies, in information systems (IS) at least, that place the concept of “co-” into a testable, theoretical framework. In their editorial introduction to an MISQ special issue on co-creation, Grover and Kohli (2012) nicely framed the overall phenomenon, a perspective that clarified future directions for research (see Figure 1), but this framework is not in itself structured as a Type IV theory for both explanation and prediction (Dubin, 1976; Gregor, 2006).

Moreover, there are other reasons why neither their insightful framing nor the papers published in the special issue (plus related papers on co-creation since 2012) sufficiently covered the theoretical waterfront hereinbefore laid out. In the MISQ special issue, for instance, Han et al. (2012) is the only paper dealing with co-development, but it is in the specific context of open innovation alliances where commitment to the consortium can vary widely from loose to strong. Whereas their findings that there are major benefits for free riders and that certain features of open innovations can lead to higher market evaluations are fascinating, they do not discuss how firms successfully engaged stakeholders to build systems. Governance is also not a key point. Both of these elements exist in MUIT, our theoretical model for the multi-realization of IT value.

In this paper, we examine how organizations need to adapt both co-creative and co-realizing methods through our MUIT logic and how this underlying conceptualization should help drive a vision of the extent to which selected resources/ capabilities greatly assist cooperating partners in appropriating value.

First, our theorizing—which includes important theoretical boundary conditions and an integration of constructs from several key referent theories—seeks to transform the ground-breaking Grover-Kohli (GK) model into a temporally phased, testable theory. To accomplish this, we bifurcate conflated constructs in the reference theories and redefine them in novel ways. Clearly, this theoretical novelty draws first from elements in the resource-based view of the firm (RBV). But the context here is quite different—that is, a consortium of organizations joining together to assemble and combine resources to drive value for large-scale platforms. Importantly, the existing RBV literature focuses almost exclusively on the single firm and its internal competencies, supplemented sometimes by a broader environmental view, namely cooperation between complementary vertical and horizontal firms in the supply chain. Today, cooperative IO ventures are widely seen in the private sector, and equally so in the public and nonprofit sectors. A partnership perspective has sprung up between providers, customers, and citizens alike (Love et al., 2005; Pinto, 2005; Wong et al., 2007), outsourcing clients and vendors (Lacity et al., 2010), and even competitors (Gilmore & Pine, 2000).

To set the stage, we initially deconstruct the GK model into its component parts. These are: (1) co-creation using complementary capabilities that have embedded IT functionalities, knowledge, and other resources; (2) appropriation across these GK layers; (3) value realization for all stakeholders; and (4) governance processes adhered to by all firms in the alliance (see Figure 2). Then, we reconstruct these intellectual concepts into our three mainline constructs, these being: (1) multi-created IT capabilities; (2) multi-appropriated IT development; and (3) multi-realized benefits (i.e., value). Our interpretation of the outermost, circular layer in the original GK framing is that a fourth construct, governance, moderates the strength of the causal links between the three mainline constructs.

Our research objectives are: (1) to articulate a testable theory based directly on the GK model, specifically designed for building large-scale, multi-party platforms and one which realizes significant consortium value from this coalition effort; (2) to illuminate the distinctions between two conflated concepts/constructs presently in this IT/management value chain literature—co-creation and appropriation—in order to facilitate greater conceptual, theoretical, and pragmatic clarity and (3) to bifurcate each of these constructs and integrate them into a testable, parsimonious theoretical framework that selects relevant intellectual components from RBV, adaptive structuration theory (AST), appropriation theory, the DeLone-Mclean systems success model, the benefits management literature, and network governance theory.

Theoretical Framing of MUIT

Preview of Theoretical Constructs and Relationships

In its modeling of the IT value chain from project initiation to live systems with post-implementation assessment and realized benefits, MUIT bifurcates two conflated constructs currently overlapping in the processes of IT value creation and value realization into three key constructs and four causal paths. A pictorial of the theoretical elements is shown in Figure 2. Boundary conditions for the theory are followed by definitions of the key terms in MUIT in Table 1. Logical details arguing for theoretical paths follow that précis.

Before laying out our theoretical logic, it is important to note that we are using the prefix “multi-” in the model to express one of the boundary conditions of the theory, namely scale. The prefix “co-” is tied to voluminous past work, connoting two or a relatively limited set of partners. Our vision is different from past work and is laser-focused on IT platforms that are both large-scale, i.e., with sizable numbers of stakeholders and co-developed (Grover & Kohli, 2012), as shown in Table 1.<sup>5</sup>

## Boundary Conditions

Addressing the need for clear theoretical boundary conditions (Dubin, 1978), our model proposes to limit the scope of MUIT in two ways. Scale is the first. Scale is an interesting boundary condition for a theory in that it can work to the advantage of organizations, as in economies of scale, but it can also work to one’s detriment, as in this case. There is little doubt that the number of different entities involved in IT value creation and realization exponentially increases the challenges of governing IT projects, effective communications between the co-producers, and achieving successful outcomes.

What constitutes “large-scale”? From the standpoint of effective project communications between partners, the number of partner linkages is a function of the number of stakeholders. Since raising the number of partners adversely impacts the number of communication linkages, the energy expended to overcome information exchange barriers grows rapidly (Brooks, 1975, 1995).

This concept has become known as “Brooks’ Law,” an idea widely cited by scholars from software engineering, computer science, management, and information systems after the initial publication of Brooks’ seminal book on the mythical manmonth (McCain & Salvucci, 2006). In Brooks’ formulation [n \* (n − 1) / 2] for ten co-developer/stakeholders, for instance, it is 10 \* (10-1) / 2 or 45 channels of communication. We argue that the degree of magnitude difference between these 45 resulting channels generated by 10 stakeholders and a lower number of stakeholders is substantial. Nine stakeholders, for instance, generate 36 channels, eight stakeholders generate 28 channels, seven stakeholders generate 21 channels, and so forth. In its multi-governance moderated construct, MUIT offers a generic reason why large-scale projects are able to manage 45 or more channels. Our more specific reasoning as to why 10 or more co-developers can still lead to desirable outcomes follows immediately below.

As noted above, there is empirical evidence that 10 knowledge co-producers is a not unreasonable lower bound for what large-scale means. Dennis and Williams (2005) conducted a meta-analysis of the brainstorming literature to determine the point at which technology-assisted groups outperformed those not having such tools (i.e., “verbal” and “nominal” groups). They determined that this point occurs when the group size is at or exceeds 10. In short, groups without technology support appear to become too large to readily coordinate when the group size reaches 10. Hence the essential need for electronic tools to assist in coordination and collaboration. Both logical and empirical evidence thus suggests that 10 is a solid enough starting point of a line of demarcation between large projects and small-to-medium scale projects. Thus, it is our initial boundary condition for MUIT.

As the number of co-producing partners increases from large to very large scales, the only viable way to coordinate would appear is to utilize technological assistance and efficacious governance mechanisms (à la coordination theory as described by Malone and Crowstone, 1996); see also Crowston et al. (2006). We also readily acknowledge that this selection of at least 10 partners for “large-scale” could well be fungible. Perhaps another way to set the boundary in future studies is on a case-by-case basis and/or to test this condition by gathering relevant empirical data from numerous consortia varying in size.

Finally, given that IT outsourcing does share some of the codeveloper characteristics we are exploring in this paper, we also examined whether the number of vendors working with a client has a bearing on a successful relationship; the purpose of this simple testing was to see if the number of vendors made any difference. The literature is quite conflicted on this issue, as thoroughly reported in Lee et al. (2019). In fact, Lee et al. (2019) did not find any configuration of numbers of vendors (one of their defining outsourcing archetypes) that led to better outcomes. However, using their dataset, if we simply regress IT outsourcing partner success on the number of vendors (varying from one to three vendors), we found that the relationship is significant at the alpha 0.05 protection level (N = 235).<sup>6</sup>

What could this mean? What it might suggest is that there are circumstances, as surmised in our logic above, where it is possible to overcome the information exchange barriers generated according to Brooks’ Law and still derive benefits, even as the number of involved groups rises. Whereas the Lee et al. (2019) dataset lying specifically in the IT outsourcing domain does not suggest a cutoff where this number might move from a medium size to a large size, it does hint strongly that without some form of control (such as efficacious multigovernance strategies and technological communications support), larger-scale projects could be even more prone to failure. One could conjecture, for example, that there exists a curvilinear relationship where too many partners could raise transaction costs whereas too few partners could lead to inadequate co-creation opportunities. Our theoretical scoping leaves exploration of these possibilities for future scholars.

Boundary condition #2, co-development (or co-production), is an important, but delimited domain for studying why and how high-quality large-scale platforms become successful. Thus, the generic statement that summarizes our theoretical scoping is platforms co-produced by buy-in, large-scale (ten or more) stakeholder consortia.

The arrows representing the moderated influence of governance in Table 2 supplement those of the direct path arrows between constructs. This captures the important role of governance in changing the basic equation in large-scale systems implementation. It is very likely that having governance mechanisms in place, either relational or formal/contractual, lubricates multiple partner interactions. Moreover, as the number of partners increases, their mutual agreements become ever more complex. Other “network” factors may also apply.

## Theoretical Logic of MUIT

## Definitions of Key Theoretical Concepts

“Co-” and “multi-” capture different aspects of an alliance. “Co-” (as in co-creation) refers to collaboration whereas “multi-” in MUIT extends this aspect of collaboration by theorizing a specific large-scale boundary condition related to the number of partners. In and of itself, “co-” implies at least two entities, but the literature does not further articulate what constitutes a “small” or medium size consortium. Pointedly, that scholarly exploration lies outside the scope of the current project.

Moreover, the conceptualization of “co-” is advanced by deconstructing and limiting the notion of co-creation to the front-end resource/capability-building and developmental stages in IT projects. Moreover, we introduce the supplemental perspective of “co-realization” for the back-end stages of implementation where systems are being prototyped and benefits are being measured and captured. Others have proposed variant distinctions between creating and capturing value; our distinctions innovate in terms of big consortia and large-scale systems.

As noted above, other studies have distinguished between value creation and value realization. There are two classes of Ricardian organizational rent in Subramani (2004), Miranda and Kavan (2005), and Coff (1999). These are termed value creation and value capture, but these are both considered to be dependent variables (DVs). Our perspective is that value creation is theoretically best framed as a purely creative process that precedes value capture, thus serving as an IV, not a DV.

Indeed, other scholars have also seen it in this way. Value creation is the combining of organizational resources in innovative ways (Bowman, 2000; Miranda & Kavan, 2005; Trang et al., 2021) whereas in Moran and Ghoshal (1999) value capture is the derivation of benefits from the deployment of such capabilities.

Building on this, our IV multi-created value construct is an IV but our DV is the final multi-realized value construct. The prefixes “co-” and “multi-” differ only in scale. In the literature, “co-” connotes a relatively smaller number of stakeholders involved in systems co-production; on the other hand, “multi-” is a MUIT boundary condition of 10 or more stakeholders. As shown in Table 1, IT co-creation is defined as “front-end activities whereby stakeholders cooperate in new software development, assembling resources to build capabilities and making cybernetic adjustments to meet unfulfilled systems objectives.” The argument implicit in this definition is that disparate groups join together for a variety of internal reasons but with a shared purpose of accomplishing jointly agreed upon goals. In IS, for instance, this would include scoping and specifying requirements for a digital platform. As the group size grows, the complexity does as well, and coordination and cooperation issues escalate.

Assuredly, each stakeholder has a different organizational raison d’etre, but for compelling strategic reasons, they cooperate. The cooperation can take many forms, but most common might be to create a novel IT product (goods, services, or both).

IT co-realization, on the other hand, is a jointly experienced outcome or a set of outcomes, specifically: “back-end interactions in the implementation of systems designed to increasingly capture co-created value, measuring the joint goal fulfillment of the stakeholders, and managing the evolving benefits” (see Table 1).

For our purposes, partners form consortia composed of a considerable number of separate organizational units. Partners can even include competitors, as, for instance, when rivals need to form a consortium to gain business from a third party (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 1996). Finally, partners can be units that interface horizontally along boundaries, as with relatively independent government agencies, agencies that represent either sequenced or independent steps in an overall citizen workflow solution.

What is key to co-realization is that it resets the priorities of traditional systems development projects, whose equally stressed, time-honored objectives or goals rest on the triple pillars of schedule (time goal), budget (cost or resource goal), and delivering quality (performance goal) (Project Management Institute, 2017). While all these goals are clearly important, the performance goal of delivering benefits is the sine qua non in the benefits management literature. These benefits have “value,” which in RBV is defined as being competitively useful;<sup>7</sup> Table 1 defines as having “great worth.”

Another distinction that needs to be made early on in theorizing is the critical term-of-art “stakeholder.” We constrain our definition to those who do not just perceive themselves as having a stake, but those who influence resources and capabilities to merit co-realizing benefits, or “residual claimants” as argued by Barney et al. (2021).

[RBV’s] model of appropriation must acknowledge that a firm has residual claimants beyond shareholders (Barney, 2018). However this conclusion does not suggest that all of a firm’s nonowner stakeholders have a claim as residual claimants. Indeed, the theory suggests that stakeholders that make resources and capabilities available to a firm that are not part of the process of creating superior economic value do not have such a claim. These stakeholders, instead, … are a source only of competitive parity (e.g., cement, electricity) and will be appropriately treated as fixed claimants (p. 13).

Clearly, examples of fixed claimants abound in the domain being studied here. If a multi-development team purchases hardware at arm’s length from OEMs, for example, these hardware providers would seem to have no residual claim to ultimate benefits nor be involved at any substantive level in systems development activities.

There are cases where, at first glance, vendors would not seem to be residual stakeholders, but might be such under different circumstances. In IT outsourcing, for instance, an emerging model of client-vendor relations is the risk-reward or profit/revenue-sharing model (Overby, 2012). This model was adopted successfully by the partnership of Bharti, one of the largest Indian telecoms, and IBM (Martinez-Jerez et al., 2006). In this and similar situations, both outsourcing clients and vendors would share the same overall benefits.

Alternatively, an argument can be made that even IT outsourcing vendors who are not party to a profit-sharing arrangement can also realize benefits from large-scale systems development efforts; such financial and learning rewards are just not the same as those who directly utilize the developed systems.

## Conflation of the Construct of Co-creation within the Literature

There is little question that many authors conflate the constructs of co-creation and co-realization, but we believe that making clear distinctions between these ideas can lead to greater conceptual and theoretical clarity. With no comprehensive study placing the concept of IT co-creation into an overall, testable theoretical framework for guidance, there is currently conceptual confusion about co-creation (and co-realization). In short, an integrated theory that takes into account the critical nomological elements of co-creation and co-realization is lacking. One more novelty in our conceptualization, as noted above, is in its boundary conditions—that is, co-developed platforms for large-scale consortia.

## Conflation of the Construct of Appropriation across the Literatures

The construct of appropriation rose to prominence in DeSanctis and Poole’s AST or adaptive structuration theory (1994). According to their logic, appropriation described the ways in which users “appropriate” systems by either using them consistently with the original system design or not. Appropriation is thus a mediating variable to performance outcomes such as decisions processes and decisions.

Their listing of types of appropriation uses (i.e., structures) illustrates the nature of appropriation. Appropriation of structures includes appropriation moves, the faithfulness of appropriation, and instrumental use (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994, p. 132). The subconstruct of “faithfulness of appropriation” was later developed in the IS literature to also represent one form of appropriation (Chin et al., 1997). Another scale emerging from AST work was the consensus of appropriation (Salisbury et al., 2002).

Contrariwise, Coff’s appropriation theory (1999) defines components of appropriation as the DVs of value creation and capture, quite different from AST, where appropriation mediates causal paths. Thus, across the literature, we have a conflation of the processes of use and deployment (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) and value realization (Coff, 1999).

## Why Bifurcate These Conflated Constructs?

First, why is it helpful to separate co-creation into the theoretical strands of creation and realization? Moreover, what is the point of separating appropriation into a mediating and an outcome variable? Clarity in causal chain and construct definitions is critical for good theorizing<sup>8</sup> and it becomes nowhere more important than when the environmental setting changes, especially in a dramatic way. More and more frequently, organizations are entering into consortia, often large-scale alliances. When collaborating, they encounter problems that go far beyond those experienced internally by a single organization. In agency theory, shared goals become critical for any effective outcomes from a cooperative enterprise; agents in transactions act opportunistically against the best interests of the principal and thereby create a moral hazard with suboptimal results for the principal.<sup>9</sup> Taking this further, goal conflicts lie at the heart of Freeman’s stakeholder theory (1998, p. 125) which argues that orders-of-magnitude agency problems can rapidly degenerate into outright failures.

For reasons like this, we argue that theoretical boundary conditions that were viable for internal, hierarchical systems development or one-to-one dyadic IT outsourcing are stretched thin when systems are co-produced, especially as the scale goes well beyond historical experience. Moreover, beginning such theorizing with confusing and conflated constructs and widely varying conceptualizations of causal linkages require a reset.

## Construct 1: Extent of Multi-Created IT Capabilities

In the management literature, a capability is created when an organization combines selected resources to create competencies (Teece et al., 1997). Resources are distinct from capabilities in that they are observable and tradeable (Makadok, 2001). In brief, capabilities are resources that are combined and deployed together to perform a task (Makadok, 2001). Moreover, according to Barney and colleagues, for sustainable competitive advantage, at least one of the underlying resources must be VRIO: valuable, rare, inimitable, and organizationally supported (Barney & Clifford, 2010).

An enumeration of different resources or capabilities that appear in the pre-2010 literature to test the effectiveness of RBV theory is daunting. Newbert’s (2007) exhaustive review finds a total of 74 resources or capabilities. Capabilities that stand out both in article numbers and amount of theoretical support (Newbert, 2007) are: (1) human resource capability, (2) innovation capability, and (3) IT/technological capability. Given the importance of technology as a strategic capability, we narrow this construct to IT capabilities only.

Multi-created IT capability building occurs specifically when organizations cooperate in building strategic capacities. The overall alliance identifies resources required and then moves these resources into actionable plans. According to Wade and Hulland (2004), IT capability is built through combinations of at least five specific, elemental resources: (1) partnership quality, (2) domain-specific knowledge of IT staff, (3) level of IT infrastructure, (4) IS strategic planning ability, and (5) wellpositioned governance.

Element #1, the relational resource, is a function of the partnership quality. Good relations between IT and other intraand interorganizational units help align tools to achieve mutual goals (Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Chen & Popovich, 2003; Swanson, 1994). In fact, cooperation influences the sharing of data needed for system success (Chen & Popovich, 2003). Well-functioning partnerships between, say, software vendors and clients, benefit both parties and helps to build new capabilities (Feeny & Willcocks, 1998; Fjermestad & Romano, 2003; Zablah et al., 2012). Element #2, the human IT resource, involves domain-specific knowledge of the IT staff that comes from human capacity-building through such mechanisms as training and trading on a wealth of experiences (Hall, 1993; Mahoney & Pandian, 1992). Element #3, IT infrastructure, includes shared technology resources, resources such as portals, platforms, networks, hardware, data centers, and software (Broadbent et al., 1999). A versatile IT infrastructure is critical for superior performance (Broadbent et al., 1999) and this is even more true when several organizations are dependent on it. Element #4, IS strategic planning ability, deals primarily with the strategic alignment of stakeholders (Bradley et al., 2006;

Deephouse et al., 1996; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). Element #5, governance, is represented in MUIT with its own moderated paths. Since element #1, relational resources, is also about governance as is element #5, it turns out that 40% of Wade and Hulland’s elemental resources (2004) revolve around consortium partners forming cooperative endeavors.

## Construct 2: Quality of Multi-Appropriated IT Co-Development

In Construct 1, partners create IT capabilities. In Construct 2, they engage in interactions/processes between the IT team and members of the consortium and then begin to appropriate value as users appropriate the systems, as pictured earlier in Figure 2. We argue that overall success dimensions can exert their influence at different levels, e.g., at the IT platform level as well as at the consortium level (Construct 3.0). Deploying capabilities and beginning to accumulate systems benefits is the widely adopted logic of the DeLone-McLean systems success model (1992, 2003) and its numerous follow-ups, largely supportive studies (Adebowale, 2017; Petter et al., 2008). The combined effect of individually successful systems is the basis for accumulative, penultimate successes. Cooke-Davies (2002) sees this as the quality whereby project success or failure will be judged and Delone and McLean (1992, 2003) represent this in their constructs of service, information, and systems quality.

Perhaps the best way to illustrate how the mediating process of Construct 2 works is with a currently popular systems development methodology that has numerous similarities with our emergent perspective here, namely, agile development methods (Turner & Jain, 2002). In agile development, prototypes are iteratively demonstrated to the beneficiaries/stakeholders of the systems and if there remain unfulfilled needs, the systems are rapidly adjusted through a return to the co-creative processes to improve quality. This type of interplay between systems building and deployment is captured in our multi-appropriated IT development construct. As in our model, agile methods can scale up to projects that depend heavily on the key characteristics of “co-”—that is, multiple cooperating partners looking for shared value (Weill & Vitale, 2001). The increased complexity of such endeavors as they grow in partner numbers, we argue, is ameliorated by governance that is captured through our two moderations.

The overlapping of value creation and value realization in our theoretical model (Figure 2) represents waxing and waning processes and is an apt description of what we envision happening in this stage. As the appropriation of systems ramps up, systems are being iteratively delivered to exercise their capabilities. This is the co-creative part of the process. Beta versions, prototypes, and individual modules and features are then systematically deployed to the “test” users, who provide feedback to developers on the extent to which their goals are being fulfilled by the platform. This is a co-realized part of the process. In large-scale co-development projects, this feedback will come from a wide variety of sources in that the stakeholders are themselves a diverse group. As time goes on, the co-creative process reaches its fullest intensity as systems move into the testing production mode and the initial benefits are cataloged. As more and more systems go into regular, live production, more and more benefits are realized. Thus, the co-realized process eventually reaches its fullest flowering as the cocreative process winds down.

As discussed in the present research, the digital platform level deals with a delivered, large-scale IT endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result (Larson & Gray, 2011). Like other large-scale, substantive investment organizational efforts, the major goal of a large-scale IT project is to satisfy the needs of multiple residual stakeholders with a high-quality platform. In this way, its success influences bottom lines and desired outcomes, as well as expected benefits at both the organizational and interorganizational levels.

## Construct 3: Extent of Multi-Realized Benefits

MUIT extends the thinking in appropriation theory to the IT value chain through Construct 3, the “extent of multirealized benefits.” Classical studies in RBV have been largely silent on value realization. Recently though, Barney (2018) has argued that economic theories support the notion that all true stakeholders (not just shareholders) can be legitimate “residual claimants” (p. 3306). Indeed, Coff (1999) made this point clear in his conceptualization of appropriation theory. Stakeholders need to include entities like shareholders, managers, employees, citizens, and parties external to the organization. Note that all definitions in Table 1 utilize the concept and terminology of “stakeholder” in spelling out constructs and concepts underlying MUIT.

MUIT adopts this basic logic, extends it to consortia, and then highlights what we argue is compelling logic for how successful large-scale projects have a concomitantly major impact, overall, on both organizational and interorganizational performance. The whole reason that organizations commit so heavily to developing their digital portfolio is so they can ensure consistent, long-term improvements in the systems that drive organizational benefits (Ahern et al., 2003; Bass et al., 2013).<sup>10</sup>

The third construct in our model highlights such multirealized benefits represented at the consortium level. The prefix “multi-” argues implicitly that benefits for each participating organization will be greater than could have been achieved in isolation. The argument for cross-level effects here is similar to that in Smith et al. (1997), where they reason that large IT outsourcing arrangements will have a marked effect on the entire bottom line of the firm.

What is the nature of these benefits? Miranda and Kavan (2005) consider interorganizational returns in outsourcing relationships in terms of value capture and value creation. Whereas we agree with Miranda and Kavan (2005) in principle, we are focused here on the overall construct and what it means. We are not making a primary distinction between what they call “value capture,” which is tightly tied to organizational performance, versus “value creation,” which they associate with innovativeness.

We align our thinking more with Straub et al. (2004) who argue that organizations engaged in networks have an imperative to develop sophisticated measures of the performance of the entire network of organizations (as opposed to individual organizational performance or simplistic aggregations of the individual organizational performance metrics). Furthermore, whereas Straub et al. Straub et al. (2004) propose multi-partner metrics for this logic, the thinking in MUIT moves this to a higher level in theorizing about partnership co-realized value. Our units of analysis thus move across two levels: (1) activities at the large-scale IT platform project level, and (2) activities at the interorganizational level, i.e., the consortium. Twenty-firstcentury methodological work argues for more cross-level thinking in IS research. According to Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007): “Although researchers have learned a great deal from studying systems usage at single levels, … studying it on one level at a time ultimately leads to an unnatural, incomplete, and very disjoint[ed] view of how organizations function (p. 658).” The basic theoretical elements in Figure 2 lead logically to the theoretical statement (Table 2).

## Path from Multi-Created IT Capabilities to Multi-Appropriated IT Co-Development

As depicted in our theoretical model (Figure 2), this path emerges from previous discussion of the IT value stream of co-creation and co-realization. The logic is straightforward and is based on the theoretical logic of RBV and prior literature that has investigated the picking of resources and combining of these to create capabilities for strategic purposes. The more careful consortium organizations are at selecting resources indicated for any given initiative, the more management will be able to combine these into strategic capabilities. The literature streams of (1) benefits management and (2) co-created IT value suggest how to deploy an integrated process given that these streams complement each other so well. Several process activities are adumbrated in the relevant literatures (Lin & Pervan, 2003; Peppard et al., 2007; Remenyi et al., 1997; Ward et al., 1996). The tight connection between creating systems and capturing value is also supported in Liang et al. (2010) meta-analysis and in our overall theoretical logic expostulated above.

What is different about the conceptualization here is that systems deployment will likely require cybernetic returns to co-creation as systems are implemented. The interplay between capability co-creation and deployment corealization represents these two aspects of the digital platform development and implementation process which is a self-feeding process. Figure 2 shows this through the parallel but overlapping blue processes of value creation and value realization in the mediating appropriation phase of the IT value chain.

Co-creation, we have argued, differs markedly from corealization, and ignoring this distinction can lead to conceptual and pragmatic confusion. Co-realization, as we have defined the term earlier, is a jointly experienced outcome (or outcomes) by the stakeholders. Although this is the end result, the co-realization process of organizing and managing the potential benefits arising from IT-based value co-creation must occur in parallel, as initially argued by Ward and Daniel (2006). The distinction between benefits realization (outcome) and benefits management (process) has been conflated in the benefits management literature, where the terms are often used interchangeably (Askedal, 2020). This conflation has parallels to the previously mentioned conflations of creation and appropriation. Our initial definition of co-realization signifies that we conceptualize this construct as entailing activities related the measurement of value realization, that is the realization of benefits. Moreover, our definition of multi-governance shows that we include key management activities to leverage this realization in our definition of multi-governance. What is equally clear is that while they do occur in parallel in the intermediate stage of multi-appropriation, the processes take more or less time and effort on the part of the developers and users. As one overall process waxes, in fact, the other wanes.

Thus, co-creation and co-realization both compete with and complement each other since they are simultaneous processes that accumulate value to the point where this construct is then measured, as is typically the case in agile IT development methods. As software is initially being created, the bulk of the process is co-creation since there are yet few end results to realize value. But as systems development projects mature and prototypes and beta code are tested and delivered, the development work tails off with benefits accruing to users from increasingly sophisticated betas. After cycling through the necessary cybernetic adjustments in the co-realization subprocess and revisiting the co-creation inputs/code transformations, the story shifts primarily to major co-realized benefits. Users appreciate the platform because they are already experiencing benefits; thus, their support of and cooperation with developers becomes manifest, an argument that has been convincingly made in the user involvement and participation literature (Barki & Hartwick, 1989, 1994).

This self-feeding process, with its heavy stress on benefits that are being realized by the many stakeholders in the consortium, is what differentiates our morphed RBV construct from classic RBV theory and from standard project management practices. Projects, employing a specific amount of resources (resource/cost goal) until a specific end date (time goal), are intended to deliver quality (performance goal) (Project Management Institute, 2017). This has been alluded to earlier but deserves repeating here in that the construct here includes a critical cybernetic looping between creation and realization.

Multi-realization is closely tied to the benefits management literature in that advocates suggest that besides investment justification and evaluation, it is necessary to establish an explicit methodology to ensure that IS development initiatives actually deliver the proposed as well as emerging benefits (Ward et al., 1996). In a typical benefits management approach, the pre-project measures of success are followed by active management and a post-project review and explicitly related to whether business needs have been satisfied. While upfront identification of potential benefits of investments in IS/IT is important, this is not sufficient for ensuring that the anticipated benefits are actually realized (Ward & Griffith, 1996). Efforts to justify potential benefits from IT investments are far more common in organizations than ensuring that anticipated benefits are actually realized (Lin & Pervan, 2003; Ward et al., 1996). MUIT articulates this point of view by including an explicit focus on co-realization and by stressing that necessary management activities need to be multi-governed.

## Path from Multi-Appropriated IT Co-Development to Multi-Realized Benefits

The argument for this path is likewise straightforward. According to the DeLone-McLean Systems Success Model (1992), benefits will accumulate across the various production subsystems. This accumulation of value has been found in studies examining the body of systems success literature (Adebowale, 2017; Petter et al., 2008). What is distinctive here is that multi-realization places its primary and foremost stress on delivering planned and promised benefits, similar to the key focus in the seminal benefits management literature (Ward & Daniel, 2006). Certainly, producing systems in a timely, costeffective manner is important. But the conceptual shift here is that support for the ongoing process depends mostly on corealization, benefits coming early and often, even from betas.

In our model, interorganizational value is realized through consortium stakeholders that combine their human and other resources with that of an IT development team; thus, stakeholders are both sponsors and participants in codevelopment. From a shorter-term perspective this can be thought of as project success for individual partners. From a longer-term perspective, though, the effect of a successful IT platform project on the entire stakeholder consortium can have substantive interorganizational impacts. Numerous scholars have likewise adopted this model in their theorizing. Smith et al. (1997), for example, argue that large-scale IT outsourcing deals are so overwhelmingly important that the firm’s proximal finances were predicted to be (and in many cases were) affected in a dramatic way.

We argue that Construct 3, multi-realized benefits, can be seen as an accumulated value from the large-scale IT initiative. If the appropriated use process is well managed across the consortium, it will produce better than acceptable results, ultimately for all partners. Partners will thus judge the entire IT initiative to be successful, on their perception of the whole. Benefits from a large-scale project will be reflected at both the project level and the interorganizational level. With this logic, thus, capabilities and IT core competencies jointly in place at the appropriation point in time can, ultimately, over the major stages of the project, yield a realization of value at the organizational level; moreover, the accumulation of benefits across the entire network will lead to interorganizational realized benefits.

## Governance, Co-Governance, and Multi-Governance

MUIT is an intentionally parsimonious theoretical model of value co-realization that integrates concepts from a divergent set of theoretical strands, but there are many remaining opportunities for improving and strengthening the model. One that calls for recognition at this point is governance. Whereas the current model extends traditional RBV theory to cooperating parties outside the firm’s boundaries, empirical extension to other externally valid elements like IO settings is itself valuable from a theoretical point of view, as Im and Straub (2015) point out. Adding new theoretical constructs and paths to explore boundary conditions is another way (Im & Straub, 2015).

What contingency would be the best candidate for such moderating propositions? In the seminal MISQ 2012 GK model of co-created value, Grover and Kohli (2012) depict a governance layer surrounding the entire set of co-created appropriation of value activities. Pointedly, since the concept of “co-” is relatively recent in business studies, there has been little-to-no theorizing as to how governance affects capabilitybuilding across multiple partners. Prior work has typically focused only on dyads in outsourcing arrangements (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Klein & Rai, 2009) and not on large consortia.

What is governance and what is co-governance? Gregory et al. (2018) define the former as “mechanisms … put in place to ensure ‘desirable’ IT-related activities and outcomes” (p. 1227). These mechanisms can be formal mechanisms, like policies and decision-making structures, or informal mechanisms, such as the relational management approaches of knowledge sharing and communication protocols (Wu et al., 2015). They go on to say that: “IT governance mechanisms in general consist of structures, processes, and relational mechanisms to enhance business/IT alignment” (p. 500). Following our argumentation throughout, co-governance thus extends these mechanisms to two or more partners. (As a term-of-art specific to our MUIT theorizing herein, multigovernance will involve 10 or more partners.)

While the IS field has a long record of studying IT governance, simply focusing on IT governance alone or governance tied to only two entities would be insufficient, given that MUIT argues how organizations need to align across a fairly large number of domains. Fortunately, research on governance is plentiful outside the IS field. Organizational science has had a longstanding focus on this, both in private and public contexts. Further, public administration and political science scholars have done extensive work here, explicitly focusing on the public sector. The IS field can benefit from the insights generated in neighboring disciplines and we next draw on such insights in theory development.

Governance of individual private organizations has historically focused on the role of the board of directors in ensuring value for shareholders (Fama & Jensen, 1983). In public management, governance relates as well to the funding and oversight roles across government agencies to ensure the realization of planned policy outcomes (Fama & Jensen, 1983). Indeed, over the past few decades, the focus has shifted from organizational governance to network governance—for much the same reasons that MUIT is being proposed, namely that value creation increasingly occurs in networks and consortia, which we term “multi-governance.” Pointedly, Yi (2018) illustrates that bridging and bonding social capital across partners lead to higher network performance, that is to say, multi-governance.

What is network or multi-governance? Network governance constitutes a “distinct form of coordinating economic activity” (Owen-Smith & Powell, 2012; Powell, 1990), which contrasts (and competes) with markets and hierarchies (Jones et al., 1997). In their seminal paper, Provan and Kenis (2008) suggest two crucial distinctions. The first distinction relates to whether a network adopts a shared governance model or a single organization assumes leadership. The second distinction is made in brokered networks by focusing on whether the network is participant governed or externally governed. Participantgoverned networks are, at one extreme, governed either collectively by the members themselves (i.e., shared), or at the other extreme, by a single network participant that takes on the role of a lead organization.

The governance model of different networks is influenced by a set of key predictors of effectiveness. Among these predictors, according to Provan and Kenis (2008), are trust, number of participants, goal consensus, and the need for network-level competencies. Vangen et al. (2015) add the nuance of governance of collaborating entities by conceptualizing governance of collaborations in terms of structures and processes that enable actors to direct, coordinate, and allocate resources for the collaboration as a whole, as well as to account for its activities. Their study also identifies a number of challenges in cross-sector collaborations that need to be addressed in order for collaborations to be successful, including the degree of centralization, degree of formalization, incentives, and control (Wegner & Koetz, 2016). Thus, the concept of network governance is widely acknowledged. In fact, Klijn and Koppenjan (2012) argue that network governance has evolved into a full-fledged theory in its own right; hence, network governance is the last theoretical strand integrated into MUIT.

The logic for the path moderation simply extends the baseline arguments proposed here. The RBV-derived paths in MUIT can be extended to consortia and alliances—that is, networks of organizational entities—and that, largely as a result of this critical extension of the theoretical scope to numerous disparate entities, governance issues are highly likely to impact the quality of systems being created. A good multi-governance structure, in short, will lead to superior IT products. Poorly organized alliances will result in marginal or failed systems and many fewer multi-realized benefits.

## Why We Need A New Theory

We have advanced several arguments throughout the paper for why we need a new IT theory that combines theoretical strands from RBV, AST, appropriation theory, the DeLone McLean model, network governance theory, and the benefits management literature. A summary of these arguments here will hopefully reify why our integrated theory makes sense for the IT field, in particular in digital platform co-development. Moreover, there are topical reasons why a new theory might be needed to deal with the always-changing technological environment.

First, Grover and Kohli (2012) set forth a highly cited framework for future work in the MISQ special issue on cocreation, but their framework is not testable per se. In our view, its conceptual elements called for a decomposing and repurposing into a Gregor Type IV model (2006) so that the IT field could rigorously test what it was implicitly proposing. Pointedly, the conceptual elements they articulate include: (1) resources like assets and knowledge (as in RBV) as well as complementary capabilities, (2) appropriation of value (as in AST and appropriation theory), and (3) value co-creation (framed as value realization in the benefits management literature). All these elements are integrated via MUIT.

Second, the theoretical and literature strands enumerated just above have led to intellectual fragmentation and construct muddiness. To clarify the phenomenon of large-scale coproduction of platforms, two conflated constructs—value creation and value appropriation—have been deconstructed, bifurcated, and reframed into parsimonious MUIT paths.

Third, large-scale digital platform development, a phenomenon that is critically important in contemporary system practice and society, has not been previously theorized from a distinctly IS perspective. Not only is scale an important consideration in why some IT initiatives are successful and some are not, we argue consortium governance is likewise absolutely crucial, explaining why MUIT is both an integrated and moderated model.

Fourth, prior theorizing is largely silent on the mediating process of IT co-development/co-production/coimplementation. Previous RBV-based studies have stressed the antecedents of single organizational success far more than either the mediation or final value realization stage. In contrast, MUIT stresses both. It envisions the co-production with its cybernetic feedback loops as a mediating process where, over time, co-creation yields to co-realization.

Fifth, RBV-based research studying how firms derive value from IT has struggled to confirm RBV as an overall strong theory base. Liang et al.’s meta-analysis of IT-focused studies (Liang et al., 2010) found only “weak” support for RBV/IT capabilities leading to higher performance. In contrast, Newbert’s (2007) meta-analysis of RBV management studies found “modest” support, which is certainly more encouraging but was still not satisfying to Newbert (2007). Indeed, to address these cautionary findings, both Newbert (2007, 2008) and Liang et al. (2010) call for improved models that respond more directly to contemporary organizational environments. As Newbert (2008) says: “[Scholars should] avoid the tendency to test models reflecting early incarnations of the RBV and instead test those that incorporate more contemporary theoretical extensions” (p. 745; bolding and italics added). Arguing that the current modeling of the IT setting is being “overshadowed” (Liang et al., 2010, p, 1151) by other factors, they suggest that we look for a “better…RBV model” (p. 1152; emphasis added). Hence our new “multi-” lens with its boundary condition of large-scale IT co-production may be able to substantially improve explained variances as could the moderating power of multi-governance.

Sixth is an argument not entirely unrelated to the previous one. It is clear that technological developments in, for example, cloud computing, machine learning, and platform co-sharing along with co-opetition continue to push boundaries of two-way, interorganizational open innovations. Many call this “digital transformation,” in that organizations are being radically altered as they integrate emerging technologies into their core processes (Vial, 2019). Not only was this effect predicted at the start of the ecommerce revolution in Wheeler’s theory of net-enabled innovation capabilities (2002), but it also lies at the heart of Weill and Vitale’s work on atomic e-commerce models (2001). Weill and Vitale’s shared resources model is a good example of a residual stakeholder model that Barney may have had in mind when he speaks about RBV studies going beyond single organizations and shareholder value (Barney, 2018). Indeed, metrics to capture value at the network level have been utilized for a while now (Straub et al., 2004), but more needs to be done to understand why co-creation, codevelopment, and co-realization are necessary for equitable distribution of value.

Our basic argument, hence, is that if we can incorporate such “other factors” into our theory-based models as the interplay between creation and realization in the overall systems development process, explained variances could very well be dramatically improved. In MUIT, the cybernetic adjustments that are part of Construct 2, quality of multi-appropriated IT development, as well as the moderating influence of multigovernance mechanisms are novel attempts to do just this. Theorizing is one thing; testing will no doubt provide either supporting, mixed, or confounding evidence for these claims.

## Implications

In this final section, we contemplate how researchers and practitioners could possibly use our theory to rethink IToriented partnerships and consortia. First, it seems likely that quantitative and even analytical modelers could choose to elaborate on or test one or more of the theoretical paths, as suggested in CT2 (Table 3). In such cases, they will need to choose settings where “co-” elements are evident and conducive to assumptions, parameter-setting, and measurement. Whereas many researchers have studied cooperative enterprises through perceptions, other approaches could include the richer documentation of qualitative work.

As articulated in CT3, we believe that network governance issues will be critical for the processes in MUIT to be effective. Placing it into the MUIT theoretical framing as multi-governance should show exactly how this enacts its influence in the form of moderation of other causal or loosely causal paths. Moreover, another aspect of cogovernance is fair attention to consortium stakeholders to avoid winner-takes-all scenarios. The importance of this is highlighted in appropriation theory (e.g. Coff, 1999) and should also be framed as a characteristic of good cogovernance in tests of MUIT.

Whereas we are fully convinced that there are important distinctions between co-creation and co-realization, we enjoin fellow researchers to examine this in practice. It is uncertain whether distinct conceptualizations for “cocreation” and “co-realization” (or even “multi-creation” and “multi-realization”) will ever gain currency, but there could well be crucial differences in the patterns of deployment of MUIT processes, as put forth in CT4. Believing that both process and variance approaches to studying this phenomenon can be devised, our implicit propositions can be further studied through mixed methods models.

## Praxis Issues

The value generation potential of a co-development consortia consists of the individual partner resources as well as the overall resources of the consortium and their using these to jointly create innovative capabilities. Carefully selected resources may strengthen other similar resources in the consortium or allow the partnership to eliminate deficiencies in its portfolio of resources. In building IT capabilities at the platform level, the consortium must carefully combine, mobilize, and deploy IT-based resources from the stakeholders.

As articulated in our causal link between capability creation and appropriated IT development efforts, we believe that capability building will be critical in building the strategic capacity of the consortium. Managers should engage in establishing and maintaining such efforts because managerial decisions affect resource accumulation as well as inimitable resource utilization and capability building in the consortium.

With respect to our mediating multi-appropriation phase, where the logic sees co-creation and co-realization operating in parallel, our theorizing joins two existing themes from the IS literature: (1) co-creation and (2) an extension of the benefits management literature that deals with realization/corealization. Both themes are practical in nature and the literature offers practical suggestions related to how organizations can work together to create value from IT and how value or benefits from IT investments can be maximized.

Our work should be seen as a sensitizing device for managers in complex, IT-intensive industries or contexts such as digital platforms. Our argument that IT value should not only be co-created at times but also needs to be corealized is essentially practical in itself, and managers in modern organizations need to be aware of and understand this distinction in order to utilize the potential of IT and maximize the return on their IT investments. Our paper offers initial suggestions on how these elements can be combined, tried in practice, and further refined.

In terms of our logic for why and how value is realized across platform consortia, this should result from streamlined collaboration with partners in the networked economy, a requirement for modern managers rather than an option. Our theory provides intellectual evidence for this point. Further, we provide practice with ideas to motivate collaboration as this is likely to yield greater benefits than organizations can hope to realize individually.

We argue that if organizations co-create and co-realize value from IT, several forms of interorganizational value can occur. In particular, two forms of return deserve mentioning. First, when organizations pool and deploy resources together in a coordinated, strategic manner, they can develop stronger and more complete products and target larger markets than they could do individually. Improved products and increasing market shares imply a potential for greater benefits. Second, coordinated and strategic pooling of resources should be seen as a means of mitigating risk, a winner-takes-all effect, as organizations gain access to additional resources without having to establish/maintain them in-house (Lacity et al., 2010).

The moderated paths are almost intuitive. Managers who pay attention to co-governance will likely have more success, and thus recognition, at the organizational and interorganizational levels. Ignoring the crucial aspect of co-governance could lead to disaster or less-than-optimal results.

## Conclusion

MUIT is a theory styled to respond dynamically to the contemporary phenomenon of digital platforms and the monumental changes in the working relationships presently occurring in the worlds of organizational theory and practice. We have drawn on a limited variety of theoretical and literature bases to advance a Gregor Type IV variance model of organizational cooperation. Testing MUIT is outside the scope of this paper, but we encourage researchers to consider this framing for their studies of large-scale consortia of private sector firms, nonprofit entities, and government agencies. The implicit goal of our theory building is, namely, to impact the success of future large-scale consortia joining together for the creation of digital platforms.

## About the Authors

Leif Skiftenes Flak is a professor of information systems in the Department of Information Systems, University of Agder, Kristiansand, Norway and former chair of the same department. He is the director of the multidisciplinary research center CeDiT—Centre for Digital Transformation. Flak has close to 20 years of experience with research on information systems in general and digitalization in the public sector in particular. He has extensive national and international project experience and has served on a variety of reference groups and expert committees in organizations such as the Norwegian Health Directorate, the Norwegian Auditor General and the Norwegian Agency for Public and Financial Management. Flak is also a cofounder and board member of GoForIT—Norway´s arena for knowledge development at the intersection between digitalization and sustainability. Flak is an active member of international academic communities related to digital government and information systems.

Hans Solli-Sæther is a professor of strategy in the Department of International Business, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Norway. He holds a doctoral degree in leadership and organization from BI Norwegian Business School and a master’s of science degree in information systems from the University of Oslo. His research interests are international business, management information systems, with a particular emphasis on topics of outsourcing and offshoring. Solli-Sæther is an active member of the European International Business Academy. Prior to his academic career he served as the chief information officer of Norway Post.

Detmar Straub is a research professor and the IBIT Fellow at Temple University’s Fox School of Business. He is Regents Professor emeritus of the University System of Georgia, formerly holding an endowed professorship in the Robinson College of Business at Georgia State University. He has published over 225 papers in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, Journal of AIS, Decision Sciences, and Organization Science. Much of his current research theorizes about contemporary issues such as digital transformation and online platforms although he has an abiding interest in a wide variety of technological innovations including cybersecurity advances.
