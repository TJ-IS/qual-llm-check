# The transformational dimension in the realization of business value from information technology

Shirley Gregor <sup>a,\*</sup>, Michael Martin <sup>a</sup>, Walter Fernandez a Steven Stern <sup>a</sup>, Michael Vitale <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Australian National University, Canberra, ACT, Australia <sup>b</sup> Australian Graduate School of Management, Sydney, NSW, Australia

Accepted 30 April 2006

## Abstract

Econometric studies have highlighted factors that appear to explain the diferential efects of information technology (IT) on productivity at the firm level. Central to these explanations is the concept of organizational transformation; that value realization from IT depends on time-consuming investments in organizational change and results in new, often intangible, organizational assets. The aim of this study was to further investigate the concept of IT-enabled organizational transformation as a component of the value realized from IT at the firm level. Survey data was analyzed from respondents from 1050 businesses of varying sizes and across industries. Transformational benefits were found to exist as a distinct benefit category and to be closely related to other forms of ITgenerated business benefits. They were also an important component of overall IT business value. Qualitative data illustrated these findings and pointed to possible complex causal relationships in the generation of IT value. The findings have implications for models of IT business value generation and for managerial practice.

## 1. Introduction

In 1987, Robert Solow, a Nobel laureate in economics, famously observed: ‘‘You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics’’ (Solow, 1987). The apparent failure of a massive investment in information technology (IT) to contribute to productivity growth became known as the ‘Productivity Paradox’, as early economic analyses failed to find evidence of the expected relationship between IT investment and productivity increases. The Productivity Paradox led to many US based research studies on the economic efects of IT and the paradox is now regarded as largely resolved. Brynjolfsson (1993) concluded that the paradox existed due to a combination of: (i) measurement errors in both IT capital and outputs; (ii) significant time lags between investment and productivity gains; (iii) management practices, and their importance in realizing the full potential of technology opportunities; and (iv) redistribution of productivity, in that some firms gain higher productivity returns than their competitors. Further fruitful streams of research have followed this line of argument. Firm-level econometric studies have highlighted factors that appear to explain the diferential efects of computer use on productivity across organizations. Central to a number of these explanations is the concept of IT-related organizational transformation: that is, the changes occurring over time in organizational systems and structures that are necessary to reap the full benefits from the use of information and communication technologies (ICT)<sup>1</sup>. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2003) showed that value realization from ICT required relatively large and time-consuming investments in complementary inputs, or organizational capital, which may sometimes be omitted in conventional calculations of productivity. Studies in the banking sector have shown that many of the efects of ICT depend upon the extent to which organizations couple ICT investment with organizational redesign and other managerial decisions (Murnane et al., 1999). Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2000b) argue that a significant component of the value of ICT investment comes from its ability to leverage complementary organizational investments such as business processes and work practices; and these investments in turn lead to productivity increases by reducing costs and, more importantly, by allowing organizations to increase output quality, along with ofering new products and improved customer service.

This emphasis in econometric studies on organizational change and transformation is not, however, fully captured in a number of prominent models of the impact of IT in organizations. DeLone and McLean (2003) in their ten-year update of the DeLone and Mclean success model (1992) depict ‘‘net benefits’’ as the most important success measure for IS, with net benefits referring to ‘‘cost savings, expanded markets, incremental additional sales, reduced search costs and time savings’’ (p. 26). Their updated model is a dynamic one, with the realized net benefits feeding back to further intention to use and user satisfaction. The notion of organizational change and transformation as an important aspect of success, however, is not included. Further, an integrative framework for information technology business value published recently by Melville et al. (2004) is uni-directional and omits the cyclical processes of organizational transformation and learning and the time-lagged nature of benefits that have been shown in econometric studies to be vital in understanding IT value generation. Although these authors recognize complementary organizational changes as a factor in achieving value from IT, they do not consider changed organizational forms and capabilities themselves as assets that result from IT imple mentation. Rivard et al. (2006) provide a model which integrates both the competitive strategy and resource-based views of the contribution of IT to business performance but their model is also uni-directional. The need to include a dynamic aspect is supported by organizational literature. As argued by Karl Weick (1979), organizations exhibit interlocking behaviours with feedback as a central phenomenon, leading to reciprocal or mutu al causality. The importance of the cyclical causal processes is related to the magnifying or reducing nature of the interaction among several variables (for example, IT use and IT skills development).

Against this background, the aim of the current study was:

To investigate further the nature of IT-enabled organizational transformation and its part in the business value realized from IT at the firm level.

The study tests the proposition that organizational transformational benefits are closely related to other benefits from ICT use, including strategic, informational and transactional benefits. The study is based on a large-scale survey of 1050 organizations of varying sizes across most industries in Australia, supplemented by structured interviews in 50 of these organizations. Australian organizations ofer a fertile ground for ICT value studies as Australia’s uptake of ICT from the mid-1990s has been strong. Australia had the third highest investment in ICT in 2001 as a share of total GDP compared with other OECD countries, up from ninth position in 1980 (Productivity Commission, 2004). Parham et al. (2001) argue that the adoption of ICT contributed to Australia’s productivity surge in the 1990s, as it did in the US.

The study has theoretical significance in that it has implications for models of IT value realization, indicating that organizational transformation should be treated as an important dimension of IS success. There are also practical implications, as the study describes the consequences of managerial attention to organizational transformation and change as an accompaniment to IS implementation. In addition, the study provides an instrument that includes a transformational dimension for assessing IT business value.

The paper proceeds as follows. The conceptualization of IT business benefits in the prior literature is explored and a transformational framework for IT value generation is outlined. The survey study and structured interview process are described, followed by an analysis of results. Excerpts from the interview data are used to illustrate the nature of ICT organizational transformation. The paper concludes with an evaluation of the find ings and their significance.

## 2. Conceptualizing it business value

There are diverse perspectives on the nature of the benefits of IT for organizations, with a meta-analysis of IT payof variables provided by Kohli and Devaraj (2003). The recent review article by Melville et al. (2004) describes IT business value as referring to the organizational performance impacts of IT, including productivity gains, increased profitability, cost reduction, and competitive advantage. These authors define IT business value as:

the organizational performance impacts of information technology at both the intermediate process level and the organizational-wide level, and comprising both efi ciency impacts and competitive impacts (p. 287).

We argue, however, that this conceptualization of IT business value should be extended to include an organizational transformation dimension, as indicated in work that has addressed the productivity paradox. In this work, organizational transformation is seen as a component of the business value resulting from IT and also a driver of further change.

There is a body of work supporting the view of transformation as a driver of change. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2000b) review the evidence that links investments in IT with higher productivity and organizational transformation and concludes that transformational aspects such as new business processes, new skills and new organizational and industry structures are the major drivers of the gains from IT. Further these complementary investments and the resulting assets, may be an order of magnitude larger than the investment in IT itself. IT is seen not as a traditional capital investment but as a ‘‘general purpose technology’’ (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg, 1995). A general purpose technology provides for a higher than normal rate of return because it facilitates complementary innovations. Case studies show that to be successful, firms typically need to adopt ICT as part of a cluster of mutually reinforcing organizational changes (Milgrom and Roberts, 1990). Large-sample statistical studies shed further light on the relationship between organizational change and IT value. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2000a) developed a fixed-efects productivity model that divided IT benefits into two categories: a category for IT investment over time and a category for fixed firm characteristics (slowly changing organizational practices). The fixed-efect component was found to significantly afect the returns to IT investment. The efects of IT have also been found to be substantially larger over longer time periods (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000a), suggesting that the longer-term returns represent the efects of organizational change accompanying IT. This need for organizational change associated with computerization is further supported by the more general arguments for organizational learning and change as advocated by writers including Argyris (1999) and Senge (1990).

There is further work that shows transformation also as a business benefit arising from IT-enabled change. A number of studies highlight problems with this conceptualization, one being the dificulties in measuring intangible assets and a second the problem of the direction of causality in relationships. Traditional growth accounting measures are thought to underestimate the efect of organizational change factors and complementary innovation (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000b). Some studies have attempted to assess quantitatively various aspects of organizational change and the relationship with IT use. Bresnahan et al. (2000) found in a study of 400 large firms that levels of IT investment were greater in firms that were decentralized and had a greater investment in human capital in education and training. Complementarities between existing IT and firm work practices were also found to be a key determinant of a firm’s ability to adopt new technologies (Bresnahan and Greenstein, 1997), in a study which suggested a pattern of mutual causation between organizational capabilities and IT adoption. A recent project in Australia provided 18 exemplar case studies of organizations that have optimized their IT spending (NOIE, 2003). This study found that the firms studied obtained a number of non-productivity related benefits, referred to as ‘‘organization transformation’’, that included changes in the business model on which the organization is based, improved skill levels and skill mix.

The current study is based on a framework that takes into account the complexity of a situation where organizational transformation and change is seen as both an asset (an output) and also an input to further cycles of change (Fig. 1). This framework links influences on IT value to factors in the external environment, in the industry, and within the organization. The model builds on prior literature including Gregor and Johnston (2001), Johnston and Gregor (2000) and Melville et al. (2004).

This framework shows that at the highest level, ICT use within an organization will be afected by the external environment, including government activities and policies, global economic conditions, society and cultural issues, technology, and the availability and accessibility of infrastructure. These influences are largely outside of the control of individ ual organizations and the industries within which they operate. These influences must be considered, however, as they can have a strong efect on what ICT strategies a firm can or must employ (Johnston and Gregor, 2000).

Further, the industry within which an organization operates is also expected to influence the manner in which ICT can be employed. The nature of the product in some industries, for example, makes online trading more or less feasible. The efects of successful use of ICT within one firm can lead to changes in an industry as other organizations in the industry copy novel ideas or react as a result of competitive pressure (Gregor and Johnston, 2001). Within an organization both management factors in general, and factors more specifically related to ICT, are expected to influence the extent to which value is gained from ICT use. General management factors include physical capital resources, human capital resources and organizational capital resources (Barney, 1991). ICT-related factors include ICT spending, ICT strategy, and ICT management capability (Dehning and Richardson, 2002).

The research framework suggests that these influences are connected in a continuous cycle of learning and change. The outcomes of ICT use are not just a matter of increased eficiency and strategic advantage, but also include organizational transformation, with new ways of doing business and organizational learning, which feeds back to further changes in management practice and the application of IT, both in the organization and possibly in the industry in which it operates. Although this transformational model is used as an overall guiding framework, the current study focuses on the components of ‘‘IT business value’’ in the right-hand portion of the model.

We identified a study by Mirani and Lederer (1998) as providing the most appropriate framework for the assessment of IT benefits at the firm level.<sup>2</sup> This study gives a comprehensive review of the literature and uses theory advanced by Turner and Lucas (1985) and extended by Weill (1992). Turner and Lucas (1985) classified IT investments in terms of the organizational objective they would achieve, being strategic, informational or transactional benefits, or a combination of these. Strategic IT investment is intended to alter the manner in which an organization competes or the nature of its product. Investment in IT for informational purposes is intended to provide information and communication for decision making in the firm. Transactional IT investment is intended to support operational management and cut costs. Some empirical support for this framework is provided by Weill (Weill and Olson, 1989; Weill, 1992). Mirani and Lederer (1998) developed a theoretical framework from this prior work with the aim of deriving an ‘‘empirically validated instrument that identifies the dimensions of organizational benefits of IS’’ (p. 804). They operationalized their framework to give an instrument that measured strategic, informational and transactional benefits, which was tested and refined in a cross-sectional study of 178 IS projects and validated with additional data. This study provides a rigorously developed instrument as a basis for assessing the organizational benefits of IS/IT.

However, we believe additional work, particularly a number of econometric studies, indicates that an additional dimension could be added to Mriani and Lederer’s framework, that of IT transformational benefit. Mirani and Lederer focussed on the organizational efectiveness literature in developing their framework, but they did not refer to studies in economics that indicate that the transformational outcomes of IT use are of considerable importance in realizing further benefit over a period of time and thus are a benefit in their own right (for example, see Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000b).

To clarify the terminology used in the current study, IT business value is an encompassing term to refer to the multiple dimensions of IT benefits, including the dimensions identified by Mirani and Lederer (1998) of strategic, informational, and transactional benefits. A further dimension of IT business value posited is IT transformational benefit<sup>3</sup>, a construct that refers to the result of changes in structure and capacity in a firm that may accompany investment in IT. These changes can be of value to the organization as they provide an asset giving a greater capacity for further future benefit. The construct is described as referring to a state of having new or improved business processes, new skills and new organizational structures (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000b). A congruent description arises from in-depth case studies (NOIE, 2003, p. 6), where transformation refers to ‘‘the increase of the capacity and capabilities of an organization to address its future business environment more efectively than in the past’’.

Fig. 2 shows the relationships between transformational and other types of benefit that were deduced from the foregoing discussion. The accompanying hypotheses are:

H1: IT transformational benefits are recognized as a distinct category of benefits

H2: IT transformational benefits are closely related to other benefits (informational, strategic and transactional) that can be realized from ICT use.

H3: IT transformational benefits can be an important component of the overall business value realized from ICT use.

## 3. The study

The study had two phases, a large-scale telephone survey of respondents from 1050 organizations and follow-up structured interviews of 50 of these firms. The study was part of a larger project, which investigated the influences on IT value generation shown in Fig. 1 more fully (Gregor et al., 2005).

The survey scales for IT business value (shown in Appendix A) were developed following the procedure described by Churchill (1979). The original pool of items for the informational, transactional and strategic benefits was taken from Mirani and Lederer (1998). The domain and definition of the transformational benefit construct was identified from the literature as described previously. Additional understanding of this construct and oth ers was derived in focus group discussions. A total of 27 organizations took part in four focus groups which were held in Melbourne, Canberra, and Sydney. An expert group comprising two senior academics and two senior IT consultants generated a pool of items for the transformational construct from the understanding developed in the preparatory work. The item pool for the whole IT business value scale was then refined and reduced through further discussion and walkthroughs with members of the wider project team (comprising five academics, three senior consultants in an IT firm and three employees of a government department concerned with IT development and use). Some of the items in the original Mirani and Lederer (1998) instrument were reworded slightly and some dis carded (reducing a 25 item scale to 17 items). Subsequent analysis of the final instrument showed Cronbach a was acceptable for each of the four sets of variables, being 0.88 for the strategic items, 0.87 for the transactional items, 0.90 for the informational items, 0.88 for the transformational items, and 0.95 for the IT business value scale overall. The scope of the study precluded the additional steps needed to establish criterion validity for the new transformational construct as recommended by Churchill (1979). It would be necessary to identify firms where other measures or estimates of transformation could be found and apply the new scale in those settings, a step requiring further research.

For the IT business value scales respondents were asked to indicate their degree of agreement that the use of IT had, over the last 18 months, contributed to each business benefit. Responses were on a scale of 1–10, where 1 was strongly disagree and 10 was strongly agree. The unit of analysis was the respondent’s view of the organization’s experience with IT over the previous 18 months, including perceptions of IT generally, inclusive of infrastructure, the full range of applications and service from the IT function. The expert group which worked on the survey development considered that this period was adequate to capture benefits from IT use without undue loss of reliability due to poor recall. The full survey instrument in the larger project of which this study formed part gathered data about the individual respondent, the organization, the ICT resources in the organization, the organization’s environment, structure, and management practices, the perceived business value of ICT use, and perceived inhibitors to efective ICT use.<sup>4</sup> The questions concerning ICT resources set the scene for subsequent questions about ICT value, by defining ICT in terms of common business applications and functions and infrastructure.

The full survey instrument was tested after its initial construction in seven cognitive interviews. Interviewees were asked to verbalize their thoughts as they responded to the survey, allowing ambiguities and possible misunderstandings to be identified and addressed. This process led to refinements in both the number and wording of questions so that the instrument was of reasonable length and all questions could be more clearly understood by respondents. Twenty interviews were then conducted as a pilot for the survey and any issues noted as relevant by the interviewers were addressed by further refining the survey. Typically these issues were related to minor changes in wording of specific questions. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), through its Statistical Clearinghouse, is required to vet and approve any survey carried out on behalf of a Government agency. Such vetting is required to ensure the statistical integrity of the survey instrument and to ensure that publicly funded surveys do not constitute an undue burden on businesses and attain relevant ethical standards. The survey instrument used in this research received ABS Statistical Clearinghouse approval.

The sampling frame for the survey was drawn from the Dun and Bradstreet database of Australian organizations. The sampling methodology used stratification by business size, so that the final sample included 225 large organizations (100 or more employees, broken down as 56 organizations with 100–150 employees, 27 with 151–200, 69 with 201–500, 46 with 501–1000, 40 with 1001–5000, and 7 with 5001+ employees), 450 medium organizations (20–99 employees, broken down as 311 with 20–50 employees and 139 with 51–100 employees), and 375 small organizations (5–19 employees), across 15 of the ANZSIC industry classifications. Appendix B shows the breakdown of the sample by size and industry. The classification of businesses as small, medium and large followed the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) designations of business sizes in Australia. Obviously, such size descriptors are particular to Australia and the descriptors should be interpreted in that context. The stratum weights were calculated using the most recent Australian Bureau of Statistics tabulation (ABS Business Survey) of the total numbers of Australian businesses of each of the three sizes, reweighting businesses according to the prevalence of their respective sizes within the set of all Australian businesses. The sample sizes chosen within each stratum reflect a strategy of deliberately oversampling within large, and to a lesser extent, medium organizations in order to more clearly explore business value realized from IT use within such businesses. Note that the scope of the survey did not include micro enterprises employing fewer than 5 employees. The initial sampling frame included 5380 organizations. Telephone contact was made with approximately 63% of the organizations. The overall response rate was calculated as 31%, being the proportion of completed surveys compared with the total number of organizations with whom a connection was made. Stratum weights were applied by business size for all analyses<sup>5</sup>.

Telephone interviewing for the large-scale survey was done by Colmar Brunton Social Research (CBSR) using a Computer Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI) facility. Using an external organization for data gathering had several advantages. The company is an accredited organization in CATI with Interviewer Quality Control Australia (IQCA) and was able to lend its considerable experience to improving survey design and survey processes. The company was able to provide interviewers who were skilled in locating an appropriate person to respond and who were well trained in interviewing and in following up to increase the response rate. They also had excellent facilities for computerized capture and organization of data. The disadvantages included the cost of the exercise (although substantial funding had been supplied by a research grant) and the fact that the researchers were at times not so intimately involved in data collection<sup>6</sup>.

The interviewee was identified as someone in a senior management position who dealt with IT issues in the organization. Telephone interviews took 15 min on average. The respondents included 256 managers, 117 owner/partners, 363 ICT director/managers, 76 senior executives, and 238 not classified. Valid responses to all 22 business-value questions were provided by 705 businesses and analyses are based on this sample.

While the response rate to the survey was quite high for a survey of this kind and scope, the potential for non-response bias needs to be considered. The use of industry best practice standards by CBSR in conducting the survey, including four repeated call-backs in the event of initial non-response and the use of a pre-survey letter of introduction/invitation, was part of a strategy to minimize non-response bias. While it was not possible to survey non-respondents, a technique proposed by Armstrong and Overton (1977) was used to assess the extent of non-response bias in our setting. The technique involves comparison of surveyed characteristics between early and late respondents, under the reasoning that the last respondents should be most like non-respondents (for example, being more likely to be those who require multiple call-backs). Demographic factors and business values were compared for the two groups of 100 early respondents and 100 late respondents, and no significant differences were found between the two samples of respondents. Formally, t tests were used to compare the two groups over a variety of characteristics, with resultant p-values ranging from about 0.1 up to about 0.8 over the various characteristics. In particular, with respect to the overall measure of business value, the p-value of the relevant t test was 0.21. As a result, the non-response bias was considered negligible for this study.

Follow-up face-to-face structured interviews were conducted by CBSR researchers with 50 of the participants who had responded in the large scale survey stage and volunteered to take part in a follow-up interview<sup>7</sup>. The structured interviews covered (i) How implementation of ICT impacted the organization; (ii) how the organization managed these uses and implementations; (iii) results achieved from using and implementing ICT; (iv) contribution of ICT to overall organizational change; and (v) current organizational challenges and the role of ICT in facing these challenges. An interview protocol was developed to ensure a consistent approach to the interviews. The interview data was analyzed using open-coding techniques borrowed from the grounded theory method to identify common themes (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). The emerging set of themes and its corresponding quotations are used here to illustrate the survey’s findings, with a fuller analysis the subject of future work.

## 4. Results and discussion

Factor analysis was used as a tool to investigate meaningful constructs within the highdimensional survey response data. The factor analysis was carried out using Varimax rotation and the resultant factors were derived using Thompson’s regression scores (see Lattin et al., 2003). Both single-factor and four-factor solutions yielded reasonable and interpretable solutions. The single factor solution indicated a single higher-order construct corresponding to the expected over-arching IT business value construct. The proportion of variability in the original 22-dimensional space captured by this one-dimensional common factor was 49.1%, a reasonably high proportion for a single-factor solution. Moreover, Cronbach’s a for the 22-variable grouping was high, at 0.95. (see Nunnally (1967). The variable for IT business value used in further analysis was formed from the Thompson’s regression scores arising from this one-factor solution (Lattin et al., 2003).

The four-factor solution, following the hypothesized number of dimensions, was also readily interpretable, as shown in Table 1, with the four factors identified as informational business benefit, transactional business benefit, strategic business benefit, andtransformational benefit. Factors were defined selecting the highest loading in each row subject to the con dition that the loading exceeded 0.35 (for a discussion of the choice and interpretation of relevant factor loadings, see Lattin et al., 2003). The four identified factors aligned very closely with the four constructs identified as representing distinct types of business value a priori. For this solution, the proportion of variability captured by the four factors was 61%. The Cronbach’s a for each of the four sets of variables showed that each set of vari ables can be satisfactorily summarized using a one-dimensional construct (see previous section). The diferences in reliability between the four factors were not statistically significant.

Note that factor analysis is an exploratory statistical tool, and, as such, is most useful in detecting meaningful structure prior to a more in-depth analysis. In the current paper, this structure is described and discussed in the context of its meaning in terms of both known and new benefit constructs. Of course, each of the four factors loads on all survey questions to some extent, and it is the interpretation of these loadings that allows us to restrict attention to the questions relevant to each factor. For each of the four factors defined above, loadings below 0.35 are regarded as negligible (Lattin et al., 2003). While such choices are common in an exploratory analysis such as this, concern over the size of the cross-loadings on items between factors led us to consider another four-factor solution using OBLIMIN rather than Varimax rotation in the factor analysis. As anticipated, the cross-loadings under this rotation were generally lower than under Varimax rotation. The four factors identified under this solution were very similar to those for which the loadings are reported in Table 1 – the alternative four-factor solution yielded factor scores highly correlated (r = 0.92–0.95) with the factor scores derived under Varimax rotation. Given the exploratory nature of our analysis, we elected to persist with the Varimax solution rather than the OBLIMIN solution as the Varimax solution resulted in a substantial increase in explained variability (61%) over the OBLIMIN solution (47%), with little qualitative diference between the resultant factor scores. We stress again the exploratory nature of our analysis – we do not intend our factor scores to be interpreted as formal measures of IT value, but rather as broad indicators of value.

A key question remaining is whether the high correlation between the organizational transformation benefit and other benefit types results in it being redundant in describing overall business value given the other benefit types (Table 3). That is, does organizational transformation benefit remain useful if we already have knowledge of other benefit measurements?

To answer this question, a partial regression approach was adopted, whereby two linear models were fit, the first model relating overall value to the three specific benefit measures (strategic, informational and transactional benefit), and the second model relating organizational transformation benefit to the same three measures. Then, a linear model was fit relating the residuals from the first model fit to the residuals from the second model fit (see Fig. 3). This final model seeks to relate that part of overall value not adequately explained by the three measures of strategic, informational, and transactional benefit to the component of organizational transformation benefit not itself explained by those three benefit measurements. In other words, this model seeks to evaluate the extent to which organizational transformation benefit can explain overall business value over and above the explanation provided by the other three benefit measures. As such, the model allows an answer to the question of whether organizational transformation benefit is a useful new benefit construct. The final regression model in this case was highly significant, with an R-squared value of 0.94 suggesting that organizational transformation benefit does, indeed, provide additional significant explanatory power over overall value above that provided by the other benefit types.<sup>8</sup> If only the first three factors (benefit types) were considered in forming a measure of overall business value, we would fail to take into account a statistically significant component of business value.

## 5. Conclusions

The aim of the study was to investigate the nature of IT-related organizational transformation and its part in the value realized from IT at the firm level. Econometric studies have indicated that processes of organizational change and transformation and the resultant intangible assets are important for productivity gains from IT. Models of value realization from IT in the information systems literature, however, have not fully captured the concept of organizational transformation as an important component of IT value.

The study used data from a large-scale telephone survey of respondents from 1050 orga nizations and 50 follow-up interviews. Factor analyses showed organizational transforma tional benefits were recognized as a separate category of benefit, distinct from informational, strategic and transactional benefits. A single factor solution was also readily interpretable, showing that the four categories of benefit were all components of one overarching factor, IT business value. The ratings given to the diferent benefit categories difered significantly. Across the organizations, informational benefits were rated significantly high er than the other benefits and transactional benefits were rated significantly lower than the others. The ratings for strategic and transformational benefits were not significantly diferent from one another. These results show that organizations believe they gain greatest value from improved informational flows with IT, with faster and easier access to information and improved information for managing the information. Transformational benefits also were rated relatively highly, with firms perceiving benefits from more highly skilled employees, new business plans and models and improved capabilities, structures, and processes.

Although transformational benefits were recognized as a category distinct from the other benefit categories, there was a relationship between the realization of the diferent benefits. That is, firms who had a high value for one benefit type also tended to have a high value on the other benefit types. The organizational transformational benefit was highly correlated with other benefits, suggesting that firms who manage to achieve transformational benefits are also likely to achieve the other benefits to a larger degree. The organi zational transformational benefit variable was also highly correlated with the first-order construct of overall IT business value. Statistical analysis indicated that the transformational benefit provided additional significant explanatory power for the first-order factor above that provided by the other second-order factors.

The potential limitations of the study should be recognized. The 22-item benefit scale asked for a subjective assessment of IT benefits, and the limitations of such an approach are acknowledged. Previous studies, however, have shown that perceptual measures of organizational performance correlate highly with objective measures (Dess and Robinson 1984; Tallon et al., 2001; Venkatraman and Ramanujam, 1987), indicating that managers are in a good position to assess the value of ICT to their enterprise. The survey instrument was not subjected to testing for criterion validity (Churchill, 1979), although the thorough development process and the fact that the items for three of the four dimensions of business value were taken from a previously validated instrument (Mirani and Lederer, 1998) adds to the credibility of the instrument.

Although the cross-sectional study cannot indicate causal relationships, the qualitative interview data gave some insights into possible causal factors at work. Relatively intangible assets mentioned in describing the benefits (outcomes) of computer systems included greater understanding of technology and the capabilities of the firm’s information systems, an increased capacity for further change, devolved autonomy and improved business processes. A number of these outcomes equip the firm for further change in a step-by-step process of mutual causation. For example, learning about a new system and its capabilities meant that a firm could undertake further changes, as in extending the system, realizing unanticipated benefits or integrating IT across business processes. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that cross-sectional studies of the type obtained from our survey do not typically allow the identification of causal factors, and that the establishment of causation requires other evidence. A longitudinal study would be desirable as a follow-up to this study to allow a more detailed assessment of causal factors.

The 18-month time-frame within which respondents were asked to assess their experiences of use of ICT within their organizations is acknowledged as a relatively short period of time over which the extent and nature of benefits could be assessed. This time-frame was chosen as one over which the reliability of survey results would be reasonable across the size, industry and other features of the firms sampled. Nevertheless, there is some question as to whether 18 months is a long enough period to assess various derived benefits, and this is a limitation of the study. This limitation notwithstanding, we believe that the 18 month timeframe under study is suficient to allow a reasonable accrual of benefits from ICT usage, and the results obtained from our study remain both relevant and important for managers.

In any one study, it is possible to investigate only a relatively small portion of reality and this study has focussed on the nature of ICT benefits using subjective assessment. The findings, however, add to the wider picture painted by other studies. The study is congruent with econometric studies that have assumed processes of organizational transformation as an explanation of the greater increases in productivity in some firms compared with others (Bresnahan et al., 2000; Bresnahan and Greenstein, 1997;Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2002a, 2002b, 2003; Murnane et al., 1999). The findings also support the argument for a time lag between investment in IT and the reaping of greater rewards (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000b), as the qualitative data indicated changes happening over time, with increased capabilities feeding back to further change and further benefits. The study is also congruent with the case studies that have shown organizational transformation as an integral part of value generation from IT (Milgrom and Roberts, 1990; NOIE, 2003).

The significance of this study is that it has added evidence to the body of knowledge concerning organizational transformation using diferent investigatory techniques from previous studies. The econometric studies have struggled with the concept of assessing organizational transformation as an intangible asset and have tended to use partial measures; for example the degree to which firms are decentralized or have invested in training (Bresnahan et al., 2000). Case studies have given convincing descriptions of transformation, but there is a question of the degree to which they are representative of larger populations. This study included both a large-scale survey and qualitative data, so provides evidence to triangulate previous findings. A further contribution of the study is the survey instrument, which was developed in a rigorous process, and provides a scale for the subjective assessment of transformational benefits. The study is the first known to have attempted to delineate organizational transformation as a dimension of IT business value and to develop a scale for its assessment. As this exploratory work has provided evidence for this construct as an identifiable component, further confirmatory work on validating and refining the scale used would be valuable.

The implications for models of IT value realization are significant. The findings of this study, taken in conjunction with previous econometric studies and in-depth case studies, suggest that consideration should be given to the extension of current static models of IT value realization (for example, DeLone and McLean, 1992, 2003; Melville et al., 2004) to dynamic models, with a category for IT transformational benefits as an asset resulting from IT use and a feedback loop to further organizational change processes in a process of mutual causation.

The study also has practical significance. There are lessons for managers in the results that show how organizations have coupled investments in IT with organizational learning and change to achieve increased value from IT. Further, in preparing business cases for proposed investments in IT, organizations should take into account relatively intangible benefits, including transformational, informational and strategic benefits, as well as the more easily quantified transactional benefits. Managers also need to recognize the time lagged nature of ICT benefits. Because the successful use of ICT involves a continuous cycle of learning and change, there is often a time lag between initial investment in ICT and realizing the full benefit of that investment. Time is required to learn a new system, to use it in the most advantageous ways and to develop new organizational structures and processes that suit new ways of doing things: that is, to transform the organization. Further research could investigate whether the same pattern of findings is evident in other countries. In-depth studies that capture both quantitative and subjective measures of value for the same sample would also be valuable as would longitudinal studies that study change processes with IT over time.
