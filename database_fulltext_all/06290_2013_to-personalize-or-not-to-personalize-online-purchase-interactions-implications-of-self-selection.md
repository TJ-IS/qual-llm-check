# To Personalize or Not to Personalize Online Purchase Interactions: Implications of Self-Selection by Retailers

Kingshuk K. Sinha

Supply Chain and Operations, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis 55455, ksinha@umn.edu

However, the choice of a retailer whether or not to select into technology-enabled personalization and its implications for customer loyalty are at best ambiguous. Theoretical contributions of the study, managerial implications of the study findings, limitations, and directions for future research are identified.

## 1. Introduction

Personalization is the tailoring of the online purchase interactions to the individual preferences and needs of customers. Personalization technologies today enable a variety of approaches to draw customers to a website and make the website more appealing to the customers (Ansari and Mela 2003, Davenport and Harris 2009). Specifically, personalization technologies enable retailers to tailor the Web experience to its customers (Ho et al. 2011, Tam and Ho 2006, Mobasher et al. 2000) and provide personalized features ranging from a simple display of the user’s name to complex catalog navigation and product recommendations (Kramer et al. 2000). Not surprisingly, therefore, retailers today are realizing the need for personalizing their online purchase interactions with customers (Chellappa and Sin 2005). With personalization being increasingly perceived as a basis for differentiating the product offering, and, in turn, a source of competitive advantage, there is a growing trend toward pursuing technology-enabled online personalization strategies (Liu et al. 2010, Tam and Ho 2006, Murthi and Sarkar 2003). For example, AOL Time Warner provides services such as personalized features and product comparisons to its customers. Similarly, Yahoo Inc.’s Movie site provides personalized movie recommendations to its customers. Another such example is Amazon.com: upon arrival at the Amazon .com website, a registered customer is greeted by the customer’s name, provided with recommendations of products that the customer might be interested in and reminders of special events like birthdays of loved ones, and when the customer is ready to make the purchase, the website retrieves the personal account (shipping and payment) information. The purchase information is then automatically stored in the personal account of the customer for the customer to come back to the website, review past purchases, and track orders in shipment. Other examples of retailers that tailor the online purchase interactions for their customers include Target.com and BestBuy.com.

Our review of the relevant academic research literature, however, indicates that the understanding of the theoretical underpinnings and empirical insights relevant to the customer loyalty implications of technology-enabled personalization of the online purchase interactions is inadequate (Murthi and Sarkar 2003, Ho et al. 2005, Chellappa and Sin 2005, Kumar and Benbasat 2006, Wattal et al. 2009). Although the extant literature on personalization has addressed issues related to consumer privacy and trust (Chellappa and Sin 2005, Chellappa and Shivendu 2006, Awad and Krishnan 2006, Chellappa and Shivendu 2010); information processing, customer behavior, and decision making (Tam and Ho 2006, 2005); and, social presence and perceived usefulness (Kumar and Benbasat 2006), conceptual understanding of the types of technology-enabled personalization strategies and empirical research on the effectiveness of personalization strategies in fostering long-term relationships with customers is lacking. The effectiveness of technology-enabled personalization strategies in improving aggregate level outcomes, such as customer loyalty, is far from conclusive (Tam and Ho 2005). This concern is also echoed in the practitioner literature. For example, Baker (2008, p. 1) observes, “The plain truth is that even the most successful, techsavvy retail Web sites still convert only 1 to 3 percent of visitors into buyers.” With the increasing significance of online retailing, it is imperative that we understand the role of technology-enabled capabilities and digital options such as personalization of online purchase interactions in effectively serving customers (Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Cenfetelli 2008, Heim and Sinha 2001a).

This paper is an attempt to address the above concern. Specifically, the paper is motivated by the questions: What are the implications of technologyenabled personalization strategies for customer loyalty in online retailing? Should retailers personalize their online purchase interactions with customers?

In addressing these questions, first, technologyenabled personalization strategies relevant to two steps of an online purchase are conceptualized. They are (i) transaction personalization strategy—the personalization of the purchase transaction for customers, whereby customers execute the purchase and post-purchase activities; and (ii) decision personalization strategy—the personalization of the information delivered to customers to help them in their decision making, such as those relevant to the identification and purchase of products that satisfy the needs of the customers. Given the nontrivial allocation of resources required to pursue personalization strategies online, a retailer’s decision to personalize (or not to) the online purchase interactions is dependent on its operating context and is self-selected. This paper builds on the relevant literature to develop the theoretical underpinnings of self-selection into personalization strategies by retailers.

Second, the paper reports the findings of an empirical investigation into the relationships between the two personalization strategies and customer loyalty, the central objective of this research. In assessing the implications of technology-enabled online personalization strategies for customer loyalty, this paper utilizes a two-step econometric model (Hamilton and Nickerson 2003, Shaver 1998, Heckman 1979) that accounts for retailers’ self-selection into the personalization strategies. In the first step, the choice of a retailer to (or not to) personalize is modeled. This strategic choice of a retailer is modeled as a probit function of independent variables representing attributes of the retailer’s operating context. In the second step, the normative effects of personalization strategies on customer loyalty are assessed. The specification includes independent variables that are posited to affect customer loyalty of retailers and additionally a component known as the inverse Mills ratio that is estimated from the probit function to account for the endogeneity in the choice of an online personalization strategy.

Third, based on a counterfactual analysis, the paper reveals the implications of making a normatively incorrect decision with respect to personalization strategy. By empirically evaluating what-if scenarios, the counterfactual analyses provide retailers with a valuable analytical framework to inform the choice of technology-enabled online personalization strategies.

Contrary to popular belief, the results of this study indicate that personalization may not be uniformly beneficial, by way of increased customer loyalty, to all retailers. Although a significant majority of retailers pursue transaction personalization and realize benefits by way of improved customer loyalty, we find that the choice of a retailer to pursue decision personalization is self-selected and dependent on idiosyncratic characteristics related to its operating context. Specifically, the study findings suggest that retailers that have relatively large-scale operations, that provide greater variety and realize higher customer satisfaction with product selection, and that do not necessarily compete on price (i.e., realize lower customer satisfaction with prices relative to competing retailers) are more likely to pursue the decision personalization strategy. Further, while some retailers pursue decision personalization because they clearly stand to benefit (by way of improved customer loyalty) from doing so, other retailers are better off not following suit. The counterfactual analyses in the study suggest that if retailers that did not actually select into decision personalization were to instead pursue decision personalization, they would fail to realize the benefits (by way of improved customer loyalty) of decision personalization. Similarly, if retailers that actually chose to pursue decision personalization were to instead not pursue decision personalization, they would realize poorer outcomes (by way of decreased customer loyalty). In other words, the study findings lend support to the theoretical argument that the choice of whether or not to personalize the online purchase interactions and the value of decision personalization is context dependent.<sup>1</sup>

The study findings make a twofold contribution to the extant literature: First, the study provides an empirical examination of the implications of online personalization strategies for customer loyalty. Given the growing interest in online personalization, empirical insights into the benefit of personalization efforts (by way of aggregate outcomes such as customer loyalty) is central to effective decisions in this regard and forms an important contribution of this study to theory and practice. Second, this study provides the theoretical underpinnings and an empirical assessment of the self-selection into online personalization strategies by retailers. Specifically, highlighting the need for retailers to examine the appropriateness of technology-enabled personalization strategies given the idiosyncratic characteristics related to their operating context (such as retailer size, nature of product category, product variety offered by the retailer, customer perceptions of product selection and prices at the retailer relative to competitors, and the presence of a physical store front) before committing resources into such strategies is a key contribution of this study.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the conceptual development of the two personalization strategies relevant to the two steps of an online purchase: decision personalization and transaction personalization, and develops research hypotheses relating the personalization strategies and customer loyalty. Section 3 contains a discussion of the theoretical underpinnings of self-selection into personalization strategies and the econometric model that estimates the implications of personalization strategies for customer loyalty. Section 4 presents the variables, the model specification, and data collection. Section 5 contains a discussion of the results. Section 6 contains our concluding remarks.

## 2. Conceptual Development

## 2.1. Online Personalization Strategies

As mentioned earlier, personalization technologies today enable retailers to personalize their online purchase interactions with their customers (Tam and Ho 2006, Liu et al. 2010) and forms the focus of this research. We conceptualize the personalization of the online purchase interactions with respect to the following two steps in an online purchase: (i) the transaction step, which involves the execution of the purchase and the post-purchase activities (Maes et al. 1999),<sup>2</sup> and (ii) the decision-making step, which involves the identification of the need and the right product that can satisfy the need (Maes et al. 1999).<sup>3</sup> Below, we provide a description of the two types of online personalization strategies.

2.1.1. Transaction Personalization Strategy. Transaction personalization involves the personalization of the online purchase transaction step for each customer. In this step, the customer executes the purchase and conducts post-purchase activities such as tracking the product delivery. Transaction personalization helps to improve transaction convenience, defined as the customer’s perceived expenditures of time and effort to effect a transaction (Berry et al. 2002) by offering personalized services, such as personal account maintenance, user recognition, and order tracking. Berry et al. (2002) posit that transaction inconvenience is a contributing factor to the high abandonment rates of shopping carts in online retailing. Using cookies, transaction personalization enables websites to identify customers upon arrival and retrieve customer’s personal account information such as billing information, shipping information, frequently ordered list of products, past purchases and status of orders, and personal preferences in terms of email reminders, thus decreasing any transaction inconvenience that the customers might have. Personalizing the transaction step for every customer enables products to be ordered with minimal expenditure of time and effort for customers, thereby increasing the transaction convenience. Srinivasan et al. (2002) suggest that personalizing the website may enable customers to execute their transactions more efficiently. Transaction personalization attempts to personalize the purchase, delivery, and the product service stages of the online purchase as defined by Maes et al. (1999). Retailers pursuing transaction personalization follow rule-based procedures for transacting with all its customers or customer segments, and the need for extensive customer information is minimal after the transaction related information is collected during a one-time registration process.

An illustrative example of transaction personalization is Target (http://www.target.com), a retailer with a wide range of products such as apparel, electronic goods, appliances, accessories, music, movies, software, and video games. On its website, Target provides customers with an option of maintaining a personal account. When a registered customer visits the Target website, it retrieves the customer’s account information (shipping and payment), allows the customer to maintain a personal shopping cart, track their purchases online, and also review their past purchases, thereby decreasing the transaction inconvenience that the customer might have.

2.1.2. Decision Personalization Strategy. Decision personalization involves the personalization of the information content delivered to the customers with an aim toward helping them in their decision making (Tam and Ho 2006). A customer’s decision making involves the identification and the purchase decision of the right product that will satisfy the customer’s needs. Hanson (2000) suggests that more often than not customers are confused by the wide array of choices they are faced with, and choice assistance— by way of matching individual customer’s tastes and needs and the products that satisfy them—reduces this confusion and creates customer value and loyalty. Decision personalization is intended to improve the decision convenience, defined as the customer’s perceived time and effort expenditures to make purchase decisions (Berry et al. 2002), by helping customers in identifying the right products for their needs from a wide set of alternatives. It involves the personalization of the need identification, product-brokering, and merchant-brokering stages of the online purchase as defined by Maes et al. (1999). Srinivasan et al. (2002, p. 42) suggest that by personalization, retailers can reduce customer frustration and confusion when navigating the website. Further, they suggest that a large product selection can irritate customers and “if the company is able to accurately tailor or narrow choices for individual customers, it can minimize the time customers spend browsing through an entire product assortment to find precisely what they want.” Decision personalization allows retailers to gather customer information, customer preferences, and customer likes/dislikes, and, based on the information gathered, it enables retailers to target customers with matching products and services. In this approach, while retailers personalize the purchase experience for its customers by offering decision aids such as personalized product recommendations, they offer standard off-the-shelf products.

An illustrative example of decision personalization is Amazon.com (http://www.amazon.com), an electronic retailer of a wide range of products. Amazon.com makes suggestions for products that could be of interest to a customer while the customer is browsing its website. Amazon.com determines a customer’s interests from previous purchases as well as ratings given to titles. The customer’s interests are compared with those of other customers to generate titles, which are then recommended to the customer during the interaction. Amazon.com also provides links such as recommendations, new releases, and shopping groups, which are personalized to each customer. Overall, the personalization strategies described above—transaction and decision personalization strategies, respectively—are relevant to the two steps of a customer’s online purchase interactions with a retailer.

## 2.2. Online Personalization Strategies and Customer Loyalty

The relatively low search costs (compared to offline retailing), greater market outreach (customers not limited by geographical distances), wider range of options (compared to limited shelf space in a store), and the easy access to competing retailers (often with a few mouse clicks) in online retailing makes it critical for retailers to identify ways to attract and retain customers (Srinivasan et al. 2002, Chen and Hitt 2002, Reibstein 2002). The need for retailers to not only attract more customers but also convert them into satisfied repeat customers is further heightened given the high customer acquisition costs—the benefits from repeat customers who return to make further purchases are significantly greater than that from new customers (Srinivasan et al. 2002, Reichheld and Schefter 2000). The gains from loyal customers are central to the long-term profitability and success of an online retailer (Reichheld and Schefter 2000, Chen and Hitt 2002). Thus, the ability to build customer loyalty is a key objective of retailers and it is imperative that retailers understand how they could foster loyalty in its interactions with customers (Koufaris 2002, Srinivasan et al. 2002, Devaraj et al. 2003). To that end, we highlight the role of transaction personalization and decision personalization strategies in improving customer loyalty online.

The need for retailers to make the online purchase interactions simple, responsive, easy to use, and useful so that customers may perceive value and return to the website for future purchases is well recognized in the extant literature (Koufaris 2002). By identifying customers upon arrival and retrieving customer’s personal account information—i.e., billing information, shipping information, frequently ordered list of products, past purchases, status of orders, and personal preferences in terms of email reminders, and providing personal account service—transaction personalization strategy simplifies the online purchase interactions for the customer and improves the ease with which customers can execute the purchase related activities on the retailer’s website. Similarly, the decision personalization strategy tailors the information content delivered to customers with an aim toward helping them in their decision making. It tailors the consideration set of products from a much broader set of alternatives for individual customers (Alba et al. 1997). The significance of providing comprehensive, high-quality, reliable, and relevant product information to customers in improving the customer purchase experience and website success has been highlighted in the extant research (e.g., Haubl and Trifts 2000, Jarvenpaa and Todd 1997, Palmer 2002, Shapiro and Varian 1999, Alba et al. 1997). By providing interactive decision tools and information that is relevant to customers, decision personalization improves the usefulness of the website and the ease with which customers can identify the right product that satisfies their requirements (Ho et al. 2005, ConsumerReports.org), improving the overall quality of their purchase decisions (Komiak and Benbasat 2006, Tam and Ho 2006, Haubl and Trifts 2000).

It must be realized that customer value or utility is inversely related to the distance between a product’s attributes and the point in product attribute space that relates to a customer’s actual needs, and it forms the basis for retailer competition (Heim and Sinha 2001a). Personalization of the online purchase interactions serves to bridge this gap between customer needs and the actual attributes of the online purchase, delivering the right product to the right customer, strengthening the customers’ repurchase intentions (Tsai and Huang 2007, Simonson 2005, Srinivasan et al. 2002, Ostrom and Iacobucci 1995). As discussed above, personalization of the online purchase interactions can create a rich experience for customers making it difficult for competitors to entice customers away (Chellappa and Sin 2005). In a national survey of 2,120 online customers, Burke (2002) found that customers valued personalization of the online purchase interactions. Customers were receptive to the retailer providing features such as personalized promotions, one-click ordering, and personal purchase lists based on their purchase information gathered on the website, a finding echoed in Tam and Ho (2006). Customers’ experience with the retailer is a key indicator of their willingness to return to the site for future purchases, with satisfied customers being more likely to be loyal (Reibstein 2002, Shankar et al. 2003, Heim and Sinha 2001b). Suggesting that retailers can cement long-term relationships with their customers by offering personalization, Rangaswamy and Balakrishnan (2002, p. 6) point out that “it is the allure of developing and sustaining close long-term relationships with customers that largely drives personalization efforts.” Based on the above arguments, we posit the following two-study hypotheses:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1.</sup> Ceteris paribus, customer loyalty for retailers that pursue transaction personalization will be greater than that for retailers that do not.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2.</sup> Ceteris paribus, customer loyalty for retailers that pursue decision personalization will be greater than that for retailers that do not.

## 3. Modeling Self-Selection of Online Personalization Strategies

## 3.1. Theoretical Background

The discussion above highlights the potential benefits by way of customer loyalty that can be realized from technology-enabled online personalization strategies. However, pursuing online personalization strategies involves the commitment of considerable organizational resources toward technology, infrastructure, and relevant management initiatives. Extant literature suggests that managers make such strategy choices not only based on expectations of whether the choice would enable them to realize superior performance but also if the choice is commensurate with their idiosyncratic organizational constraints and objectives (Shaver 1998, Hamilton and Nickerson 2003, Skinner 1974, Venkatraman and Prescott 1990, Zhu et al. 2006, Huckman and Zinner 2008). For example, when faced with the make versus buy decision, organizations that choose to make may have production capabilities idiosyncratic to its operating context (unobservable to the researcher) such as proprietary technologies, tacit knowledge, supply contracts, and human resources that make it a highly profitable choice, whereas those that lack the production capabilities may instead choose to buy. Similarly, it can be inferred from the extant literature that a retailer’s choice of whether or not to pursue online personalization strategies is dependent on its operating context. A host of idiosyncratic organizational and product market characteristics related to its operating context (cf. Zhu et al. 2006, Chaney et al. 2004, Shaver 1998) may serve to influence retailer decisions related to online personalization strategies and customer loyalty. Further, some contextual factors may uniquely affect the choice of either transaction personalization or decision personalization strategy. This can be attributed to factors such as differences in the focus of the two types of personalization strategies—whereas the former is focused on tailoring the transaction step, the latter is focused on the decision-making step in the online purchase; variation in the technology and data requirements of the two personalization strategies; and, the relevance of the two personalization strategies to the operating context of the retailer. Below, we provide a brief summary.

3.1.1. Organizational Characteristics. Retailers’ selection of whether or not to pursue technologyenabled online personalization is likely to be influenced by their organizational characteristics including scale and type (pure click versus brick-andclick) of operations, people (incentives and expertise), technology and processes, business strategy, and other resources (such as capital) at the retailer.

Specifically, the scale and scope of operations at a retailer can be expected to affect the retailer’s personalization decisions primarily owing to the resources that a large volume of transactions may bring in. Given that personalization efforts of a retailer can be resource intensive, it is likely that retailers with a relatively large scale of operations may be more inclined to pursue online personalization strategies. The scale and scope of operations also uniquely affect the decision personalization efforts of a retailer because of informational externalities it engenders. For example, it is plausible that the accuracy of recommendation systems may improve with diversity in the customer base. Similarly, spillover of knowledge from physical store operations, such as insights gained from customer interactions in store, may serve to make personalization of the online interactions more effective and a relatively more attractive option for brick-and-click retailers. Given that decision personalization often builds on data on preferences and purchase behavior of a large set of customers, access to a diverse customer base may serve as a key motivation for retailers to pursue decision personalization effectively.

Similarly, retailers with distinct organizational competencies, product offering, and business goals may pursue varied business strategies, resulting in varied motivation to pursue personalization strategies online. For example, retailers competing on a differentiation strategy may be focused on providing a superior purchase experience to its customers and extract better margins on products, in contrast to retailers focused on a cost strategy and competing on price. This may, in turn, influence their motivation to pursue online personalization strategies.

Also, managerial capability and incentives may influence the personalization strategy decisions of a retailer. From an agency theoretic perspective (Jensen 1994), it can be argued that managerial incentives may distract from the organizational attempts to adopt new technologies owing to uncertainty in the long-term benefits and short-term disruptions (McAfee 2002). More generally, the role of top management support, effective coordination mechanisms, and organizational structure in effectively managing technology-enabled strategy has been well recognized in the literature (Zhu et al. 2006, Chatterjee et al. 2002, Mata et al. 1995).

Additionally, although advances in the technology environment (sophistication and accuracy of recommender systems, database technologies, and search algorithms) may motivate retailers to pursue personalization strategies online, existing contractual obligations with vendors, prior technological commitments, compatibility, relative advantage, technology infrastructure, and the absorptive capacity of retailers may serve to influence their decisions (Forman 2005, Premkumar et al. 1994, Angst et al. 2010).

3.1.2. Product Market Characteristics. Institutional theory suggests that an organization’s technology decisions are influenced by mimetic, coercive, and normative pressures from its environment (Teo et al. 2003, Ang and Cummings 1997, Burns and Wholey 1993, DiMaggio and Powell 1983). To that end, the characteristics of the product market a retailer operates in such as the competitive intensity, nature of product offering and customer purchase behavior, and the role of suppliers may influence its decisions and outcomes related to personalization strategies.

A central factor influencing the personalization decisions of a retailer is its product offering and the associated customer purchase behavior. For example, customer expectations of purchase experience at a retailer that offers a broader selection of products may be different from expectations at a retailer that offers a focused range of commodity products. While offering a wide array of choices may provide a greater selection set of products for customers to choose from, customers are usually overwhelmed by the variety finding it too tedious and stressful to look through information on products identified for consideration, and are often unable to evaluate all available alternatives in great depth (Alba et al. 1997, Haubl and Trifts 2000). Hence, customers at these retailers may be more receptive to decision personalization that decreases their search and information processing costs, providing retailers a stronger incentive to pursue decision personalization. On the contrary, customers may find the provision of personalized decision support in the routine purchase of commodity products (e.g., groceries or supplies) unwarranted and possibly intrusive, thus making it a less attractive strategy for retailers.

Similarly, the product purchase behavior has a unique influence on the retailer decision to offer transaction personalization. For example, for consumable goods such as office supplies, groceries, and books, customers may be expected to make repetitive purchases and would value personalized services by way of one-click purchases, the ability to maintain a list of frequently purchased items, save shipping and billing information, and look up past purchases. Retailers offering such products may be more inclined to offer transaction personalization. On the contrary, for retailers that offer products that are purchased infrequently, customer motivation to share personal information and set up an online account may be relatively weak, thus making transaction personalization an unattractive option for retailers of such products.

More generally, the competitive online environment may serve to influence retailer decisions related to personalization as well. For example, retailers may be more inclined to pursue personalization strategies to gain competitive advantage or follow suit to achieve parity if competing retailers offer personalized online interactions to customers. Lastly, suppliers that cater to multiple retailers across channels may exercise control of retailer selling strategies, which may, in turn, influence retailer decisions related to personalization.

Overall, it can be inferred that retailers’ choice of whether or not to pursue personalization strategies online and its outcomes are dependent on a host of characteristics idiosyncratic to its operating context. It follows that whereas it may be optimal for some retailers to personalize the online purchase interactions, it may not necessarily be the case for others. Thus, retailers may choose to pursue transaction personalization, decision personalization, or both, as appropriate to their operating context.

## 3.2. Methodological Implications of Self-Selection

It must be noted that many of the contextual factors highlighted above that influence the choice of a retailer to pursue technology-enabled personalization online and its customer loyalty are often unobserved, tacit, or immeasurable (Shaver 1998, Hamilton and Nickerson 2003, Li and Prabhala 2007). This has implications for evaluating the benefits (by way of customer loyalty) of the technology-enabled online personalization strategies, the central objective of this research. If retailers self-select themselves into online personalization strategies appropriate to their operating contexts, an empirical model that does not account for the self-selection would be misspecified, and the empirical estimates and the normative conclusions drawn from the analysis can be misleading (Heckman 1979, Maddala 1983, Masten 1993, Shaver 1998, Hamilton and Nickerson 2003, Ghosh and John 2009). Specifically, when retailers make a decision in their choice of personalization strategy, a comparison of customer loyalty for retailers that have chosen personalization to those that have not following a traditional regression analysis is not feasible given that retailers are not randomly assigned to these treatments. Extant literature has identified multiple methods in addressing selection bias in cross-sectional research settings. Table 1 provides a summary of the key considerations and references from the literature relevant to these methods.

In this research, we utilize the endogenous switching regression model that takes into account the self-selection in estimating the effect of online personalization strategies on the customer loyalty of retailers. This model has been used extensively in the measurement of treatment effects and program effectiveness (Greene 2003, Maddala 1983, cf. Nakosteen and Zimmer 1980, Masten et al. 1991, Shehata 1991, Shaver 1998, Chaney et al. 2004, Ghosh and John 2009). It is preferred to alternative approaches such as instrumental variables regression and propensity score matching methods in modeling self-selection into a discrete strategy choice (Hamilton and Nickerson 2003, Shehata 1991) owing to multiple reasons:<sup>4</sup> first, it is often difficult to find strong instruments that are strongly correlated to the choice variable but not correlated with the outcome variable in instrumental variable techniques (Kennedy 2003); second, the switching regression model is better suited to accommodate the heterogeneous impact of covariates across choice regimes (Maddala 1983); and third, unlike propensity score techniques that are based on matching on observed covariates, the two-stage selection model enables us to explicitly capture the role of private information (unobserved factors related to its operating context) and estimate the counterfactual outcomes, thus providing valuable insights into retailers’ strategic actions (Li and Prabhala 2007). Next, we provide a brief overview of the selection model and the counterfactual analysis.

3.2.1. Selection Model. Selection models form a class of truncation models and are often referred to as incidental truncation models (Greene 2003). In self-selection models, the truncation in the dependent variable of interest occurs because of the selfselection by individuals into the treatment (strategy choice). In this study, retailers are faced with the choice of whether or not to personalize the online purchase interactions. Retailers will choose to personalize if they believe the net benefit from personalization is nonnegative given their idiosyncratic operating context. The dependent variable of interest (customer loyalty) is observed under both options—i.e., we observe customer loyalty for retailers that pursue personalization and the customer loyalty for retailers that do not. In evaluating the benefits of personalization it is important that we account for the truncation in the dependent variable owing the self-selection by retailers into their personalization strategy choice. The endogenous switching regression model addresses this concern. Below we describe the two-step model.

Step 2. Normative Effects. This step assesses the normative effects of personalization strategy, accounting for the selection process. As mentioned earlier, not accounting for the selection leads to misleading estimates of the normative effects of the chosen strategy. Let $P _ { i j }$ represent the customer loyalty of retailer i corresponding to the chosen strategy $j ,$ where, $j = 1$ represents the retailer’s decision to personalize, and $j = 0$ represents the retailer’s decision to not personalize. Consider the example (discussed earlier) of assessing the performance implications of a manufacturer’s decision to make versus buy. A regression analysis estimating the performance model for the manufacturer assumes that the make versus buy decision is exogenous and is unaffected by factors affecting performance. However, such an assumption may not hold true. Unobserved factors related to the operating context of the manufacturer such as proprietary technologies, managerial skills, and supplier contractual agreements can affect not only the sourcing decision but also the performance of the manufacturer. Failure to account for such unobserved factors results in biased estimates in the regression model. An econometric selection correction procedure (Heckman 1979) captures the effect of such unobserved factors through its inverse Mills ratio term. In the context of this study, the inverse Mills ratio terms can be interpreted to capture the effect of unobserved factors related to the operating context of a retailer discussed earlier (such as resource availability, business strategy, managerial expertise and incentives, technology commitments, and vendor/supplier contracts) that may affect not only the personalization choice of a retailer but also the customer loyalty of the retailer.

## 4. Research Design

## 4.1. Variables and Model Specification

4.1.1. Selection Equation. As mentioned earlier, we model the selection process for each of the two personalization strategies—transaction personalization and decision personalization—as a discrete choice model that is driven by retailer specific variables indicative of their inclinations to pursue personalization strategies. Although it is desirable to include all the factors affecting the personalization strategy selection process, it is often not possible to observe or measure all the variables. Consequently, in the probit specification we include variables that we have information on and the error term is set to include the effects of the unobserved and unmeasured factors. Specifically, in this study, we model the retailer’s strategy selection process as a function of the following variables: satisfaction with price and product selection, variety, number of customers, number of years, and physical.

The variable price refers to the customer satisfaction with the extent to which the prices on the website are comparable to the prices of the same or similar products as offered by other competing retailers. A lower satisfaction with price is indicative of a higher price at the retailer compared to other retailers. Consistent higher priced product offerings at a retailer suggests that retailer’s operating characteristics may not be suited to compete in the market based on a low-cost strategy and instead may be focused on a differentiation approach. And, retailers trying to differentiate their product offering would be more inclined to pursue transaction and decision personalization strategies. Further, from a customer’s viewpoint, higher price tags are associated with relatively higher purchase involvement. Customers perceive greater levels of risk in the purchase and spend relatively greater effort in the purchase of these products. Consequently, provision of transaction and decision personalization that are oriented toward improving the transaction and decision convenience would be valued by customers. In this vein, we suggest that retailers with lower satisfaction with price (indicative of a relatively higher priced product offering) would be more willing to pursue personalization strategies in an attempt to improve the overall purchase experience on its website.

The variable product selection refers to the customer satisfaction with the selection of products within a product category. Variety refers to the breadth of product categories offered by a retailer and is assessed objectively from the retailer website. Although the marginal costs of variety for an electronic retailer are low compared to that of a brick-and-mortar retailer, adding variety nevertheless adds to the overall costs by way of indirect costs such as warehousing and distribution. Greater variety and product selection in spite of the added costs, among other things, are indicative of a retailer’s attempt to cater to heterogeneous product needs of customers within and across product categories so that customers find product offerings that are more closely matched with their requirements. Hence, retailers are likely to be more willing to offer personalized services so as to not only provide customers with a greater product selection and variety but also serve them by improving the convenience with which they can select the right product that best matches their need from a wide product selection and execute the purchase once they have identified the product. “Convenient functions for customers to obtain all the information they need to make a purchase decision are important for the functional convenience of Internet businesses” (Kim et al. 2002, p. 242). As mentioned earlier, more often than not, customers are overloaded with daunting amounts of information and are confused by the wide array of choices they are faced with. Alba et al. (1997) suggest that customers find it too tedious and stressful to look through information on hundreds of products identified for consideration. While making purchase decisions, customers are often unable to evaluate all available alternatives in great depth (Haubl and Trifts 2000). Provision of services that improve the ease with they can select their product by way of choice assistance (Hanson 2000) and execute the purchase would be valued by customers. Consequently, we believe that retailers with greater product variety and higher satisfaction with product selection (indicative of a broader product selection) would be more likely to pursue personalization strategies to be able to serve their customers better.

The variables, number of years, number of customers, and physical are surrogates for the number of years a retailer has been existence in the Internet domain, the number of customers visiting the store, and whether the retailer is a pure online player versus a brickand-click operation, respectively. We use these variables in our study to capture the extent and size of a retailer’s operations. We believe that a large retailer serving a broad base of customers across multiple channels would be more disposed toward pursuing personalization strategies, given their access to a wide array of resources such as a greater know-what of customer needs and preferences.

The variables, transaction personalization and decision personalization, form the dependent variables for the selection models. Based on our review of the relevant literature (e.g., Barnes and Vidgen 2001, Burke 2002, Kim et al. 2002, Lin and Wu 2002, McKinney et al. 2002, Srinivasan et al. 2002, Zhu and Kraemer 2002, Wolfinbarger and Gilly 2003) and keeping in perspective the research objectives in this study, we assess transaction personalization and decision personalization strategies in terms of binary indicator variables.

4.1.2. Customer Loyalty Equation. For the customer loyalty equation in step $2$ of our analysis, the dependent variable $P _ { i }$ is customer loyalty. Customer loyalty is defined as the willingness of the customer to return to a retailer to make a purchase, given the purchase experience at the retailer. Although desirable, tracking individual customers over time to assess repeat purchase behavior is difficult. Consequently, the willingness of a customer to return is often used as an indicator of customer loyalty toward the retailer (Koufaris 2002). The choice of customer loyalty as the dependent variable of interest over other metrics such as customer satisfaction, an antecedent of loyalty (Devaraj et al. 2003), or financial performance, an outcome of loyalty (Chen and Hitt 2002), was primarily driven by the significance of customer loyalty to retailer success (Reichheld and Schefter 2000, Chen and Hitt 2002, Koufaris 2002).

The independent variables in the customer loyalty equation capture different aspects affecting the customer willingness to make a repeat purchase from a website. As indicated earlier, customers’ experiences with the retailer is a key indicator of their willingness to return to the site for future purchases—satisfied customers being more likely to be loyal (Reibstein 2002, Devaraj et al. 2003, Shankar et al. 2003, Heim and Sinha 2001b). Consequently, to account for the customer satisfaction with the purchase experience at the retailer, we assess customer satisfaction with various aspects of the online purchase including satisfaction with prices at the retailer relative to that in similar stores (price); the types of products offered (product selection); the convenience of ordering (ease); the layout, links, and speed of the website (website performance); the clarity and quantity of information provided (product information); the shipping options and charges (shipping); the delivery of the purchased product (on-time delivery and order tracking); the ease of contacting and courtesy of customer service (customer support); and match between the product description and what was received (product met expectations). Because the customer satisfaction with the various aspects above are correlated, we created summarized factor scores<sup>5</sup> related to customer satisfaction with order procurement and customer satisfaction with order fulfillment (Heim and Sinha 2001b). Customer satisfaction with the order procurement is the extent to which the electronic interface facilitates an easy, informative, and relevant ordering process—it is reflective of the variables price, product selection, ease, website performance, product information, and shipping. Customer satisfaction with the order fulfillment is defined as the extent to which a firm is able to fulfill the online purchase as promised—it is reflective of the variables on-time delivery, order tracking, customer support, and product met expectations. Overall, the independent variables $W _ { i }$ in the specification include order fulfillment, order procurement, variety, physical, number of customers, number of years, and the inverse Mills ratio terms.

As mentioned earlier, variety measures the breadth of product categories offered by a retailer; number of customers and number of years refer to the customer traffic to the store and the number of years a retailer has been existent in the Internet domain, respectively; and physical refers to whether the retailer is a pure online retailer versus a brick-and-click retailer. These variables affect a customer’s ease and trust in making a purchase from a retailer and are posited to affect customer willingness to return to the retailer to make a purchase.

## 4.2. Data Collection

The data collection for this research involved collecting two sets of information for every online retailer, our unit of analysis. The first set included the data on customer loyalty and customer satisfaction with the different aspects of the online purchase at a retailer, which was gathered from BizRate (http://www.bizrate.com). Information on customer loyalty and customer satisfaction with the different aspects of the online purchase are provided in the detailed ratings section for each retailer on BizRate’s website. BizRate is a marketing research firm that surveys customers on their actual shopping experience. BizRate uses data from the last 90 days when performing the calculations to arrive at the ratings for an online retailer. In other words, information on ratings provided for each retailer on BizRate is never more than three months old. BizRate’s aggregated customer ratings of electronic retailers are considered to be among the most credible indicators of online customer satisfaction and loyalty (Venkatesan et al. 2006, 2007; Hansell 1999; Weintraub 2000; Heim and Sinha 2001b; Reibstein 2002; Zeithaml et al. 2002). Consumer Web Watch (http://www.ConsumerWebWatch.com) lists Bizrate.com as one of 29 “praiseworthy” companies committed to preventing confusion, deception, and fraud on the Internet (New York Times, June 20, 2003, p. C5).

There are two parts to BizRate’s online customer survey. Located on the receipt page of a website, the first part of the survey is presented immediately after a purchase is completed. Actual online buyers are invited by BizRate to participate and complete a survey whenever a purchase is completed. The first part of the survey assesses the levels of satisfaction with the different aspects of the online purchase such as ease of ordering, product selection, product information, prices, website performance, and shipping on a tenpoint scale. After the scheduled delivery date, in the second part of the survey, customers are asked to rate their satisfaction levels with the fulfillment of the online purchase including on-time delivery, order tracking, product met expectations, customer support, and the likelihood of their returning to the website for future purchases. To ensure that the respondents to BizRate’s survey are representative of the population and are not just fans or detractors, BizRate.com conducts multiple validity checks to see if the responses from the nonrespondents differed from those who had responded earlier. No significant nonrespondent bias was found (Reibstein 2002). Further, the information on customer loyalty and satisfaction with various aspects of the online purchase is aggregated from ratings provided by a large number of customers (the average number of respondents across retailers in the study sample is 13,425), which serves to overcome any systematic bias arising from fans and detractors in the responding customers.

The second set of information that was collected for each retailer includes data on personalization strategies of retailers. This was collected through a direct observation of retailers’ websites. Each retailer’s website was visited and the different personalization features (e.g., personalized recommendations or personalized account service) on the website were identified. This observable content was then used to code the indicator variables measuring transaction personalization and decision personalization (see Table 2). The criteria used to assess the transaction and decision personalization strategies of retailers were derived from the conceptual domains of the two personalization strategies. Specifically, for decision personalization, the retailers’ websites were assessed for the provision of personalized decision tools such as personalized recommendations, the presence of which indicated the use of decision personalization. Similarly, for transaction personalization, the retailers’ websites were assessed for the provision of personalized features such as a “My Account” with the ability to store billing and shipping information for future use, the presence of which, indicated the use of transaction personalization by the retailer. Given that the assessment of retailers’ online personalization strategies involved analyses of website content, we additionally checked the quality and reliability of the measurement process. Measurement quality of such data can be assessed by comparing evaluations by two or more independent raters for the same set of cases. In our study, we employed three raters to assess the quality of the measurement process. The three raters were recruited (chosen randomly) from respondents to an advertisement that was put out seeking participants for the research study. All three raters had college education, some work experience, and had purchased products from online retailers ensuring familiarity with the online purchase setting, a precondition for participating in this study. The three raters were provided the variable definitions, coding schemes, and were instructed on the data collection process. Data was independently collected by the three raters for a randomly chosen set of 50 websites from our overall list of 422 websites. Each rater collected the data on a coding form consisting of columns for a list of the websites, transaction personalization, and decision personalization. The coding scheme, as mentioned earlier, followed an indicator variable format. The disagreements among raters realized from the coding formed the basis of our assessment of the quality of the measurement process. The proportional reduction in loss (PRL) approach (Rust and Cooil 1994) was adopted to assess the interrater reliability. The interrater reliabilities were found to be 0.97 and 0.93 for the variables measuring transaction and decision personalization, respectively. Based on Nunnally’s (1978) rules of thumb, Rust and Cooil (1994) suggest that a PRL measure of 0.7 is adequate for exploratory work and 0.9 is adequate for advanced studies. The high levels of reliability in our study indicate the quality and stability of the measurement process. In the next section, we discuss the results from the empirical analyses.

Our results also indicate that if retailers that chose not to personalize had instead chosen to personalize (a move from quadrant IV to quadrant I), then their customer loyalty would have dropped significantly from 8.36 to 7.69 (p < 0001). We believe that given the operating context for such retailers, decision personalization may be unsuited, and could lead to potentially adverse effects on customer loyalty. For example, as discussed earlier, when the product offering is relatively focused with limited variety, customers may not value decision personalization and may find them potentially intrusive. However, retailers in our study sample that do not actually pursue decision personalization tend to offer relatively lower variety and product selection (they realize lower customer satisfaction with the selection of products carried relative to that of competing retailers). Hence, if such retailers were to offer personalized decision support, their customers may find them unwarranted and distracting. Another factor could be related to the availability of data on customer preferences in appropriate volumes. As discussed earlier, decision personalization efforts (such as collaborative filtering techniques) benefit from information related to the purchase behavior of a wide range of customers. However, retailers in our study sample that do not actually pursue decision personalization tend to have a smaller customer base. Hence, when such retailers pursue decision personalization efforts, they realize relatively less information and may deliver poorly in their personalization efforts. This may, in turn, result in dissatisfied customers who find the purchase process on the retailer website to be ineffective and potentially annoying. Similarly, as discussed earlier, pursuing decision personalization involves the commitment of considerable organizational resources toward technology, infrastructure, and relevant management initiatives. Such resource commitments may in turn require retailers to extract additional margins on products. However, retailers in our study sample that do not actually pursue decision personalization tend to compete on price<sup>9</sup> (they realize better customer satisfaction on price relative to competing retailers)—if these retailers were to extract additional margins on their products, they may face a potential backlash from their customer base. Thus, retailers may face adverse reactions in their attempts to pursue decision personalization when it is not conducive to the observed and unobserved characteristics of their operating context.

These results provide conditional support for Hypothesis 2. These findings suggest that retailers need to take into consideration the organizational and product market characteristics relevant to their operating context in the selection of decision personalization strategy. Our study findings show that although some retailers are better off pursuing the decision personalization strategy, it is not necessarily better for other retailers to follow suit. Further, the study findings also suggest that making an incorrect choice that does not account for the retailer’s operating context affects customer loyalty adversely. Overall, the counterfactual analyses highlight the implications of selection into decision personalization strategy,<sup>10</sup> an insight that is not readily evident from traditional regression analyses.

## 5.4. Additional Analysis—Assessing the Role of Competition

We conducted additional analyses to further examine the impact of personalization strategies on customer loyalty under varying levels of competition among retailers across product categories.<sup>11</sup> The competitive intensity in the market for a product category is identified by the number of retailers offering products in that category (Venkatesan et al. 2007, Pan et al. 2003).

The larger the number of retailers operating in a category, the greater the competitive intensity in that category (Clay et al. 2001, Gielens and Dekimpe 2001). To identify categories of relatively high and low competitive intensities, we identified the number of retailers offering products in each product category in our study sample. We found that relatively fewer retailers competed in product categories such as books, music, DVD, video, toys, and electronics (ranged from 24 to 98) compared to the number of retailers competing in apparel, gifts, flowers, food, computer hardware, and home categories (ranged from 101 to 143) in our study sample. This is consistent with Venkatesan et al. (2007, p. 316), who find that “the mean number of retailers in individual product-markets is lesser for books, DVDs, and video games as compared to other product categories.” This suggests that competitive intensities in the product categories estimated from the study sample are broadly indicative of the online retailing environment. Next, to ensure homogeneity and to eliminate any confounding effects arising from multiproduct category retailers, we only included single-product category retailers for this analysis. This resulted in a subsample of 135 retailers offering products across product categories with an average competitive intensity of 102, close to the median of 101. We then created an indicator variable competition (0/1) to represent relatively high and low levels of competition. Retailers with a competitive intensity score below 101 were coded 0 (low-competitive intensity) and the rest were coded 1 (high-competitive intensity). The low-competitive intensity subgroup included 52 retailers offering product categories such as books, DVDs, and toys, with an average competitive intensity of 67. The high-competitive intensity subgroup included 82 retailers offering product categories such as apparel, gifts, flowers, food, and computer hardware, with an average competitive intensity of 123.

We then repeated the study analyses, now additionally including the variable competition in the model. 91 of the 134 retailers in the subsample offered transaction personalization and 52 of the 134 offered decision personalization. Results from the analyses (see Tables B.7–B.9 in Appendix B) indicate that although there is no selection into transaction personalization, as earlier, transaction personalization has a significant positive association with customer loyalty, accounting for the competition. As for decision personalization, we find that the choice of a retailer to pursue decision personalization is self-selected. Whereas some retailers selected into decision personalization and benefit from doing so, other retailers are better off not following suit, accounting for the competition.<sup>12</sup> Overall, results from the additional analyses accounting for the competition are consistent with our earlier results, reinforcing our study findings that transaction personalization and decision personalization strategies may enable retailers to improve their online purchase interactions and build customer loyalty in online retailing.

## 6. Conclusion

There is a heightened awareness of the need to account for the significant role of technology-enabled personalization in the strategic decision making of a firm (Murthi and Sarkar 2003). Despite such awareness, theoretical underpinnings and the empirical analysis of technology-enabled personalization and its normative consequences remain an underresearched topic (Murthi and Sarkar 2003, Tam and Ho 2005, Ho et al. 2005, Chellappa and Sin 2005, Kumar and Benbasat 2006, Wattal et al. 2009). This study addresses the above concern. Specifically, this study provides a conceptualization of personalization strategies (transaction personalization and decision personalization) relevant to two steps of an online purchase. The study then draws on the relevant literature to develop the theoretical underpinnings of self-selection by retailers into personalization strategies and its implications for customer loyalty. The empirical analysis in the study is based on data collected from 422 retailers. The study findings reveal that although a significant majority of retailers pursue transaction personalization and realize improved customer loyalty, the choice of a retailer to pursue decision personalization is self-selected. Retailers that have relatively large-scale operations, that provide greater variety and realize higher customer satisfaction with product selection, and that do not necessarily compete on price (i.e., realize lower customer satisfaction with prices relative to competing retailers) are more likely to pursue the decision personalization. Further, whereas some retailers pursue decision personalization because they clearly stand to benefit (by way of improved customer loyalty) from doing so, other retailers are better off not following suit. The study findings reveal the negative implications of making a normatively incorrect choice with respect to decision personalization strategy.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

The contributions of this study to the academic literature are twofold. The first contribution is in identifying relationships between the two online personalization strategies—transaction personalization and decision personalization—and customer loyalty, and empirically analyzing the relationships. The theoretically grounded econometric analysis of the relationships between strategies for the personalization of the online purchase interactions and customer loyalty and the insights emerging from the results of the analysis are, to the best of our knowledge, among the first of its kind to be reported and serve to address a gap in the extant literature (Murthi and Sarkar 2003, Tam and Ho 2005, Ho et al. 2005, Chellappa and Sin 2005, Kumar and Benbasat 2006, Wattal et al. 2009).

Second, the study findings indicate that the choice of whether or not to personalize the online purchase interactions and the value of online personalization is context dependent. Specifically, the study provides the theoretical underpinnings and an empirical assessment of the self-selection into online personalization strategies by retailers. The study findings also demonstrate that there is a significant negative implication (by way of decreased customer loyalty) when retailers make normatively incorrect choices with respect to online personalization. Furthermore, highlighting the need for retailers to examine the appropriateness of technology-enabled personalization strategies to the idiosyncratic characteristics of their operating context (such as retailer size, nature of product category, product variety offered by the retailer, customer perceptions of product selection, price at the retailer relative to competitors, and the presence of a physical store front) before jumping on to the technology bandwagon is a key contribution of this study.

## 6.2. Managerial Implications

Beyond contributing to advancing the academic literature, the study findings have the following managerial implications: first, the study findings inform managerial decision making related to online personalization strategies by providing empirical insights into the benefits (by way of improvements to aggregate outcomes such as customer loyalty) that retailers could realize from pursuing such strategies. Given the growing interest in online personalization, this is a key contribution of this study to practice.

Second, contrary to expectations, the study findings indicate that retailers may continue to reap the benefits of pursuing online personalization in competitive market settings. This suggests that the personalization strategies of retailers may not only enable them to differentiate their product offerings and possibly gain first-mover advantages but also serve their customers better and create a loyal customer base even as other retailers offer online personalization.

Third, the study findings suggest that transaction personalization should be considered by retailers as a necessary component in the design of online purchase processes. An overwhelming majority of retailers pursue transaction personalization strategy and benefit from it by way of improved customer loyalty. This insight serves to motivate the choice of retailers to pursue transaction personalization strategy in improving their online purchase interactions with customers.

Fourth, the study findings indicate that decision personalization may not be uniformly beneficial to all retailers. The counterfactual analyses in the study demonstrate the negative implications (by way of decreased customer loyalty) when retailers incorrectly choose to pursue decision personalization when they should not, or vice versa, highlighting the need for retailers to examine the appropriateness of decision personalization strategy to their idiosyncratic operating context before committing resources to pursuing such a strategy. This insight is valuable in informing retailers whether or not to pursue decision personalization strategy.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

We believe there are several possible extensions of this study, including ones that would overcome the limitations of this study. One line of future investigation could focus on replicating the study using more elaborate measures for personalization strategies. Although this study followed a binary measurement of personalization strategies assessing the presence (or absence) of personalization—a key consideration that enabled us to map back the varied personalization approaches of online retailers on a measurement scale and keep the measurement clean and comparable—future research could assess the extent and sophistication of online personalization strategies.

Second, future research could inform decisions related to the specific elements of the design and implementation of decision and transaction personalization strategies and provide a comparative evaluation of the effectiveness of the different technologies underlying the personalization strategies. Additionally, research on the effectiveness of the personalization strategies with respect to the different types of customers: “aural,” “visual,” and “kinesthetic” (Baker 2008, p. 3) may be valuable.

A third line of future investigation could focus on the role of customer trust in the effective deployment of personalization strategies by retailers. For example, while customers may be motivated to participate in the online personalization strategies of a retailer, they may be discouraged by privacy concerns (Chellappa and Sin 2005, Awad and Krishnan 2006). Similarly, Chellappa (2011) highlights the impact of customers’ perceived privacy and perceived security on their trust in online retail transactions, with significant implications for personalization efforts of a retailer. Customers may be unwilling to provide information to a retailer with low reputation or when the perceived risk of information misuse is high.<sup>13</sup> Hence, it is imperative that retailers understand how they can build trust with customers and identify mechanisms by which they could offer personalized services in the presence of privacy concerns (Chellappa and Shivendu 2010). Further research in this regard would be valuable to both the theory and practice of online personalization.

A fourth line of future investigation could focus on evaluating the impact of personalization strategies on financial measures of performance, accounting for the fixed costs of personalization, thereby providing insights into the financial justification of investments in technologies enabling personalization. Also, while this study was focused on the personalization of the online purchase interactions, another line of future
