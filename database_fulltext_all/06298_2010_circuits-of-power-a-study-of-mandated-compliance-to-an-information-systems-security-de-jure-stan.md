## Abstract

Organizations need to protect information assets against cyber crime, denial-of-service attacks, web hackers, data breaches, identity and credit card theft, and fraud. Criminals often try to achieve financial, political, or personal gain through these attacks, so the threats that their actions prompt are insidious motivators for organizations to adopt information systems security (ISS) approaches. Extant ISS research has traditionally examined ISS in e-commerce business organizations. The present study investigates ISS within government, analyzing power relationships during an ISS standards adoption and accreditation process, where a head of state mandates that all government agencies are to comply with a national de jure ISS standard. Using a canonical action research method, designated managers of ISS services across small, medium, and large agencies were monitored and assessed for progress to accreditation through surveys, interviews, participant observation at round table forums, and focus groups. By 2008, accreditation status across the 89 agencies participating in this study was approximately 33 percent fully accredited, with 67 percent partially compliant. The research uses Clegg's (1989) circuits of power framework to interpret power, resistance, norms, and cultural relationships in the process of compliance. The paper highlights that a strategy based on organization subunit size is helpful in motivating and assisting organizations to move toward accreditation. Mandated standard accreditation was inhibited by insufficient resource allocation, lack of senior management input, and commitment. Factors contributing to this resistance were group norms and cultural biases.

Keywords: Information systems security (ISS), ISS de jure standards, politics and power, circuits of power, resistance, norms, culture, institutionalization, canonical action research, e-commerce

## Introduction

Use of a de jure standard, as specifications for securing information systems against unwarranted attacks that can compromise their operation, is fundamental to the process of implementing and accrediting organizations' security. National and international instances of targeted organizations being subject to hacking, website defacement, and redirection of transactions to false websites are highlighted in a recent government crime commission study into future directions of technology-enabled crime (Choo et al. 2007). These attacks targeted private and government organizations and their leaders, prompting implementation of information system security (ISS) measures by political or executive leaders' authority. Standards are initiated, developed, and diffused so that organizations can be accredited. There are many other frameworks that could be used or adopted. Evidence shows that implementation of IS or related processes often incurs problems of power relations, political games, and resistance, often occurring as a result of norms or culture in an organization (Doolin 2004; Markus 1983).

The present study analyzes how power, politics, resistance, norms, and culture affect the implementation of ISS de jure standards across all agencies in the New South Wales (NSW) State Government, Australia. NSW is the largest state government in Australia by size (full-time employees, gross revenue). Successful implementation of ISS for NSW using a national (and later using an international) ISS standard created a secure systems environment and was seen as paramount for its reputation with citizens, businesses, governments, and internally as a successful e-commerce deliverer.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section discusses the background of ISS de jure standards. We then discuss the power theory literature and the main concepts of our theoretical approach using the circuits of power framework and institutionalization. This is followed by an explanation of our research methods, the case study background, interpretation sections, and a case summary. Final sections of the paper discuss contributions, limitations, future research, and our conclusions.

Backhouse et al. (2006) chronicle the creation and development of the first de jure standard (BS7799) in ISS management using a case study of key organizations that took part in the process. They used a framework adapted from a circuits of power perspective (Clegg 1989) together with an institutionalization lens. The development of an ISS de jure standard began in January 1993 in Britain with the establishment of an industry working group. The first British Standard, BS7799: Part 1, was published in October 1995 and became widely adopted on a national basis (Backhouse et al. 2006; von Solms 1999). Countries that adopted the British Standard (such as Australia) usually moved to their own national standard and then to international equivalents to benefit from international recognition.

In Australia, the current ISS de jure standard comprises the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standard ISO27002:2007 and companion ISO27001:2006 (Standards Australia 2006, 2007). A major advantage of adopting a de jure ISS standard over other frameworks (where there are no specific ISS laws) is that it eliminates the cost of creating a coherent framework, which may be unnecessarily idiosyncratic and based on limited expertise. For example, in the United States, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Federal guidelines are individually useful but (outside of the United States) do not provide a cohesive and explicit framework for managing information security.

## Literature Review

Previous research into IS has used a power lens to investigate a number of aspects of the implementation of information systems, including resistance to the implementation of management IS (Doolin 2004; Kling 1980; Markus 1983; Pettigrew 1972), power over users (Markus and Bjørn-Andersen 1987); and power relations in IS design, development, and implementation (Koop and Grant 1993) within organizations. As a discipline IS often interfaces with other disciplines to draw theories, frameworks, or themes (such as power, resistance) to aid, extend, or improve the investigation of IS phenomena or processes. One IS exemplar is Markus' (1983) study, "Power, Politics and MIS Implementation." However, relatively few IS researchers have used concepts from power theory to investigate ISS de jure standards implementation, a surprising gap in the IS literature. Exceptions include Fragos et al. (2007) and Lapke and Dhillon (2008). Our study draws on power theory, the circuits of power framework (Clegg 1989; Clegg et al. 2006), studies of resistance (Markus 1983), and, from within sociological institutional theory, processes of institutionalization (Berger and Luckman 1971) as approaches that help identify and explain critical issues for achieving successful accreditation or inhibiting compliance to ISS standards in a large organization.

Sociologists, organizational scientists, political scientists, and others have contributed to an understanding of the concept of power. For example, in social psychology (Cialdini 2003; Cialdini and Goldstein 2004; Elias 2004, 2008, 2009; Foucault 1977; French 1956; French and Raven 1959; Nolan et al. 2008; Pierro et al. 2008; Raven 1965, 1993, 2001; Raven et al. 1998; Schein 1996), organizational theory (Clegg 1989; Clegg et al. 2006; Weber 1947; Wrong 1979), and political science (Bachrach and Baratz 1962; Dahl 1957; Lukes 1974; Parsons 1963) the literature is rich with theoretical and empirical research offering different conceptualizations of power.

The field from which IS can draw is, indeed, rich when it comes to topics related to power. Common themes of power in the IS literature were reviewed and categorized into authority, centralization, influence, politics, and other (Jasperson et al. 2002). Some theories gain further development over time as new concepts are examined more closely. For example, French and Raven's (1959) five bases of social power theories have had additional conceptual contributions added (Raven 1965; Raven et al. 1998) to include direction of attitudes to change in organizations (Elias 2004, 2008, 2009). In addition, other authors have expanded this work in other power theory directions, incorporating concepts such as culture (Bunker et al. 2008; Schein 1996) or norms (Cialdini 2003; Cialdini and Goldstein 2004; Nolan et al. 2008; Pierro et al. 2008). In the investigation of norms, a promising area relevant to this study is analysis of descriptive norms (what people usually do) and injunctive norms (what is approved or disapproved) (Nolan et al. 2008).

Essentially, these approaches to power distill to a limited range of approaches for analysis in IS and organization theory. First, there are those that focus on substantive phenomena as bases or resources for power to be exercised, usually in terms of an empirically observable causal sequence of action. For instance, the theory often draws from Dahl (1957) and French and Raven (1959) as exemplars of this approach. A second approach is more structural, stressing different faces or dimensions of power (Bachrach and Baratz

1962; Lukes 1974). A third approach draws on Foucault's (1977) accounts of power as both a positive as well as a negative phenomenon. There have been some attempts at integrating different approaches and in this research we draw extensively on one such approach: Clegg's (1989) circuits of power framework, which is already familiar to IS audiences from the work of researchers such as Silva and Backhouse (2003).

Previous reviews of the ISS literature have revealed both a lack of research into power and politics and that ISS studies in security or management journals are rare, with a need for more research in these areas (Dhillon and Backhouse 2001; Jasperson et al. 2002; Keen 1981; Siponen and Willison 2007). These reviews also point out that the number of theory papers in ISS research is relatively low (Wareham et al. 2005). One reason posited for the lack of qualitative ISS research is that, by its very nature, it is intrusive and requires trust between the organization and researcher (Kotulic and Clark 2004; Silva and Backhouse 2003). In addition, there has been a call by Lee (1989), echoed by Backhouse et al. (2006), to apply the circuits of power theoretical framework in other contexts. This paper fulfils the call from Backhouse and Lee by applying the circuits of power framework to ISS standards implementation.

The circuits of power framework (Clegg 1989) has been applied to IS cases in industry and government organizations (Backhouse et al. 2006; Fragos et al. 2007; Introna 1997; Lapke and Dhillon 2008; Silva 1997, 2005, 2007a, 2007b; Silva and Backhouse 2003). The circuits of power framework (see Figure 1) comprises three circuits or meta-power theories known as agency, social integration, and system integration (Clegg 1989). These are open systems, open to influence by exogenous environmental contingencies as well as being open to endogenous changes in the dynamics of power relations. The circuits' institutionalize the obligatory passage points (Backhouse et al. 2006). These obligatory passage points (OPP) or nodal points (Callon et al. 1986) are "the construction of a conduit through which traffic must necessarily pass. Power consists in part in the achievement of this positionality" (Clegg 1989, p. 205). Exogenous environmental contingencies influence circuits of social and system integration and, through them, the agency circuits' social relations (shown in Figure 1 by arrows pointing to the right) channeled via the OPP where "interests" agencies are enrolled over time (Clegg 1989). Agency social relations in the system integration circuit are empowered or disempowered through rules fixing (fix/refix), relations of meaning and membership in the social integration circuit or via innovation in techniques of discipline and production (Clegg 1989).

The episodic agency circuit, or the one-dimensional perspective of power in Lukes (1974) is premised on the idea of some subjects being able to exercise sovereign power, much as Hobbes conceptualized it in terms of power being causal (Reynolds and Saxonhouse 1995). A fundamental prescription of the episodic circuit is that A makes B do something that B would otherwise not do (Dahl 1957).

Power is never one-way traffic: an A rarely gets a B to accede to direction without some resistance (shown in Figure 1 with arrows pointing to the left). Resistance theories have been developed in IS contexts (Kling 1980; Markus 1983) and other disciplines such as management (see the 2005 special issue of Management Communications Quarterly, particularly Fleming 2005; Mumby 2005; Putman et al. 2005) and resistance is a critical concept in power relationships. Kling (1980) postulates six resistance theories categorized into systems rationalization (rational, structural, and human relations theories) and segmented institutionalism (interactionist, organizational politics, and class politics theories) approaches. Markus (1983), following Kling, defines three resistance theories: people-determined, system-determined, and interaction theory. Interaction theory has two variations: the socio-technical variant and the political variant. The three resistance theories are applicable to IS situations to explain user or design resistance when systems are implemented. Of the three theories used by Markus, the interaction theory is the most sophisticated (Lee 1989). Interaction is generally seen as being guided by norms embedded in culture. Concepts related to power and resistance that have been developed in the power literature (e.g., Clegg 1989; Clegg et al. 2006; Raven 1965) include norms (Cialdini 2003) and culture (Schein 1996) (see Table 5). Norms “can refer to (a) what is commonly done or (b) what is commonly approved and disapproved” (Kallgren et al. 2000, p. 1002). “Culture can be viewed as taken-for-granted, shared, tacit, ways of perceiving, thinking, and reacting, was one of the most powerful and stable forces operating in organizations” (Schein 1996, p. 231). Recently, an increasing number of researchers have used a discursive approach to investigate resistance and organization (see Fleming 2005; Mumby 2005; Putman et al. 2005), an approach specially suited to identify norms and specifying cultural relations.

Agency (the A's and B's) may refer to any level of analysis that is capable of decision making, such as in an organization, where individuals, teams, groups, or departments make decisions that organizational authorities seek to control through disciplinary power, limiting approvals, authorities, and actions, for instance (Clegg 1989). Causal power, seen through the agency circuit, can flow through circuits where components of power (rules, relations, and resources) are either translated, reproduced, transformed, or possibly remain fixed (Clegg 1989). Resistance to power may be of at least two types.

Under rare conditions called “organizational outflanking,” resistance to power may consolidate itself as a new power and thus constitute a new fixity in the representation of power, with a new force field altogether. On the other hand, it may be resistance to the exercise of power which leaves unquestioned the fixity of the terms in which that power is exercised (Clegg 1989, p. 207).

In such a situation the resistance will be less systemically and more subjectively organized. Markus correctly pointed out that power should not just be seen in terms of employees resisting management, but also in terms of management resistance.

The social integration circuit focuses on symbolic power associated with rules of meaning and membership. It is related to legitimation, authority and access to resources (Backhouse et al. 2006; Weber 1978). The system integration circuit sees power exercised through techniques of discipline that constrain and channel action prohibitively and production that seeks to drive action through normative desires (Foucault 1977). System integration will have an effect on working practices as a result of ISS standards adoption (Backhouse et al. 2006).

The key to the model of circuits of power is clearly that juncture at which social and system integration “meet,” as it were, in the construction of obligatory passage points in an organization field. The stabilization and fixing of rules of meaning and membership, and techniques of production and discipline, in an organization field which is capable of extensive reproduction over space and time are the central issue (Clegg 1989, p. 241).

Such stabilization occurs through what sociologists refer to as institutionalization (Berger and Luckman 1971). Silva and Backhouse (2003) argue that institutionalization of IS occurs when the subsystems and actions are seamlessly integrated into routine business processes without anyone noticing. They further argue that the use of power is required to institutionalize an agency; if it resists change then it becomes a source of power.

This paper extends and integrates prior research on power, norms, and culture, using a circuits of power lens while investigating three research questions (refer Figure 1). To help answer these questions, a longitudinal case study of all 89 single- and multi-site NSW government agencies was undertaken from 2001 to 2008. $^{2}$

## Research Methodology

A canonical action research approach (Baskerville 1993; Baskerville and Wood-Harper 1998) was used by this study to both facilitate adoption of and evaluate compliance with the security standard over four project phases (see the Appendix). The development of a standard is shown to be a social process by Backhouse et al. (2006) and its adoption is a continuation of the overall social process of adoption and diffusion of innovation.

The fundamental contention of the action researcher is that complex social processes can be studied best by introducing changes into these (social) processes and observing the effect of these changes (Baskerville 1999, p. 4).

In other words, action research is eminently suitable to the study of an introduced social process and its diffusion over time. The action research approach with the circuits of power (i.e., repetition and routinization) framework (refer to Figure 1) highlights a line of “social enquiry” (Baskerville 1999, p. 5) into the dynamic adoption of, and compliance with, a standard, in a bounded social setting, rather than undertaking the study from a more positivist perspective. The differences between the objectives of positivistic science and action research have been highlighted (see Susman and Evered 1978). The use of the action research approach is at once a dynamic force of change within the social setting and a highly effective method of studying and evaluating this change.

Canonical action research is the most effective means to answer our research questions. Canonical action research is iterative in nature (repeated cycles); has a rigorous and well articulated structure (definition of researchers and subjects, qualification of their actions in the context of the study, definition of benefits and boundary of the research, entry and exit of the researchers); is collaborative in nature (researcher works closely with the subjects); and adds both to the development of the organization and the body of knowledge on the phenomenon under study.

The canonical process model outlined in Figure 2 indicates the rigorous steps in specifying and enacting the action research cycle within the organization (Susman and Evered 1978). This entails (1) diagnosing, or the holistic identification of primary problems and underlying causes of the organization's identified need for change; (2) action planning, or a theoretical framework which highlights the “desired future state” of the organization and the intervention required (from the action cycle) to be in this state; (3) action taking (directive or nondirective), or implementation of the planned action where researchers and their subjects (and perhaps third parties) collaborate in the planned intervention which causes planned change; (4) evaluating outcomes, “where the change was successful, the evaluation must critically question whether the undertaken action, among the myriad routine and non-routine organizational actions, was the sole cause of success” (Baskerville and Wood-Harper 1998, p. 97); and finally (5) specifying learning through “double loop learning” (Argyris and Schön 1978), which indicates the changes that have occurred to organizational norms through new knowledge or failure to learn, where the canonical action research process feeds back into itself for the start of another cycle of diagnosing → action planning → action taking → evaluating → specifying learning.

The action research cycle illustrates how “the organization thus learns more about its nature and the environment, and the constellation of theoretical elements of the scientific community continues to benefit and evolve” (Baskerville and Wood-Harper 1998, p. 97). Utilizing the canonical form of action research in conjunction with the circuits of power framework also avoids a limitation of ex post data collection previously experienced in the standard development study by Backhouse et al. (2006).

## Action Research and Validity

It is acknowledged that findings from an action research study are not able to be validated in the traditionally accepted positivistic science paradigm, that is reductionism, replicability, and refutation (Baskerville 1999; Checkland and Howell 1998; March et al. 1991; Susman and Evered 1978). However, this does not mean that action research should not be used as a technique. The validity of learning (the outcome of action research) can be increased by aggregating findings over several events as well as by making “a more intense examination of the individual case” (March et al. 1991, p. 7), which are both central characteristics of canonical action research (iteration and organizational development) and also evident in our research study design. A “declared-in-advance methodology” aids researchers in being able to demonstrate an action research study’s “recoverability” that, while not being able to make claims of scientific replicability, presents a much stronger case of validity than mere “plausibility” of a story (Checkland and Howell 1998). To this end, canonical action research requires a rigorous predetermined structure. Our research study meets the requirement for recoverability, “to declare in advance the epistemology in terms of which a piece of AR [action research] will acquire what counts as knowledge” (Checkland and Howell 1998, p. 18). There are a number of studies in IS that categorize the various action research forms that may be used to add to our understanding of IS and their social settings (Baskerville and Wood-Harper 1998). The canonical action research method is a rigorous approach to defining the characteristics of a study’s boundary context characteristics and their definition. ## Organizational Social Setting (NSW State Government and Agency Description)

The NSW government's preference for ISO 27001:2006 (and predecessors) was due to its availability and open and nonproprietary nature subject to a regular peer review and updating regime by global experts (best practice); risk management approach and flexibility to meet organization needs; information security as a basis for business governance and management issues requiring executive involvement, not merely as an information and communications technology (ICT) problem for the ICT group; accommodation of legislative and other legal requirements (e.g., Sarbanes Oxley 2002 for U.S. organizations operating overseas or visa versa; or Basel II recommendation in the finance sector); wide recognition and expanding pool of expertise in its use; and addressing of all the matters identified by the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD 2003).

In the adoption of ISS standards across the NSW public sector, the impetus was driven from the head of state (premier) as information security had arisen as a whole-of-government problem. The premier can give a mandate directly to a minister (a memorandum) of a portfolio or bypass ministers directly to chief executive officers, CEOs (a circular) of an agency. In this study, a Premier's Circular (PC 2001-46) was the mandate because it was directed to all agency CEOs, thereby exerting indirect discipline and influence by bypassing ministers. Agencies are established under portfolios to administer the policies of the government and to provide such services as required under legislation such as law enforcement and health. A portfolio is a grouping of government agencies by similar function. The NSW Shared Corporate Services (SCS) strategy classifies agencies into three groups: small (less than 350 full time equivalent staff), medium (from 350 to 1,000 FTEs), and large (more than 1,000 FTEs).

The recognition that differences occur between government agencies in size, function, and funding has not always been accounted for in the extant literature (Cushing and Pardo 2005). In order to avoid this shortcoming, and to assist study participants to more meaningfully understand the learning outcomes of the action research cycles, we have categorized and analyzed compliance findings contained in our data by agency size (number of full-time equivalent employees, FTEs) as an applicable contributing factor toward success in adopting an ISS standard (Kotulic and Clark 2004). We follow the SCS strategy categorizing agency size by FTEs (refer to Table 3). Categorizing agencies by FTE aligns this study with other literature within government (State of California State Information Security Office 2007) and industry domains (Kotulic and Clark 2004).

## Data Collection

The study involved eight online surveys complemented with interventions between surveys, such as interviews, round table discussions, and quarterly information system manager users group (ISMUG) forums, a focus group, and a continuous review of secondary documentation to facilitate and monitor ISS standard compliance to accreditation (see the Appendix for the data collection timetable). Survey data was collected in this study to track government compliance to a mandated ISS standard. Information systems security managers in different agencies (by size, Table 3) were asked 85 questions relating to the 127 controls (safeguards) from the security standard, in order to understand their progression toward accreditation with the standard.

The unasked questions, relating to the remaining controls, are regulated or legislated for by other NSW government policies (personnel category 4). It was initially estimated that each agency's ISS manager would take several hours to complete this survey, which was thought to be excessive. As a consequence, the survey was administered in four phases (shown in column 1 of the Appendix) (Keen 1981). This meant that a base level of data gathering for the 10 categories of Australian/New Zealand Standard, AS/NZS 17799.1:2001 (Standards Australia 2001) was not obtained until the fifth survey (Quarter 4 2002) was completed. Data collected from the meetings consisted of issues raised by participants (notes), audio recorded interviews, short surveys and questionnaires relating to security issues. Sixteen senior ISS managers participated in these meetings. The intent of the meetings was to draw out the perspectives and viewpoints of the government agency staff without biasing the data collection and to report on any noteworthy aspects of their comments. Secondary data was collected throughout this study and included annual reports, external reports, newspaper clippings, government publications, de jure standards, and database searches.

## Case Study Background and Interpretation

In 1997 the NSW government released its “Information Management and Technology Blueprint for NSW.” In this blueprint, the NSW government developed strategies to use new technologies as part of its policy to provide more customer-focused service delivery. The year 1997 also coincided with a dramatic increase in the number of government agency websites and portals interfacing with the public.

## Exogenous Contingencies Initiate Compliance to ISS De Jure Standard

The prime motive for this research is the importance of the ISS area to organizations (government agencies) and its strategic significance in the light of e-crime and terrorism threats, specifically identity crime, cyber crime, and denial-of-service attacks (Jamieson et al. 2009). When considering the security risks associated with information, it is not just the information itself that has to be considered. Compromise of information within an organization affects perceptions of that organization by those outside it. Notions of carelessness or untrustworthiness may take hold in the minds of customers, suppliers, and other external stakeholders and undermine their willingness to transact with the organization. Public confidence and reputation are also important intangible assets for government agencies.

Secondary data identified several exogenous contingencies triggered by regulation or legislation, national and international security incidents, and two state elections held within our study period. The elections and change in government, premier, and ministers caused some agencies to be merged which resulted in more agencies being ISS standard compliant after the Quarter 4 2004 online survey. This also had an effect on the three agency's size categories.

Other exogenous contingencies that affected the mandated adoption of the ISS de jure standard included legislation (non-negotiable statutory requirements), bills, exposure drafts, codes, rules, or policies. Examples with a direct impact on ISS standardization and its institutionalization include the NSW Privacy and Personal Information Protection Act 1998, Health Records and Information Privacy Bill 2001, December (Exposure draft) (Griffith 2002), and outsourcing in the public sector (Griffith and Figgis 1997).

## Episodic Power Relations

The next four sections address research question 1.

RQ 1: What is the effect of mandated compliance (to achieve full adoption of the standard and accreditation by an external agency) on ISS in e-government?

## Episodic Power Relations: Social Relations

The episodic power social relations are led by the democratic head of state (premier). The premier of the NSW government issued Circular PC2001-46 where he directed CEOs of all State government agencies to undertake measures to protect electronic information (NSW Government Department of Premier and Cabinet 2001a). Circular PC2001-46 was one of six circulars and memorandums (each superseding the previous) handed down by NSW premiers over our study period Quarter 1 2001 to Quarter 1 2008, with Memorandum (premier directs ministers) PM2007-04 designated as current policy (NSW Government Department of Premier and Cabinet 2001a, 2001b, 2001c, 2003, 2004, 2007).

## Episodic Power Relations: Agency

Episodic power relations concept of agency refers to an individual or organization (Clegg 1989). In November 2001 the premier of the NSW government sent out a circular to all government agencies PC2001-46 for the security of electronic information. This mandated that the NSW government had a duty to safeguard its large information holdings. The premier directed that all agencies were to undertake measures to protect electronic information, which included developing and implementing policies and plans for information security management by 2002; assigning responsibility for IT security to a nominated officer; ensuring that all staff, including contractors and consultants, understand their responsibilities for information security; establishment by the NSW Department of Commerce a program for external penetration testing of agencies' IT systems; and agencies having their IT systems certified to the national standard.

The events of the four-year election cycle also had an impact on mandated adoption processes through pre-election promises and organization reform, such as restructuring, mergers, or creation of new agencies. These promises also extended to budgetary controls (a management cause of resistance) including IT capital projects supervised by the NSW Department of Treasury impacting some agencies with their ISS standards compliance (this is a type of resistance that Markus (1983) discusses in her theory). Ministers' portfolios are often changed after a new premier is elected or after elections and there were several different IT ministers over our study period. These organizational changes have an impact on the ISS standards implementation processes and often cause resistance through personnel and role changes in agencies. The framework that agencies develop to enact the premier's mandate for ISS accreditation passes through an OPP as a positive control of power and authority by the agency (Clegg 1989). However, organization norms and culture can exert resistance through this type of mandate where they are not already part of the CEO's key performance indicators, as ISS may not be viewed by the agency's culture as a high priority (see Table 5). The CEOs would be more cooperative if their ministers directed them to implement ISS standards to accreditation because it would be meeting their key performance indicators due to the direct power relations.

## Episodic Power Relations: Standing Conditions

The episodic power relation standing conditions are the control lever or gate for resources, primarily appropriate funding. Funding is requested by agencies via business cases for the deployment and management of resources. In July 2006, the NSW Minister for Commerce launched the innovative government ICT plan. The plan sets the framework for a coordinated government-wide approach to planning, expenditure, and allocation of ICT resources. It represents a major change in ICT policy direction and paves the way for a new and more effective way of service delivery for the NSW government (NSW Government CIO Executive Council 2006).

## Episodic Power Relations: Outcomes

The episodic power outcomes of the mandated compliance, which contributed to change and helped agencies toward compliance and becoming accredited, included a video (“Wish It Wasn’t Me”), an independent report, online security surveys and reports, ISS forum, and quarterly focus/user groups (ISMUG). Recently more agencies have achieved full accreditation and this changes their focus to maintaining their continued certification status. These outcomes transformed the way agencies conducted their day-to-day activities. While these episodic power relational outcomes may have contributed to change, we need to be cautious that causal inferences cannot be made with great certainty.

Episodic power relations resistance problems arose from a lack of understanding of security in the early stage of project implementation. In a whole-of-government context, resistance to the project came from a cost-benefit perspective (Markus 1983). The risks associated with cyber terrorism were high on the government's agenda and were initially extremely difficult to quantify. However, the cost of doing nothing (after September 11, 2001) was a risk the NSW government wasn't prepared to bear. ## Rules of Practice

The next two sections will address research question 2.

RQ 2: What are the critical issues to be addressed in mandated compliance to achieve full adoption of a standard?

## Rules of Practice: Social Integration

There is no inclusive corporate governance policy for the whole of NSW government. Each government agency has autonomy to set their individual processes, including the requirement to achieve certification to AS/NZS17799.1:2001 (Standards Australia 2001) or seek additional funding from central government. This is a major omission and source of project resistance for any government and, in fact, discussions with operatives in the Commonwealth Public Service (federal government) reveal a similar deficiency. A practical suggestion would be that a central agency undertake an overall risk assessment of the whole-of-government information systems together with a whole-of-government business impact analysis.

The first minister for Information Technology in the NSW government was appointed in November 1997. In 1999, the NSW government endorsed a reporting framework to monitor the progress of electronic services online. Commitment was monitored through a reporting framework called Electronic Service Delivery (ESD) and measured the number and categories of services available via the web. The Department of Commerce published revised information management and technology guidelines for electronic security in January 2001 (Memorandum 3.3 [Version 4], NSW Government Department of Premier and Cabinet 2001a). The guidelines encapsulated the ISS processes to be followed by all government agencies in NSW and are aligned with AS/NZS 17799.1:2001 (NSW Wales Government Department of Premier and Cabinet 2001a, p.1). In the period 1997 to early 2001 the functionality of websites changed from static hyperlink pages to dynamic database-driven websites capable of greater interaction with users and the ability to accept and process financial transactions. Unfortunately, this exposed an opportunity for hackers, identity threats, and cyber crime perpetrators to exploit vulnerabilities in early operating systems. Incidents regarding identity theft, cyber crime, and security of critical infrastructure in the United States, Australia, and elsewhere prompted the need for better ISS measures in NSW government and an increase in legislation to protect physical assets and critical infrastructure against unwanted intrusion or terrorist attacks.

The updating or amendments of national ISS standards over time and the subsequent conversion from national to international ISS standards also affected adoption of the standards by government agencies. These driving factors can only be achieved where public confidence, and hence appropriate security, is established with the relevant IS within the public service. These factors are a direct reference to the critical success factors of AS/NZS17799.2001/Amendment:1-2004, and underpin the successful implementation of the standards and the community expectations it reflects (Standards Australia 2004). The policies and rules developed by agencies for ISS accreditation pass through OPP, usually with an approved or authorized signature by the executive or CEO of that agency.

## Rules of Practice: Obligatory Passage Points

There is evidence of changes in IT/IS security but these are driven by an increase in the number of cyber attacks and threats as demonstrated by the general increase in the number of reported attacks (Richardson 2008). Conversely, the number of agencies and organizations seeking to become certified to the standard AS/NZS17799.1:2001 or International standard ISO 17799:2001 is increasing (Standards Australia 2001). The time taken to achieve certification is dependent on a number of factors (such as agency size, organizational culture, group or subunit norms, type of business, or resources). To further complicate this process, over recent years the rapid changes and growth of predominantly Internet-based systems has placed security concerns at a lower priority. Thus for some agencies the challenge of achieving compliance and accreditation/certification, have required a significant restructure of their business processes and, in some cases, this is an insurmountable task. As a result of this research study, a number of policy changes have occurred within NSW government. These changes have resulted as an effect of this research project on the agencies and subjects under investigation. The use of a canonical action research approach to conduct this project has directly informed and impacted IT/IS security practices within the NSW government in a positive manner by improving the level of ISS knowledge among ISS managers.

## Domination

The next section addresses research question 3:

RQ 3: How do government agencies move to comply with the IS security standard?

## Domination: Systemic Integration

At the start of this research project, the discipline of ISS was relatively unfamiliar to the majority of organizations as e-government was itself only in the early stages of adoption. Literature over the last decade in both academic and general business arenas has now paid more attention to ISS, and the adoption of de jure security standards has emerged as a robust measure to provide rigor in the process of improving security within this domain.

The 10 categories of AS/NZS17799.1:2001 (Standards Australia 2001) provided a universally recognizable description of an information security framework to the survey respondents. Table 4 summarizes the survey results across 9 of the 10 categories of the national standard, AS/NZS 17799:2001 from Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 1 2008. The 2008 survey was based on the international standard, ISO 27001:2006 that has 11 categories. In 2008, not all questions were exactly the same as those asked in the three earlier surveys and as a consequence several were not applicable for direct comparison purposes (We omit category 11, “incident management,” for similar reasons but discuss reported incidents within category 6, which includes 2008 survey results). The objective of the first category of the standard, “security policies,” is to give management clear direction in establishing an information security policy framework. The important elements of the comments raised in the forum on the need for senior management support in the development of a security policy include “the reason management support is lacking in many projects (security policy development) is the poor explanation of the issues and benefits middle management provide to senior management.”

Table 4 (category 1) highlights the percentage of agencies with a security policy across small, medium, and large categories and the total population. The percentage increase in the number of ISS policies demonstrates a commitment to security within the agencies, indicating that management was becoming serious about the ISS problem. In terms of agency categories for the small agencies the percentage with ISS policies increased from 21 to 88 percent and for medium from 29 to 93 percent from the Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 1 2008 surveys. The percentage of large agencies with ISS managers that had security policies increased from 64 to 94 percent over 5.5 years. It is clear that the smaller the agency was, the more resistance there was to installing security policies, which was a stipulated process in PC2001-46. The resistance was due mainly to a lack of funding resources. The following interview responses highlight funding issues: “supported by senior management...the resources are given to you and it’s an achievable outcome” and “most business cases for security upgrades are poorly structured and written and do not clearly explain the benefits of security and the tasks to be carried out.”

Category 2 (in Table 4), “security organization,” highlights the increase in the number of ISS managers and demonstrates a commitment to security within the agencies, indicating that management is starting to become serious about the ISS problem. One respondent mentioned that “money/resources will be required (for a security manager)” (follow-up interview participant 3). Table 4 (category 2) demonstrates that the number of agencies with an ISS manager increased over the period from Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 1 2008. An analysis on the resources committed to ISS within agencies showed that resources have risen from an average of A\$54,000 in 2001 to an average of A\$92,000 in 2008, an increase of 70 percent in the budget allocated to ISS.

The objective of category 3 of the standard is to track “asset classification and control” and maintain appropriate protection of organizational assets. All major information assets should be accounted for and have a nominated owner that helps to ensure that appropriate protection is maintained. In summary, the small- and medium-sized agencies are able to adequately manage the protection of asset classification (adequate to high range) with the exception of the larger agencies, which can extend over many sites or locations, making it difficult for an exact asset register over items, such as decentralized computer hardware. Users within the decentralized agencies could have seen the mandated ISS implementation as a means of centralization of information, causing some resistance (Markus 1983).

The objective of category 4 of the standard “personnel” is to reduce the risks of human error, fraud, or misuse of facilities. No questions were asked in this survey category because government policy stipulates all new government employees must attend and pass an induction program.

Category 5, “physical/environment security,” indicates a Likert score range from adequate to very high, for this section, with controls for physical and environmental security as shown in Table 4. Total physical and environmental security improved over the three surveys from Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 4 2004. The importance of this section of the standard is highlighted by the following comment from the forum: “With a physical loss so we need a driver…in understanding the need for protecting the assets” (Participant 2).

Category 6, “communication and network management,” seeks to identify the number of threats being acknowledged by agencies. The majority of detected incidents were targeted at small agencies, the largest group, compared with medium- and large-sized agencies (see Table 4). Estimated incidents in medium-sized agencies more than halved from 71 to 29 over the study period. An increase in the use of automatic virus updates and the decrease of manual updates occurred over the 7 years of the study. There were also deserving critical assessments with a “lack of monitoring security logs of incidents and unusual events” in some instances (forum participant 4). The number of agencies selecting automatic/ daily updates for their virus updates has increased dramatically.

Category 7, “system access control,” seeks to identify the level at which access control is rated within government agencies. Table 4 Likert score ratings of in-house controls to prevent unauthorized access to systems highlights that these incidents declined from the Quarter 4 2002 survey to Quarter 4 2004 (Phase 3) in total from 5.23 to 4.01. The following quote hints at some resistance, which may have caused some of the estimated decline if this view was wide spread. “IS security is a necessary evil which hindered productivity” (participant 1). Access control covers many security measures and is probably the single most important security measure. Passwords are the usual method of providing access control and are its weakest link. New change password policies and authentication techniques like biometrics are becoming available to deal with this problem.

Category 8, “systems development and maintenance,” seeks to identify the level which security is built into ISS. Table 4 shows the percentage of ISS managers whose responses were “yes” for in-house controls to prevent unauthorized access where total improvement almost doubled from 11 to 21 percent to systems from the Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 4 2004 surveys. Most improvement was for agencies with less than 350 FTEs, however, “it is not an imaginary concept; it is real, it is a pragmatic approach to managing their business in a day-to-day environment” (forum participant 4). Moreover, “if IT [security systems] is not being pushed then it’s going backward" (one-on-one interview participant 4). It is important to the future of agencies to develop both policies and management systems to support newer types of controls such as cryptography during systems development and subsequent use.

“Business continuity planning” (BCP), category 9, remained a problematic area for government agencies because of the high cost of developing and testing plans, particularly for large agencies (Smith and Jamieson 2006). This factor is indicative of resistance by management (Clegg 1989; Clegg et al. 2006; Markus 1983). Under NSW Y2K legislation, all NSW government agencies were to have adequately tested BCP including contingency planning in place (refer PC98-09, NSW Government Department of Premier and Cabinet 1998). The results for BCP completeness in Table 4 indicate an adequate level but over half of the agencies rated the effectiveness below an adequate level. Although BCP is generally supported in principle at an operational level, it does not appear to have the necessary impetus for successful implementation of a security program from an organizational perspective. More detail surrounding each BCP issue regarding resistance due to culture or norms is provided in Table 5. While not all of these issues are applicable to all agencies, they would appear to be relevant to a large number of agencies in respect to their security and BCP processes. The key issue is that senior management should develop an understanding of their agency. The need for management support is essential and necessary as it is senior management that is charged with the task of initiating and supporting important projects both with funding and resources. The failure of senior management to fully support ISS is limited by the expectation of the outcome measured against the performance of other projects within the organization. There are also inappropriate perceptions from management as commented on by interview participant 7: “The perception or assumption [is] that security is solely an IT responsibility rather than recognizing that it affects the whole business.” Therefore, security of business information cannot be solely handed to the IT agency. Essentially, an effective level of security includes an effective and tested BCP.

The objective of category 10 of the standard, “IS policy compliance,” is to avoid legal breaches of the security requirements. No questions were asked about the extent of legal compliance in this survey; however, questions about the requirement to ensure compliance of systems with the organizational security policy were included in this survey. The objective of this control is to ensure compliance with the agencies ISS policy, including the safeguarding of organizational records. The level of compliance with the staff handbook/manual covering security practices is reasonably high. Table 4 shows, using a Likert score, that compliance increased for small agencies from 4.56 to 4.64 from Quarter 4 2002 to Quarter 4 2004. Similarly, total compliance increased marginally from 4.40 to 4.49. An intervention (distribution of a security video) was effective in supplementing this control. The ISS accreditation passes through OPP, usually with an approved or authorized signature by the executive or CEO of an accrediting or certifying organization (e.g., SAI Global).

## Progress in Mandated Adoption of the ISS Standard and Accreditation Process

In our study we assumed that the adoption of the ISS standard would be achieved when all agencies (whole-of-government) achieved accreditation as per PC2001-46. Australian-based ISS certifying bodies, the British Standard Institute, Lloyds Register Quality Assurance, and SAI Global, are significant actors in an organization receiving full accreditation. By 2008, of the 30 agencies that achieved full accreditation, 55 percent were small-size agencies followed by 38 percent large-size agencies. This indicates that IS staff and management of these agencies had, or had acquired through shared services or outsourced arrangements, the required knowledge, understanding, and expertise to identify the need for effective security and begin their ISS standard adoption project. While the total number of agencies that managed full accreditation was low in percentage terms, the greater majority did make substantial progress toward this goal. A further 30 agencies expect to be accredited within the year.

Small agencies (fewer than 350 FTE staff) present a more varied picture. Improving ISS across these agencies requires significant improvement due to the lack of resources and staff assigned to improving security. Some small agencies host their IT systems with a central government agency bureau, a structure that allows the creation of cluster groups and streamlines their path to accreditation. These clusters could be based on shared arrangements by business type or portfolio affinity. This is achieved by reducing either time or resources expended on improving security for individual agencies.

Medium-size agencies (from 350 to 1,000 FTE staff) have demonstrated the most commitment toward accreditation for their security program. In addition, they also participated in internal shared corporate services (mainly business process reengineering) to consolidate services into fewer hardware platforms, which in turn allows more streamlined IT/IS systems.

Large agencies (more than 1,000 FTE staff), have sufficiently large corporate services structures to undertake a security program to achieve accreditation to the standard of AS/NZS17799.1:2001. They generally have enough resources to utilize technology wherever possible to achieve compliance to the standard. However, many large agencies are highly decentralized across long distances and many buildings, which creates difficulties achieving accreditation across so many facilities. This problem is addressed in the risk analysis stage of accreditation where the highest risk business functions/processes are mitigated first and lower risk functions subsequently. For those agencies covering a wide geographic area, the regional or decentralized offices are usually similar in terms of organizational structures and duties performed. The process of accreditation can be scoped to permit a reduction of the number of regional offices needing accreditation for the entire organization.

## Case Summary

Figure 3 highlights the three circuits of power with actions from the case study. Circuit 1 is the relationship between the social relations (SR) and agencies, which exercises a control to check and verify the affiliation between social relations power and agencies' resistance. In our study it verifies the conformity of agency progression to accreditation via a survey and longitudinal study (as summarized in Table 4). The results from the action research survey (the OPP in this case) are the authorization that the information supplied is an accurate depiction of the agencies security compliance.

Circuit 2 is the path of the relationship between the following nodes: SR ↔ Agencies ↔ Standing Conditions ↔ Outcomes ↔ Rules. These nodes exercise a regulate and management role to develop policies, guidelines, and strategic direction. In our study, it is policy adoption of an agency to set about achieving compliance to the ISS standard as directed by the social relations. The agency would free resources that are to produce a policy statement and develop an ISS manager role (see Table 4, categories 1 and 2) to comply with the PC2001-46 directive. For example, Table 4 (category 2) shows the ISS manager level of compliance changed from 30 percent in 2002 to 92 percent by 2008.

Circuit 3 is the path of the relationship between the following nodes: SR ↔ Agencies ↔ Standing Conditions ↔ Outcomes ↔ Rules ↔ Innovation. These nodes exercise an action and process role to achieve accreditation of an agency via external certification. In our study, it is the process to transform systems (IT and non-IT) to the level required by the ISS standard and the external certifying agent. The level of certification changed from four agencies in 2001 to 30 agencies in 2008.

## Contributions, Limitations, and Areas for Future Research

This study is unique for its coverage of the whole-of-government in ISS de jure standards implementation, in the methods used for analysis (canonical action research by multi-methods: survey, interviews, focus groups, forums), and in its use of Clegg's (1989) framework of circuits of power as a lens to study this phenomenon. While the case study is unique in its coverage and extent, the case is representative and may be generalizable (recoverability) to other governments around the world. All governments of this level have a similar structure including such agencies as Agriculture, Education, Health, Justice, Law Enforcement, Utilities, Transport, and Tourism, so the findings may be applicable. Further, whole-of-government may be viewed as a large organization with multiple but integrating parts, similar to any other large decentralized hierarchical business organization, so the findings may also be generalizable to these organizations. Both government and large organizations face common issues of subunits differing in size (FTEs), resource allocation, skills and staff constraints, management buy-in, managing resistance (culture and norms), monitoring progress, and implementing security standards. Figure 3 provides a summary of the case showing the major elements of the case superimposed diagrammatically on the three circuits of power and their related nodes.

Table 6 provides a summary of our findings and their relation to assumptions regarding ISS standards and the circuits of power framework. The paper extends the use of the circuits of power framework to cover the adoption of a standard from implementation to accreditation and continued compliance through independent audits by accredited third party organizations. We overcome an earlier limitation noted by Backhouse et al. (2006) regarding retrospective investigation by being able to use a canonical action research approach to gather data on the entire adoption process. The action research approach used a change agent in situ who was able to interpret information arising from the case in real time. In addition, Backhouse et al. researched a single case study, in contrast to our study where we investigated a whole-of-government case with 89 agencies as of 2008.

The study also contributes to our understanding of resistance to the implementation of an ISS de jure standards in a government setting, and supports the literature (Clegg 1989; Markus 1983) in its account of where examples of resistance occurred, which are detailed in the case (see Table 5). Management in other governments or large organizations should be mindful of these areas when implementing ISS standards.

A limitation of this study is that the agency findings were based on a whole-of-government case study. However, the single-case study method is appropriate when the selected case is the typical case (Lee 1989). Limitations of the circuits of power framework have been previously identified (Backhouse et al. 2006; Silva and Backhouse 2003) as arising from its complexity and interpretive nature. This action research study design was proposed in an attempt to develop and define a more accurate, descriptive, and specifically focused research project than would have been possible through following a single method approach.

Another limitation is claiming causal inferences from the action research interventions (survey and reporting, videos, interviews, forums, and focus groups) to changes of behavior, attitudes, and movement toward ISS implementation and accreditation. While these action research interventions may have contributed to change there needs to be caution because causal inferences cannot be made with great certainty. Alternate explanations may apply. For example, one explanation for the changes might be that, over time, more IT employees (and even the technology itself) are becoming more sophisticated and that employees adopt new standards and procedures as a result of this, not because of some external policy change effected from above, or as a result of some other intervention. Further research is required to confirm the subjective components of the interpretive inquiry and any causal relationships.

Future research could also be undertaken at the level of one organization (or a single government agency) to understand how power, resistance, norms, or culture impact implementation of standards. It may also be applied to the mapping and interpretation of compliance or adherence to accreditation with the ISS de jure standard. This could also be investigated in other whole-of-governments or in decentralized hierarchical industry organizations.

## Conclusion

The circuits of power framework was used to analyze power relationships where there was a mandate to adopt a de jure ISS standard across whole-of-government agencies. The effect of mandated compliance on e-government was to mitigate the political risks associated with the cyber threats to government information assets. This was achieved by 30 agencies fully accredited and 59 agencies progressing toward accreditation by 2008. The delay of full accreditation for 59 agencies demonstrates resistance due to norms and culture within some agencies. Also, resistance was demonstrated by agency management's response to a lack of resources being committed by Treasury, which further delayed full accreditation.

The key lesson learned from this study was that a large-scale IS/IT project conducted across multiple government agencies and sites of varying sizes requires that the implementation be staggered and suited to agency size, thus breaking down the complexity of the tasks enabling resources (people and budgets) to be put in place and allocated to future project phases. These facts demonstrate that this study corroborates earlier research on IT implementation and power relationships (Keen 1981). There is no inclusive corporate governance policy for the whole of NSW government. Each government agency has autonomy to set their individual processes, including the requirement to achieve certification to

AS/NZS17799.1:2001 or seek additional funding from central government. A practical suggestion would be that a central agency undertakes an overall risk assessment of the whole-of-government information systems together with a whole-of-government business impact analysis.

Our study highlighted the need for adequate financial resourcing and management support and buy-in as keys to effective implementation. The research process undertaken in this study (canonical action research) may be applied to private organizations (such as large decentralized, hierarchically controlled multinational corporations or nonprofit organizations) to see if the same power relationships hold for other organizational types. The power of institutionalization (agency accreditation) is driven by the categories of the ISS standards. In addition, managing resistance in the areas of access to funding resources and to senior management commitment is critical.
