# The Deeper, the Better? Effect of Online Brand Community Activity on Customer Purchase Frequency

## Abstract

Researchers disagree on how to determine customer purchase frequency based on the level of activity in an online brand community. Consequently, there is a great need to reconcile the mixed findings obtained so far. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, we hypothesize that the effect of community participation may be contingent on participators’ goal-pursuit focus (prevention or promotion). Our analysis of customer blog data in a company-sponsored community together with transactional data of customers significantly increases purchase frequency; however, deep participation has a very different effect among prevention-focused customers.

Keywords: Online brand community, community participation, regulatory focus theory, prevention versus promotion, purchase frequency

## 1 Introduction

Online brand communities, which are always enabled by social media technology, are designed to facilitate consumers’ communication and interaction with each other to discuss product-, brand-, or company-related information, as well as share their experiences [3]. Unlike the traditional brand community, an online brand community provides a virtual forum for consumers to communicate without limitations of time and place. Such communities not only allow companies to observe what consumers think, but can be crafted as channels to promote new products, spread brand image, and cultivate consumer loyalty [2, 6, 63, 76]. Realizing a series of advantages, both large (e.g., Nike, Apple, and Coca-Cola) and small companies<sup>1</sup> have begun to build their own communities to improve customer contributions like increasing their visits or purchases. To this end, the understanding of customer behavior in online brand communities attracts much attention from both researchers and practitioners.

Customer behavior can differ varying on their participation level (i.e., from shallow to deep) [29, 54]. Some customers join a community only to read the information available in order to make better purchase decisions. Customers can also engage in the community to exchange or share something with others with similar interests, and post new messages or reply to others’ messages. Customers may also become leaders in the community through organizing group activities or discussions [57]. Compared with the people who just read the information posted, leadership members are considered to be more deeply involved [56]. Recognizing that different types of participator join communities and that they have different participation levels, the question arises: are they all beneficial to a company?

Several recent studies have attempted to identify the relationships between participators’ behavior in an online brand community and their value to the company (for a summary, see Table 1), and generated mixed findings. For instance, through an econometric analysis based on second-hand data from a third-party brand community (i.e., Facebook) of a wine company, Rishika et al. [62] found that more deeply involved customers increased their product purchases. This result is reinforced by the work of Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56], who focused on examining the relationships between customer participation level and purchase behavior in the music industry (i.e., Last.fm), and demonstrated the positive relationship between them. In contrast, Shang et al. [64] did not find a direct and positive effect of online brand community participation level on customer purchase behavior.

Unlike these studies, which focus on the average effect of participation level (i.e., do not realize that these can differ even among shallow/deep participators) on customer purchase behavior, in this article, we examine one contextual factor (i.e., consumer-specific characteristic) that moderates the relationship between these two. Based on the regulatory focus theory [38], which indicates that customers with different self-regulatory focuses respond differently to the information presented in a community, we conjecture that participator purchase behavior is not only determined by how deeply a participator is involved in a community, but also is contingent on the his/her regulatory focus. Through model building and empirical testing, our findings make two significant contributions to the extant knowledge. First, faced with the inconsistent findings of prior studies regarding the relationship between consumer brand community participation and purchase frequency [56, 64], this study offers a new perspective through consideration of consumers’ regulatory focus, thus, to some extent, reconciling the mixed findings. Second, although it is widely observed that consumers with different regulatory focuses (i.e., promotion-focused versus prevention-focused consumers) respond differently to the same piece of information [34, 70, 74], little is known about how consumer regulatory focus influences consumer purchase behavior. By using archival data to test our proposition, this study proposes that, in a natural environment, consumers’ different regulatory focuses typically affect their purchase behavior.

## 2 Theoretical background

## 2.1 Ladder of participation in online communities

An online brand community is “a specialized, non-geographically bound community based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a brand” [53]. Compared with a traditional offline brand community, an online brand community is much easier and cheaper for a company to develop [23]. Companies can choose to build communities by themselves as independent forums, or to use third-party platforms like Google Groups, MSN Groups, and even Facebook. Some examples are www.nikonians.com, www.saabnet.com, and Fans of Apple on Facebook (https://www.facebook.com/fansofapple).

Various classification frameworks have been proposed in prior studies to examine how customers participate in brand communities. For instance, Madupu et al. [51] classified participators as either non-interactive and interactive. Non-interactive participators are those who are not highly involved in a community, passive activities constitute the majority of their behaviors. In prior studies, non-interactive participators have been referred to as “lurk,” and the participators who exhibit such behavior are called “lurkers” [16]. There are two types of “lurker.” One is the passive lurker, who joins a community silently, merely browsing the brand community and reading messages posted there. The other is the active lurker, who not only browses and reads the shared information, but also joins in with information transfer behaviors like forwarding community information to others through different channels. On the other hand, an interactive member is a participator who joins a community deeply by engaging in various activities such as posting messages, responding to other participators’ queries, participating in contests in the community, and leading others to join activities.

In line with but deviating from Madupu’s work, Andersen [7] classified participators’ behaviors according to different levels of interactivity: light browsers, browsers, enthusiasts, and contributors. Light browsers and browsers focus on discussing the shallow behavior of participators in the community without contributing, while enthusiasts and contributors are more committed and contribute more. Preece and Shneiderman [60] provided a comprehensive framework explaining the behavior of brand

#

community members from “reader” to “leader.” They categorized various types of behavior into this framework. For instance, focusing on the “reader” section, they presented and discussed the behavior of reading, browsing, searching, and returning; while in the “leader” section, behavior like promoting participation, mentoring novices, and setting and upholding policies are identified. Based on Preece’s framework and working from different perspectives (e.g., content consumption), Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56] classified participators’ behaviors as content consumption, content organization, community involvement, and community leadership.

While researchers have provided a series of frameworks for studying participators’ behaviors in online communities, the central thesis of these frameworks is that customers can differ in their participation level (i.e., from shallow to deep). In reality, most participators engage in brand communities shallowly, that is, they only read the information shared in the community, sometimes forward interesting information, and make limited contributions to the community. However, researchers argue that although most participators engage shallowly, they can still be valuable to companies. Different kinds of participation lead to different results, including participators’ attitude and behavior [9, 42]. Researchers have not reached a consensus with respect to the way participation level relates to customer value. For instance, focusing on the context of product purchase, Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56] concluded that there is a positive relationship between participation level and customer product purchase. However, researchers have also demonstrated that a high level of participation does not necessarily bring an increase in purchase and loyalty, while shallow attendance does [64].

To reconcile the mixed findings of prior studies regarding the relationship between participation levels and final customer purchase frequency, we draw on regulatory focus theory. In this context, we conjecture that customers involved deeply in the community with different regulatory focuses (i.e., promotion-focus versus prevention-focus) will present different behaviors and thus lead to varying results. By integrating the “ladder of participation” framework, we focus on examining four different types of participation: content consumption, content contribution, community collaboration, and community leadership. The former two represent relatively shallow participation, while the latter two represent deep participation.

## 2.2 Regulatory focus theory

Regulatory focus theory is used to explain why and how promotion-focus and prevention-focus influence people’s information processing and decision making [38, 40]. A promotion focus reflects people’s accomplishments, hopes, and aspirations, and greater concern with the presence of positive outcomes. A prevention-focus reflects avoidance, responsibility, and safety, and preference for the absence of negative outcomes. The regulatory focus has been widely studied, both as a chronic individual personality feature (e.g., [34, 39, 70]) and as a situational state (e.g., [8, 73, 77]) that can be activated by situational demands, such as through experimental promotion versus prevention incentives. Previous experimental studies have investigated different manipulations, like reading persuasive information framed in terms of gain versus non-gain, or loss versus non-loss [37, 47].

Relating the regulatory focus theory to specific research contexts, Lee and Aaker [48] found that promotion-focused consumers are more likely to be persuaded by appeals that are framed in terms of gains, while prevention-focused consumers are more likely to be persuaded by appeals framed in terms of losses. In advertising, researchers like Chang and Chou [17] and Jain et al. [43] have suggested that prevention-focused and promotion-focused consumers respond differently to negative and positive framings (e.g., this attribute of product A is better than product B versus this attribute of product B is

#

worse than product A). In addition, connecting regulatory focus theory and other contextual factors, researchers have also identified that the fit notion between regulatory focus and temporal orientation influences consumers’ information processing and preference of information, thus leading to different responses to focal types of advertising [45]. This finding is further reinforced and extended by Lee et al. [49], who found that a typical piece of information (concrete versus abstract) is more persuasive and effective on an individual when there is a fit between regulatory focus (i.e., promotion-focus versus prevention-focus) and construal level (high versus low). In conclusion, studies examining consumer regulatory focus and their behavior suggest that regulatory focus plays an especially significant role in processes related to persuasion, judgment, and choice.

Other than the focus listed above regarding the effect of regulatory focus on consumer information processing and decision making (i.e., behavioral effects), researchers have also studied the association between regulatory focus, as one of the main consumer characteristics, and consumer emotions in expression [14, 50, 72]. For instance, Higgins et al. [40] analyzed why and how regulatory focus is related to emotions. They argued that promotion-focused people are more likely to be happy or satisfied with a positive outcome. Prevention-focused consumers are more likely to be calm and relaxed when faced with a positive outcome, rather than enjoy positive emotions like promotion-focused consumers. On the other hand, when there is no positive outcome, promotion-focused individuals would at most be disappointed or discouraged, while prevention-focused consumers would be tense or uneasy. In the same vein, Brockner and Higgins [14] theorized and proposed that regulatory focus is a precursor or predictor of emotionality. That is, promotion-focused and prevention-focused people respond differently even to the same piece of information, regardless of whether the outcome is positive or negative. Further, Yen et al. [72] used field experiments to empirically validate the relationships between customers’ regulatory focus and their emotional expression.

Applying regulatory focus theory to our research context, we conjecture that the community involvement of participators with different regulatory focuses is motivationally distinct, though both attempt to acquire information from online communities. Below we will discuss how regulatory focus influences participators’ behavior in online brand communities.

## 3 Research model and hypotheses

The research model is presented in Figure 1. We study the main effect of different levels of participation on customer value exploration and the moderating role of regulatory focus. Customer value can take various forms, such as customer creativity or innovation performance, customer visit frequency, customer purchase frequency, and so on [13, 32, 62]. In our study, we focus on examining purchase frequency, not only because it is the most prominent and direct reflection of customer value a company can gain from its community, but also because purchase frequency is more likely to be the final step of customer behavior in online brand communities, which, to some extent, also represents customer loyalty. Meanwhile, the examination of purchase frequency has also been an especially prominent part of previous studies, like Goh et al. [32]. As noted in our theoretical section, we use the ladder framework of brand community participation to examine the relationship between participators’ behavior and their purchase frequency. Integrating this framework into our study, we take both shallow and deep participation into consideration, defining content consumption and content contribution as shallow participation, and community collaboration and community leadership as deep participation. With regard to regulatory focus, we consider both promotion-focused and prevention-focused participators.

## 3.1 Main effects

As noted above, an online brand community is a platform provided for customers to obtain information, interact with each other, or share knowledge. Participators can engage in different activities ranging from shallow to deep, like reading messages, posting messages, or leading others to join the community. Content consumption, like reading behavior, is a primary mechanism through which customers gain informational benefit in order to make better judgments or purchase decisions [15]. Customers who reply to messages are seeking to improve their reputation [65], and contribute new information to help others [69]. In addition, participators can also be highly involved through community collaboration and community leadership [4].

These behaviors in the ladder of participation imply the underlying commitment level of participators. Bateman et al. [10] comprehensively examined different types of commitment (continuance commitment, affective community, and normative commitment) and explored their different effects on customers participation behaviors. The results showed that continuance commitment is more likely to promote shallow consumer participation in the community, like content consumption; affective commitment is more related to the behavior of content contribution. Much deeper, the behaviors of collaboration and leadership are typically determined by normative commitment. Regardless of the type of commitment behind typical customers’ participation behavior, marketing researchers have widely argued that there is a tight and positive relationship between customer commitment and their purchase behavior [11, 26, 36]. Therefore, based on four types of customer activity, we posit: H1a: Content consumption in an online brand community is positively associated with participators purchase frequency. H1b: Content contribution in an online brand community is positively associated with participators’ purchase frequency. H1c: Community collaboration in an online brand community is positively associated with participators purchase frequency. H1d: Community leadership in an online brand community is positively associated with participators purchase frequency.

## 3.2 Moderating effects

Regulatory focus theory posits that participators with different self-regulatory systems perceive and process information in distinct ways. Promotion-focused regulation, focusing on the fulfillment of a task, involves a risky information processing style, in which positive aspects of a piece of information are more favorably received; prevention focus is associated with a more conservative, risk-averse, and vigilant processing style, in which negative aspects of a price of information are emphasized and generated greater impact [28]. This implies that promotion-focused and prevention-focused people place different emphasis even on the same piece of information, thus biasing their judgment and decision making [20, 68]. In particular, consumers’ regulatory focus determines the information they deliberately seek out and the information that attracts their attention [46, 70]. This can be seen from a series of previous studies [1, 75]. For instance, Zhao and Pechmann [75] found that information is more persuasive when the framing (e.g., positive framing versus negative framing) caters to the consumer’s regulatory focus.

In an online brand community, although a variety of information is included (i.e., both promotion-oriented framed and prevention-oriented framed), it is equally available to both prevention-focused and prevention-focused participators, that is, the participators with different regulatory focuses share the same opportunity to search and process typical information. However, as suggested by the theory, the persuasion effect is more effective when there is a fit between the promotion-oriented framed information is more related to focus on gains and advantages, while prevention-oriented framed information is more related to focus on losses and disadvantages. Based on this, we conjecture that promotion-focused participators are more likely to be affected by promotion-oriented information, thus leading to a positive effect on final purchase behavior; while the opposite would be true for prevention-focused participators. To this end, we posit:

H2a: Participators’ regulatory focus moderates the relationship between content consumption and purchase frequency.

H2b: Participators’ regulatory focus moderates the relationship between community contribution and purchase frequency.

H2c: Participators’ regulatory focus moderates the relationship between community collaboration and purchase frequency.

H2d: Participators’ regulatory focus moderates the relationship between community leadership and

purchase frequency.

## 4 Research methodology

## 4.1 Research context and data

The data for this research were collected from an e-commerce company that designs, produces, and sells apparel in an Asian market. As one of the leading companies in China, its revenue was over 50 million CNY in 2011. In June 2010, the company set up a firm-sponsored online brand community to implement its social media marketing strategy. The community is connected to its e-commerce website, and consumers can register and access it using their account login for the e-commerce website. In addition, this community enables users to create a personal profile (similar to profiles on social networking websites), contribute to the community by replying to others’ inquiries, link to friends’ pages, join groups, and post information in groups.

The dataset we obtained comprises three main parts. The first is related to users’ registration information and behavioral records, which include user demographics (i.e., user ID, age, sex, and date of registration), the activity records of time spent in the community, the number of replies contributed, the number of social ties created, and the number of posts in a given time period. The second part covers the transactions of all users from May 2011 to December 2012, both those who were part of the community and those who were not. The third part is a post-consumption record, namely the review information, including product reviews (i.e., rating and content), the reviewers who posted them (i.e., user ID), and the products for which these reviews were written (i.e., product ID) in the given time period. More than 2,300 consumers produced about 11,000 online reviews for 1,300 products during the given time period.

The focus of this study is the relationship between customer online brand community behavior and purchase frequency. Therefore, we selected customers who registered in the brand community after May 2011. Later on, in order to delete those community members who were totally inactive, we further screened those who had bought at least once from the platform, because totally inactive users were not relevant to this research. Finally, 2,998 users were identified from the whole user pool. Since our study also aims to study the influence of customers’ regulatory focus on their purchase behavior, it is essential to analyze user-generated content to identify whether a customer is promotion-focused or prevention-focused. For this reason, we further deleted the users who did not generate product reviews. Ultimately, 570 customers were included in our further analysis.

## 4.2 Variable operationalization

The operationalization of all principle variables and control variables is described below.

Dependent variable: Purchase frequency is a measure of quality in customer value research, and is widely used in previous studies [13, 30]. It not only represents how active a customer is in the platform, but also indicates his/her value to the platform. Therefore, our study takes this variable as the main measure and operationalizes it as the number of purchases made in a given time period (denoted by ).

Independent variables: The independent variables in this study cover two aspects. One is the ladder of participation, and the other is regulatory focus. As noted in the theoretical section, by integrating previous studies on the classification of participation [10], we considered four types of participation ranging from shallow to deep: content consumption, content contribution, community collaboration, and community leadership. In line with prior literature, like Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56], we operationalized content consumption as the measure by computing the total time a consumer has spent

in the online brand community in a given time period ( ). Following Bateman et al. [10], we

operationalized content contribution as the total number of replies posted in a given period of time

( ). The next level of participation is community collaboration. Community collaboration

involves participators linking to each other and sharing information [25]. Following the definition of

Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56], community collaboration was measured as the number of

social connections a consumer builds in a given time period (denoted by ). Finally,

leadership in online communities is developed based on the work of Cooren et al. [22]. Studies also

indicate that a significant characteristic of leaders in online communities is frequent posting [44, 55].

Hence, the highest level of participation, community leadership, was measured as the number of times

a consumer posts to initiate discussions or activities in a given time period (denoted by ).

The operationalization of regulatory focus is in line with Wu et al. [71], who used a completed

bipartite network and a SentiStrength algorithm to identify participators as promotion-focused or

prevention-focused. Based on regulatory focus theory, which argues that there is a tight relationship

between consumers’ regulatory focus and their emotional expression [40, 72], and that a consumer’s

own self-regulatory characteristic (promotion-focused or prevention-focused) can be reflected in his/her

response to a piece of information or his/her evaluation of a product [14], the authors used the text

mining method (i.e., sentiment and emotion analysis) to classify participators as promotion-focused or

prevention-focused (for the specific steps, see Wu et al. [71]). Using the text mining method to analyze

emotional biases in online product reviews, we classified participators as either promotion-focused or

prevention-focused. Promotion focus ( ) is marked as a binary variable that takes the value

of 1 if a consumer is promotion-focused, and 0 otherwise.

Control variables: We included several control variables in our model, such as purchase characteristics and customer demographics. First, two important consumer purchase characteristics – share of premium product purchase and deal proneness – are involved in our analysis. We followed prior literature and measured the share of premium product purchases ( ) as the ratio of the number of premium products to the total number of products purchased by consumers in a given time period (e.g., [62]). To operationalize this variable, we needed to determine which products were premium. In order to do this, we calculated the mean price per unit of all the products and designated as premium those products that were one standard deviation above the mean. Second, in terms of demographics, the income variable ( ) is a zip code-level estimate of household income as provided by national statistics. It is a category variable showing the income level for each consumer. We also included consumers’ current age in years ( ), the number of days a consumer engaged with the community since registering ( ), and a dummy variable for . Finally, we also controlled freight and the prices paid for products. Freight ( ) is the average shipping fee a consumer paid for his/her products. Price ( ) is the average expenditure of a consumer across all his/her purchases.

## 4.4.2 Hypotheses testing

As expected, the time users spent in the community had a significant effect on purchase frequency, though the effect size of was small. When the time users spent in the community increased by 1 percent, their purchase frequency increased by 0.07 percent. The number of replies was also strongly and significantly associated with participators’ purchase frequency. The elasticity of purchase frequency with respect to the number of replies is 0.11, implying that when the number of replies contributed by a user increased by 1 percent, his/her purchase frequency increased by 0.11 percent. Therefore, both content consumption and contribution have significant effects on product purchase, supporting H1A and H1B. However, two independent variables, the number of friends and the number of posts, were not significantly associated with purchase frequency. Thus, H1C and H1D are not supported.

Furthermore, we empirically tested the moderating effects of regulatory focus on the relationship between the ladder of participation and purchase frequency. We hypothesized that the impacts of different levels of community activities on purchase behavior would be moderated by participators regulatory focus. ## 4.4.3 Propensity score matching

To check the robustness of our empirical results, we used the propensity score matching (PSM) approach to estimate the effect of community activities and regulatory focus on product purchase [56, 62]. Although our preceding econometric analysis controls for both demographic characteristics and usage characteristics of customers, the nature of observational data still raises concerns about the causal interpretation of our findings. Unlike an experimental study, our study cannot randomly assign users to self-select themselves into community activities. In addition, promotion-focused users may present fundamentally different characteristics from prevention-focused users, such as demographics. It is reasonable to think of variables that might influence users’ community activities and purchase frequency simultaneously, hence creating a self-selection bias.

We adopt PSM to address the self-selection issue. PSM can be used to explore causal relationships and help us mimic a randomized experimental setup by identifying a matched control group [35]. The biggest problem in studying the treatment effect using archival data is incomplete information. Although we observe whether the treatment occurs and whether the outcome is conditional on treatment assignment, the counterfactual outcome is not observed. Based on observed variables, PSM uses a probit or logistic model to estimate the propensity of being treated for each individual. The estimated propensity scores are then used to identify matched groups through matching algorithms.

Mathematically, let be an indicator of the occurrence of treatment, and take the value of 1 if the treatment occurs, and 0 otherwise. $\gamma _ { 1 }$ is the outcome if the treatment occurs ( ) and $y _ { 0 }$ is the outcome if it does not $( T = 0 )$ . In reality, no person can be observed in two different states at the same time. Based on the assumption that treatment status is random, conditional on a set of observed covariates , PSM constructs treatment and control group persons by estimating the propensity of being treated $p ( \mathbb { Z } ) = p r ( T = 1 | \mathbb { Z } )$ . Finally, the average treatment effect on the treated can be acquired:

$$
\tau = E [ y _ {1} | T = 1, p (Z) ] - E [ y _ {0} | T = 0, p (Z) ]
$$

To implement the matching technique, we first identified two groups of participators in the online brand community. Group 1 consists of users who engaged in low-level participation, and Group 2 of users who engaged in high-level participation in the community. A user belongs to Group 2 if he/she has ever made a social connection or posted, and belongs to Group 1 otherwise. Therefore, users in Group 1 only engaged in content consumption and contribution, while users in Group 2 also collaborated with others or initiated activities. We also divided each group into two subgroups (Group 1A, Group 1B,

Group 2A, and Group 2B). Group 1A consisted of prevention-focused users and Group 1B of promotion-focused users; the same applied to Group 2 (see Table 5).

## 5 Discussion

## 5.1 Key findings

The findings of this study enhance the current understanding of how customer participation in brand communities varies according to participation level, thus influencing their purchase behavior. We find that shallow participation in a brand community (i.e., content consumption and contribution) can significantly and positively influence purchase frequency, while deep participation (i.e., community collaboration and community leadership) typically does not significantly influence final purchase frequency. Interestingly, when we consider the moderating effect of participators’ regulatory focus, we further find that it only moderates the relationships between deep participation behavior and final purchase frequency. This suggests that shallow participation in a brand community increases purchase frequency regardless of whether participators are promotion-focused or prevention-focused; while the purchase frequency of customers who participate deeply in the community increases contingent on their personality (i.e., promotion-focused or prevention-focused).

The findings explain why deep participation cannot lead to a significant increase of purchase frequency when we consider all the participators (i.e., both promotion-focused and prevention-focused

#

participators). As shown in Figure 2, the nature of the interaction effects between deep participation and regulatory focus on purchase frequency suggests that when participators are promotion-focused, their deep participation would increase purchase frequency; the deep participation of prevention-focused participators, however, cannot significantly influence purchase frequency, but still follows the negative trend. Regulatory focus theory suggests that promotion-focused and prevention-focused participators have different information processing styles [18, 74]. Specifically, promotion-focused users are more concerned about positive signals or information, which is more persuasive for them than negative information. Among prevention-focused customers, however, negative information has a greater influence on their judgment and decisions. Thus, even in the same community, presented with the same information, promotion-focused customers are more influenced by positive information than prevention-focused customers, which provides a possible explanation of our findings [34]. To this end, we conclude that deep community participation alone cannot determine whether a customer will purchase more on a platform, but is contingent on the customer’s regulatory focus.

In contrast to deep participation, our results suggest that the main effect of the relationship between shallow participation and purchase frequency is significant, while the moderation effect is not. A possible explanation for this is that the key objective of shallow participators in the brand community is learning about product performance. Compared with community collaborators or leaders, they are novices of the community, where most of their activity is centered on acquiring shallow information like product performance rather than company performance, which makes it difficult for them to gain a deeper understanding of the community and the spirit of the company. As suggested by prior studies like Chevalier and Mayzlin [21], most shallow information, like online product reviews, is often presented as positive. In addition, Dellarocas [24], examining online forums, suggested that information can be manipulated by sponsor firms, which hinders novices’ understanding of products and company. Therefore, despite their different regulatory focus, shallow participators’ participation can significantly influence their final purchase behavior.

## 5.2 Implications for theory

Our research makes two significant contributions to the extant literature.

First, the findings of our study reconcile the previous mixed findings regarding participation level and customer purchase frequency. Focusing on examining the benefits of brand communities to companies, Shang et al. [64] found that relatively shallow customer participation in a brand community is beneficial to community sponsors, while deeper involvement in the community is not necessarily valuable. Some other studies, however, like Rishika et al. [62] and Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56], have demonstrated that the deeper a customer’s participation in the brand community, the greater value a company can gain from his/her participation. Building on regulatory focus theory, this study found that customer purchase frequency is not determined solely by customer participation levels, but is contingent on customer regulatory focus. Consistent with the findings of Shang et al. [64], we found that shallow participation can increase customer value. Partially consistent with the studies of Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson [56] we have demonstrated that deep participation could increase a customer’s value to the company only when the customer is promotion-focused.

Second, consumers’ regulatory focus is widely considered to be an especially significant factor for implementing marketing strategies [1, 45, 75]. Previous studies have typically used experimental methods to manipulate customers’ regulatory focus, thus observing their behaviors in information processing or decision making, while neglecting the effect of their personality characteristics in a natural e-commerce environment. Adopting the method of Wu et al. (2014), we identified customers as either promotion-focused or prevention-focused in a natural environment, and investigated their varying interactive effects on different participation levels. In this specific research context, our results provide initial evidence suggesting that regulatory focus moderates the relationships between community activities and purchase frequency when users’ participation levels are high. Hence, reliance on community activities to explain consumer behavior would overlook and omit the persuasive effect of individual regulatory focus. Our results provide a better understanding of consumer behavior in online brand communities.

## 5.3 Implications for practice

The results of our study yield several important managerial implications for social media marketing.

First, this study provides new evidence of the economic value of activity in online brand communities. Based on a systematic examination of the relationships between community participation patterns and customer purchase behavior, we found that it is beneficial for firms to encourage customers to engage in community activities in most situations. Firms’ community activities increase customer purchase frequency via information dissemination and social interaction, and this effect is significant even for users whose engagement is shallow (e.g., users who merely consume information shared in the community). The success of an online brand community depends on the continuous participation of customers. Firms should therefore take steps to keep users consuming content, sharing product information, and interacting with others in the community.

Second, our results suggest that encouraging all users to move up the participation ladder may not be the most effective way to drive consumer purchases. Managers need to understand that not all consumers will respond the same way to a firm’s online brand community efforts, and the effects of community activities on customer value also differ among customers. The results also provide actionable recommendations for managers in terms of segmenting and targeting customers based on their regulatory focus. An ideal strategy to improve customer value would be to find the right combination of an individual regulatory focus and community activities.

Finally, our study also shows brand managers how to increase the impact of online brand communities. One way is to encourage participators to move up the levels of participation. Another is to recruit new customers to engage in the community. The implementation of the first strategy requires managers to look at users’ regulatory focus, and most participators in online communities are likely to be lurkers. Based on our empirical results, the second strategy is more feasible and efficient. However, this result is limited to communities initiated by firms.

## 5.4 Limitations and future research directions

As with all studies, this work suffers from limitations that provide directions for future research.

First, our results may be limited by the research context. In particular, the focal firm in our study manufactures and sells apparel, which is an experience product in academic terms. Previous studies have typically argued and demonstrated that consumers respond differently to experience and search products [41]. In addition, researchers have also indicated that customers’ purchase behavior regarding products and services can differ [31, 58]. Therefore, we suggest that future studies consider investigating whether different types of product (i.e., search products and service products; or search products and experience products) change participators’ purchase behavior in a brand community, thus extending the current findings.

Second, the community in our study was initiated and sponsored by a company. Researchers have argued that company-initiated communities differ from consumer-initiated communities in terms of information control and exchange, thus leading to distinct participator behavior [19, 27]. Future research could extend our study to consumer-initiated brand communities if the resources are available.

Third, although text mining method has been proved to be a good approach to explore online consumer behavior, our analysis on basis of a relatively small sample size would also bring a potential bias. Therefore, we expect future work to collect larger number of data to improve the accuracy and robustness. In addition, since we use specific methods like PSM to supplement the shortage of data selection issue, it may still be biased by unobserved variables. Future study could collect panel data and use fixed-effect model to overcome this limitation

## 6 Conclusions

With the rapid development of electronic commerce and social network, the exploration of great value of online brand community has been becoming a fertile soil both researchers and practitioners are interested in. Since this study reveals the interesting findings which suggest that the effects of brand community participation levels on consumers’ purchase behavior is moderated by the regulatory focus of focal consumers; and also demonstrates that consumers’ regulatory focus affect their shallow and deeper level participation differently. We believe much more interesting research questions there to wait for researchers to dig, and more rich findings to be explored.
