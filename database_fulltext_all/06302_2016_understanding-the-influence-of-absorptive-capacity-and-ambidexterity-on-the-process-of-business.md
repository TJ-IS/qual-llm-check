# Understanding the influence of absorptive capacity and ambidexterity on the process of business model change – the case of onpremise and cloud-computing software

Johann J. Kranz,\* André Hanelt & Lutz M. Kolbe

Institute of Information Systems, Georg-August-University Goettingen, Platz der Goettinge Sieben 5, Goettingen 37073, Germany E-mail: jkranz@uni-goettingen.de

Abstract. The increasing popularity of Software as a Service has strongly affected the established business model of on-premise enterprise software. Software as a Service has distinctive characteristics of disruptive innovations that typically create several dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms, in particular with regard to adapting business models. To date, however, little empirical understanding exists regarding the dynamics of business model change – a topic of special importance to the highly dynamic software industry, which is characterised by rapid and regular emergence of disruptive innovations. As disruptive innovations require gathering distant knowledge and experimenting with new ideas, this study addresses theoretical gaps regarding the role of absorptive capacity and organisational ambidexterity in the process of business model change owing to the emergence of a disruptive innovation. Drawing on evidence from multiple case studies of six incumbent vendors of enterprise resource-planning software and informed by a thorough review of related secondary data, we investigate the pace and path of incumbents business model adaptations. We propose a theoretical model that re<sup>fi</sup>nes prior literature on absorptive capacity and organisational ambidexterity, particularly with regard to the process of business model change. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>es further tech nological factors that determine how and why incumbents change business models. In addition, our study provides in-depth insights on the technological trajectory of enterprise resource-planning software switching from on-premise to on-demand software services. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: business model change, absorptive capacity, organisational ambidexterity, disruptive innovation, cloud computing, Software as a Service

## INTRODUCTION

Cloud computing, which has been characterised as a disruptive innovation (DaSilva et al., 2013), <sup>fl</sup>exibly provides information technology (IT) resources and services to distributed users through the internet. Around 15% of the global cloud-computing market comprises Software as a Service (SaaS), an on-demand software delivery service that offers ubiquitous access to ap plications and shares a pool of common resources managed by SaaS providers (Armbrust et al., 2009). Although the established license model for on-premise software still dominates the market for business software, it is losing its supremacy to SaaS (PWC, 2013). This shift also applies to the market for enterprise resource-planning (ERP) systems. Owing to the increasing popularity of SaaS, incumbent ERP vendors have needed to adapt their long-established business models, which has created several major challenges.

Disruptive innovations typically have this effect because they fundamentally change the rules of competition in established markets, often leading to the incumbents’ failure as they react too late or falteringly (Christensen, 1997; Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006b). While early work on disruptive innovation focused on technologies (Christensen, 1997), recent research highlights the role of business models that lead to commercialization of the innovation (Chesbrough, 2010; DaSilva et al., 2013). Although business models are constantly adapted, especially in high velocity environments like the software industry, prior research has surprisingly examined business models predominantly from a static perspective, yielding limited evidence on the dynamics of business models (Kamoun, 2008; Aspara et al., 2011; Lambert & Davidson, 2012). Particularly understudied are internal organisational processes regarding the change from a dominant business model to another model in response to disruptive innovations over time (Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006b; Ansari & Krop, 2012).

Disruptive innovations challenge a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s dominant logic as embedded in established business models, routines and organisational knowledge bases (Prahalad, 2004). Therefore, disruptive innovations force companies to revalue prior intellectual capital and to identify, acquire, assimilate and exploit new knowledge to create new offerings – an organisational capability referred to as absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). However, as ‘future absorptive capacity is determined by the current absorption of new knowledge’ (Todorova & Durisin, 2007, p. 783), absorptive capacity can be a double-edged sword in that it limits a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to acquire new knowledge from distant and loosely coupled knowledge domains, from which disruptive innovations typically emerge (Yu & Hang, 2010). While absorptive capacity has a positive impact on innovations primarily based on existing knowledge (Helfat, 1997), its effect on disruptive change is less understood. Another gap in the literature on business model dynamics is the role of organisational ambidexterity, understood as ‘an organisation’s ability to be aligned and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in its management of today’s business demands while simultaneously being adaptive to changes in the environment’ (Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008, p. 375) requiring organisations to be able to explore new alternatives and exploit existing competences at the same time (March, 1991). Owing to the multiple trade-offs arising from simultaneously engaging in exploring with new business models and exploiting existing ones, organisational ambidexterity is of particular value for understanding business model change – especially in times of disruptive change (Markides, 2013). This study also addresses the understudied question if and how <sup>fi</sup>rms adapt their exploration/exploitation balance owing to changing environmental conditions over time (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2008; Luge et al., 2013). This question is of particular relevance to the software industry, where disruptive innovations emerge rapidly and regularly (Veit et al., 2014).

To summarise, we seek to re<sup>fi</sup>ne theory with regard to the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing how and why incumbents change a dominant business model in response to an emergent disruptive innovation over time. Our study was guided by the following research questions:

1 How does an incumbent’s absorptive capacity shape the perceived potential of emergent disruptive innovations?

2 How do organisational ambidexterity and technology-related factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence organisationa processes regarding business model change in response to an emergent disruptive innovation?

We conducted a multiple case study analysis comprising six incumbent vendors of ERP sys tems to study the phenomenon in depth. Our study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature on business model change in three major ways. First, we re<sup>fi</sup>ne the literature on absorptive capacity. Our results imply that not only technology-related absorptive capacity bu also the absorption of market-related knowledge and the balancing of both sources of knowl edge play a critical role in fostering incumbents’ ability to correctly time the required business model changes. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fi</sup>rms able to dynamically shift between exploitation and exploration of business models dependent on external and internal conditions have changed business models more successfully. Third, our study provides new insights into how and why ERP vendors have changed business models, switching from on-premise to cloud-computing software.

## BACKGROUND

## The rise of SaaS in the on-premise ERP market

ERP vendors’ traditional business model consisted of selling licenses and services for onpremise software to typically larger organisations for automating enterprise-wide processes. With the rise of SaaS in 2000, new business model designs have emerged, delivering single ERP functionalities on demand through the internet (Stuckenberg et al., 2011). Using SaaS enables companies to focus on core competencies, because they are relieved of the burden of <sup>fi</sup> nancing, managing and maintaining their own IT infrastructures (Armbrust et al., 2009). Because services are provided on a pay-per-use basis, SaaS allows for the shifting of capital expenditures to operational expenditures. Furthermore, strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility increases, as sunk costs are lower and resources can easily be scaled up or down in response to short contract periods. Hence, the key drivers for adopting SaaS solutions are that they are readily available, reduce vendor lock-in and minimise capital expenditures, while the main inhibitors – especially for larger ERP enterprises – are potential performance losses, con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and security concerns, unanticipated costs and IT know-how drain (Benlian & Hess, 2011b).

It is important to note that the idea of remotely providing software ‘on-demand’ through the internet is not new. In the mid-1990s, application service providers (ASPs) allowed customers to outsource hosting and management of client–server applications (Valente & Mitra, 2007). However, the business model innovation failed at the time owing to low broadband penetration and inadequate software design. In addition, the single-tenant architecture of ASPs precluded economies of scale. In contrast, SaaS builds on a multi-tenant architecture that permits customers to share a single code base and data structures for an application, allowing economies of scale (McAfee, 2011).

## Theoretical background

## Business model research in the software industry

This study conceives of a business model as ‘a template of how a <sup>fi</sup>rm conducts business, how it delivers value to stakeholders (e.g. the focal <sup>fi</sup>rms, customers and partners) and how it links factor and product markets’ (Zott & Amit, 2010, p. 222). A business model is therefore com posed of interlocking components that specify how a product or service offering creates value (value proposition), which customer segments are targeted, how they are served and how the relationship is managed (customer interface), which competences, resources and partners a company needs (infrastructure management) and how products or services produce costs and generate revenues (<sup>fi</sup>nancial aspects) (Osterwalder et al., 2005; Al-Debei, 2010). Because business models – especially in high-velocity markets – depend heavily on IS as an enabler and trigger of innovation (Nambisan, 2013), they are a valuable unit of analysis for IS research (Rai & Tang. 2014). A recent analysis of prior literature identified three important pillars of business model research in IS: business models in IT industries, digital business models and IT support for developing and managing business models (Veit et al., 2014). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst category – which is the focus of this study – the software industry is particularly relevant for studying business model dynamics owing to the rapid technological shifts.

Until recently, business model research has been dominated by conceptual and theoretical contributions de<sup>fi</sup>ning and delineating the business model concept (e.g. Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2009) and relating it to adjacent <sup>fi</sup>elds, such as strategy, innovation and management (e.g. Porter, 2001; Chesbrough, 2010; Teece, 2010). Although companies – particularly those in turbulent environments – constantly need to adapt their business models to stay successful (Wirtz et al., 2010), the literature does not re<sup>fl</sup>ect how and why <sup>fi</sup>rms change business models over time (Kijl & Boersma, 2010). In recognition of this gap, the focus of business model research is shifting toward a more dynamic perspective (Burkhart et al., 2011). In this regard, research has found organisational capabilities in identifying and reacting to discontinuous inno vations that are of major importance (Demil & Lecocq, 2010; Aspara et al., 2011). The extent of business model change therefore depends on the <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c perceived environmental pressure to adapt (Linder & Cantrell, 2000; Madjdi & Hüsig, 2011) and varies in degree from slight adaptations to major revisions or the creation of new or combined business models (O’Reilly & Tushman, 2008; Cavalcante et al., 2011).

To determine what is known about business model change in the software industry, we conducted a thorough literature review of major information systems and management journals, as summarised in Table 1.<sup>1</sup> This review indicates that the majority of prior studies focused on emerging technologies (e.g. cloud computing) or development paradigms (e.g. open source software) and their potential in<sup>fl</sup>uence on established business models. However, these studies primarily analyse what has changed (e.g. developing taxonomies of future business models), while research using a dynamic perspective on intra-organisational processes that determine how and why incumbents adapt established business models is scarce.

## Enablers and inhibitors of disruptive business model change from an incumbent’s perspective

Disruptive innovations are new technologies or services that are typically cheaper or of initially lower quality or performance than existing market offers, however still good enough to satisfy the needs of less demanding and least-pro<sup>fi</sup>table customers at the low end of the initial value network or to create whole new value networks (Christensen, 1997; Christensen & Raynor, 2003). Owing to the inferior quality, established <sup>fi</sup>rms often do not regard these innovations as seriously affecting their competitive advantage. Furthermore, incumbents tend to incremen tally ‘<sup>fi</sup>ne-tune’ existing products much faster than low-end customers can take advantage of Hence, these users are typically the <sup>fi</sup>rst to adopt disruptive innovations because they value the non-standard performance attributes (Christensen & Raynor, 2003). However, over time disruptive innovations’ performance increases, so that they can gradually satisfy the needs of more mainstream customers. The theory of disruptive innovation is often misunderstood as stating that disruptors are generally start-ups and incumbents as well as predominant business models are inevitably replaced (Danneels, 2004). However, numerous examples exist of incumbents that have either introduced disruptive innovations themselves (e.g. Intel’s Centrino chip set, IBM’s personal computer and Charles Schwab’s online trading) or continue to offer their established business model in parallel to a disruptive one (e.g. Lufthansa’s low-cost airline, Allianz’s online direct insurance and BMW’s car-sharing service) (Markides, 2006). Prior research suggests that the less an emergent disruptive technology destroys the value of an incumbent’s existing resources, skills, and expertise and those of its customers and partners, the higher incumbents’ propensity to make use of the technology (Tushman & Anderson, 1986; Afuah, 2000). Tripsas (1997) further provides evidence that the pressure of technological shifts on incumbents is attenuated if incumbents have unique assets that cannot be easily replicated and cause signi<sup>fi</sup>cant switching costs for existing customers However, disruptive business model innovations typically require more than adapting established technological (sub-)systems as they affect key logics of how established <sup>fi</sup>rms create and capture value (Khanagha et al., 2014). Therefore, they often have a broad impact on all business model components leading to a devaluation of established <sup>fi</sup>rms’ assets and resources (Markides, 2006).

Prior literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed various organisational factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing incumbents’ ability to develop disruptive innovations (see Yu & Hang, 2010 for an overview). In this study, we focus on absorptive capacity and organisational ambidexterity (see Table 2 for de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the study’s core constructs) as literature suggests that both organisational abilities play a key role for incumbents in sensing, understanding and responding to changing environmental conditions in order to successfully revise business models (Lucas & Goh, 2009; Markides, 2013; Khanagha et al., 2014) but lacks rich empirical evidence on how they affect the process of business model change.

O’Reilly and Tushman (2008, p. 190) propose that <sup>fi</sup>rms need to develop ambidexterity ‘to compete simultaneously in both mature and emerging markets – to explore and exploit’ to be successful in the short term and to adapt to technological changes in the long run. Other researchers however argue that disruptive innovations’ initially inferior performance inevitably leads managers and existing customers to pay less attention to them than to incremental or radical innovations (Christensen & Bower, 1996; Yu & Hang, 2010). Thus, Christensen (2006) suggests that with regard to disruptive innovations, organisations cannot pursue both, explore disruptive opportunities and exploit existing assets. Thus, he concludes that <sup>fi</sup>rms should set up autonomous business units that are independent of the traditional business. These units should be free to experiment and embrace an organisational culture that motivates employees to take risks, develop creative ideas and behave like ‘in-house entrepreneurs’ (Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006a). Another much debated question is whether organisations should alternate between explorative and exploitative periods (Nickerson & Zenger, 2002) or should simultaneously conduct explorative and exploitative activities (Tushman & O’Reilly, 2002; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). To integrate both views, researchers recently have proposed to conceptualise ambidexterity on a continuum between exploration and exploitation defining the focus of ambidexterity as an intermediate point between the two ends of the continuum (Lavie & Rosenkopf, 2006; Luger et al., 2013).

Disruptive innovations require <sup>fi</sup>rms to accept that they devalue their prior intellectual capital. Incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms therefore need to develop absorptive capacity, which allows them to constantly identify and acquire new knowledge and to assimilate and apply this knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). For disruptive innovations, however, absorptive capacity may be both a blessing and a curse as it strongly depends on prior related knowledge (Roberts et al., 2012). Therefore, the acquisition of external knowledge is path dependent, in that past experiences and decisions determine future absorptive capacity in a self-reinforcing way (Todorova & Durisin, 2007; Sydow et al., 2009). Path dependency generally leads to <sup>fi</sup>rms get stuck in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c ‘corridor of limited scope of action’ (Sydow et al., 2009p. 704). Thus, while absorptive capacity increases the depth of knowledge in domains in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm is already active, it may also limit a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to acquire knowledge from other domains, which would help to increase the breadth of knowledge. As disruptive innovations typically originate from distant and loosely coupled knowledge domains and require major changes in established business models, the role of absorptive capacity is important, even though it is understudied (Lane et al., 2006).

Prior research has largely neglected the fact that the concept of absorptive capacity encompasses not only technological knowledge but also market knowledge (Lane et al., 2006). Market knowledge provides <sup>fi</sup>rms with insights on how to commercialise technological knowledge (Teece, 2007) and increases the organisational capability to solve complex problems in other domains (Weigelt & Sarkar, 2012), which is especially important for disruptive innovations because they initially appeal to new customer segments. Hence, a unilateral focus of absorptive capacity on technological knowledge, as well as on knowledge closely related to existing intellectual capital, may harm an incumbent’s ability to respond adequately to emerging disruptive innovations. We follow Roberts et al. (2012) in conceptualising absorptive capacity as a capability encompassing organisational routines and processes regarding the absorption of knowledge and the ability to adjust those dynamically, whereas much of the prior literature treated absorptive capacity as an asset (e.g. number of patents).

To summarise, literature suggests that to identify and assess the potential of disruptive innovations, absorptive capacity is essential, while ambidexterity is a vital organisational ability for integrating and reconciling exploratory and exploitative activities with regard to new and established business models. The importance of the two constructs for the process of business model change is indicated by Lucas and Goh (2009, p. 54) who state that regarding disruptive innovation ‘the most important observation is that management has to recognise the threats and opportunities of new information and communications technologies and marshal capabili ties for change’. ## METHODS

We use a multiple-case study approach to investigate the complex temporal process of business model change in depth. We chose a multi-case design because it allows study of complex organisational processes ‘involving multiple actors and interacting sequences of events (Bhattacherjee, 2012, p. 95) and comparison of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings among <sup>fi</sup>rms, resulting in higher externa validity of the results (Yin, 2009). As we aim at extending theory, our research can be categorised as a positivist exploratory case study (Benbasat et al., 1987; Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009). We used replication logic to identify consistent patterns across cases, treating each case as an experiment that con<sup>fi</sup>rms or discon<sup>fi</sup>rms emerging theory (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2003). Table 3 summarises how we ad dressed key methodological guidelines (Klein & Myers, 1999; Paré, 2004; Yin, 2009).

We chose the software industry as our research setting because its high-velocity nature makes it an attractive context for the study of dynamic changes (Martin, 2011) and it is frequently confronted with potentially disrupting digital technologies (Veit et al., 2014). We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focus on the market for ERP systems because it is one of the most established segments within the enterprise software market, making it an interesting setting for examining business model change.

Our research sample contains six software vendors (Table 4). Because we seek to examine how and why business models changed, our sample did not include ERP vendors that did not change business models. We selected the cases following a theoretical replication design (Yin, 2009) with the aim of increasing the variance regarding the observable timing and extent o business model change (Bhattacherjee, 2012). In each case, we conducted interviews unti we reached a point of saturation such that additional interviews yielded no new <sup>fi</sup>ndings with reference to our research questions (Paré, 2004).

## Data collection

Our study relies on various data sources comprising interviews and follow-up emails with 26 informants along with internal sources (e.g. reports, blogs and presentations) and external documents (e.g. <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports and market analyses) collected over a period of 14 years. On the basis of these data, we were able to verify the consistency of interviewees’ statements on past developments. Using multiple data sources allowed triangulation of the data to increase internal validity and mitigate potential biases (Yin, 2009). We stopped conducting furthe interviews if a comprehensive and consistent understanding of the cases could be established.

Drawing on prior theorising, we designed a semi-structured interview guide consisting of open questions (Appendix A). Interviewees came from different organisational departments and hierarchy levels and had signi<sup>fi</sup>cant experience in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of business software. The interview length ranged from 48 to 97 min (median: 68 min), and all interviews were tape recorded and transcribed usually within 24 h.

## RESULTS

## Change patterns of incumbents’ business models

All of the incumbents we investigated did not terminate their established on-premise business model but instead began to additionally offer SaaS. We therefore analysed an incumbent’s busi ness model portfolio in the ERP market in its entirety, consisting of the established on-premise and the emerging SaaS business model. The temporal evolution of SaaS business models that emerge from our data can be categorised into three stages as depicted in Figure 2. In the following, we elucidate how and when our case companies changed their business models, separately for each business model component.

## Value proposition

Because SaaS users need only a computing device and a Web browser to use the software, in the SaaS business model, customer <sup>fi</sup>rms are exempted from much of the cost and effort of operating and <sup>fi</sup>nancing their own IT infrastructure. When SaaS emerged in the early 2000s, most providers – such as ZetaCo’s SaaS, launched in 2002 – offered stand-alone applications providing single ERP functionalities widely standardised across industries. This is the reason why AlphaCo’s initial offering of a newly developed ‘cloud-based ERP system’ covering a wide range of common ERP functionalities failed:

Customers rather wanted a cloud solution for single ERP functions. That was why <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Salesforce were successful and we were not. (Interviewee AlphaCo#4)

Once AlphaCo realised it had misjudged customer needs, it adapted the value proposition to allow customers to choose among several functions according to their needs in a signi<sup>fi</sup> cantly revised version. Other <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample were more cautious when adapting their established business model’s value proposition. BetaCo eschewed major changes but reacted to competitors’ SaaS offerings and an emerging customer demand. However, they merely reworked their existing on-premise ERP software, so they could be offered as a service via the internet:

Actually, our products were not real cloud products. You just install the core ERP software in a central data centre and thus just change the kind of provision; the rest [of the software] stays untouched. (Interviewee BetaCo#3)

An important factor in SaaS’s gradual move up the market was the progress in virtualisation techniques, which enabled single-tenant, customisable private or hybrid cloud solutions, which are speci<sup>fi</sup>cally demanded by larger enterprises. In a private cloud, the software runs on dedicated resources that are individually con<sup>fi</sup>gurable and controllable while many of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the public cloud remain, including scalability, rapid implementation of innovations and elasticity. In a hybrid cloud solution, resources of the public cloud are used in addition to the private cloud for operational elasticity. Therefore, the current dominant value proposition for incumbent ERP vendors is to offer integrated on-premise and on-demand software solutions.

This is the perfect solution for a symbiotic amalgamation of on-premise and SaaS. (Interviewee DeltaCo#2)

As soon as complementary offers were feasible, GammaCo entered the market with a newly developed SaaS solution, which allowed individual customisation and integration with on-premise ERP software.

## Customer interface

According to our interviewees, SaaS initially attracted low-end customer segments and prior non-users of ERP systems that valued SaaS because it offered scalable, rapidly implementable and easy-to-use software services. These customers, mainly SMEs, were over-served by existing ERP systems.

For smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms, ERP was a solution from an ivory tower, too complex and costly to implement. (Interviewee GammaCo#1)

Then, lines of business with standardised processes across industries (e.g. human resources, purchase management, accounting and customer relationship management) began to demand SaaS as it is easily accessible from anywhere at any time and more <sup>fl</sup>exible in terms of cost and time. Simultaneously, many interviewees pointed out that business-unit managers increasingly bypass corporate IT and purchase SaaS on their own.

Business units buy software along the lines of ‘this solution solves my problem, it is mobile, I like it.’ (Interviewee BetaCo#2)

As customers in emerging markets in Asia or Eastern Europe were less restricted by legacy systems, they could adopt SaaS much faster than customers in mature markets. Thus, Poland based ZetaCo entered the SaaS market for SMEs very quickly and began offering special SaaS solutions for larger enterprises as early as 2006. Further, cultural aspects regarding data security considerably in<sup>fl</sup>uence SaaS adoption. Some interviewees noted that particularly, customers from Western European are reluctant to use cloud software because of concerns regarding data security, which were fuelled by recent revelations of international espionage.

Software as a Service has changed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of distribution channels through which customers are reached. Interviewees observed that customers interested in SaaS now prefer to gather pre-purchase information on the internet and test downloadable trial versions of different providers. Thus, the internet gains weight as a direct distribution channel affecting indirect distribution networks, which many of our investigated ERP vendors rely on heavily in the on-premise ERP market.

## Infrastructure management

In the on-premise software business, customers are responsible for running the software and the required IT infrastructure, often with support from software vendors or their partners. In the SaaS business, software vendors control and manage the provision of software over the entire lifecycle, including development, deployment, maintenance and updates, which requires adapting and building new competencies.

Our cloud offerings enable our customers overcoming educational and skill shortage in their IT departments and the <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment for hardware. (Post in AlphaCo’s corporate blog)

While in the past, new releases were usually launched every 2 years, development cycles are now much shorter, and new features are added continuously. In response, all case companies but one now use agile software-development methods supporting iterative and incremental progress, such as Scrum. Furthermore, interviewees of case companies that had no experi ence with on-demand software mentioned that a top priority is extending internal knowledge and integrating external knowledge regarding Web technologies (e.g. Ajax and HTML5) and on-demand software delivery (e.g. capacity management, pricing methods and support). Thus, these <sup>fi</sup>rms stressed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of recruiting and human resources development to expand their internal knowledge base.

## Financial aspects

Regarding revenues, the single most important change for ERP vendors offering SaaS is the dominant pay-per-use pricing method, which increases revenue streams’ volatility. With onpremise business, customers usually pay a one-time license fee and an annual fee for longer-term maintenance contracts. As all case companies offer SaaS on a pay-per-use basis, cash <sup>fl</sup>ows in continuously but less predictably. With regard to cost structures, upfront invest ments are necessary for software development and setting up the SaaS infrastructure. Aside from <sup>fi</sup>rst copy costs, offering SaaS implies permanent overhead costs for keeping the service infrastructure ready at all times – a major change in ERP vendors’ cost structures. As a result, the current priority for SaaS providers is to swiftly gain market shares, as economies of scale are crucial for pro<sup>fi</sup>tability in the SaaS market.

## Organisational process of business model change

In the following, we elaborate on the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the organisational process of busi ness model change starting with identifying external knowledge, followed by assimilating and valuing external and internal knowledge and then applying it in business model recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations (Figure 3). In particular, we focus on how absorptive capacity in<sup>fl</sup>uences a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s sensing activities shaping its perception of the disruptive innovation and how organisational ambidexterity and technology-related factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent and timing of business model change.

## Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation phase

The interviews revealed major differences in how broadly case companies searched the external environment and what were the main sources of external knowledge. Across <sup>fi</sup>rms, two major sources were market analysis reports and existing customers and partners. While these are valuable sources of information regarding developments in the ERP market, they only consider innovations and customer needs in the established market and fail to cover disruptive innovations and customer demands emerging from outside the focal market.

As a consequence, those <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample (e.g. BetaCo) that relied primarily on analyst laden and existing customer’s views on the market, adjusted business models only incrementally to meet the current needs of existing customers, thus missing customers’ unstated and future needs. Their search breadth was strictly limited to sources with which they had had positive experiences. Therefore, those <sup>fi</sup>rms did not tap into new markets or act as disruptors of the established ERP market.

Some <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample have started applying alternative approaches to identify external knowledge. AlphaCo and GammaCo created global centralised specialist teams whose aim is to sense emerging trends and disseminate them into the organisation. An important task of these teams is to identify worthwhile acquisition targets, which helped both <sup>fi</sup>rms to swiftly acquire external knowledge needed to extend their internal knowledge bases. However, while AlphaCo’s team focus is on new technologies, GammaCo’s team’s explicit task is to acquire wind of new developments outside the target market that may have an effect on established markets. In addition, owing to its activities in consumer markets for off-the-shelf software and hardware, GammaCo’s market-related absorptive capacity has pro<sup>fi</sup>ted from experiences and knowledge regarding these markets. For instance, a factor positively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing SaaS adoption was that a new generation of decision makers has entered working life, whose members grew up with digital technologies. These ‘digital natives’ are technology savvy and experienced in using private cloud services. GammaCo realised that this development will eventually spill over into the market for enterprise software:

Because of consumer technologies such as mobile devices popular in private contexts, cloud computing is becoming disruptive in the ERP market as customers also demand mobile, <sup>fl</sup>ex ible and easy to use services in the business context. (Interviewee GammaCo#2)

## Assimilation and valuation phase

With regard to assimilating and assessing external and internal knowledge, our results indicate that the ability to balance and combine market- with technology-related knowledge was important for both, how <sup>fi</sup>rms assessed the potential of SaaS and how the business model changes corresponded to timely customer needs. In this respect, substantial differences in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ ability to align and relate both knowledge types emerged. Some <sup>fi</sup>rms assessed SaaS pri marily on the basis of technological knowledge, leading to overvaluing of SaaS’s current marke potential (ZetaCo) or misjudging of customer needs at the time (AlphaCo):

We were fascinated by the possibilities cloud technologies would bring along. Blinded by that, we somewhat disregarded customer needs and our competitors’ actions, so that back then, the market was not assessed properly by us. (Interviewee AlphaCo#1)

On the other hand, some incumbents in our sample assessed SaaS’s potential mainly on the market knowledge they gathered on the ERP market. BetaCo, for instance, whose perception was biased toward the status quo and moreover had rather limited knowledge about cloud technologies and possible <sup>fi</sup>elds of application followed a ‘wait-and-see’ policy until their target cus tomers began to demand SaaS features. Conversely, GammaCo’s assessment of when mainstream customers and the technology were ready was rather precise as it based on its ability to absorb a balanced and diverse set of market and technology knowledge.

We were interested in observing the activities of some of the companies that went into the market early with their ‘ERP-light’ SaaS-models. We were curious, but we knew from our partners that are in day-to-day contact with the customer <sup>fi</sup>rms, that the need on the customer side did not exist back then. However, from our business in the private sector we were convinced that SaaS will gain acceptance, so we prepared internally. (Interviewee GammaCo#4)

The ability to align and relate market knowledge with technology knowledge, along with the diversity of knowledge sources, also helped <sup>fi</sup>rms build new cognitive structures and thus avoid common traps of path-dependent assessments. However, if <sup>fi</sup>rms lacked this ability, the assessment of SaaS was often biased by managers’ established beliefs and mental models regarding the market and technology. For instance, as the basic concepts of ASP and SaaS are very sim ilar, BetaCo was very reluctant to adopt SaaS after having massively invested in ASP, which could not prevail in the market. On the other hand, DeltaCo was positive about SaaS’s potential, as it had had positive experiences with service-oriented architectures.

Our data furthermore revealed that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ initiatives to foster social interactions in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the effectiveness of organisational knowledge assimilation processes across and within subunits. Following the logic of design thinking, AlphaCo sets up small interdisciplinary teams for almost any new innovation project to foster the assimilation of diverse external and internal knowledge across corporate departments with the hope that those teams would be better at assessing and creating new ideas. To foster company-wide knowledge exchange, AlphaCo and GammaCo make extensive use of IT, especially cooperative work and organisationa memory systems such as corporate social networks, blogging, wikis, instant messaging or bulletin boards. As both companies operate globally, they regard collaborative software as essential to quickly spreading knowledge within the organisation and to receiving feedback on ideas:

Using collaborative platforms, I often receive feedback from people from all over the world, I didn’t know before. Because of that, sometimes new ideas and synergies between projects have emerged. Further, collaborative platforms motivate people to share their knowledge. First, they receive credit and are happy when 2,000 co-workers have read their blog in a day. (Interviewee AlphaCo#2)

## Recon<sup>fi</sup>guration phase

The ability of incumbents to recon<sup>fi</sup>gure business models by drawing on internal and externa knowledge was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the compatibility between the established and new business models and organisational ambidexterity.

With regard to ambidexterity, an interesting phenomenon emerged from the data. GammaCo had developed a capability allowing dynamic balance between exploitation and exploration, depending on external and internal conditions. The company switched between modes in response to how it assessed the technology and market potential of SaaS and the extent to which the internal organisation and partners were ready to change in terms of competencies, resources and willingness. In 2010, drawing on its experience in adjacent markets, GammaCo sensed that their target customers will soon be ready to adopt SaaS. Consequently, GammaCo rapidly shifted into a more exploratory mode and allocated substantial personal and <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources to develop a new SaaS-based business model for the ERP market. In contrast, the constantly more exploratory <sup>fi</sup>rms in our sample (ZetaCo and AlphaCo) were not able to adjust their exploration/exploitation balance adequately and thus entered the SaaS market too early, while constantly more exploitative <sup>fi</sup>rms such as BetaCo and EpsilonCo acted tentatively and now lag behind their competitors. Therefore, an organisation’s capability to properly balance and combine market and technology knowledge is an important antecedent of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to <sup>fi</sup>nd the right balance between exploration and exploitation. We further observed that companies that lacked slack resources were limited in their ability to switch to a more exploratory mode owing to resource restrictions. DeltaCo and EpsilonCo, for instance, both needed several years to develop the necessary internal competencies because their software developers had to work on both incrementally improving the existing on-premise software and developing a new on-demand solution. Well-<sup>fi</sup>nanced <sup>fi</sup>rms, in contrast, could acquire companies to quickly increase their manpower and internal knowledge, allowing them to put higher emphasis on exploration and therefore decreasing the time to market.

The interviews further revealed that the more exploratory <sup>fi</sup>rms established structurally separated units for SaaS to avoid that exploratory activities are constrained by exploitative needs. ZetaCo, for instance, established a spin-off for SaaS very early in 2001 that was free to experiment with the new business model. We found that the freedom to experiment is closely linked to organisational cultures of risk and entrepreneurship. The cultures of AlphaCo and ZetaCo in particular promote experimentation and openness to new ideas:

For us, it started to get almost a little bit boring, as there was no real movement in the market. (Interviewee AlphaCo#1)

As SaaS requires <sup>fi</sup>rms not only to adapt to new organisational structures and processes but also to unlearn deeply rooted principles, such as prioritisation of customer segments or new product ideas, <sup>fi</sup>rms with an innovation-embracing culture were better prepared for exploration.

The extent and timing of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ business model recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations were further in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by technology-related factors. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the more compatible the established and new business models were perceived by incumbents, the more rapidly and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly business models were overhauled.

The new on-demand software business model signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changes the importance of established partnerships in the ERP market depending on a partners’ function. Software partners offer industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c extensions and general add-ons that build on the ERP’s core. Most case companies either plan to or already provide application-programming interfaces to co-opetitively create value in eco-systems around their SaaS platforms, stimulating indirect network effects. Thus, while still important, established software partners will need to develop new skills and adapt to a new business environment. The importance of implementation partners in the SaaS business model drops dramatically because one key feature of SaaS is its fast and easy deployment, which makes local implementation partners obsolete. Moreover, the collaboration with hosting partners is about to change, as the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of offering cloud-based services depends heavily on economies of scale. Thus, many interviewees believed that the market for hosting services will consolidate considerably, with market shares of SaaS continuing to rise. Hence, SaaS greatly affects established partnerships. As most case companies heavily depend on partners in the on-premise business, they try to maintain collaboration with their established partners, even though collaboration has a negative impact on pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins and competitiveness of the SaaS products’ pricing.

An important reason why our case companies could sustain SaaS’s disruptive threat thus far is that ERP systems are deeply entrenched in customers’ organisational processes. Thus, substantial switching costs of existing ERP customers attenuated the pressure on incumbents to change business models. It was only when complementary on-demand and on-premise offers were feasible that most of our case companies thoroughly adapted their business model as they could leverage existing assets. We further found that <sup>fi</sup>rms with greater internal knowledge of web and cloud technologies adapted their business model much faster than <sup>fi</sup>rms that <sup>fi</sup>rst needed to recruit human resources to compensate for knowledge de<sup>fi</sup>cits:

We tried to lure university graduates for our cloud developer team. Even though we wanted to recruit more, which was hard to do because larger companies pay signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more than we are able to, eventually we could hire some graduates. Those brought new knowledge to ou company that we did not have before. (Interviewee DeltaCo#3)

Another factor enabling <sup>fi</sup>rms to launch overhauled business models more rapidly was prior experience with agile software development methods. As, in the SaaS business model, the soft ware is continuously updated, more agile development approaches are necessary because they allow for adapting to changing business requirements more swiftly:

We <sup>fi</sup>rst had to adapt our development procedures due to the fact that release-cycles are much faster. That’s why we started using Scrum. (Interviewee EpsilonCo#3)

Also perceived synergies between on-demand and on-premise technologies were an important catalyser for incumbents to adapt business models.

We started to develop solutions on service-oriented architectures. When cloud computing emerged, we could build on this foundation to design new, customer-oriented products. (Interviewee DeltaCo#1)

## DISCUSSION OF FINDINGS

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our case studies led us to re<sup>fi</sup>ne our initial model as depicted in Figure 3. First, we found that we need to differentiate the concept of absorptive capacity by accounting for the type of knowledge involved. In our sample, we found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences regarding <sup>fi</sup>rms ability to absorb market and technology knowledge. While the majority of case companies had developed a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient absorptive capacity regarding market and technological knowledge relevant to the ERP market, many <sup>fi</sup>rms lacked breadth in absorptive capacity concerning adjacent markets and general environmental trends. This lack limited their capacity to make sense of and connect weak market and environmental signals, an ability crucial for correctly assessing an innovation’s contemporary and general disruptive potential (Weigelt & Sarkar, 2012). Because disruptive innovations often emerge from technology domains and customer needs distinct from a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s established base, possession of a broad sensing scope that includes a variety of external sources is important (March, 1991). Firms in our sample with a narrow focus on identifying knowledge relevant to their established ERP business model failed to recognise emerging customer needs and technology opportunities. Their assessment of SaaS was strongly biased by their focus on existing customers and partners – a restriction that resulted in ‘cognitive lock-ins’ that limited their ability to sense potential new dominant business models in the ERP market (Prahalad, 2004).

Beyond that, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed differences between <sup>fi</sup>rms regarding their ability to balance and connect market-oriented and technology-oriented knowledge, which we refer to as balancing capability. We found that an imbalance between market-related and technology-related absorptive capacity results in <sup>fi</sup>rms either underestimating or overestimating a disruptive innovation’s market or technology potential. leading to misalignments regarding the reguired timing and extent of business model change. Only when market and technology knowledge are balanced and combined, with accurate connections with each other, can value be created (Shane, 2000; Song et al., 2005). By drawing on the ability to absorb both market and technology knowledge of the ERP and other software markets from a large variety of sources, GammaCo could balance the two sources of knowledge, allowing the company to sense when to put emphasis on exploration. In contrast, the technology-biased companies such as AlphaCo and ZetaCo lacked adequate market knowledge. Their assessment of SaaS was strongly technology driven, which was why they misjudged the commercialisation potential at the time they overhauled their business models. Market-biased <sup>fi</sup>rms such as DeltaCo and EpsilonCo sensed market needs rather early, but – lacking technology knowledge – changed their business model only incrementally until they were able to implement major changes. Falling short in marketrelated and technology-related absorptive capacity, BetaCo did not recognise $\mathtt { S a a s s } ^ { \mathtt { , } }$ disruptive potential for a long time and is now seeking to catch up with competitors’ offers. Second, we observed that path dependency not only in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the depth and breadth with which <sup>fi</sup>rms acquired external knowledge, but also moreover how <sup>fi</sup>rms assessed the market and technol ogy potential of SaaS such as in the case of BetaCo whose judgement of SaaS was strongly biased by negative past experiences with ASP.

Third, our data suggests that organisational and innovation-related factors have a moderating effect on the relationship between the perceived potential of a disruptive innovation and business model change rather than a direct effect as we expected based on prior literature. At the same level of perceived disruptive potential, the higher a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s capability to integrate and reconcile explorative and exploitative activities related to business model innovation and the greater the compatibility between a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s established and new business model, the more promptly and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly business models were changed. Another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding emerging from our data is that <sup>fi</sup>rms able to dynamically shift between exploitation and exploration, depending on external and internal conditions, are more likely to enter the market in a timely manner with a contemporary business model. Having this ability, GammaCo shifted from exploitation to exploration upon recognising that the rising adoption of private cloud solutions will soon spill over to organisational users as well. The other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the sample were not able to dynamically adjust their exploitation/exploration balance but had rather developed a static form of ambidexterity (Luger et al., 2013).

## IMPLICATIONS

## Theoretical implications

This study addresses theoretical and empirical gaps in the IS literature on business model dynamics. Overall, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study contribute re<sup>fi</sup>ned constructs and theory that explain capabilities of business model change and the relationships among them. We now address the research questions outlined earlier, elucidating the major elements of the revised theoretical framework (Figure 3). This study contributes to the IS literature on business models in three major ways. First, our study re<sup>fi</sup>nes theory on absorptive capacity regarding business model change resulting from the emergence of disruptive digital technologies. Prior literature conceptualised absorptive capacity predominantly as technological or scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge (Lane et al., 2006). However, disruptive innovations often build on inferior technologies and initially attract non-users or low-end customers (Christensen, 1997). Hence, a single-sided focus on technology knowledge can have negative conseguences. As our findings indicate especially for disruptive innovations, the ability to absorb knowledge from various sources on the target and adjacent markets as well as general environmental trends is essential for the right timing and extent of business model change. Therefore, regarding the debate about market-orientation vs. technology-orientation (Govindarajan & Kopalle, 2006b), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that in each stage of business model change, insights on markets and technologies are equally important. We found that companies engaged in different markets were more ready to <sup>fi</sup>nd new ways of doing business or recognise business opportunities than were companies focused on single markets. This <sup>fi</sup>nding con<sup>fi</sup>rms results from prior research that market-related absorptive capacity increases a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ‘capacity to correctly generalise and link internal with external knowledge to increase its responsiveness to customer needs’ (Weigelt & Sarkar, 2012, p. 196). However, this is only one manifestation of market-related absorptive capacity. In addition, we found that for disruptive innovations, the ability to identify and assimilate knowledge from distant and loosely coupled domains from various external sources is pivotal. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, searching broadly enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to sense emerging changes in customer behaviour and business environments outside their core market where disruptive innovations typically originate (Yu & Hang, 2010). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings corroborate that the ability to integrate and balance heterogeneous knowledge grows in importance because of the increasing pervasiveness of digital technologies (Yoo et al., 2012). Second, we found that the capability to assimilate, value and connect market and technology knowledge is crucial to make sense of external and internal knowledge and to draw the right conclusions on when and how to respond to emerging disrup tive innovations. Having access to new and diverse knowledge and integrating this knowledge internally also help <sup>fi</sup>rms to decide when and how to respond to a disruptive innovation. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that organisations can increase the effectiveness of these processes by fostering knowledge exchange and social relationships among employees and different corporate departments, e.g., through using collaborative IT platforms (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006).

Third, this study found that dynamic ambidexterity is an important organisational capability with regard to business model changes owing to disruptive innovations (Lavie et al., 2010; Luger et al., 2013). In contrast to <sup>fi</sup>rms pursuing a relatively stable alignment between exploration and exploitation (Tushman & O’Reilly, 2002) or <sup>fi</sup>rms shifting between discrete phases of either exploration or exploitation (sequential ambidexterity) (Nickerson & Zenger, 2002), <sup>fi</sup>rms able to dynamically adjust to a given point in between the exploration/exploitation continuum in order to meet changes in external and internal environments managed the transition of busi ness models most successfully. Our results suggest that contingent on changes in markets and technologies, dynamic ambidexterity allows <sup>fl</sup>exibility in recon<sup>fi</sup>guring <sup>fi</sup>rm resources, which is important in turbulent environments as the software industry (Veit et al., 2014). Once the disruptive innovation’s market and technology potential reaches a certain threshold, the primary orientation shifts toward exploration. However, dynamic ambidexterity requires that organisa tions be prepared for and committed to recon<sup>fi</sup>guring organisational resources in case the external environment demands business model adaptations, which can be regarded as a dynamic capability (Teece et al., 1997).

## Managerial implications

Managers can draw several insights from this study. Our results show that achieving a balance between market-related and technology-related absorptive capacities is crucial for <sup>fi</sup>nding of a contemporary value proposition. To assess whether a technology has the potential to satisfy future needs of their target customers, <sup>fi</sup>rms should not rely too much on current customers and partners but rather focus on general developments in consumer behaviour, technology, society and the economy.

Another necessity is experimenting with new business models to reduce uncertainties concerning their market potential and to create new knowledge (Khanagha et al., 2014). Because managers’ decisions are often in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by cognitive biases, a more effective approach might be to establish a core team with direct exposure to markets and technologies and the freedom to experiment with new business models (Christensen, 2006). However, we found that <sup>fi</sup>rms with structurally separated units and slack resources face a higher likelihood of failures, indicating that creating favourable conditions for organisational experimentation may decrease discipline in the innovation process.

A way to overcome these pitfalls is to combine business model experimentation with a structured and systematic approach that allows to translate creative ideas into speci<sup>fi</sup>c activities. Here, business model roadmapping (De Reuver et al., 2013) is of speci<sup>fi</sup>c value in guiding the way from one dominant business model to another one, which is particularly relevant in the case of disruptive innovations. Moreover, the approach allows to identify path dependencies and synergies between two different business models.

As this study has shown, most <sup>fi</sup>rms in dynamic and competitive industries have developed a stable balance between explorative and exploitative activities (Jansen et al., 2006). However, our results imply that a capability to dynamically adjust the levels of exploration and exploration on the ambidexterity continuum – regardless the prior exploration/exploitation balance – in response to the degree of environmental change yields superior results. In more turbulent times of transformative or game-changing environmental conditions, the emphasis should be on exploration, while the focus under more stable conditions should shift toward exploitation (Siggelkow & Levinthal, 2003; Jansen et al., 2006).

## Limitations and further research

In this study, we have proposed a tentative model of factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the process of business model change based on the evidence of six case studies with established ERP vendors. As is the case for any theoretical model, our model simpli<sup>fi</sup>es some of the complex nonlinear relationships between social and psychological variables. However, as we checked for face validity and consistency in our data, the relationships accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the interviewees’ beliefs and attitudes. Nevertheless, generalisation of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings requires quantitative approaches that test the model. Future research should further study business model change from different theoretical perspec tives focusing on other in<sup>fl</sup>uencing factors such as institutional logics or strategic orientation.

Further research could also investigate the transferability of our model to different contexts because the software environment is a high-velocity environment in which change occurs regularly, and in such industries, the experience with change builds an important adaptive capability (Hill & Rothaermel, 2003). In other contexts, additional manifestations of marketrelated absorptive capacity may emerge, as well as different types of absorptive capacity, e.g., regarding software engineering. Another avenue for future research is to investigate the relationship between absorptive capacity and ambidexterity in the context of business model change. As we have found that incumbents offer on-premise software and SaaS at the same time, another avenue for further research is to investigate how <sup>fi</sup>rms manage the multiple trade-offs between exploitation and exploration of two co-existing business models within a single market.
