## Abstract

This paper examines the influence of information technology (IT) on a distinct but closely related industry, the video game industry. We conceptualize the effects of IT as a process of translating three related dimensions of a technological frame – technology performance, industry practices, and use vision – from one industry to another. Through historical examples, we argue that the impact of IT on the video game industry is shaped and limited by this translation process, particularly when tensions between the two industries lead to the development of new complementary or replacement technologies, practices, or visions. Although heavily dependent on IT, the video game industry has had to ignore, postpone, or substantially modify important IT software tools, processors, storage media, graphics, and networking technologies because of these industry contradictions. ## Introduction

H <sup>ow</sup> <sup>does</sup> <sup>information</sup> <sup>technology</sup> <sup>(IT)</sup> <sup>change</sup> <sup>an</sup>industry? Can easily available information processing, industry? Can easily available information processing, storage, and transmission cause a significant change in how an industry performs its work? Does the influence of IT depend on the invention of complementary practices, standards, or technologies? How much strategic choice do firms in an industry have with regards to IT, and how do these choices affect industry evolution? These important questions about the role of IT in industry change are relatively unexplored in the information systems research literature, compared to questions of organizational change. In contrast, business historians (e.g., Chandler, 2001; Cortada, 2004), sociologists of technology (e.g., Bijker, 1995; Rosen, 2002), and economists studying technology strategy (e.g., Schilling, 2003; Shankar and Bayus, 2003) regularly work at the industry level when exploring the significance of IT-related change.

In this paper, we investigate the influence of IT on a distinct but closely related industry, the video game industry. According to traditional innovation theory, a lack of knowledge or familiarity with a technology is a major barrier to adoption, and ultimately industry change (e.g., Allen, 2000; Cortada, 2004). This limitation does not apply to the video game industry, making this industry an important arena for studying the effects of IT at one end of a continuum from IT-related to non-IT-related industries.

Compared to other manufacturing, retail, or service sectors, the video game industry has very close ties to, and deep knowledge of, IT. Modern video game consoles and personal computers (PCs) are essentially two different configurations of almost identical technology: two of the three market-leading consoles use PC industry processors; two out of three use graphics chips from PC industry companies; two out of three use standard CD/DVD drives; and all three use PC industry standard modem or broadband networking hardware (Aoyama and Izushi, 2003). The video game industry and the PC industry, however, have had different development paths over the past 30–35 years, with perhaps the most notable difference being the lack of a Wintel monopoly equivalent for any extended period of time in the video game industry (the nearest equivalent being Nintendo’s dominant position in the late 1980s).

We agree with the critics of technology determinism (e.g., Williams and Edge, 1996; Oudshoorn and Pinch, 2003) that viewing technology as a purely external factor causing industry change will inevitably downplay the role that industry users and customers play in reshaping technology goals, selectively appropriating technologies, and creating new industry practices. We argue that this is true even in an industry as closely tied to IT as the video game industry. Instead of conceptualizing technology as an external factor that causes industry change, we treat IT and video games as two industries that have the potential to influence each other over time. We focus on three dimensions of this interrelationship, based on Bijker’s (1995) definition of a technological frame, and supported by a review of video game industry history. These three dimensions are: the use vision of what the technology is intended to achieve; the industry practices associated with the technology; and the performance of the technology.

We conceptualize the effects of IT on an industry as a process of translating a technological frame from the IT industry to another industry. Through historical examples, we argue that the impact of IT on the video game industry is shaped and limited by this translation process. In some cases, the video game industry may adopt IT visions, practices, and technology performance without substantial modification. In other cases, the video game industry has ignored or postponed the use of new technology developments from the PC industry. In still other cases, the video game industry has created complementary or replacement innovations along certain dimensions of the technological frame. For example, new technology performance (such as more powerful graphics chips), combined with particular industry strategies and historical conditions (such as Sega’s strategy in the early 1990s to compete with Nintendo by emphasizing more adult-oriented games with violent graphics), can lead to particular institutional practice innovations (such as voluntary game ratings) that in turn caused significant changes in the video game industry. Sometimes, these visions, practices, and technologies return to influence and change the IT industry itself, particularly now that for the first time, in 2004, integrated circuit shipments for consumer products exceeded the value of those used in the corporate marketplace (Friedenberg, 2005). By investigating the links between technology performance, industry practices, and use visions as technological frames are translated between industries, we hope to better understand when and how an industry is affected by IT availability.

## Theoretical review: the effects of it on industries

Previous studies of IT and industry change broadly agree that the outcomes of technology deployment are shaped by a process of adoption, expectation setting, and fitting with existing practices and institutions. In this section, we briefly discuss four research literatures that address the role of IT in industry change: the business value of IT literature, the business history of IT, the technology strategy literature, and the social shaping of technology literature.

The business value of IT literature in information systems research (e.g., Dedrick et al., 2003; Melville et al., 2004) examines the impact of business IT investment on corporate performance. This literature focuses mostly at the level of individual firms and national economies, trying to find relationships between IT investment and productivity measures. While relatively few studies in this tradition have looked at industry impacts, Dedrick et al. (2003) report on research which suggests that positive IT productivity impacts are easier to find in manufacturing than in service sectors, and in IT-producing rather than IT-using industries. Reviews of the business value literature agree that the effect of IT is moderated by the presence or absence of complementary assets and practices – in essence, the changes that must be made in order to take advantage of IT capabilities. Even if there are efficiency gains due to IT use, there is no guarantee that the profits from those gains will be captured by particular firms, or by any firm if competition forces businesses to pass their savings on to customers (Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996).

The business history of IT literature (e.g., Chandler, 2001; Cortada, 2004) gives accounts of IT use in particular companies and industries, over periods of years or even decades. While accepting that IT can have an impact on the way industries work, there is also an emphasis on how specific industry conditions, expectations, and tasks affect technology adoption. Because the adoption of particular ITs was not mandatory in most industries, the effects of IT are, to some extent, moderated by managerial choices and perceptions: ‘in general nobody was forced to use this technologyythe story is about millions of small decisions to use computer-based technologies, choices made because they seemed to hold out the promise of economic advantage’ (Cortada, 2004: 4). To explain the diversity of IT impacts in different industries, IT business history studies focus on three issues: how IT was used in the industry; industry expectations for the technology; and the role of technology in causing the work of specific organizations to change (Cortada, 2004: 5). The effects of IT on an industry may vary because of rates of deployment, different rationales for adoption, specific industry initiatives, or simply because a positive exemplar (such as the IBM PC) is so successful that it dramatically shapes future developments in an industry.

The technology strategy literature (e.g., Schilling, 2003; Shankar and Bayus, 2003) focuses on the competitive positioning of firms within technology-intensive industries. Technological advances affect industries by offering firms opportunities to strengthen their competitive position, or by creating threats to their existing market position. For technology strategists, the value of product offerings is dependent not simply on technology functionality, but also on their complementary goods (such as software), and their installed base (Schilling, 2003). Technological advances offer occasions for industry change, via decisions about first mover advantage, investments in complementary goods (Gallagher and Park, 2002), customer lock-in, and the creation of partner networks (Shankar and Bayus, 2003; Venkatraman and Lee, 2004). Further complicating the technology strategy process are perceptions of the future value of products, based on projections of how many complementary goods and/or partners a technology platform will have in the future (Schilling, 2003). For the technology strategy literature, the effects of IT on an industry are moderated through the strategic choices of producers, partner firms, and customers.

The social shaping of technology literature (e.g., Bijker, 1995; Williams and Edge, 1996; Rosen, 2002) offers a fundamental critique of attempts to identify the impacts or effects of technology, referring to such arguments as ‘technological determinism’. For social shaping studies, technical and social issues have to be treated simultaneously – technological artefacts should not be studied independently of the social groups, practices, and goals that structure interaction around a technology. According to this tradition, conceptualizing technology as an external factor causing impacts is likely to neglect the role that producers and consumers play in reshaping technology goals, selectively appropriating technologies, and creating new industry standards (Oudshoorn and Pinch, 2003).

While the literatures on IT business value, IT business history, and technology strategy do not conceptualize technology and social interaction as linked together to the same extent as the social shaping literature, their theoretical models do have points of commonality. For both the IT business value literature and the technology strategy literature, practices and investments that complement IT are an essential part of understanding the effects of technology-centered change. The IT business history literature examines technology visions in an industry, and argues that varying expectations for a technology help explain the different effects of IT across industries (Cortada, 2004). Taken as a whole, these literatures envision IT effects as a process involving technology, practices, and expectations for use.

## Study design

The purpose of this study is to create a theoretically informed historical account of the influence of IT on a closely related industry. In this section, we discuss our choice of theoretical framework, our choice of case study, and our data sources and analysis.

## Theoretical framework

We are persuaded by arguments from the social shaping of technology literature that viewing technology as a purely external factor causing industry change will inevitably downplay the role that users and customers play in ITcentered industry change. Our preferred theoretical framework would therefore recognize IT effects as a relationship between two existing industries. For IT to influence another industry, there would have to be a translation process (e.g., Ma¨hring et al., 2004) from one industry to another that could either be relatively problematic or unproblematic. The theoretical framework should also span the main concepts identified in the literature review: technology, practices, and expectations for use.

Drawing from the social shaping of technology literature, we propose using a modified version of Bijker’s (1995) technological frame. For Bijker (1995; 123), a technological frame ‘structures the interactions among the actors of a relevant social groupyexisting practice does guide future practice, though without logical determination.’ The technological frame includes ‘all elements that influence the interactions within relevant social groups and lead to the attribution of meanings to technical artefacts – and thus to constituting technology.’ (Bijker, 1995: 123) While technical artefacts are part of a technological frame, social interactions around a technology can only be understood in the context of the artefacts themselves, the goals of the technology (as defined by relevant social groups), and the social practices that accompany the technology.

Using the definition of our modified technological frame, we can now state our conceptualization of the effects of IT on an industry: IT impact is a process of translating three related dimensions of a technological frame – technology performance, industry practices, and use vision – from an IT industry to another industry. This translation process may be more or less problematic, depending on the extent to which deep-seated elements of a technological frame may have to be replaced or changed. Mutually exclusive elements across the industries may lead to the development of new complementary or replacement technologies, practices, or visions.

## Industry choice

This study chose the video game industry, an industry deeply dependent on IT, especially from the PC industry. Industries are usually categorized either by common production processes and inputs, or common markets, or a mixture of both (Malone and Elichirigoity, 2003). If we follow the NAICS classification used by North American governments, common production processes are the most agreed-upon basis for industry categorization (Krishnan and Press, 2003). By this standard, the PC industry and the video game industry are very closely related, compared to other economic sectors.

Our choice of a closely related industry represents one end of a continuum. ## Sources and data analysis

Our data on translation attempts and technological frame elements comes from an analysis of secondary sources on the PC and video game industries. For the PC industry, we used four main sources: Campbell-Kelly (2003) for the history of PC software through 1995; chapter 5 of Chandler (2001); chapters 2 and 3 of Gawer and Cusumano (2002) for the history of Intel-based PCs; and Freiberger and Swaine (2000). For the video game industry, we also used four main sources: chapters 4–8 of Kline et al. (2003); chapters 1 and 2 of Wolf (2001); Schilling (2003) for consoles; and Gallagher and Park (2002) for consoles. While these sources provided a comprehensive list of significant technological artefacts, their coverage of industry practices and use visions was uneven, depending on their particular biases and purposes in writing their histories. By using multiple sources, we hope to focus on substantial areas of agreement between these histories.

The first step in our analysis was to identify the specific elements of our technological frame that were relevant for an analysis of PC and video game industry interaction, according to our historical accounts. The elements are listed in Table 1. For technology performance, the researchers agreed on software, hardware, storage, graphics, and networks as the most relevant elements in the historical data. During the history of these two industries, available technologies from the PC industry have not always been translated smoothly to the video game industry, such as CD-ROM storage or Ethernet networking. For industry practices, a number of specific elements emerged from the sources: a revenue model that either bundled or unbundled hardware and software sales; the practice of maintaining a standard configuration for a period of years (such as a console) vs constant updating; how training/support delivery takes place, either through mass media or direct personal support; the use of copy protection; and to what extent producers control software development for their hardware. For the use vision dimension, the elements emerged from the subjective judgments of the researchers, but were consistent with the historical sources. These elements included: the intended audience of the technology, in terms of demographics; the target retail cost of hardware and software; the stability of technology in terms of crashes and data loss; and the extent to which there is openness to consumer and third party innovation.

In the main part of our analysis, we constructed a history of the interactions between the PC industry and the video game industry, based on specific attempts to translate elements of the technological frames identified above from one industry to another. Our analysis searched for tensions, or areas of mutually exclusive practice, between the two industries that could explain the impact (or lack of impact)

of a particular technology on the video game industry. The analysis also explored the relationships between elements of a technological frame, and how connections between, for example, the latest processor hardware, the practice of maintaining standard configurations, and the vision of a particular retail price point affected the influence of new CPUs on the video game industry. The end product of this analysis is the historical account described in the next three sections.

## Background: industry overview

In this section, we briefly review the history of the video game industry, and its relationships with the PC industry, since 1970. The main events and products releases are summarized in Table 2, dividing the historical periods in the video game industry using the framework from Kline et al. (2003).

Since the arrival of the first arcade games in early 1970s, the video game industry has become a substantial economic and cultural entity. Estimates for the global size of the video game industry range from \$25-\$60 billion US (KGDI, 2004), depending on whether specific sectors such as arcade games and computer games are included. Video games now make up 40% of the US toy market (Fetto, 2002). Approximately 248 million video games, including 53 million computer games, were sold in the US in 2004, according to the Entertainment Software Association, with roughly the same sales volume in Europe. While daily TV viewing time has decreased over the past 10 years in the US for 18—34-year-old males, the amount of daily video game playing is increasing for this group at a double-digit rate (Reynolds, 2004). One video game industry survey claims that teenagers find playing video games more fun than watching TV or movies by a 2 to 1 margin (Anders, 1999).

The commercial video game industry began with early arcade games such as Pong (1972) and Space Invaders (1978). Compared to home console units, such as the Magnavox Odyssey (1971) and the market-leading Atari 2600 (1977), arcade games provided a business model with less up-front investment, allowing for more experimentation and instant feedback. The feedback from early Pong arcade games – called in for ‘repair’ because their coin boxes were overflowing – gave console makers the confidence to produce and distribute specific games in volume. Arcade game revenues dominated the early industry: in 1982, arcade revenues were \$8 billion in the US, compared to video game sales of \$3 billion, recorded music sales of \$4 billion, and \$3 billion movie receipts (Kline et al. 2003). Arcades were a significant social phenomenon as well (Fox, 1997). By 1980, Atari created a dominant position in the US home console market, with an 80% market share.

By 1984, however, video game revenues in the US dropped 50% from their peak, and the market collapsed. Atari was sold, and soon afterwards simply abandoned console production altogether. This set the stage for Nintendo to adapt their Japanese Famicom (1984) console, and bring it to the US as the NES (1985). A common explanation at the time for the earlier collapse of the video game industry was that too many companies were releasing video games of poor quality (Wolf, 2001). Nintendo responded to these concerns by maintaining strict control over software licensing and production. Nintendo did much of their game development in-house, including the wellknown Super Mario games, and strictly limited the number of games that outside software developers could release. Nintendo was the sole manufacturer of game cartridges, and a special hardware chip ensured that unauthorized games could not run on an NES console. Atari was able to reverse engineer the ‘lock-out’ chip, but through a series of American court cases Nintendo was able to stop production of these unauthorized cartridges (1989). With a relative lack of competition, and with their strict control of game production, Nintendo had an 80% market share of the US video game industry by 1988.

In 1989, Sega introduced Genesis, a console based on a 16-bit CPU, compared to the 8-bit CPU of the NES. One strategy Sega used to differentiate itself from Nintendo was to target older children (414 years old) and young adults, the most infamous example being the explicit violence featured in the popular Mortal Kombat game (1990). In the US, the ‘uncensored’ Sega Genesis version of Mortal Kombat outsold the ‘toned down’ Nintendo version by 3 to 1 (Kline et al., 2003). Public outcry and government hearings in the US soon followed, leading to the creation of the ESRB voluntary content rating scheme by the industry itself (1994). Nintendo lost an important anti-trust case in 1990, limiting their ability to stop game developers from making games for other platforms. By the early 1990s, Sega’s US home console market share was almost equal to Nintendo’s.

In 1995, a major multinational company from outside the video game industry, Sony, launched their PlayStation home console. This console featured a 32-bit CPU, and used a standard CD drive instead of game cartridges. Sony spent \$200 million US on marketing for the product launch (Kline et al., 2003). Sony, Nintendo, and Sega continued to compete during this period, through the launch of the consoles based on 128-bit CPUs in 1999 (Sega Dreamcast), 2000 (Sony PlayStation 2), and 2001 (Nintendo GameCube). The entry of Microsoft into the console market with the Xbox (2001) brought a PC-industry standard hard disk and network connection to consoles. Today, the video game console market features competing platforms that are expensive to develop and launch, but remain mostly unchanged throughout their useful life. Video games are increasingly elaborate, typically requiring 2 years and tens of millions of dollars to develop (Poole, 2000).

In comparison to the video game industry, particularly the home console segment, the PC industry has had much less platform competition. About 174 million PCs, worth \$194 billion US, were sold worldwide in 2004. The commercial PC industry also began in the 1970s, initially with many competing platform choices. One of the most significant, Apple Computer, was started by two ex-Atari employees. The release of the VisiCalc spreadsheet program (1979) was an important early step, steering the PC towards business acceptance. It was the unprecedented success of the IBM PC (1981), and its choice of a Microsoft operating system and Intel CPU, that continue to shape the industry today. IBM’s far-sighted strategy was to create ‘a system, not an appliance’ (Chandler, 2001: 136). By 1983, the IBM PC division alone was the size of a Fortune 500 company, and by 1985 IBM owned 50% of a \$5 billion US market. However, IBM gave up control over the most important, and lucrative, standards, and later attempts by IBM to establish new standards for the PC operating system, CPU, or even interface ports were largely unsuccessful (Gawer and Cusumano, 2002). The loss of Apple Computer’s copyright case against Microsoft Windows (1992) signified an end to any serious challenge of the Microsoft PC operating system monopoly.

The PC industry, however, has been much more profoundly affected by networking and the Internet than the video game industry, as of 2004. The release of the Netscape browser for PCs (1994) and the opening of the Internet to commercial traffic (1995) led to a dramatic shift in PC usage towards networking. Unlike video game consoles, where networked gaming as a small (but rapidly growing) niche began only recently, PCs have had networking hardware and standard applications for many years.

The point at which the video game industry and the PC industry interact most directly is in the computer games arena. In the US, computer games are a significant segment of the overall video game market, comprising over 20% of sales in 2004. While computer games were occasionally successful in the 1980s and early 1990s, with programs such as the Microsoft Flight Simulator (1983), the IBM PC standard did not emphasize high quality graphics or sound. The earliest versions of Microsoft Windows were particularly ill suited for high-speed, memory-intensive graphics (Gawer and Cusumano, 2002). The release of highly popular games that were unique to PCs, such as Doom (1993) and Myst (1993) focused more attention on PCs as a gaming platform, particularly for the 3-D first person shooter games that could operate over a network. Both Microsoft (DirectX, 1995) and Intel (AGP port, 1996) created extensions to the standard PC platform that supported improved graphics and sound. By 1995, over 30 companies (including nVidia) had received venture capital funding to create new graphics chipsets that, they hoped, would be the new standard in the PC industry (Takahashi, 2002). The price difference between PCs and consoles, while still significant, continued to drop. The new PC graphics and sound standards, combined with the unique attractions of networked gaming offered by shooter games and on-line role-playing games, have made PCs a significant yet distinct alternative to standard video game consoles.

Analysis: tensions between the PC and video game industries In this section, we discuss historical examples of attempts to translate technological frames from the PC industry to the video game industry. Tensions between the two industries, in the form of mutually exclusive technological performance, practices, and use visions, sometimes lead to deployment problems, and sometimes lead to unexpected innovations in complementary elements. We group our discussion of specific attempts to influence the video game industry into three sections. First, we discuss the relationships between use visions and industry practices in terms of the major application of IT, either for entertainment or for business productivity. Second, we discuss the relationship between technology adoption, industry practices, and use visions, in terms of the role of the consumer. Third, we review examples of how the inter-related elements of the standard video game console affect the translation of use visions and technology performance from the PC industry.

Major application of the technology: entertainment vs productivity After the first commercial success of the IBM PC in 1981, the PC industry defined its vision of technology primarily as promoting business and personal productivity (Cortada, 2004). The design and pricing of the PC could assume that a set of industry practices for training and support delivery existed at the point of use that would make a complex, highly customizable technology workable in practice. While some of the early PCs did provide game functionality at low prices, such as the Commodore 64 and Sinclair Spectrum (Kline et al., 2003), the industry support practices for delivering training and support for these still complicated machines – hobbyist clubs, magazines, and computer fairs – proved difficult to sustain for the average user. The technological performance demanded of PC productivity software was primarily to give a (properly trained) user immediate access to a full range of powerful features.

The video game industry modified this vision significantly. Video game machines and software have to plug in and work, with no institutional support, no maintenance, no training independent of what can be provided in the game, and no fatal errors that will stop the user (Takahashi, 2002). The differences in use vision in terms of stability, and industry practices for training and support, have put severe restrictions on the technology performance that the video games industry may import from the PC industry. The lower price is one severe use vision constraint, but perhaps more significant are the lack of maintenance and support. These goals encourage the development of console products that do not change over the course of their useful lives – rapidly changing hardware and systems software, allowing multiple configurations, is a major cause of maintenance and support issues (Glaskowski, 2002). Standard configurations are deeply entrenched industry practice in the video game industry.

The vision and practice of successful software production has also been modified by the video games industry. Video game manufacturers, as suppliers of entertainment products, cannot assume that there will be will be a productivity motivation to start using a complex software package, gain proficiency, and continue to use it over time. Game designers have modified usability performance goals around the notion of play (Poole, 2000). Instead of presenting the user with a huge range of features and functionality, games must be designed to have an appropriate challenge level at all times, motivating the player to continue and develop more skill. As levels of challenge are mastered, game software continues to unlock new possibilities and rewards. The industry support practices for this use vision – help for players stuck at levels through hints, strategy guides, and ‘cheat codes’, plus entertainment industry-style trend magazines that review the latest blockbuster games – were pioneered by Nintendo in the mid 1980s (Kline et al., 2003). The willingness of game players to play for hours on end without any external motivation has been used as an argument to modify the use visions of PCs to make them more game-like, particularly in areas such as education and professional training (e.g., Khaslavsky and Shedroff, 1999), although the PC industry has had its own difficulties trying to adopt this vision of computing as more ‘immersive’, ‘experiential’ or even ‘seductive’.

The video games industry has been able to smoothly translate aspects of the PC industry technological frame, but the tensions discussed above have led to partial adoption and industry practice innovations. The video game industry has a natural incentive to import cheap PC industry technology whenever possible, due to the greater economies of scale, but must select components that will be consistent with a use vision of entertainment product pricing. The importation of hardware technology performance differs, because they are tightly linked to different industry training and support practices that require consistent IT products that maintain a standard configuration over their useful lifetime. The video game industry has had to innovate in specific technology performance areas (sound and graphics) when the PC industry technological frame de-emphasized these goals, and has had to invent the fundamentally different vision, practices, and performance of the home console. The innovations of the video game industry, in response to its unique conditions, are now having some influence on the PC industry as it attempts to expand its own offerings in settings outside of business productivity.

## The role of the consumer: content vs data

From the beginning, the PC industry’s use visions, industry practices, and technology performance emphasized the easy creation, storage, and sharing of data. Experiments with copy protection in the PC industry rarely included special hardware, and software copy protection schemes have generally been lightweight and easy to defeat. With its initial focus on business applications, there were few calls for any restrictions on the sharing or availability of data. Two major areas of data restriction that have been discussed in association with PCs are adult content through Internet surfing, and privacy violations from collecting personally identifiable information. Neither concern has required the PC industry to develop content restriction standards. Even after years of legal debate about music (and now video) downloads, major PC software companies such as Microsoft and Abode are just beginning to develop Digital Rights Management standards.

The video game industry has sought to use the power of PC technology, but at the same time to develop technology performance and industry practices that control the use of content. After the collapse of the video game industry in 1984, Nintendo was able to re-establish the industry, and create a dominant platform, by taking extraordinary steps to control the production of game software. Nintendo used cartridge technology to deliver games, produced all the cartridge hardware itself, and created a lock-out chip to prevent counterfeits. Nintendo demanded high minimum orders for cartridges, and limited each of its game developers to a small number of titles, in the hope that this would force game developers to release a small number of high quality games (Wolf, 2001).

The deeply entrenched industry practice of copy protection made Nintendo reluctant to embrace standard data storage technologies from the PC industry, such as CD-ROM drives and, later, DVD-ROM drives. Both Sega and Sony used standard CD-ROM drives in their competing consoles, allowing these companies to take advantage of technological performance that was a little slower for data transfer, but three to five times cheaper to produce, and could be made economically in much smaller quantities. Microsoft’s Xbox took this trend even further, by introducing a hard disk as standard equipment on a video console for the first time. Even in their current GameCube console, Nintendo still chooses not to use a PC-industry standard DVD-ROM drive.

When members of the video game industry adopted these optical drives, they imported the possibility of unapproved experimentation and copying as found in the PC industry. While unauthorized copying has likely increased since the days of the cartridge, the video game industry has imported the use of a significant copy protection practice from the consumer electronics industry: the Territorial Lockout (TL) (Ip and Jacobs, 2004). Legal cases in the US have also been used to copy protection practices, based on the provision in the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (Rogers, 2002) that prevents users from trying to break copy protection schemes.

The most significant industry practice innovation created by the video game industry, in response to the performance offered by PC industry technological frame, is the voluntary rating scheme of the Entertainment Software Review Board (ESRB). The video game industry was forced to start controlling software production in a new way when improving technology performance allowed games to portray increasingly graphic and life-like violence. The first wave of political outcry came in the early 1990s, as the competition between Sega and Nintendo created a strategic opening for more ‘adult oriented’ game content such as Mortal Kombat (1990). Public hearings were held in the US, and an influential report released by the American Psychology Association (1993) claimed that children who watched more graphic violence become more violent themselves (Hemphill, 2003). The second wave of concern came in response to the Columbine High School shootings in the US (1999), when it was revealed that the killers often played first person shooter computer games. Despite constant questioning of game rating validity (e.g., Walsh and Gentile, 2001), the industry innovation of content ratings has allowed the video game industry to survive – and continue to publish highly violent game content – without serious political damage.

The video game industry has been able to smoothly translate aspects of the PC industry’s data storage and delivery technology. The standard optical drive technology has been unevenly adopted, in part because of tensions with industry copy control practices and a use vision of little customer innovation. The industry practice innovation of voluntary ratings has allowed the video game industry to keep using ever-greater graphics performance for explicit violence. Some minor technological innovations, such as adapting copy protection and Territorial Lockout technology, have allowed the video game industry to deploy the open data sharing technologies of the PC industry. Little of the copy protection practices, use vision, and technology performance has influenced the PC industry in return, but that may change as the PC industry searches for new technological frames in an era of Digital Rights Management.

## The role of producers: product vs standard

Both the arcade game and the home console have provided a stark contrast to the PC. The technology performance, industry practices, and use vision of the video game console provide a strongly different development path to the PC, while using many of the same technological artefacts and standards within them. As the technology strategy literature has argued, the success of standards depends on a robust network of independent software and hardware vendors that provide products that conform to, and complement, an established platform (Schilling, 2003; Venkatraman and Lee, 2004). The early success of Microsoft in the PC industry has strongly reinforced the PC industry practice of controlling standards, rather than trying to create all the hardware and software for a product.

The vision of the video game industry has been more complex, recognizing the need for independent companies to create software-based games and hardware add-ons, while at various times maintaining strict licensing conditions in an effort to restrict software and hardware development to approved vendors. Nintendo’s initial vision, practices, and technology deployment supported stricter control of game developers, as discussed above. Nintendo’s actions set a pattern against which competitors could react by attempting to break into Nintendo’s network of game developers. Developers could be attracted by luring them with open standards from the PC industry that would be easier to program, or through more generous licensing terms, or both.

The technological frame of the home console, however, implies a very different revenue model from the PC industry: video game console sales are subsidized at or below production cost, and the profit is made from software sales. The video game platform companies must develop and sell a large fraction of the games themselves, and obtain royalties from other developers, in order to subsidize the hardware cost. These industry practices put a limit on the attractiveness of the PC industry technology performance that is closely tied to a use vision of openness. An early console competitor, 3DO, tried to sell hardware at a profit, independently of software sales; the retail price was too high, and the product failed (Takahashi, 2002). Microsoft considered not charging royalties on Xbox games, in order to follow PC industry practices and attract more independent software vendors, but the revenue model of the video game industry took precedence and royalty fees were established (Takahashi, 2002).

Standard PC industry operating systems and hardware continued to be a powerful attraction for the video game industry, particularly for networked games. By maintaining tighter controls on software production through licensing practices, the video game industry is not able to take advantage of industry practice innovations in the PC industry for software production. The early computer game Doom (1993) was developed by a company without any certification or licensing, and distributed through the Internet initially as shareware (Kline et al., 2003). These computer game developers also created software development tools that allowed other programmers and hobbyists to modify and create related games more easily, often through open-source software sharing. These established practices of the PC industry, although they create powerful new kinds of computer graphics performance, are difficult for the video game industry to adopt. Without licensing, royalty revenues, and significant in-house game production, the revenue model becomes strained, preventing the video game industry from taking full advantage of PC industry technology performance.

## Discussion: mutual shaping of the PC and video game industries

We summarize our historical account of the relationships between the PC industry and the video game industry by briefly discussing both sides of the relationship – how technological frames from the IT industry shape the video game industry, and in turn how technological frames from the video game industry shape IT. We call this a process of mutual shaping.

## IT shapes the video game industry

Not surprisingly, IT significantly shapes the video game industry. The video game industry makes substantial use of technological performance developed by the larger PC industry. As stated in the Introduction, modern video game consoles and PCs are essentially two different configurations of almost identical technology: two of the three market-leading consoles use PC industry processors; two out of three use graphics chips from PC industry companies; two out of three use standard CD/DVD drives; and all three use PC industry standard modem or broadband networking hardware (Aoyama and Izushi, 2003).

The computer gaming segment of the video game industry, being most closely related to the PC industry, has had the most success deploying the use vision and industry practices of the PC industry to the world of video games. Innovations such as networked applications, shareware, open-source software, standard development tools, and the ability to modify game content all bring standard PC industry practices to video game development, opening multiple pathways for technological change. The video game industry also deploys standard PC industry processor chips that are constantly improving in terms of hardware performance, but the timing and expense of launching new video game consoles puts restrictions on the ability of unapproved vendors and hobbyists to influence technological change.

As gaming and entertainment become increasingly mainstream PC industry applications, there is scope for the PC industry to have an even stronger influence on the video game industry. Sophisticated graphics and sound chips are now almost standard on PCs, giving the video game industry even more of an incentive to deploy standard PC technology performance.

## Video games shape the PC industry

The video game industry has by no means been a passive recipient of IT visions, practices, and performance. The video game industry has the potential to influence the PC industry in return.

At the level of vision, the dramatic success of video games in motivating users to play for extended periods, and develop new skills, is leading traditional IT industries to question their traditional goals for usability and functionality – perhaps this is now the time to make traditional IT applications more ‘immersive or seductive’ (e.g., Khaslavsky and Shedroff, 1999). Although the vision of a lowmaintenance computing appliance has often been raised, but never been embraced, by the PC industry, the new discussions of home entertainment and PC/consumer electronics ‘convergence’ (e.g., Anonymous, 2001; Takahashi, 2002) have motivated PC companies such as Microsoft to introduce game consoles and ‘Media PCs’. If this trend continues, it should not be surprising to see video game industry practices and technologies, such as home consoles, introduced in the PC industry. Attempts to put restrictions on content sharing may also make use of standard video game industry practices.

At the level of hardware performance, the video game industry vision has been influential in encouraging the PC industry to develop improved sound and graphics standards. Even at the most basic level of hardware performance – CPU instruction design – Intel has made changes to basic language of computing by analyzing the popular applications that run on PCs. For example, the 57 new instructions of the Intel MMX extension were developed in response to the increasing amounts of graphics and sound processing requirements found in commonly used software (Peleg et al., 1997). Game machines are now a small but significant segment of PC industry sales.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we have tried to clarify important aspects of how IT influences an industry. The influence of IT is shaped by a process of technology performance adoption, expectation setting, and fitting within existing practices and institutions. The three dimensions of our technological frame concept, and the elements within those dimensions, are an attempt to bring together important commonalities from previous research in a way that places some structure on historical analyses, without reducing history to a list of technological artefacts.

Even in a closely related industry such as video games, we argue that new technological artefacts from the IT industry have rarely led to specific outcomes independent of existing relationships between technology performance, industry practices, and use visions. Although heavily dependent on IT, the video game industry has had to ignore, postpone, or substantially modify the use of important IT software tools, processors, storage media, graphics, and networking technologies. Even technology performance with proven video game uses, such as network connections, have proven to be too difficult to decouple from other elements of the technological frame. Tensions between existing technology performance, industry practices, and use visions in these two industries affect the difficulty of translating technology performance to the video game industry.

Although limited by our particular reading of these industry histories, and our choice of theoretical framework, we believe that our work may be of value for studies of other industries. Within the three dimensions of the technological frame – use visions, industry practices, and technology performance – the most relevant elements will likely vary from industry to industry. The key insight of the technological frame approach is that the interactions between industries are vital for understanding the effects of IT. The mere existence of technological artefacts in an IT industry does not tell enough of the story. If the receiving industry and the IT industry are not closely related in terms of production processes and technology, then issues of familiarity with the technology and task fit may be more significant (e.g., Cortada, 2004). However, the video game industry example shows that industries using closely related technologies and production processes might experience very different outcomes in the presence of IT. The PC industry and the video game industry use similar IT, yet both have had very different competitive trajectories, technology adoption patterns in products, industry practice innovations, and consumer use patterns.

The differences between the IT and video game industries suggest that the search for IT impacts that apply across industries could be difficult. If two industries that are so similar have had such different experiences in the presence of IT, it seems unlikely that the impacts of IT could be identical across the much more diverse range of industries that make up our modern world economy. Following the lead of many sociologists of technology, economists, and business strategists, it could be that the industry level is the right place to look for, and understand, the true implications of IT.
