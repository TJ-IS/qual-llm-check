# Compliance with institutional imperatives on environmental sustainability: Building theory on the role of Green IS

Tom Butler ⇑

Department of Accounting, Finance and Information Systems, College of Business and Law, University College Cork, Cork City, Ireland

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 4 November 2010

Keywords: Green IT Green IS Compliance Institutional theory Sense-making Decision making Knowledge sharing

## a b s t r a c t

Addressing the complexity of the growing number of regulatory imperatives from global institutional environments has prompted firms in the IT sector to leverage the enabling effects of IT-based systems to help manage environmental compliance and related organisational risks. Thus, a new breed of IS—Green IS—emerged in recent years. This paper presents an integrative theoretical model that: (1) employs institutional theory to help explain how a range of exogenous regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive factors from the institutional environment and the organisational field influence IT manufacturers’ decisions on the design and manufacture of environmentally sustainable products; and (2) uses organisational theory to describe the strategic endogenous arrangements that organisations institute using Green IS in order to support sense-making, decision making and knowledge creation around environmental sustainability. The paper employs the findings of a case study of Compliance and Risks' Ltd. Compliance-to-Product (C2P) application and its implementation in two US-based Fortune 500 IT manufacturers to help validate and refine the a priori theoretical model. The paper therefore makes a significant contribution to theory building on the phenomenon of Green IS, through its articulation of empiricallybased theoretical propositions which employ conceptual mechanisms to explain how Green IS can support organisational sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing and creation around the design and manufacture of Green IT.

## 1. Introduction

Business enterprises are under increasing social, legal, and economic pressures to adopt environmentally sustainable strategies for their products and services (Murugesan, 2007; Ryan, 2008a,b; Campbell, 2007; Melville, 2010). Drawing on Scott's (1995) perspective on institutional theory, previous theoretical research on corporate social and environmental sustainability argues that such influences are regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive in their origin (Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995; Campbell, 2007). Consequently, firms are increasingly attending to a triple bottom-line (Elkington, 1997), which includes being accountable for what they are doing in terms of environmental and social responsibilities, in addition to their financial obligations of maximising profits (Schwab, 2008). The trend towards corporate social and environmental responsibility is noted in the literature (Lynes and Andrachuk, 2008; Petersen and Vredenburg, 2009), and it is now common practice for many business enterprises to describe policies or measures aimed at environmental sustainability as part of their annual statements on CSR or in special corporate sustainability reports (Melville, 2010). The focus of the present study is the IT manufacturing sector, which is argued to be a leader in the corporate environmental sustainability movement (OECD, 2009a,b), chiefly because it is under a wide range of institutional pressures (Hristev, 2006; Field, 2008).

This paper posits that regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive forces from the institutional environment are shaping the organisational field of the IT manufacturing sector and, consequently, responses of individual organisations (cf. Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995; Campbell, 2007; Chen et al., 2008; Reid and Toffel, 2009), particularly in terms of the design for environment (DfE) of Green IT (Hristev, 2006; Johnston, 2008; Hojlo and Jacobson, 2008). The purpose of regulative influences is to coerce a uniform response across organisational fields in order to induce organisational isomorphism in terms of environmental compliance (cf. Scott, 1995, 2004; Chen et al., 2008). The key institutional pressure to comply with environmental regulations is that IT manufacturers face exclusion from major markets, stopped shipments, and product recalls if found not to be in compliance (Aberdeen Group, 2006). Non-government organisations (NGOs) and industry standards bodies are also bringing normative pressures to bear on business enterprises, while customers, shareholders, and competitors exert cultural-cognitive/mimetic influences (Aberdeen Group, 2006; Murugesan, 2007; Campbell, 2007; Field, 2008). Normative and cultural-cognitive influences are more subtle, yet none-the-less effective, in shaping organisational responses towards environmental sustainability Take, for example, that mimetic isomorphism is evident in the adoption of environmental standards by organisations, the promotion of corporate sustainability initiatives (Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995), and the corporate disclosure of sustainability strategies (Reid and Toffel, 2009). In an overall context, then, the consequences of IT manufacturing organisations in not addressing environmental issues are significant and include, for examples, a loss of revenue and potential damage to product brand image and corporate reputation (Aberdeen Group, 2006; Butler and McGovern, 2008, 2009)

Identifying and responding to regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive influences involves taking strategic decisions (Oliver, 1991; Oliver, 1997a,b; Frankenberger, 2006; Johnson and Greenwood, 2007; Reid and Toffel, 2009; cf. Esty and Winston, 2006: Murugesan. 2007, 2008: HBR. 2007), In terms of the present study, such decisions revolve around the environmentally sustainable design, manufacture, packaging and distribution of IT artefacts—i.e. Green IT. Taking strategic decisions is complicated by the fact that global regulatory environments are highly complex and organisations are facing enormous challenges in understanding compliance legislation and in making appropriate responses (Aberdeen Group, 2006; Hristev, 2006; Butler and McGovern, 2008; cf. Kurtz and Snowden, 2003). Accordingly, IT is being proposed as a key enabler for sustainability initiatives (GeSI, 2008; OECD, 2008) to help companies manage environmental compliance imperatives and to minimize related risks (Avila, 2006; Butler and McGovern, 2008, 2009); there is, however, a paucity of suitable IT-based systems in the marketplace to help organisations address what is a significant challenge (Kerrigan and Law, 2003; Avila, 2006; Butler and McGovern, 2009; see also Appendix A, Table A.1).

## 1.1. Green IS and the design and manufacture of Green IT

The concept of environmentally sustainable or Green IT has only recently been adopted by information systems researchers (Murugesan, 2008; Melville, 2010). The term was originally applied by practitioners to differentiate IT artefacts that are designed with environmental sustainability in mind—that is, to contain the minimum amount of hazardous materials, to be energy efficient during their use, and to be disposed or recycled with the minimum effect on the environment and human health (Murugesan, 2008). Green IT-enabled information systems—or Green IS—have a significant role to play in making both business processes and the products they create environmentally sustainable (Boudreau et al., 2008). Green IS could be applied, for example, to monitor and report on GHG emissions; control and report on waste, toxic and hazardous materials use; manage energy-consuming facilities (e.g. transport and buildings management); help design greener products; and to redesign business processes across the enterprise so as to be environmentally sustainable (Boudreau et al., 2008; cf. GeSI, 2008; OECD, 2009a,b). Furthermore, Green IS may include existing IT-based platforms that have been transformed such as to support environmentally sustainable business processes (Butler and McGovern, 2009).

Although arguably dated, The Aberdeen Group Report (2006) indicated that 80% of IT companies were estimated to be employing spreadsheet solutions to manage product compliance. Subsequently, this figure was confirmed by the author’s own informal research at practitioner conferences and from polls taken of practitioners during software vendor webinars. Although, recent market research confirms that this figure may have dropped to 65–70%. Thus anywhere between 65–80% of companies lack an integrated IS infrastructure to track, audit and manage issues around product compliance. Given the inherent complexity of legal regulations, national and international, it is argued that a piecemeal approach to the management of compliance and risk is no longer viable (Goosey, 2007)—hence, practitioners and researchers maintain that an enterprise-wide IS is required (Avila, 2006). Such a system would permit firms to anticipate, plan, and track compliance initiatives, taking into account, existing and proposed regulations, and would interface seamlessly with other organisational IS (Sammer, 2005). Thus, Avila (2006) argues that an IS needs to be implemented that not only offers material compliance analysis capabilities, but which can also account for rapidly changing environmental regulations across multiple markets and geographies, while enabling process change and reducing the costs of compliance. Support for Avila’s assertion comes from SAP AG.

In order to provide a rationale for the adoption of its Green IS, the Compliance for Product (CfP) application, which was then offered as a module for MySAP under a separate licence, SAP conducted an analysis of the time and effort spent in some of the sense-making and decision-making activities around product compliance. See Appendix A, Fig. A.1 for the results of this study. Briefly, SAP calculated that using an Excel spreadsheet and having ALL the RoHS, WEEE, REACH and EuP data interpreted, validated, and on record, it would take at least 35 h for an Ad Hoc Evaluation of a product with 700 parts, 2 suppliers per part, and 60% of which are self manufactured. With SAP CfP this takes approximately 3 h. Furthermore, implementing compliance with regulatory updates is estimated to take 28 h, as opposed to 2.5 h with SAP’s Compliance for Product. This gives a total of 63 h of effort for 1 product with 700 parts to conduct an ad hoc evaluation not, it must be noted, to conduct a systematic evaluation. Just to put this in context, a typical notebook has upwards of 1200 discrete parts, each of which is typically sourced from at least 3 suppliers. If SAP had included sense-making activities (i.e. analysing and interpreting regulations, and various legal instruments globally) then the effort expended in spreadsheet calculations rises considerably to weeks of effort. Also note that SAP’s study did not include the analysis of key normative or cultural-cognitive information flows. Based on the limitations of the SAP analysis and the findings adduced below, I argue that SAP are underestimating the information processing burdens facing manufacturers. Take, for example, SAP assumes that all regulatory sources are codified in the system and are up-to-date. What all this means is that manufacturers of electrical, electronic and IT-based products require sophisticated IS support to address what are intensive information-processing problems. Clearly, however, SAP’s solution was not at the time of writing the only one on the market, as Appendix A indicates.

## 1.2. Theoretical proposition and related research questions

The study of exogenous regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive influences from the institutional environment that shape organisations is termed the institutional environments perspective, while the study of the endogenous organisationa responses to such influences is termed the institutional arrangements perspective (Williamson, 1998). This study embraces both perspectives in that, on one hand, it employs Scott’s (1995, 2001, 2004) ‘three pillars’ conceptualisation of institutiona theory to elaborate on the various exogenous influences on strategic policy in order to operate at the institutional level of analysis—including societal and organisational fields. On the other hand, theoretical perspectives from organisation theory (e.g. Weick, 1995; Campbell, 2005; Choo, 2006; Kurtz and Snowden, 2003), are applied to help explore the endogenous strategic responses such pressures (cf. Oliver, 1991, 1997a,b; Frankenberger, 2006) in order for this study to operate at the organisational level of analysis

Research into the relationship between institutions and organisations illustrates that the institutional environment and organisational fields shape and influence business strategies and related objectives, as well as organisational structures and processes (cf. Scott, 1995, 2004; Oliver, 1991, 1997a,b; Frankenberger, 2006; Reid and Toffel, 2009). This is especially true of the institutional environment and organisational field of the IT manufacturing sector, which is characterised by numerous, complex, equivocal global regulations and a multifarious web of normative and cultural-cognitive influences on environmental sustainability. This study explores how one type of Green IS—an Environmental Compliance Management System called Compliance-to-Product—can support IT manufacturing organisations in formulating strategic responses to such influences in order to design and manufacture Green IT artefacts and report on corporate sustainability, both of which have become strategic imperatives for IT manufacturers (cf. Field, 2008; Butler and McGovern, 2009). The theoretical proposition underpinning this study is, therefore:

If Green IS are being employed by organisations to address problems of compliance with regulative, normative, and culturalcognitive obligations and social responsibilities, caused by complex and equivocal information from the institutional environment, then such IS will need to support sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing/creation in order to achieve environmental sustainability objectives.

Two related research questions are also posited to guide the empirical element of this study.

1. What features and functions of Green IS are required to support: (i) sense-making; (ii) decision making; and (iii) knowledge sharing/creating activities in response to signals from the institutional environment?

2. How are the organisations studied using Green IS to support such activities?

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: The second section first applies concepts from institutional theory to help explain the web of conditions and factors that are influencing organisations to adopt Green IS. It then integrates concepts from organisation theory to explain the institutional arrangements that organisations need to put in place to respond adequately to these influences. The third section describes this study’s research approach, while the fourth presents the findings. The final section conducts a brief discussion and theoretical analysis, while offering several conclusions and presenting four propositions which help refine this papers a priori theory.

## 2. Institutional environments and organisational responses: integrating institutional theory with organisation theory

Previous research illustrates the utility of institutional perspectives in understanding corporate social responsibility (Campbell, 2007) and building ecologically sustainable organisations (Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995). Both of these theoretical papers argues convincingly for the use of institutional theory in explaining why organisations become socially and environmentally responsible. However, they do not examine how organisations respond in terms of strategic decision making. In addressing the lacuna in extant research, Section 2.1 first presents (a) a brief overview of key concepts in institutiona theory; (b) a rationale for its utility in IS research; and (c) an applied explanation of regulative, normative, and cultural-cog nitive pressures on organisations operating in the IT manufacturing industry. Section 2.2 then draws on organisation theory to elaborate on the relationship between sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing/creation and the organisational responses to regulative (R-), normative (N-) and Cultural-cognitive (C-C-) signals from the institutional environment.

Briefly, following Scott (1995, 2001) the model posits that the institutional environment and organisational field provide regulative (R-signals), normative (N-signals), or cultural-cognitive (C-C-signals) information to organisations. The complex, comprehensive, and equivocal nature of these information signals need to be interpreted and made sense of. These interpre tations are then enacted, i.e. shaped by the beliefs of organisational actors (which are in turn a product of organisational culture, identity and strategy) and then applied for decision making (Choo, 2006). Such sense-making activities therefore (i) provide a context for organisational decision making by identifying problems and opportunities; (ii) help identify knowledge gaps in terms of the organisation’s strategy, processes and products, triggering the creation of new knowledge and capabilities; and (iii) facilitate organisations to learn from the outcomes of action taken (cf. Weick, 1995; Choo, 2006). Of course, organisations can and do provide signals to the institutional environment and organisational field, as indicated by the double-headed arrows. However, this paper posits that given the complexity and equivocality of the environment, sense-making, decision making, and knowledge sharing/creation need to be enabled by an information system(s)—in this scenario a Green IS—if the organisational outcome of environmental sustainability is to be achieved

## 2.1. Influences from the institutional environment and organisational field on IT manufacturing organisations

Information systems researchers such as Robey and Boudreau (1999) identify institutional theory as an appropriate theoretical perspective to investigate IT-related organisational change. Likewise Orlikowski and Barley (2001) argue that researchers should apply institutional theory to study the influence of regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive processes and structures in the design and operation of IT-based systems. Papers in the special issue of the Journal of Information Technology on the use of institutional theory in IS research provide additional support for the use of institutional perspectives (cf. Mignerat and Rivard, 2009), particularly those that operate at multiple levels of analysis (cf. Currie, 2009). While contemporaneous with this study of Green IS, the paper by Jensen et al. (2009) on IS implementation integrates institutional theory with sense-making theory and therefore provides particular support for this paper’s approach (cf. Weber and Glynn, 2006). Hence, an integrative approach applying perspectives from institutional and organisation theory provide this paper with its overarching theoretical underpinning. Before exploring the regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive cognitive influences on Green IT/IS, I now present a short overview of key concepts in institutional thought.

Institutional theory is concerned with the influences that shape social and organisational structures, schemas, rules, norms, routines and ultimately the behaviour of social actors (Scott, 2004). Scott (1995, p. 33) defines ‘‘Institutions [as consistingl of cognitive, normative, and regulative structures and activities that provide stability and meaning to social behayiour. Institutions are transported by various carriers—cultures, structures and routines—and they operate at multiple levels of jurisdiction’’. Thus, Scott (1995) argues that institutions are constituted by (1) regulatory processes, (2) constitutive and normative rules, and (3) meaning systems and behaviours. In Scott’s (1995, 2004) conceptual schema, the institutional environment consists of a societal field which structure organisational fields and organisations. Scott (1995) argues that at a societal level, institutions influence (through infusion or imposition) the formation of organisational fields. Scott (1995, 2004) integrates into his conceptual schema DiMaggio and Powell’s (1983) perspective which argues that all organisations operate within an institutional framework defined by their organisational population within a structure called an ‘organisational field’. According to DiMaggio and Powell (1983, p. 143), an ‘organisational field’ is comprised of ‘‘[t]hose organisations that, in the aggregate, constitute a recognized area of institutional life: [it consists of] key suppliers, resource and product consumers, regulatory agencies, and other organisations that produce similar services or products.’’ Thus, Scott (1995, 2004) argues an ‘organisational field’ and its constituent organisations are shaped by coercive (regulative and legislative) mechanisms originating in government departments, state-sponsored agencies, the judiciary, and so on, in addition to normative mechanisms (from professional bodies and industry/trade standards bodies/associations, suppliers, consulting organisations, distributors, and so on) and mimetic mechanisms—operating on competitors, shareholders, non-government organisations (NGOs) and society-at-large.

## 2.1.1. Regulative influences impacting environmental sustainability through Green IT/IS

IT manufacturers have, for several years, been confronted with diverse regulations governing the design, production and performance of computers and other IT artefacts (Aberdeen Group, 2006; Kellow, 2002). Briefly, such regulations focus on the direct effects of Green IT throughout the product life-cycle. Of particular concern to the organisational field of IT manufacturers is the European Union’s (EU) Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Directive, the Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) Directive, the Registration, Evaluation and Authorisation of Chemicals (REACh) Regulation, and the Eco-Design for Energy Using Products (EuP) Directive (Hristev, 2006; Bush 2007). The implementation of WEEE and RoHS Directives, for examples, resulted in highly complex legislation in EU member states, which does not lend itself to easy comprehension, application, and integration into an organisation’s research, develop ment, manufacturing and logistics processes (Pecht, 2004). However, the task of maintaining compliance has become even more arduous for the IT manufacturing industry and related sectors with the institution of the Registration, Evaluation and Authorisation of Chemicals (REACh) Regulation in 2007: this new law requires organisations to specify the possible dangers that combinations of chemicals present in their products not only on disposal, but also while in use (Bush, 2007). While the EU’s environmental laws have received much attention from manufacturers, similar legislation in other parts of the globe are no less rigorous. Take, for example, that the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in the US has issued comprehensive legislation covering all hazardous substances across the whole range of manufacturing sec tors, while Japan also has similar laws (Hristev, 2006). Over the last number of years, Korea, Australia, Canada, and US states such as California, have introduced legislation similar to the RoHS and WEEE directives, while China has instituted the Methods for the Control of Pollution by Electronic Information Products Directive or China RoHS. The need to address compliance legislation in different geographical locations adds complexity for global IT manufacturing organisations; however, determining the applicable regulation for a given geographical area can be complicated by the problem of understanding which products are covered, or are exempt, by sets of seemingly conflicting regulations (Kellow, 2002). The scope and scale of regulatory imperatives is indicated by Atrion Inc., as this software company’s ACE solution helps manufacturers across a range of organisational fields to design products with respect to over 300,000 chemical substances, 15,000 regulatory phrases in 44 languages, over 7500 proprietary rules, and 100 templates, spanning over 50 regulatory jurisdictions. Such a complex regulatory environment presents particular sense-making problems for organisations (cf. Kurtz and Snowden, 2003).

## 2.1.2. Normative pressures for Green IT/IS

Normative and cultural-cognitive pressures are also at play in shaping organisational responses. Accordingly, Schwab (2008) reports that organisations are now attending to a triple bottom-line of accounting for environmental and social externalities, in addition to their financial obligations of maximising profits. Schwab argues that this concern spans the entire value chain to include suppliers and customers. Take, for example, the Ford Motor Co. which is using the supply chain as a vehicle to drive environmental responsibility across the value chain: Ford now demands all suppliers to be ISO14001 compliant. Closer to home, Original Equipment Manufacturers of IT and electrical products or components are also exercising normative pressure on suppliers (Rao and Holt, 2005; Hochman, 2007). It is clear that while the ISO14001 standard in normal use is normative in its application—i.e. moral suasion is applied to adopters to comply rigorously with the standard (cf. Boiral, 2007)-it may also ne employed as a coercive mechanism.

It is clear from Collett (2008) that industry standards bodies exert significant normative forces. Take, for example, Germany’s Blue Angel Environmental Label initiative which certifies products as being environmentally sustainable. Nevertheless, the most influential of these normative influences is the Environmental Protection Authority’s (EPA) Energy Star programme—which concentrates on the energy consumed by products in use. Almost all major IT manufacturers now produce Energy Star compliant products. The Electronic Product Environmental Assessment Tool (EPEAT) was introduced by the Green Electronics Council and includes the EPA, U.S. Department of Energy, and industry standards bodies This standard is covers (1) the reduction/elimination of environmentally sensitive materials: (2) materials selection: (3 design for end of life; (4) product longevity/life-cycle extension; (5) energy conservation (including the Energy Star standard); (6) end of life management (take-back and recycling); (7) corporate performance; and (h) packaging (including toxic substances and labelling).The normative force of EPEAT was given a regulative impetus when an Executive Order was issued by President Bush in 2007 requiring all US federal agencies to purchase only EPEAT-certified artefacts (Ryan, 2008a). Recent years have also witnessed the formation of new industry associations such as Climate Savers Computing Initiative, Global eSustainability Initiative (GeSI), The Climate Group, and The Green Grid, which have shaped the organ isational field through normative influences of a general nature and have elicited particular responses from individual organisations.

## 2.1.3. Cultural-cognitive pressures for environmental sustainability

One example of cultural-cognitive or mimetic pressures on corporations to take responsibility for addressing climate change comes from The Carbon Disclosure Project. This involves organisations voluntarily measuring and disclosing their CO emissions and climate change avoidance strategies (Melville, 2010; cf. Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995). McKinsey’s (2007) cross-national study on the market for green products shows that 87% of customers state that they worry about the environmental consequences of their purchasing decisions; hence, organisations are eager to cultivate a Green organisational identity in order to attract customers (see for examples the Corporate Annual Reports of Dell, Intel and HP—cf. Ryan 2008a,b). However, organisations also create false environmentally sustainable identities and indulge in ‘greenwashing’ (cf. Laufer, 2003). Nevertheless, there are indications from industry that firms, particularly IT and technology firms, are beginning to incorporate environmental sustainability concerns into their core business strategies (Ceres, 2008; GeSI, 2008; OECD 2009b). Corporate environmental sustainability also affects organisational attractiveness to Green investors (Mincer, 2007). Further evidence of such influence is reflected by the institutionalisation of the Dow Jones Sustainability Index and the FTSE4Good Index, both of which measure the performance of firms that conform to established corporate social and environmental responsibility standards. Research also indicates that firms who have been publically censured for environmental transgressions are punished on the stock market (Chan and Milne, 1999).

There are also an increasing social or cultural-cognitive pressures on organisations in the IT sector. Take, for example, that environmental concerns by citizens and consumers have given rise to various social movements (Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995; Doh and Guay, 2006). Undoubtedly, the non-Government Organisation (NGO) Greenpeace (Doherty, 2002), the World Wildlife Fund (WWF), and Friends of the Earth exert much influence on organisations (cf. Doh and Guay, 2006; Campbell, 2007). Previous reviews of the literatures on corporate social and environmental sustainability indicates the role that NGOs have in moving organisations towards environmental sustainability (Kong et al., 2002), and structuring societal and organisational fields (Jennings and Zandbergen, 1995; Doh and Guay, 2006; Campbell 2007).

## 2.1.4. A theoretical proposition on Green IS

The above analysis of regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive influences indicates that IT manufacturers face a complex, equivocal institutional environment where concerns about (a) energy efficiency and the reduction in CO emissions of IT artefacts; (b) the use of hazardous substances in IT components; (c) short product life-cycles and the concomitant effect on waste and disposal; (d) the need to recycle IT products as efficiently as possible; and (e) concerns about packaging and the carbon footprint of logistics, shape the institutional and organisational arrangements that IT manufacturers put in place to respond to such pressures (cf. Patterson and Wigle, 2008; GeSI, 2008; OECD, 2009a,b). Nevertheless, Butler and McGovern (2008) argue that the IT industry has generally responded well to such influences and has made significant progress in making IT artefacts more environmentally sustainable by establishing particular institutional arrangements at the level of the organisational field and organisation (cf. OECD, 2009a,b). IT manufacturers have, predictably, employed IS to help deal with the aforementioned complexity and equivocality of signals from the institutional environments and the organisational field (cf. Butler and McGovern, 2009; Melville, 2010). Following Weber and Glynn (2006), this paper argues that institutional influences (R-, N-, and C-C-signals), many of which are equivocal, prime sense-making in organisations and inform decision making, knowledge sharing and creation. Thus, I propose the following proposition:

If Green IS are being employed by organisations to address problems of compliance with regulative, normative, and culturalcognitive obligations and social responsibilities caused by complex and equivocal information from the institutional environment, then such IS will need to support sense-making, decision making and knowledge creation in order to achieve environmental sustainability objectives.

As indicated, Fig. 1 presents this study’s conceptual integrative model of institutional environments and organisational sense-making, decisions making, and knowledge sharing/creating. The following subsection elaborates on the remainder of the model’s conceptual elements by drawing on organisation theory, particularly Choo’s (2006) sense-making theory which builds on the seminal ideas of Karl Weick (1995). Significantly, the section extends extant theory on sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing by elaborating the social mechanisms that help explain how organisations enact their interpretations, share knowledge, and take decisions in concert with other members of their organisation and other entities in their organisational field (Campbell, 2005)

2.2. On the relationship between sense-making, knowledge sharing and decision making and the organisational responses to influences from the institutional environment

“Information systems are an important but inadequately understood weapon in the arsenal of organisations in their quest for environmental sustainability by enabling new practices and processes in support of belief formation, action formation, and outcome assessment’’ (Melville, 2010, p. 14). While Melville proposes Coleman’s (1986) Beliefs-Action-Outcome framework, which originates in sociology, to investigate Green IT/IS, Coleman’s approach is similar to Weick’s (1995) much richer organisational sense-making theory. Take, for example, that beliefs underpin interpretation, just as decision making precedes action, while the results of action (routine or otherwise) are interpreted as feedback and may change beliefs, and so on (Choo, 2006). However, I argue that Choo’s (2006) integrative conceptualisation transcends both due to its wider scope, clarity and pragmatic, applied nature. Nevertheless, I also argue that it too requires extension to incorporate certain explanatory theoretical mechanisms (cf. Davis and Marquis, 2005; Campbell, 2005).

Drawing on organisational theory and management science, Choo (2006) argues that his integrative theoretical model of the knowing organisation transcends the limitations of traditional sense- and decision making approaches, as it integrates both while also incorporating a knowledge creating perspective. Table 1 integrates the theoretical concepts in his model with conceptual mechanisms identified by Campbell (2005) and which are discussed below. Based on well-documented case studies, Choo illustrates that organisations which focus on applying decision making routines often fail to detect and incorporate signals from the external environment. Making sense of and understanding the implications of such signals is vital if organisations are to develop strategic responses. Hence, Choo (ibid. p. 310) argues that organisational sense-making, which involves the application of beliefs, interpretations and the enactment of such interpretations, is vital as ‘‘we need to know ‘what is going on and why’ before we are able to decide ‘what is to be done’. Making sensible interpretations is as critical as making the right moves.’’ In the institutional arrangements perspective in institutional theory, organisations respond to their institutional environment by instituting new or modified procedures, methods of operation, and so on (Scott 1995), or by copying what other organisations are doing (cf. DiMaggio and Powell, 1983, on mimetic and competitive isomorphism), or deinstitutionalise defunct processes or activities (Oliver, 1991).

Returning to the broader institutional context described in Section 2.1, I argue that understanding complex regulatory environments, changing strategies and attitudes of competitors, and pressures from customers and stakeholders requires sophisticated sense-making by IT manufacturing organisations (cf. Chen et al., 2008). Thus, an organisation needs to ‘‘adopt a greater openness toward information: they would need to view information from multiple perspectives; ask new questions: try new sources: and be willing to reconsider beliefs and assumptions" in order to make better and more informed decisions (Choo 2006, p. 310). In many respects Choo (2006) echoes Alter (2004, p. 319), who argues that there is a need to consider the wider aspects of ‘‘decision support in real world settings, and how decision support can come from many sources other than technical artifacts such as DSS.’’ The emergence of corporate social and environmental sustainability indi cates that organisational decisions will need to be informed by broader perspectives (Petersen and Vredenburg, 2009), that incorporate softer variables, and which reflect the multifaceted nature of decision support (Alter, 2004) in an informationintensive knowledge economy (OECD, 2009a,b). Following Perrow (1984) and Fransman (1998), I argue that while bounded rationality (Simon, 1991) adequately informs decision making around, for example, competitive strategies aimed at profit maximization, accounting for complex social and environmental concerns requires a different approach, one based on institutional rationality. Fransman (1998, p. 165), for example, points out that because Simon’s (1991) focus is on the individual rather than miso- or macro-levels, ‘‘an explanation of the social processes which are responsible for the emergence of a social consensus regarding rationality and, on occasion, the emergence of social conflict [is required]. Such an examination requires an investigation of factors that go beyond Simon’s writings.

In answer to Fransman’s (1998) call for an ‘explanation of the social processes’ that underpin sense-making and decision making, and following Weber and Glynn (2006), this paper therefore introduces mechanism-based concepts to enhance the explanatory approach of extant sense- and decision making perspectives. According to Hedstrom (2005, p. 24) researchers in organisation studies should focus on the mechanisms generate change in social entities, rather than statistical regularities between variables: following this approach results in a foundation for powerful explanations. In mechanism-based theorising, a theoretical explanation will not take the form of a universal social law (Hedstrom and Swedberg, 1998), rather it will be ‘problem-driven’ (Davis and Marquis, 2005). In many ways, this echoes Scott’s (1995) conceptualisation of coercive, normative and mimetic mechanisms in structuring institutions and institutional environments. Operating at a lower level of analysis, Campbell (2005) proposes the following social mechanisms to build explanatory theory in organisations and on social movements: (a) Framing involves the use of metaphors and symbols which influence how issues are perceived and which inform social action in the context of socially constructed realities; (b) Diffusion refers to the dissemination of concepts, social structures, and practices, mainly through social networks; (c) Translation refers to how diffused concepts and ideas are transformed for application in new social contexts; (d) Bricolage involves the recombination of concepts, practices, etc. from other social contexts to produce new forms of social activity; (e) Network Cultivation, which involves creating social and institutional movements and associations; and (f) Strategic leadership (or institutional entrepreneurship), in which social actors decide on which direction a social, institutional or organisational entity should take. The conceptual mechanisms of framing and translation are clearly analogous to the concepts of beliefs and enactment, posited by Choo (2006) and which emanate from sense-making theory. The remaining mechanisms reflect the reality that members of organisational field form alliances and associations with others to achieve organisational objectives—chiefly through the diffusion, translation, and recombination of knowledge of organisational practices using complex co-located or distributed (or virtual) social and institutional networks.

This has clear implications for IT manufacturing firms that need to integrate (1) legal knowledge of compliance imperatives and the issues these create for product design specifications with (2) knowledge of materials used in products produced by the firm (past, present and future) and (3) product sub-components provided by suppliers, and so on. Practitioners also recognise this, as Avila (2006) and others argue that information systems support is required by organisations to deal with the growing number of complex, equivocal regulatory compliance imperatives, and other signals from the institutional environment, if they are to achieve environmental sustainability objectives.

## 3. Research method

An explanatory case study design was chosen for the study (Yin 2003). The case and the units of analysis were purposively selected so that the units of analysis predicted/exhibited similar findings: Yin terms this a literal replication strategy in a multi-case design. However, as this is a single case study design with two embedded units of analysis, Yin also advocates the development of a rich theoretical framework, such as that presented. This paper’s integrative model of institutional theory and sense-making in organisations indicates the conditions under which the phenomenon is manifested. Support for this approach comes from Jensen et al. (2009) in the IS field, Weber and Glynn (2006) and Weick et al. (2005) in the broader field of organisation theory. In regard to this study’s rigor, the issue of (a) construct validity or objectivity in the conduct of the case study was addressed through the use of multiple sources that enable triangulation, building a chain of evidence, and member checks; (b) internal validity was addressed through pattern-matching, explanation-building, and in considering rival explanations; (c) external validity was addressed through the use of theoretically informed replication logic; finally, (d) the study’s reliability was indicated through the use of a case study protocol and a database of research artefacts. Data gathering was carried out through the use of semi-structured interviews, observation, and primary and secondary sources and documentation. Data analysis involved the use of constant comparative analysis and rigorous coding procedures (e.g. open, axial and selective coding) and the use of field research memos (cf. Patton, 1990; Strauss and Corbin, 1998). The theoretical constructs in the a priori theory articulated above were employed as seed categories for coding.

The case chosen for study was Compliance and Risks Ltd. (C&R), while the units of analysis were two Fortune 50o IT manufacturers that are using C&R’s Compliance-to-Product (C2P) environmental compliance management system (ECMS). Participants in the case study were the company’s CEO and founder, his European Business team including the CFO, Operations Manager, Marketing and Sales Directors and representatives and 6 members of the Legal Data Team (LDT). Data was also gathered from several members of the Application Development Team, including the Project Manager and senior software engineers, which are now headquartered in Northern California. Data gathering at this organisation began in August 2005 and continues to date in what is an in-depth longitudinal study. The two units of analysis are well-known Fortune 500 IT manufacturers, Lincoln Inc. and Jackson Inc., both of which wish to remain anonymous due to the sensitive nature of the research topic. Data was gathered at both sites in 2006–2007 and again in early December 2009 from several key informants—senior managers and practitioners charged with implementing corporate environmental sustainability initiatives. In both the case and units of analysis semi-structured interviews were conducted, while data was also gathered during several meetings and on-site visits in Europe and the US from August 2005 to December 2009: participant observation was also employed whenever possible (cf. Yin, 2003). Internet-based teleconferencing technologies were also employed to facilitate meetings, while emails were also employed to clarify particular issues. The data was interpreted and analyzed on an ongoing basis and augmented by company documentation, training manuals, technology architecture documentation, and so on (cf. Yin, 2003). It must be noted, however, that the researcher had no access, at any time, to confidential client data, in accordance with C&R’s non-disclosure and confidentiality obligations to its clients.

## 4.1. The emergence of the Compliance-to-Product (C2P) application

The C2P application was the brainchild of a lawyer who recognised the potential for such an application in 2004 while working in the area of governance, risk and compliance for an international consultancy. He was familiar with the problems that IT manufacturers were having with new and proposed regulations on environmental issues and sustainability. In light of the relative inaccessibility of legal instruments drafted by the European Union and similar regulatory agencies, he conceived of an application that would obviate the need for expensive and often ineffective legal consultancy and equally ineffective equivocal reports. In his schema, legal experts would play a key role in his Green IS, as it was they who would reduce the burden of sense-making placed on end users by converting complex, equivocal legal documents into an easy-to-understand format that would render unequivocal the legal implications and obligations on IT manufacturers to design, manufacture and distribute Green IT in an environmentally sustainable way.

Without the legal competencies to make sense of complex legal documents, peppered with synonyms, homonyms, and so on, organisational actors generally rely on a small army of internal and/or external lawyers and other regulatory and subject matter experts, who, as an output of their analysis, generate a mountain of reports and related documents. ## 4.2. Enabling sense-making with C2P

In order to help answer the above research questions, this section first considers the high-level conceptual model presented in Fig. 2. In presenting this figure, the author drew on a conceptual model first articulated by practitioners at Compliance and Risks Ltd. and incorporated the institutional dimensions on the left-hand side of the figure. The processes and information flows described in the remainder are faithful to those articulated by practitioners. In order to provide further context on the features and functions here described, it should be noted that the C2P application is based on Web 2.0 technologies<sup>1</sup> which enables the social construction of collaborative networks consisting of actors from the institutional environment and organisational fields. It is significant, however, that the application is underpinned by such a network of actors viz. Compliance and Risks’ Legal Data Team (LDT); C&R’s Data Partners (e.g., Global business-oriented legal firms such as Baker and McKenzie); and an expanding social network of industry and other subject matter experts. Within adopting organisations, users are typically executives and officers in an organisation’s governance risk and compliance (GRC) function; the Corporate Social/Environmental Responsibility function; Product Stewards; Product Designers; Operations and manufacturing engineers; Supplier relationship management personnel; and Customer Relationship managers. It is also noteworthy that C2P is implemented using a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) architecture and business model.

Perhaps the most impressive feature from a user perspective is that much of the labour-intensive sense-making is effected by the external Regulatory Requirements Gathering Processes (see Fig. 2). Table 2 illustrates the features that support C2P’s Regulatory Requirements Gathering Processes and which underpin and support sense-making by users. These processes are executed by Compliance & Risk’s Ltd. Legal Data Team (LDT) and augmented by other experts. In terms of the current study, C2P implements a two-tier approach to sense-making, with Tier 1 being performed by the LDT. The LDT is comprised of lawyers, paralegals, engineers and environmental specialists in Europe and the United States. Practitioners in Compliance and Risks’ LDT argue that understanding and contextualizing compliance imperatives is informed by a deep knowledge of the way in which such imperatives develop over time and are applied in different ways across diverse regulatory jurisdictions. Thus, the LDT’s primary function is to apply their explicit and tacit knowledge to convert sources of legislation (regulative), compliance standards (normative), and broader cultural-cognitive influences from diverse global environments into explicit knowledge contained in structured legal and compliance data in Sources, Scopes, Terms and Def initions, and related elements. The LDT also tracks and monitors such signals from the environment in realtime. Apart from key descriptive fields, a Source is defined by its Scope, relevant Sections, Dates, Related Sources, and Terms and Definitions. As of 2010, over 5300 Sources have been identified and codified into C2P. Regulative Sources are categorised as In-force (default), Supporting, or Pending. Non-regulatory sources that are normative and cultural-cognitive in origin are also captured: take, for example, a World Wildlife Foundation (WWF) report on energy efficiency is categorised as a Supporting Source in C2P on Identifying and Assessing the Opportunities to Reduce ${ \mathrm { C O } } _ { 2 }$ from ICT Use and is related to EU ICT for Energy Efficiency Reports.

From a theoretical perspective, sense-making commences in the wake of a breakdown in understanding (Winograd and Flores, 1986; Butler and Murphy, 2007). Drawing on phenomenological hermeneutics, Butler and Murphy’s (2007) study on the design of knowledge management systems illustrates that social and organisational actors break the complex, equivocal ‘whole’ of phenomena into their component ‘parts’ when cycling through the ‘circle of understanding’ of the phenomenon being interpreted (cf. Klein and Myers, 1999; Cole and Avison, 2007). This activity is termed bracketing by sense-making theorists such as Weick (1995). Campbell’s (2005) mechanism of framing explains the effectiveness with which institutional signals are perceived by users. Clearly, legal and other subject matter experts will bracket or frame regulatory and other signals more effectively and can, therefore, play an important role in organisational sense-making.

Jensen et al. (2009) argue that users bracket different cues about the IT-based systems they use and this shapes their experiences of system use. Leaving issues of usability aside, and focusing on the use of the Compliance-to-Product application to make sense of equivocal institutional influences, users of C2P are presented with pre-bracketed cues or frames by the application in the form of the Sources contained in the Regulations Database, along with an explanation of the Scope of each, in addition to Structured Compliance Data, which presents Sections, Dates, and Related Sources, and Terms and Definitions Additional information is also supplied in the form of Daily Data Updates and Expert Commentary. From a theoretical perspective, this reduces the equivocality of R-, N-, and C-C-signals from the institutional environment and facilitates users to derive meaning in a process of enactment, that is in identifying the consequences of regulative, normative, or cultural-cognitive influences for products and processes. This then is Tier 2 of the two-tier approach to sense-making supported by the application.

## 4.2.1. Problems with Sense-Making at Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc

Lincoln Inc. was, in 2005, experiencing significant and growing issues in identifying and making informed product-related decisions in the face of the number and complexity of environmental regulations globally. Lincoln Inc, is a highly centralised organisation in terms of the control and management of its operations. Manufacturing and a range of related support functions are, nevertheless, performed on a global basis. However, these activities are tightly controlled and coordinated from the company’s HQ in Silicon Valley. Lincoln Inc.’s environmental compliance processes involve the following core processes: (1) Tracking and Monitoring Regulations; (2) Assessing Risk; (3) Raising awareness and communication; and (4) Implementing compliance solutions. Key actors, such as product stewards, designers, procurement and logistics personnel, in these processes are distributed globally, but are coordinated by the Corporate Environmental Compliance function. Lincoln Inc. was, like the majority of its competitors, codifying a mountain of legal documents from a plethora of legal sources across the world into Excel spreadsheets. Process (1) Tracking and Monitoring was therefore heavily dependent on sense-making and, as will be seen, was labour intensive for Lincoln Inc. Unfortunately, this meant that processes (2)–(4) were sub-optimal in terms of their effectiveness, all of which endangered the organisation’s identity as a environmentally sustainable business Hence, Lincoln Inc. was searching for an innovative technology that transcended the limited functionality of existing compliance applications, which had their origins in organisational document, risk, and product life-cycle management (PLM) Environmental compliance officers at Lincoln argued that such applications were deficient in that they typically added compliance-related functionality to the basic data or document management functions and PLM features, while omitting key collaborative and knowledge-sharing capabilities, although vendors were marketing and had positioned their offerings as enterprise-wide environmental compliance management systems.

Prior to the adoption of C2P in 2006, Lincoln Inc. used data on restricted or hazardous substances, energy efficiency, product disposal, recycling and reuse, contained in spreadsheets to inform the decision making of its product stewards, design engineers, and logistics and procurement functions—i.e. Processes (3) and (4) above. E-mail was the primary communication mechanism between Lincoln’s Environmental Compliance Officers and other organisational actors. However, it proved to be wholly inadequate to manage both the volume and complexity of queries and responses:

‘‘Sifting through a maze of emails to find previously documented solutions...or to provide a documented audit trial for why design decisions were made was proving an impossible task...I was getting 300 plus emails a day, and no matter how I grouped or filed them away in folders, there were significant problems in searching and locating key emails I sent or received’’ (Environmental Compliance Manager).

Thus, the company’s internal environmental compliance processes could not cope with the management of this complex web of regulatory imperatives and other signals from the external environment. Lincoln’s Environmental Compliance Manager was quite concerned that:

‘‘Policy imperatives are exponentially growing...policy is focusing increasingly on product issues (RoHS, Power management, labelling, and packaging design)... and is enforced at multiple levels, adding regional, national and local level data points.’

This statement is indicative of the complexity of the institutional environment and the frustration felt by practitioners at attempting to make sense of it using existing technologies.

Jefferson Inc. is a Fortune 500 IT manufacturer. Significantly, Jefferson is a board member of Global eSustainability Initiative (GeSI), which is an industry–based association of IT and telecommunications companies whose aim it is to foster sustainable development in the IT sector—GeSI does, however, have the WWF on its board. Jefferson participates in the Climate Change, Materiality, and Supply Chain working groups, whose objective it is to build common industry processes and applications. The corporation therefore has a high profile when it comes to environmental sustainability. In 2008, its Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) function evaluated and adopted C2P. Before this it was using the application on a trail basis. Given difference in organisational structure between it and Lincoln Inc., a different emphasis was placed on the use of this Green IS by CSR executives at Jefferson. Both Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc. have an obligation to respond to institutional queries from a range of stakeholders; however, while Lincoln Inc. is very much a centralised organisation, Jefferson is heavily decentralised in terms of product design decisions, sales and marketing, and logistics. It also has a greater network of partner organisations and participates in several industry bodies.

In terms of this study’s institutional perspective, Jefferson’s relationships with other entities in its organisational field are more complex than that which Lincoln Inc. enjoys, although the latter’s markets are more competitive. Furthermore, unlike Lincoln, and because of its decentralised method of operation, its CSR function is not as closely integrated, spatially or temporally, with other functions in the organisations. Thus, is more reliant on IT-enabled communication with geographically dispersed virtual teams and units.

‘‘We depend a lot on cross-functional collaboration to get things done...it’s about local control and global direction...Consequently we have virtual or spontaneous teams here in [Jefferson] and we use tele-presence type applications to make the communication links work...we can call anyone at anytime’’ (CSR Executive).

These linkages become increasingly important as Jefferson attempts to communicate information about its environmental sustainability and performance to regulators, investors, and a range of other stakeholders and interested entities like the World Wildlife Foundation (WWF), Greenpeace, among others.

CSR managers were dependent on information service providers such as EIATRACK to provide them with data on industry regulations, which was then linked to spreadsheets, with email being used as the primary mechanism for diffusion and translation. Echoing the points made by Lincoln Inc.’s Environmental Compliance Manager, a CSR executive at Jefferson Inc. pointed out that:

‘‘Email kills...it’s a waste of time even trying to use it to generate simple answers to complex questions from stakeholders about, for example, carbon disclosure...even simple questions can generate too many emails...back and forth...the downside is if we get an answer wrong it could cost us dearly...Email on top of email, and people spend all the time organising the data. 60 emails and no work at all gets done. With C2P we are totally focussed on creating a great answer.’’

The first observation that can be made from this statement is that in performing complex information-intensive activities, email can be a double-edged sword. Managing and merging multiple email threads is difficult at the best of times, but when managing strategic corporate activities, its deficiencies can have significant unintended consequences. Such difficulties have been long recognised in the literature, as email use in IT organisations has been criticised because of problems such as ‘‘lack of time, lack of structure, inability to single out the important from the trivial, and over-use of email are the major problem areas’’ (Stenmark, 1998). Issues of security, data integrity, and so on, are just the tip of the iceberg, as the above comment indicates. In many ways email became part of the sense-making problem for actors in both organisations.

## 4.2.2. Solving the sense-making problems at Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc.

## 4.3. Providing support for decision making with C2P

The outcome of C2P supported sense-making is argued to be better decision making by users, as their understanding of the impact of compliance imperatives for products and processes is enhanced. For example, in scenarios where compliance requirements/imperatives detailed in Sources are found to impact on product components, users make decisions and take appropriate action to bring the product/component, whether currently being manufactured or in the design phase, back into compliance. For example, the materials or substances used in a product may be deemed substances of very high concern (SVHC), e.g. carcinogens, or restricted or hazardous substances (RoHS), e.g. mercury and lead. Other impacts might concern energy consumption or efficiency. In the former case, actions will be taken to reduce the quantities of, or simply eliminate, such substances.

As with sense-making in relation to signals from the institutional environment, the application facilitates also sense-making in relation to decision making processes, as the features and functions presented in Table 3 bracket or frame a series of activities that users may engage in order to enhance decision making. Issue management lies at the core of C2P’s ability to inform decisions around product design and compliance. The central hypothesis underpinning the approach adopted in C2P is that regulatory or other Sources result in compliance Issues for products and processes. Client users can view the Impacts of the compliance imperatives included in regulatory and related Sources in terms of their effects on products and their attributes. This helps identify compliance-related Issues for environmental compliance officers and product design engineers in order to inform their decision making and take appropriate action in the form of Tasks. Other features that enable compliance-related decision making is that the C2P application supports users to collectively and collaboratively evaluate, escalate, and address compliance Issues: for example, compliance officers or managers can quickly delegate to, or monitor Issues, with product design engineers and other actors. The application can also enable users to generate Project-based Action Plans so they can manage decisions taken to address compliance Issues. The Reminders feature (Table 4 below) helps users track and manage compliance Issue-related responsibilities assigned to relevant others as Tasks. The Risk Ratings facility helps illustrate the level and history of risk for each compliance Issue, which is highly visible throughout C2P. Collectively these features constitute the compliance decision making and management aspects of C2P.

The above features help define the context for decision makers at several levels. ## 4.3.1. Enhancing decision making at Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc.

Lincoln Inc. is now a mature user of the C2P application. When asked which features of C2P are in most use, the Environmental Compliance Manager recently stated that, notwithstanding his earlier positive comments on the sense-making capabilities, ‘‘we’re using the Issues section, the Projects groupings, the Security feature is used a lot to protect viewing rights around certain topics, and the search function. Those are the main things we are [currently] using it for. Basically, within that, I’m using those functionalities within my dashboard’’ (see Fig. 3). This is an indication that Lincoln Inc. was activel focusing on risk assessment, raising awareness, communication, and implementing compliance solutions, and was trusting the sense-making being performed by Compliance and Risks Ltd. legal and other subject matter experts. The following observation supports this point:

‘‘Most important is that Issues can be linked back to Sources...That’s hugely important for us because that’s what differentiates C2P Issues from a regular ‘to do list’ on your Microsoft Outlook or your notebook or whatever you might be using for your ‘to do list’. So, this way I can have people in Germany or Washington all tapping into Issues that are linked to the right documents or the right commentary and standards and clarifications.’

Thus that application’s ability to diffuse information securely to a network of product stewards, design engineers, and logistics and procurement professionals globally illustrates the effectiveness of the above features. However, additional insights into the ability of the application to support strategic decisions comes from a Compliance Officer at Lincoln. He stated that an Expert Commentary which appeared on his C2P dashboard indicated that South Korea was planning to change the maximum standby power consumption of all electronic devices. This regulatory change affected one category of IT artefact which Lincoln manufactured and sold into the South Korean market, It was not the loss of market share that worried Lincoln’s executives when they were appraised of this development, but the potential damage to the company’s ‘Green’ image. This information had product designers decide to modify the IT artefact ahead of the change of legislation and maintain the product’s compliance not only with South Korean regulations, but also with future changes in Europe and the US.

Like users in Lincoln Inc., the key features that support decision making at Jefferson Inc. revolve around Issue management. Stakeholder questions, on for example, energy efficiency of IT artefacts will result in a Project being created to help answer the question, Such questions may not be put directly to the CSR function by an entity (e.g. WWE. Greenpeace, Green Investors, etc.) in the organisational field, but may be channelled through Sales and Marketing in Europe. This typically results in a Project being created in C2P by the CSR Executive, with all related organisational actors across Jefferson being enrolled in a formal or ad hoc virtual team to help answer the query or address an identified problem. Issues will then be created and Tasks assigned. However, C2P’s knowledge sharing functionality (see the next section) such as Contexts and Comments are drafted on Issues, and answers to the questions entered into the system. Such answers will typically be linked with regulatory sources governing the particular area (e.g. REACh, RoHS, WEEE, and EPEAT etc.). Explanatory or supplemental documents such as technical data sheets or test results may be attached to the Issues, Comments and so on. Tags will be used to help explain the links or relationships between all of the aforementioned ‘‘objects’’ viz

‘‘What you end up with is crisscrossing, zig-zagging lines between those celestial objects—the difference [between this and the spreadsheet/email approach] is you can understand why each one exists and you can come back in three or six months and you can see at a glance, well not quite a glance...well, you can figure out quickly how a question was answered and on what data the answer was based.’’

Thus, the application helps support the implementation of decisions to answer queries from a range of external actors in the institutional, while also maintaining an easily accessible auditable record of such decisions, which is an indication of the knowledge-sharing capabilities of such applications.

## 4.4. Supporting knowledge sharing and creating activities with C2P

Support for knowledge management/creation, over and above sense-making and decision making, is, according to C2P’s designers, provided by several of the application’s features; for example, the multiple views/features in the C2P application affords users an opportunity to view information from all sources in one place. In addition, C2P captures users’ discussion threads on compliance imperatives and their Impacts and Issues as Comments. Furthermore, users can create Contexts for classifying and reporting the evolving implications of Issues. In addition, History Links provide an audit view of all changes and updates. Furthermore any changes to a user area of responsibility triggers an Alert, in the form of an automatic email notification to responsible users (this has proven a key feature as it draws users to the application). There is also an Attachment feature that permits users to link related documents for easy storage and retrieval. Sophisticated Search features permit users to run queries and generate reports based on specific parameters. Finally, robust security features ensure that highly confidential information on products and on sensitive decisions are available on a need to know basis. Finally, as with most Web 2.0-based applications, C2P has Personal Dashboard (see Fig. 3) with features permit users to view their own compliance Issues, Searches, Bookmarks, and with the ability to generate Reports, monitor Reminders and create Alerts.

Collectively, the above features are characteristic of those found in knowledge management systems (KMS), in which users are facilitated to make organisationally tacit knowledge explicit, so that they can share that knowledge with others in their ‘communities of practice’ (cf. Butler and Murphy, 2007). Applying the principles articulated by Butler and Murphy (2007), the Personal Dashboard may be argued to comply with Principle 1, which basically addresses ease-of-use (i.e. so that the application itself does not become the object of sense-making, rather it enables sense-making with respect to the phenomenon of interest). These features help bracket or provide ‘cues’ for users as to how to enact or structure their tacit or explicit knowledge with respect to Sources, Impacts, Issues and Tasks, as Comments, Tags, Contexts, or Attachments. Such features also help users explain why certain decisions were taken: Accordingly, they may also help identify ‘knowledge gaps and elaborate on ‘learning outcomes’ (cf. Choo, 2006)

## 4.4.1. Support for knowledge sharing and creation at Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc.

In outlining the context in which compliance-related knowledge sharing is being enabled by C2P’s Issues management, Project functionality, etc. outlined in Tables 3 and 4, the Environmental Compliance Manager at Lincoln Inc. pointed out that:

‘‘There are two types of company that manage the compliance risks as well as these market risks that are out there. There are those who can afford to have people on the ground that are employed into positions with a certain personality that looks around the next corner for you. And we are in that bucket. So, I have a team of people whose fingers are very much on the pulse and are hand selected to have the personality to really be looking around the next corner. So, C2P becomes a bank of their knowledge, a place where all of these individuals are employing are feeding the bank; they’re putting ‘knowledge credit’ into the bank by creating Issues, evaluating risks, delegating or completing Tasks, adding Contexts, drafting Comments, Tagging links, and so on.’

One of the goals of an IT-based system to support knowledge sharing is to create and manage a corporate memory (cf. Butler and Murphy, 2007). Although Lincoln’s Environmental Compliance Manager was not familiar with such systems, he neatly captures the application’s capabilities in this regard:

‘‘We don’t rely on C2P to tell us what is coming next but we do rely on it to keep our knowledge of what is coming next safe and referenceable and structured so that if I lose a person, or if somebody critical ends up under a bus, that knowledge is there. If I need to share that person’s knowledge with my food chain, then it’s there and I know where to get it, and if I need to expand the knowledge - meaning I need an external counsel to weigh in on some requirement that someone looking around the corner has put into C2P, then there is a way for that additional value to be put into the same knowledge bank.’’

The CSR function at Jefferson Inc. echoes this view on the knowledge-sharing and management capabilities the application as this example of leveraging the knowledge created from making previous decisions:

‘‘I answered a CDP (Carbon Disclosure Project) question in June for Gartner Group. In September it needed to be updated for WWF. I go back and link this to the Gartner question, and the Tagged Post-it-note says ‘Gartner answer used for CDP response’...without the audit trail we would have go back and recheck everything, just to be sure that the answer we gave is the right one...I just open the Project and it’s all there...In a very short period of time C2P gave us 124 collaborative Projects and a history of why decisions were taken.’’

The 124 collaborative projects indicate the importance of features that support the mechanism of network cultivation, as well as information diffusion, translation, and bricolage to close knowledge gaps and leverage learning outcomes.

The strategic nature of an ECMS application such as C2P was also evident at Jefferson Inc., as his ability to respond quickly and accurately to stakeholder questions has an impact on how the company was perceived by investors viz.

‘‘C2P was used by Jefferson Inc. to make our [stock market] score go up...because we were able to answer the questions faster, to better marshal our [internal] resources’’ (CSR Executive).

Thus, we can induce from the above observations that managing external complexity using Web 2.0 functionality and ‘‘integrating everything under one roof and making these relationships’’ (Compliance Officer, Lincoln Inc.) was highly important in (a) reducing the sense-making workload and (b) informing decision making for environmental compliance activities. Overall, Lincoln’s Environmental Compliance Team reported that as of 2009 they were able to focus on important activities such as risk assessment and compliance management due to better informed decision making and knowledge sharing, in stead of engaging in extensive sense-making around equivocal environmental laws.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

This study draws on institutional theory to explain the exogenous forces acting on organisations from the institutional environment and organisational fields in relation to environmental sustainability and regulatory compliance. It then draws on organisational theory to explain and understand the endogenous responses that organisations make in reaction to such stimuli. It was seen that the complexity of the institutional environment requires sophisticated sense-making in order to inform decision making and to share and or create new knowledge, with the objective of identifying problems and opportunities, closing knowledge gaps, and leveraging learning outcomes. Inter alia, it was revealed that all three activities require the support of a Green IS in order to effectively address the problems of product compliance with environmental regulations and equivocal normative and cultural-cognitive mechanisms. In order to provide support for its thesis, the study’s findings describe the features of a Web 2.0-based IT-enabled application and how it was being applied by two Fortune 500 IT manufacturers—Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc.—in their efforts to maximize global compliance with environmental regulations and minimize related risks.

This paper’s integrative theoretical model is now employed to examine the contribution of the Green IS studied within the context of its use in Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc. Prior to the implementation of Compliance-to-Product (C2P) Green IS, Lincoln Inc.’s compliance team and Jefferson Inc.’s CSR team were faced with a heavy sense-making burden, when confronted with compliance-related decisions relating to the design of products, recycling and waste, packaging, reporting to institutional stakeholders on all environmental issues, and so on. This burden was due to the complexity of meeting all compliance imperatives across a global marketplace. Thus, satisficing on bounded rationality was not really an option for decision makers at either Lincoln or Jefferson, given the potential consequences to the organisations in terms of damage to brand image, exclusion from markets, and financial penalties levied by regulatory institutions, to say nothing of share price movements. Thus, tracking and monitoring global regulatory requirements was proving to be increasingly problematic for both organisations, due to deficiencies in sense-making capabilities within the organisations, and the poor levels of support provided by regulatory agencies, and by legal consultants in reducing the equivocality of the information the aforementioned agencies provide. Compliance and Risks’ Ltd. use of dedicated legal subject matter experts in interpreting and making sense of regulatory requirements, and in capturing their interpretations in a highly structured format in C2P, reduced the sensemaking burden on Lincoln’s environmental compliance team and Jefferson’s CSR executives.

It is clear, however, that the informating capabilities of an application such as C2P, in terms of its abilities at enhancing communication and knowledge sharing within and across functions, permitted effective compliance management by Lincoln Inc. and Jefferson Inc. For example, while Lincoln’s compliance team might have been aware of the regulatory requirements and compliance imperatives concerning particular hazardous substances in specific geographical areas, the full range of compliance-related impacts and issues were not understood—the application’s knowledge sharing features therefore enabled compliance officers, product engineers and other users to question the assumptions on which product decisions were made. Practitioners at Lincoln were pleased, but not necessarily surprised, to discover that a growing number of previously unidentified ‘Issues’ and ‘Alerts’ began to emerge (e.g. the power consumption of IT artefacts in standby mode) in relation to product design/attributes and compliance with particular regulatory regimes—these surfaced through IT-enabled communication- and collaboration-based knowledge sharing among the environmental compliance team and design/manufacturing engineers. Hence, the C2P application provided Lincoln's environmental compliance team and other related organisational actors with a shared context, integrated analytic and communication tools, and a data/information/knowledge repository that enhanced sense-making, decision taking, and knowledge sharing. The experience of practitioners at Jefferson Inc. mirrored that of Lincoln, in terms of the use and capabilities of the C2P application as a Green IS of the type described in this study’s theoretical proposition. Here, evidence was provided to the effect that practitioners can employ a Green IS to manage the complexity of both the external institutional environment and organisational field in tandem with managing internal governance, risk and compliance problems and opportunities in complex centralised and decentralised organisational structures.

## 5.1. Implications of the empirical findings for this study’s integrative theoretical model

This paper adopted Yin’s (2003) analytic generalisation strategy with respect to the case study of the C2P application. The findings support this study’s theoretical proposition that IT manufacturers require Green IS with sophisticated sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing/creating features to respond adequately to regulative, normative and cultural-cognitive forces/signals from the institutional environment. Furthermore, the literal replication strategy (Yin, 2003) applied to the embedded units of analysis supports this contention by examining how two organisations that differ in size, structure, culture and process can employ a Green IS to engage in sense-making and decision taking and ultimately share knowledge and create value for the organisation. However, the proposition requires extension and further elaboration to take account of the complex nature of the external institutional environment and the internal organisational environment, specifically in respect of a Green IS’ support for endogenous mechanisms that: (1) address problems and opportunities; (2) close the knowledge gaps identified by sense-making; and (3) to codify and institute learning outcomes that emerge from decision making and knowledge sharing/creation. I now, briefly, expand on this by offering a series of related propositions based on the empirical findings of the case and its units of analysis.

The first theoretical proposition suggested by the empirical findings is that:

If the information signals from the external institutional environment are complex and equivocal, due to a high level of subject-matter specificity, then a two-tier, IS-enabled, approach to sense-making is required.

This schema gives rise to a second, related, proposition viz.

If subject matter experts, in this case legal, regulatory, and industry-oriented actors, constitute Tier 1 in order to make sense of legal, normative and cultural-cognitive signals and bracket (Weick, 1995; Choo, 2006) or frame (Campbell, 2005) this complexity, then organisational actors can make sense of and understand the consequences of such signals for their organisation, its processes and products.

Furthermore, I argue for the following qualifying or Auxiliary Statement (AS) to the above proposition (see Putnam (1981) on the use of AS to put boundaries on the scope of a theory): Tier 1 sense-making is dependent of the ability of the Green IS (in this case) to help facilitate: (1) the diffusion of information through Web 2.0-enabled social networks; (2) the translation of diffused information into organisational contexts; and (3) network cultivation that enables each organisation using the Green IS to be part of a virtual sustainability movement within their organisation field, while also reducing information-re lated coordination and transaction costs (cf. Malone and Crowston, 1994).

The third theoretical proposition that emerges from the findings, and which extends the second proposition, is that:

If the Green IS supports institutional rationality, by incorporating, channelling and coordinating the diffusion of suitable framed or bracketed regulatory, normative and cultural-cognitive signals to organisational actors, then the sense-making burdens at the Tier 2 or organisational level are greatly reduced and the problems associated with information search caused by bounded rationality will be mitigated for organisational actors.

The fourth and final theoretical proposition is that:

If the Green IS facilitates: (1) Network cultivation such that virtual and/or ad hoc teams can be established to enable shared decision making and knowledge sharing; (2) the diffusion of governance, risk and compliance practices, aimed at meeting the expectations formed in the institutional environment and organisational field; (3) the translation of the concepts for application with the organisation; (4) bricolage, so that (a) processes and products can be modified to manage compliance and reduce risk and/or (b) quickly leverage and modify previous decisions/compliance reports to signal to the external environment the organisation’s compliance or environmental sustainability practices, then the outcomes associated with organisational decision making and knowledge sharing/creation will be optimal in terms of achieving organisational objectives regarding environmental sustainability

The use of such mechanism-based, theoretical propositions (Campbell, 2005) to augment extant perspectives on IS-enabled sense-making, decision making and knowledge sharing/creation (cf. Choo, 2006; Jensen et al., 2009), I believe, makes a modest theoretical contribution not only to the IS field, but also to reference disciplines of institutional and organisation theory. In addition, this paper’s theory of one type of Green IS, supported as it is by the empirical findings, also makes a contribution to practice, in that organisational actors will better understand the capabilities and limitations of such systems.

In conclusion, a few short words on this paper’s limitations. First, the length of the paper placed restrictions on the scope and depth of the findings that could be adduced in support of the arguments presented—this also applies to an exposition of the application of its qualitative research design, including coding and data analysis. Second, the paper does not attempt to make statistical generalisations to the general population of IT manufacturing organisations, rather the paper makes its generalisations to the integrated institutional and organisational theory proposed herein. Third, while the Green IS studied might have implications for other types adopted to support environmental sustainability in different contexts, it must be remembered that the above explanatory propositions are limited in scope and may not apply to other Green IS. Finally, any potential limitations in terms of the study’s rigor should be considered alongside its relevance to practice.
