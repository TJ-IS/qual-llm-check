# An interorganizational perspective on the use of electronically-enabled supply chains

Yuliang Yao <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Jonathan Palmer <sup>b,1</sup>, Martin Dresner <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business and Economics, Lehigh University, Bethlehem, PA 18015, United States <sup>b</sup> College of Business, College of William and Mary, Williamsburg, VA 23185, United States <sup>c</sup> Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, MD 20742, United States

Received 2 February 2006; received in revised form 3 January 2007; accepted 22 January 2007 Available online 27 January 2007

## Abstract

Electronically-enabled supply chains (ESCs) offer the potential for reduced costs and higher sales. Using data from a survey of 183 managers in the food industry, we estimate a series of structural models to test the influence of supply chain position and other key variables on the perceived benefits derived from ESC use and on ESC use itself. Major findings include the following: First, top management support and external influences are both important determinants of ESC use in the food industry. Second, perceived benefits to customers, perceived benefits to suppliers, and perceived internally focused benefits, are all found to influence positively ESC use. Third, distributors are more likely to perceive greater customer benefits from ESC use than are manufacturers and retailers. This perception of greater benefits from ESCs may lead distributors to implement these technologies, thus, potentially, helping distributors forestall direct manufacturer-to-retailer sales. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Electronically-enabled supply chains; Interorganizational information systems; Food industry

## 1. Introduction

The emergence of electronically-enabled supply chains (ESC) has changed both the quantity and the velocity of information flowing among supply chain partners. ESCs utilize information technologies, including electronic data interchange (EDI) and the internet, to enable and enhance supply chain processes, creating efficiencies in the flow of materials and goods (e.g., [31,40,53]). These information technologies offer the potential for reduced costs and increasing sales for all members of the supply chain. However, ESCs may also increase the risk to intermediaries, such as distributors, as ESCs can facilitate information flows directly between manufacturers and retailers [18]. Electronically-enabled supply chains have the potential to improve organizational and supply chain-wide performance by increasing transaction efficiencies and coordination effectiveness.

Firms must evaluate potential costs and benefits when deciding to use information technology and other technological innovations. In the context of interorganizational systems such as those used among partners in a supply chain, individual firms must also consider potential benefits to, and pressure from, supply chain partners in evaluating new technologies and processes. Previous research has identified external pressure, perceived benefits, and “readiness” as key factors motivating adoption and use of EDI [10,11,47].

The research reported in this paper builds on the foundation of earlier work on technology adoption and use at the organizational level by examining both organizational and supply chain motivations behind the adoption and use of ESCs. Using data from a survey of managers in the food industry, we make the following contributions: First, we determine how a firm's position in the food industry supply chain may influence the use of ESCs. As noted above, ESCs may increase the use of direct manufacturer-to-retailer sales. However, if intermediaries, themselves, are invested in ESC technologies, this risk may be reduced [17]. Second, we differentiate between internal benefits from ESCs, benefits to customers, and benefits to suppliers, and construct models to show how each of these classes of benefits may affect ESC use. In providing this breakdown in benefits, we address an unanswered research question in Hill and Scudder [25]; that is, whether it is customer or supplier characteristics that most affect the adoption of ESCs. Third, we examine the motivations behind the use of ESCs, including influences from other firms in the supply chain and from top management support. Finally, our work extends the EDI and interorganizational literature by examining ESC use at three levels of the supply chain: manufacturer, distributor, and retailer.

The major findings from our paper include the following: First, perceived benefits to customers (e.g. reduced inventory levels at customers), perceived benefits to suppliers (e.g., reduced inventory levels at suppliers), and perceived internally focused benefits (e.g., increased data accuracy) are all found to positively influence ESC use. Second, distributors are more likely to perceive greater customer benefits from ESC use than are manufacturers or retail procurement specialists. The perception of greater benefits from ESCs may lead distributors to implement these technologies, thus, potentially, helping distributors forestall or avoid direct manufacturer-to-retailer sales. Finally, both top management support and external influences are important determinants of ESC use in the food industry. Both of these variables positively affect the perceived benefits from ESC use and indirectly the actual use of ESCs.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews the literature and develops our theoretical model and hypotheses. Section 3 describes the research methodology used and Section 4 our findings. Finally, Section 5 presents discussions and conclusions from our results, including limitations and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses development

This research focuses on the motivations to adopt and use electronically-enabled supply chains. The IS literature is replete with work on the adoption of technology by users (e.g., [15,30,59,60]). This literature suggests a strong association between the usefulness, ease of use, and perceived user benefits of ESCs, and the intention to adopt and use technology. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) has often been examined at the level of individual adoption and use of technology. Our work focuses on use at the interorganizational level.

A related area to IT adoption is the literature on IT and performance. Much of the prior research on the performance impact of IT adoption and use examines economic benefits for the individual firm in the supply chain [11,27,28,40,51]. Other papers look at manufacturers and suppliers [5], or insurance carriers and agents [42], but again concentrate on the individual firm's benefits. This paper examines how benefits to other firms (i.e., supply chain partners) may affect technology adoption and use by a focal firm.

Another stream of research examines the factors that influence the adoption and use of interorganizational systems, such as EDI. This research focuses on the individual firm's capabilities [11,22,27,29,49,39,43], position [44] and structure [26,48]. Our work extends this examination to internet-enabled interorganizational information systems (IOIS). Internet-enabled IOISs are different from traditional EDI in terms of structure, technology, usage, and information exchange capabilities [10,46,65]. However, we recognize that traditional EDI may also be used to facilitate open, multilateral electronic markets, a form of IOIS typically enabled by the Internet [35,50].

Transaction cost economics (TCE) provides the basic proposition that interorganizational information systems, including ESCs, will overcome information asymmetries by making information more visible to market participants, leading to relationships less subject to opportunistic behavior [64]. Benefits from the use of IOIS include transaction cost savings (e.g., [63]). The underlying concept for electronic transactions predicts information technologies should allow closer integration of adjacent steps in the supply chain [36]. The electronic integration effect provides tighter coupling of the processes that create and use information. Realtime and accurate demand information, together with visualization of inventory throughout the distribution process, helps firms reduce process and cycle time variance, data errors, and safety stock and achieve better channel coordination [23]. Research suggests that information sharing activities between firms in a supply chain can mitigate the information distortion, known as the bullwhip effect [7,32]. Bakos [2, 3], studying both electronic markets and electronic hierarchies, indicates that electronic markets reduce a buyer's search costs, promote price competition, and weaken the market power of sellers. Electronic hierarchies lower coordination costs by reducing inventory and monitoring costs.

The use of IOIS has been found to provide both operational and strategic benefits to a firm [34,41]. Information technology impacts the strategic options available by providing connectivity for value-added partnerships, electronic negotiations, and enhanced procedures and practices. Benefits of IOIS adoption and use include strengthened buyer–seller relationships [38,57,58,62], facilitation of purchasing transactions [54], improved transportation practices [13], and the potential for competitive advantage [28]. For example, it is found that EDI use contributes to higher inventory turnover, lower obsolescence costs, and lower transportation costs [40].

By using IOIS, firms may be able to smooth supply and demand. This smoothing has been suggested as a key benefit of Efficient Consumer Response (ECR), a series of electronically-enabled linkages among manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers that generate more efficient replenishment and inventory management based on customer demand information [19]. Continuous Replenishment Programs (CRP), enabled by ESCs, have also provided improved information sharing and demand forecasting, benefiting both manufacturers and retailers [48].

One study that examines the impact of information technology in the specialty retailing industry suggests significant performance improvements for firms using Quick Response technologies, a retail industry IOIS [44]. In the logistics operations at automobile assembly centers, EDI facilitates the implementation of just-in-time (JIT) shipments leading to significantly lower shipment errors [53]. We extend the earlier research on benefits of EDI and IOIS to the examination of how IOIS benefits to suppliers and buyers might affect the adoption and use of an IOIS by a focal firm.

Specific work has been conducted in the food industry examining the use and impact of EDI. One study [12] suggests positive benefits for firms using information technology in support of the supply chain. A later reporting of the results [31] finds that both the initiators and the followers of the information technology adoption receive performance benefits. A research survey of the food industry [54] finds that supply chain coordination enabled by advanced information technology decreases inventory levels, decreases order cycle time, and decreases order cycle variance.

It is clear from the literature that ESCs can provide substantial benefits for firms and that firms will adopt ESCs if they perceive benefits from adoption. Furthermore, the extent of ESC adoption and use should be directly related to the degree of perceived benefits. Therefore our first hypothesis is as follows:

H1. Firms perceiving greater internal benefits from ESCs will have greater use of ESCs.

Benefits from ESC, however, may not flow just to the focal firm, but to its supply chain partners as well. For example, researchers examining the use of EDI at the Campbell Soup Company [31] find that EDI provides benefits in terms of lower inventory levels and reduced stockouts, not only to the EDI “champion”, but also to other firms in the supply chain. Other research results show that a supplier may derive large strategic benefits when its customer initiates a business-to-business (B2B) procurement system and the supplier enhances the system's capabilities [41]. Furthermore, improved information sharing and demand forecasting through ESCs can benefit supply chain partners [7,48]. In a competitive marketplace, a firm perceiving customer benefits from an ESC may feel compelled to adopt the technology in order to maintain market share. Likewise, a firm perceiving supplier benefits from an ESC may feel that it is in the focal firm's best interest to adopt the technology in order to lock-in supply sources or to bargain for lower material costs. These arguments suggest that it may not just be internal benefits that motivate the adoption and use of ESCs, but benefits to supply chain partners as well. Therefore, our next two hypotheses are the following:

H2. Firms perceiving greater customer benefits from ESCs will have greater use of ESCs.

H3. Firms perceiving greater supplier benefits from ESCs will have greater use of ESCs.

A number of factors can affect a firm's perception of benefits derived from the use of ESCs and, indirectly, their use. One such factor may be a firm's position in the supply chain. It is suggested that distributors can reduce their vulnerability to disintermediation by investing in IOIS [42]. To distributors, IOIS investments can provide strategic advantages. In fact, investments in ESCs may be key to avoiding disintermediation and surviving in the “digital economy” [4,17,18]. On the other hand, there is literature that suggests that retailers and manufacturers may generate greater benefits from ESCs than distributors [41]. Given the different perceptions in the literature on the “winners” from ESC use, we pose the following hypothesis:

H4. Distributors will have different perceived benefits from ESCs as compared to manufacturers and retailers.

This approach, in formulating and testing a nondirectional hypothesis, has been used in prior research when the direction of the outcome cannot be predicted a priori, for example in Chen and Hitt [8]. If the hypothesis is not rejected, this may indicate that distributors perceive significantly different benefits from ESCs than do manufacturers and retailers.

Sociopolitical influences, such as pressure and exercised power of other firms in the supply chain, internal need, and top management support, may also affect the perception of benefits to be derived from the use of ESCs [47]. Use of IOIS has been linked to competitive influences and the desire for enhanced competitive advantage [45]. Partner power and trust [24], channel power [61] and external pressure [11] have been identified as antecedents to ESC use among purchasing managers. ESCs can provide additional leverage to existing organizational resources and the opportunity for improved relationships with supply chain partners [56]. Therefore, our fifth hypothesis is as follows:

H5. Firms with greater competitive and external influences will have greater perceived benefits from ESCs.

Prior research has suggested that two key organizational factors are also candidates for influencing the use of ESCs. As noted above, top management support has been found to be a key influence on adoption and use [33,47,49]. Firm size, as well, has also been found to play a key role in adoption [14,38,45,61]. The suggested additional hypotheses on firm size and top management support are therefore, as follows:

H6. Larger firms will have greater perceived benefits from ESCs.

H7. Firms with top management support of ESCs will have greater perceived benefits from ESCs.

Finally, within an industry, there may be more than one market segment. To the extent that firms in the different market segments use alternative production processes (e.g., production flow vs. batch), have different supply chain arrangements (e.g., contract purchases vs. spot market transactions) or use distinct distribution channels (e.g., wholesalers vs. retailer-owned distribution centers), there may be differences in the use of ESCs, or in the perception of their benefits across segments. Therefore, without positing a hypothesis, we propose to control for market segments through the use of dummy variables.

## 3. Research methodology

Responses from a mail survey provide most of the data used in this study. The development of the survey and the sampling procedures followed the guidelines suggested by Dillman [16]. The survey instrument was developed based on concepts from previous studies [10,11,44,47,56] and results from a series of interviews conducted with fifteen executives in the food industry.

Fig. 1. Research design.

An initial draft of the survey was first developed by the authors and then sent to ten industry professionals to pre-test the objectiveness and clarity of the questions. The eight questionnaires returned resulted in changes to the phrasing of three questions. In addition, industry interviews of executives at various levels of the supply chain, including 4 technology providers, 2 manufacturers, 6 distributors, and 3 retailers, were undertaken to broaden the authors' understanding of the food industry supply chain.

The survey sample was derived from the database of subscribers to Food Logistics magazine. Food Logistics provided the researchers with subscriber names, firm addresses, phone numbers, and job titles. The survey instrument was targeted at firms across the entire food industry supply chain, with respondents representing top logistics or general management positions at these firms (i.e., those likely to be knowledgeable of their firm's electronically-enabled supply chain strategy and practices). A stratified random sampling procedure was used to ensure that three levels of the supply chain– manufacturers, distributors, and retailers– were sampled.

A total of 1399 surveys were sent to firms at the three levels of the supply chain; 432 to manufacturers, 523 to distributors or wholesalers, and 444 to retailers or food service operators. Three rounds of mailing were sent with intervals of 3 weeks. One hundred and nine surveys were returned with delivery problems, reducing the effective sample size to 1290. A total of 209 responses were received from the manufacturers, distributors, and retailers for a response rate of 16.2%. Among the returned surveys, 26 were discarded since fewer than 80% of questions were answered, leaving 183 usable surveys.

To address the potential for non-response bias, 20 addresses were randomly selected from the nonrespondents. A set of key questions from the survey was sent to the 20 addresses by express mail, followed by a round of telephone calls. Seven of the surveys were returned due to either undeliverable addresses or because the contacts were no longer with the company. The 13 returned questionnaires were combined with 183 usable surveys to conduct MANOVA tests for nonresponse bias. There was no significant difference between the respondents and non-respondents at the 5% level. As an additional test, answers on a number of key questions from late respondents (surveys received after the third mailing) were compared to answers from earlier respondents to determine if there were any statistical differences. Prior research has found that the profile of late respondents may resemble those of nonrespondents [6,37]. Again, using a MANOVA test, no significant differences (p = .7) were found between the early and late respondents, suggesting that non-response bias was not a problem.

Responses to our initial survey were received from all of the segments in the supply chain, with 16% of the respondents representing manufacturing firms, 42% representing distribution establishments, and 42% representing retailers (retail head office procurement specialists or retail distribution center managers). Size of firms ranged from under \$10 million (54%) to over \$1 billion (7%) in annual sales, reflecting the general makeup of the food industry [55]. Table 1 presents a detailed description of the respondents. Respondents represented managers in top positions at target firms, with 129 (70.5%) indicating that they worked in corporate management, 32 (17.5%) stating that their position was in operating management, 14 (7.7%) stating they worked in logistics management, and 8 (4.4%) whose position was in purchasing management.

Multiple items are used to measure the research variables. Most items are measured using a five point Likert scale with 1 indicating “strongly disagree” and 5 indicating “strongly agree”, while two categorical items are used to determine firm size and position in supply chain. Given that firms may conduct business using electronic linkages with both their upstream and downstream partners, the use of ESCs is operationalized in two areas: use with customers and use with suppliers. Total Use is measured as a sum of the scores for each of the activities (0 = no use, 1 = light use, 2 = moderate use, 3 = heavy use) (see Appendix).

The perceived benefits are of three types: internal, customer, and supplier, and include transaction cost savings, inventory reductions, pricing optimization, order processing improvements, and improved customer services [10,56]. ## 4. Findings

In order to test our hypotheses, a series of structural equation models were estimated. Our empirical models were derived from our hypothesized theoretical model (Fig. 1). This model suggests that perceived benefits act as a mediating variable between the other antecedents of ESC use and ESC use, itself. The model contains 3 manifest or observable variables (Firm Size, Industry Segment, and Position in Chain) and 4 latent variables (Use of ESC, Perceived Benefits, Top Management Support, and External and Competitive Influence).

Confirmatory factor analysis was used to assess the unidimensionality, reliability, and validity of the constructed variables. The statistical package, SAS, was used for this purpose. Confirmatory factor analysis was first performed for the individual latent variables to assess their unidimensionality [21]. Results show that each of the latent variables demonstrates an acceptable fit (as assessed by GFI, AGFI, the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees of freedom ratio, NFI, and RMSEA) suggesting that the indicators form single, underlying constructs. In addition, all of the four latent variables were constructed as reflective, rather than formative, indicators [9]. As reflective variables one would expect, for example, that a decline in the total use of ESC (one of our latent variables) would be reflected in a decline in the use of

ESCs with suppliers and customers. The measurement model, which includes all of the latent variables, demonstrates an acceptable fit. GFI, AGFI, and NFI are all at or above the recommended minimum levels (.9 for GFI and NFI; .8 for AGFI) at .93, .89, and .93, respectively. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ to degrees of freedom ratio and RMSEA are below their recommended maximum levels of 3 and .08 (respectively) at 1.66 and .064.

Table 2 presents the results of the confirmatory factor analysis and the ensuing constructs. Use of ESC and Perceived Benefits (Internal) are second order factors. Perceived Benefits (For Customer), Perceived Benefits (For Supplier), External and Competitive Influence, and Top Management Support are first order factors. All second order factor loadings are above .8 and significant at the .01 level, indicating that the model has convergent validity [1]. First order factor loadings are all above .5 and significant at the 1% level (see Appendix). The composite reliability of the constructs also is acceptable with perceived internal benefits (.88), perceived benefits for customers (.92), perceived benefits for suppliers (.82), external and competitive influence (.82), top management support (.84), and ESC use (.87), all displaying composite reliability in excess of the .70 minimum [20,21]. Confidence interval tests were performed to assess discriminant validity among the constructs [1]. None of the correlation coefficients between the constructs are within two standard deviations of 1.0 (which would indicate positive correlation) or −1.0 (which would indicate negative correlation), thus providing evidence of discriminant validity among the constructs. In summary, the constructs all appear to exhibit acceptable measures of unidimensionality, validity and reliability.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

The purpose of this paper is to determine the motivation for the use of ESCs; to determine whether firms that are primarily intermediaries in the supply chain (i.e., distributors) are motivated to use ESCs at the same rate as retailers and manufacturers, and to determine how customer, supplier, and internal benefits influence ESC use. The research uses a sample obtained from managers in the food industry.

Our work defines a set of factors that exhibit strong construct validity and reliability for internal, supplier, and customer benefits. The empirical models focus on interorganizational reasons for technology use, and the results suggest that there are clearly definable factors that benefit the firm, its customers, and its suppliers supporting ESC use.

The major findings from our paper include the following: First, position in chain is found to influence ESC use through the provision of perceived customer benefits; that is, distributors (as opposed to retailers and manufacturers) perceive greater benefits to their customers from the use of ESCs and this is more likely to lead to ESC use by distributors. Distributors in the food industry are often key components in the supply chain, especially in the food services industry, where they supply restaurants, delis, and other outlets with shortlived, processed food products. To the extent that distributors invest in ESCs, they may be able to reduce the chances for disintermediation.

Second, customer benefits, supplier benefits, and internal benefits, are all found to influence, positively and significantly, ESC use. These results imply that firms may take into account benefits to be realized by supply chain partners when determining whether to invest in and employ ESCs. This represents a significant expansion of the definition of benefits from previous work [10,44,56] and suggests a much richer set of benefits can be analyzed in the decision to adopt and use technology in an interorganizational context.

Firms may adopt an ESC that is beneficial to its customers or suppliers for two reasons. First, the firm may face a mandate to do so. This approach is discussed in Barua and Lee [5] where a “stick” approach (reduction in supplier volume) that penalizes a supplier for not joining an EDI system is introduced. Unless the focal firm adopts an EDI system that benefits its customer, it loses business. A second reason for adopting an ESC that benefits a supply chain partner is that the adoption may be mutually beneficial; that is, the ESC results in benefits to both the focal firm and its supplier or customer. This approach has also been discussed in Barua and Lee [5], for example. IT use in supply chains may lead to closer buyer–supplier relationships ultimately resulting in improved performance [57]. It has been found that both suppliers and buyers can benefit from the adoption of electronic B2B procurement, for example, although the size of the benefits to a particular adopter depends on who initiates and/or who enhances the system [41]. There may also be redistribution of the benefits between supply chain partners in the form of price discounts, side payments, or other redistributive procedures so that all parties can achieve positive results from the ESC implementation.

Finally, we found both top management support and external influences to be important determinants of ESC use in the food industry. While this finding is consistent with earlier work (e.g., [11]), our results suggest that these influences work across the three echelons of the supply chain and that both these variables positively affect the perceived benefits from ESC use and indirectly the actual use of ESCs. Other variables, such as firm size and industry segment, were not found to significantly influence perceived benefits from ESCs or, indirectly, ESC use. The insignificant outcome of firm size may be due to the fact that the majority firms in our sample are relatively small in size. Future research may examine the perceived benefits and the use of ESCs for larger firms (e.g., more than \$5 billion in sales) in order to provide further evidence. Future research may also examine additional antecedents of the perceived benefits for the grocery products and food services segments, respectively, to gain a deeper understanding of differences between the two segments in food industry.

In summary, our findings suggest that managers involved in developing successful electronically-enabled supply chains should examine both internal and external benefits. In addition, managers should anticipate that top management support and external pressure from customers and/or suppliers will play a key role in the use of ESCs.

Our research makes a number of contributions to the literature. The work provides an analysis of electronically-enabled supply chains that incorporates benefits to suppliers and customers as well as internal benefits from using ESCs. The model articulates multiple influences on electronically-enabled supply chains that go beyond those studied in earlier research, much of which has focused only on EDI. The model also more fully articulates the different potential benefits that may arise from ESC use. In addition, this research contributes to the understanding of supply chain disintermediation. From a theoretical perspective, the research adds the perspective of the supply chain research to the IS literature. The research identifies position in chain as an important new variable that can influence perceived benefits, and offers insights on how competitive and external influences may affect the perceived benefits from ESC use.

The limitations of the research reflect concerns, in general, regarding a cross-sectional survey of a specific industry. The single respondents from each firm represent key personnel, but their responses may not fully reflect the organization's direction in regard to ESC use. In addition, the single respondents are a potential source of common method variance. The cross-sectional survey method limits the ability to examine the use of technologies and organizational developments over time. The cross-sectional data also limits the ability to infer causal relationships between the variables. The use of the food industry as a basis for analysis may limit the generalizability of the survey findings, but does provide evidence for industries featuring time-sensitive products with frequent delivery and fulfillment.

A possible threat to the data validity is the timing of the collection (i.e., 2000), which was a period when “web-hype” occurred. Data collected during the period may not be able to be re-created, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. However, Internet penetration indicated from our sample (about 40% of all firms in the sample use at least one of the ESC activities) appears reasonable for the data collection period, suggesting limited impact from the “web-hype”.

Our findings suggest that the perception of customer benefits and ESC use is more closely linked for distributors than for manufacturers and retailers. Future work may focus further on how position in the supply chain affects the adoption and use of ESC. In order to overcome the limitations of cross-sectional research, it would be beneficial to examine ESC benefits and use over time. This approach would require re-surveying respondents to determine how changes in perceived benefits affect ESC use. Finally, a wider analysis of managers across industry groups would increase the generalizability of the results.
