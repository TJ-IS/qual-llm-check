# READING BEHAVIOR ON INTRA-ORGANIZATIONAL BLOGGINGSYSTEMS: GROUP-LEVEL ANALYSIS THROUGH THE LENS OFSOCIAL CAPITAL THEORY

## ABSTRACT

Aiming to explore the factors that potentially determine the continued reading behavior of users on intra-organizational blogging systems, this paper proposes a group-level model consisting of constructs regarding structural, relational, and cognitive social capital. The model is empirically tested with system record data collected from a large telecommunications company. Testing results illustrate that social capital factors do have significant impacts on the continued reading behavior, but that part of their influence is subject to the moderation effects of workgroup characteristics. The findings of this study contribute to the literature on intra-organizational social networking, and contain helpful managerial implications for related practice.

Keywords: intra-organizational blogs, reading behavior, social capital theory, social network applications.

## INTRODUCTION

Social network applications, such as blogs, wikis, photo and video sharing communities, have enjoyed great popularity in recent years. New applications and services that facilitate collective action and social interaction online through the rich exchange of multimedia information and evolution of aggregate knowledge are considered a force shaping the future of the Internet, with potential impacts on the business environments, societies, and daily lives of people all around the world (Parameswaran & Whinston, 2007b). Empirical research also reveals that social networking applications may have considerable impacts on the organizational efficiency and business performance of contemporary companies (Aggarwal, Gopal, Sankaranarayanan, & Singh, 2012).

Social networking tools have also been introduced into corporate contexts (Bente & Karla, 2009), creating new intra-organizational information system platforms that are sometimes described as “Enterprise 2.0” (McAfee, 2006). Social network applications, typically corporate internal blogging systems, have been established in companies such as Microsoft (Efimova & Grudin, 2007), IBM (Jackson, Yates, & Orlikowski, 2007), HP, Dell and Amazon (Lee, Hwang, & Hong-Hee, 2006). Corporate internal blogging systems as well as other internal enterprise social medial platforms have also aroused increasing interest in academic research arena (Lockwood & Dennis, 2008; Yardi, Golder, & Brzozowski, 2009; Leonardi, P. M., Huysman M. & Steinfield C., 2013). Research efforts have addressed issues regarding the behaviors of employees on the new platforms, especially the adoption and use of intra-organizational blogging systems (Efimova & Grudin, 2007; Y. Huang, Singh, & Ghose, 2010; Leidner, Koch, & Gonzalez, 2010; Raeth & Smolnik, 2010; Singh, P. V., Sahoo, N., & Mukhopadhyay, T., 2014).

The ever increasing population of blog readers presents a promising and important research field (E. Baumer, Sueyoshi, & Tomlinson, 2008). Regarding an intra-organizational blogosphere, understanding users’ reading behavior is crucial in order to promote posting (Singh, Sahoo, & Mukhopadhyay, 2010), since blog readers play an indispensable role in building an active blog community (Yardi et al., 2009). As a social networking platform, a blogosphere would lose its sociality and could not be sustainable without the active participation of readers. In other words, a blogosphere is a social practice having a duality structure, where the blogs create the audience and the audience enables the blogs, as has been

#

observed in previous studies (Nardi, Schiano, & Gumbrecht, 2004). Such interactions between reading and writing also distinguish intra-organizational blogging systems from traditional communication tools such e-mail or instant messaging, and are unique characteristics of “Enterprise 2.0”. Existing literature has largely focused on the writing behavior of users on blogging platforms, whereas relatively scarce work has examined blog reading (E. P. S. Baumer, Sueyoshi, & Tomlinson, 2011). Generally, writing and reading are two essential aspects of user participation on blogging platforms, comparable to “producing and consuming” in social-ecognomic systems (E. P. S. Baumer et al., 2011; Y. Huang et al., 2010). In this sense, research issues regarding blog reading are at least as worthy of investigation as those regarding blog writing.

Furthermore, a company also needs to understand the reading behavior of its employees in order to facilitate effective knowledge sharing (Singh et al., 2010). With active interactions between readers and writers, an enterprise blogosphere can effectively support the exchange of ideas, and therefore provide a venue for improved transfer, absorption and reproduction of knowledge. Such effects of an intra-organizational blogosphere are especially important when readers and writers belong to different units in the formal organizational structure of the company, because organizations with more effective knowledge transfer abilities between units are usually more productive and innovative (Argote, Ingram, Levine, & Moreland, 2000).

Inspired by such insights, this paper aims to explore the question of why employees from one organizational unit would continue to read the blogs by employees from other units on an intra-organizational blogosphere, from the angle of social capital. Here, we focus our research on the group-level analysis due to its increasing practical and academic importance. In contemporary organization, individuals are first nested in work groups, which in turn form

#

larger units (Hitt, Beamish, Jackson, & Mathieu, 2007). Groups or teams have become one of the most common types of organization design (Wu, Hsu, & Yeh, 2007). As fundamental organizational building blocks, small groups or teams are deemed by practitioners and academics as increasingly important factors to help achieve organizational success (Henttonen, 2010). Since blogging is a new form of social activity (Nardi et al., 2004), we base our research on the Social Capital Theory, in order to develop a conceptual model that captures the most important features of blog usage and reveal the social factors that potentially drive readers to continue reading in the blogosphere.

Using system record data collected from a large telecommunications company, we empirically test the proposed model. The results illustrate that the model effectively explains the continued reading behavior at the group level. We believe that the findings of this research will contribute to the emerging literature on intra-organizational social networking, as well as provide insights helpful to the practice of Enterprise 2.0.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first present a review of the existing literature related to blog reading and Social Capital Theory. Second, we discuss the theoretical foundation and propose our research model and related hypotheses. Third, we describe the research method for empirically testing the proposed model and present the results. Fourth, we provide discussions on the statistical analyses and results. Finally, we conclude the paper by summarizing the contributions, managerial implications, and limitations of our research.

## RELATED LITERATURE

## Reading Behavior in Blogospheres

It has generally been recognized that blog reading is the key factor in the economics of attention in an intra-organizational blogosphere, playing an important role in maintaining a

#

healthy internal corporate blogging community (Yardi et al., 2009). Therefore, in the past several years, participants’ blog reading behavior has gradually aroused interest in IS field. Some research efforts have attempted to explore the motivations and consequences of blog reading. One typical study in this stream postulates four dimensions of reading motives, namely, affective exchange, information search, entertainment, and bandwagon effect (L. S. Huang, Chou, & Lin, 2008). Furthermore, it was argued that these motives would impact the users’ behavior in three major areas - opinion acceptance, interaction intentions and word-of-mouth (WOM) intentions (L. S. Huang et al., 2008). This stream also includes several studies which extend traditional user adoption models, such as TRA and TTF, to explain the intra-organizational blogging participation of employees which may include reading behavior (e.g., Hsu & Lin, 2008).

Other scholars have examined blog reading from a “pattern analysis” viewpoint, which is aimed at capturing the dynamical, structural or network characteristics of employee blog readership in the context of the enterprise blogosphere using mathematical models. Singh et al. (2014) develop a dynamic mathematical model. The research finds that there exists a kind of variety-seeking behavior among employees in reading blogs, which determines the tendency of an individual reader to switch between a set of topics. They also investigate how the textual characteristics of blogs affect readers’ attraction and retention. Huang et al. (2010) propose a dynamic structural framework utilizing hidden Markow model (HMM). They contend that in the context of enterprise blogosphere, there is strong competition among employees in attracting readership and significant spillover effect is detected on the readership of work posts from the creation of leisure posts. The “regular reading” relationships in intra-organizational blogospheres are also examined from a social network perspective, and a decision tree model based on these relationships is proposed to predict the

#

regular reading behavior (Furukawa, Ishizuka, Matsuo, Ohmukai, & Uchiyama, 2007).

Moreover, the macro features and results of blog reading behavior also receive some attention One study of users’ attitudes and usage patterns on intra-organizational blogging systems discusses the issues of “attention allocation” for blog reading and “feedback mechanisms” to facilitate reciprocal attention (Yardi et al., 2009). Another study sheds light on the “reading considered “a habit” and the problems of “information overload” and “synchronicity” (E. Baumer et al., 2008). Baumer et al. (2011) further investigate readers’ contributions to blogs considering factors such as political ideology, political participation and reading habits.

Various research methods have been employed in related work. For instance, social network analysis techniques are adopted in a study based on data collected from Doblog, a Japanese weblog hosting service (Furukawa et al., 2007). Other relevant methods include questionnaire surveys (Hsu & Lin, 2008; L. S. Huang et al., 2008; Shang, Li, Wu, & Hou, 2011), qualitative and ethnographic investigations (E. Baumer et al., 2008), archival analysis based on blog server log files and interviews (Yardi et al., 2009), and mathematical modeling based on longitudinal datasets from intra-organizational blogging systems (Singh et al., 2010).

Generally speaking, research on blog reading behavior is still rare in the existing literature compared to research on blog writing behavior, especially in the intra-organizational context. One possible reason for this phenomenon is that “blog reading, unlike publishing on blogs or commenting on blogs, is usually invisible to external observers” (Singh et al., 2010). But thanks to the reading behavior data recorded by the intra-organizational blogging system, we can now investigated more on this important usage behavior, providing some new insight in this research arena. Moreover, existing studies concern more on individual-level analysis, whereas much less work is now avaible on the group-level research.

#

Another research gap is that most of existing studies are either descriptive oriented or analytical techniques focused, a theoretical framework is still rare for understanding why people engage in this type of regular reading behavior. It is both academically and practically important to find the reasons for conducting usage behavior and theoretically explain the underlying mechanism. Our research tries to fill this gap by proposing a model based on social capital theory and empirically validate the explanation power.

## Social Capital Theory

Blogging is a social activity and a collaborative process of co-creation in which both bloggers and readers engage (E. P. S. Baumer et al., 2011). The characteristic of sociality in blogospheres has been widely recognized and discussed by scholars and numerous studies have examined weblogs and in particular their social aspects (Furukawa et al., 2007). To understand the sociality of blogging systems, researchers often consider them as a typical form of “social computing” (IP & Wagner, 2008), and techniques of “social network analysis” (SNA) have enjoyed popularity as powerful tools for exploring the social characteristics of blogospheres (Lento, Welser, Gu, & Smith, 2006; Teng, Pai, & Chen, 2009). In considering blogging as a “social activity” (Nardi et al., 2004), social relations (sometimes also called social ties) are treated as a structural aspect of blogging practices (Schmidt, 2007). Furthermore, as social relations exist on the blogosphere, “social capital” is correspondingly created through blogging practices. Having its foundation in the academic realm of sociology, social capital is typically defined as “resources embedded in a social structure that are accessed and/or mobilized in purposive action” (N. Lin, 2001; McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005), or “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). On a blogosphere, the practices of reading, citing and

commenting enable the formation of relationship networks, which simultaneously shape the social structures of the blogosphere. As a consequence, social capital is possessed by participating units on the blogging platform at various levels, including the individual level, the group level, and the organization level.

The concept of social capital was originally developed to describe the useful relational resources in the community of social organizations (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998). It was later introduced into the research area related to information and knowledge management. Many scholars have employed the social capital theory as the theoretical foundation for investigating various topics, such as knowledge contribution in electronic networks (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005), motivations for knowledge sharing in virtual communities (Chang & Chuang, 2011; Chiu, Hsu, & Wang, 2006), employees’ tacit knowledge sharing (Yang & Farn, 2009), usage of communication technologies (C.-P. Lin, 2011), knowledge integration in organizations (Robert, Dennis, & Ahuja, 2008), information diffusion through emails (C. C. Huang, Lin, & Lin, 2009), and transfer of knowledge between network members (Chow & Chan, 2008; He, Qiao, & Wei, 2009; Inkpen & Tsang, 2005).

In terms of Enterprise 2.0 applications, the perspective of social capital has been adopted by some researchers (Fulk & Yuan, 2013; Leonardi, Huysman, & Steinfield, 2013). Particularly, blog-related behaviors have also been discussed from the angle of Social Capital Theory in the existing literature. It is stated that social capital is one resource provided by “ego-centered networks” (a type of blog-based networks) (Schmidt, 2007). Drawing upon this point of view, later work investigates how published information can create value and social capital for bloggers (da Cunha Recuero, 2008). A comprehensive review on social computing research argues that social capital theory has a direct relationship to the community aspects of and motivation for participation in social computing, and is a major theory that may be brought to

#

bear on social computing research (Parameswaran & Whinston, 2007a).

Theoretically, social capital is not a one-dimensional concept (Chiu et al., 2006). In this regard, one of the most widely cited frameworks for examining social capital is the conceptual model proposed by Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), which focuses on the combining and exchanging intellectual capital. The model is considered as “having integrated many of the various facets in previous work” (Bolino, Turnley, & Bloodgood, 2002), as it “focuses on group level social capital factors” (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005) and “is ideal for examining intra-organizational social capital” (Bolino et al., 2002). In this case, we adopt Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998)’s conceptual model as our research’s theoretical foundation. According to this model, social capital includes three dimensions, namely, structural capital, relational capital and cognitive capital (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998).

Structural social capital refers to the “overall pattern of connections between actors” and includes facets such as “network configuration, density, connectivity and hierarchy” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). As the overall pattern is emphasized in the definition of structural social capital, typical examples of structural social capital include the degree of centrality of a participating unit in the network (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005), network configuration (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005), and social interaction ties (Chiu et al., 2006).

Relational social capital is defined to represent assets created and leveraged through relationships and has facets such as trust, norms, obligations and expectations (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). As compared with structural capital, relational capital is emphasized to be a “kind of personal relationships people have developed with each other through a history of interactions” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 244). Examples of relational social capital in existing research include trust (Chiu et al., 2006; Inkpen & Tsang, 2005; Yang & Farn, 2009), norms (Chiu et al., 2006) and commitment (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

Cognitive social capital refers to the “resources providing shared representations, interpretations and systems of meaning among parties”. The major facets of this dimension include “shared language, codes and shared narratives” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Examples of cognitive social capital include shared goals and shared culture (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005), shared language (Chiu et al., 2006), and shared vision (Chiu et al., 2006; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998).

In the following part, we will develop our research model and corresponding hypotheses from these three social capital dimensions.

## RESEARCH MODEL

As discussed above, the research of this paper is intended to explore group-level blog reading behavior on intra-organizational platforms by utilizing a social capital theory lens. In a blogosphere, online social relationships may facilitate users to read from others so as to obtain useful knowledge. This is especially true in the case of intra-organizational blogging systems, where readers and writers in the same organization share large amounts of common interests. Relationships are the resources that compose the social capital embedded in the relational networks of blogospheres. In this sense, social capital will enact employees to actively and continuously read others’ blogs. The concept of social capital may be helpful in achieving more in-depth insights on blog reading behavior in the context of intra-organizational blogging systems.

## Structural Social Capital

In an enterprise blogosphere, participating units’ usage behaviors such as reading and commenting help establish the relationships between each other, resulting in a “network structure”. Usage behaviors differ in the effectiveness of communication and recognition among units, so the strengths of the corresponding relationships vary to some extent. Compared with usage behaviors that involve less interaction between units and are not effective in establishing stable and robust relationship, usage behavior like commenting is a relatively ideal indicator of the online relationship between units, since it requires deeper understanding about others’ ideas and the motivation to show “I care what you said”. In this case, we choose the directional commenting relationship as the foundation for the network structure in our research.

Based on the commenting relationship network defined above, different patterns of connections are possessed by various participating units, which can be regarded as a activities” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). From this perspective, the structural social capita embedded in the network of an enterprise blogosphere will facilitate participating units reading behaviors, which play an important role of obtaining information and knowledge.

We adopt two constructs in the dimension of the structural social capital, namely “Centrality Owned” and “Centrality Accessed”. In a social network, centrality represents the degree to which a point is “in the thick of things” (Freeman, 1978). In previous research, centrality has been widely accepted as an important indicator for structural social capital (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998).

In our research, two levels of “centrality” are considered. The first level is “centrality owned by the focal unit”, which is defined as the number of units whose blogs have been commented on by the focal unit. Here, we name the units that are commented on by the focal unit as its “friends”. In light of the impacts of structural social capital, we deduce that the more commenting relationships form, the more “direct” resources a focal unit can access through the friends, thus propelling the exchanging or participating behaviors, i.e. reading blogs in the enterprise blogosphere in our research context.

The second level is “centrality owned by friend units”, which is defined as the total amount of comments received by the friends of the focal unit. To be short, we name this construct as “centrality accessed”. Intuitively, this construct aims to reflect how “popular” or “attractive”

the focal unit’s friends are. Owing abundant relationships and attracting more attentions, popular or attractive friends themselves own much structural social capital, so the focal unit can access more “indirect” resources by establishing relationship with those units. Exiting literature has underlined the importance of “the structural social capital owned by friends”. For example, in a network of advice, individuals with high in-degree centrality are sought after for work-related input; in a network of friendship, those with high in-degree centrality are sought after for companionship (Klein, Lim, Saltz, & Mayer, 2004). Since a friend’s popularity or importance concern not only how many relationships the friend owns, but also how strong these relationships are, we adopt the weighted centrality or Node Strength, which takes the commenting amount into consideration. Again, following the logic of structural social capital’s function, the more “centrality accessed” by the focal unit, the more reading behavior it will conduct.

Therefore, based on the discussion above, we propose the following two hypotheses:

H1a: The Centrality Owned by a unit will positively impact the amount of blogs it reads in an

H1b: The Centrality Accessed by a unit will positively impact the amount of blogs it reads in an intra-organizational blogosphere.

## Relational Social Capital

In the Social Capital Theory, relational social capital mainly provides “access to parties for combining/exchanging intellectual capital” as well as “motivation to combine and exchange” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). In the context of intra-organizational blogging systems, the online relationships will encourage employees to gain access to others or read others’ blogs to be specific. Therefore, we propose that relational social capital will also facilitate a participating unit’s amount of blog reading

The essential focus of relational social capital is the quality and the nature of network connections (Bolino et al., 2002; Robert et al., 2008). Typical constructs discussed in this dimension include reciprocity, liking, trust, norms, identity, obligations, expectations and commitment (Bolino et al., 2002; Chang, H. H., & Chuang, S.-S. (2011);McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). In our research, we follow the fundamental logic of defining relational social capital and include two constructs “Connections Reciprocity” and “Connections Continuity” in this dimension.

Analogical to “Reciprocity”, the construct “Connections Reciprocity” aims to depict the “bilaterality” of relationships owned by the focal unit and its friends during a certain period. Practically, establishing reciprocal connections in the intra-organizational blogging system indicates stronger interaction and effective communication among units, thus resulting in “high-quality” relationships as well as more relational social capital. Existing literature has also adopted reciprocity related constructs in the dimension of relational social capital (Chang & Chuang, 2011; C.-P. Lin, 2011; McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

The other construct “Connections Continuity” aims to reflect “how continuously” the focal unit establishes or maintains relationships with its friends during a certain period. As we know, whether employees continuously keep touch with others affects how well relationship develops and more contacts provide more opportunities to establish affective relationships. Relational social capital just concerns affective relationships between employees in which coworkers like one another, trust one another, and identify with one another (Bolino et al., 2002) In this case, it is import to take into account the continuity of connections between the focal unit and its friends. We propose that contacts with higher continuity in the enterprise blogging system help establish and maintain stable and robust relationship, thus contributing to the quality of employees’ relationships and increasing the relational social capital.

To wrap up, two hypotheses are proposed in the relational social capital dimension:

H2a: The Connections Reciprocity a unit has will positively impact the amount of blogs it reads in an intra-organizational blogosphere.

H2b: The Connections Continuity a unit has will positively impact the amount of blogs it reads in an intra-organizational blogosphere.

## Cognitive Social Capital

According to the Social Capital Theory, cognitive social capital facets positively affect the combination capability, the anticipation of value and the ability to gain access to people and their information (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). In this sense, more cognitive social capital can help the focal unit better understand what others say in blogs and anticipate more valuable gains by knowing them, finally making blog reading more desirable and rewarding

Since meaningful communication requires at least some sharing of context between parties in order for them to realize such an exchange, shared codes/language and shared narratives were proposed to be the constructs reflecting cognitive social capital (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Later studies have proposed a number of new facets so as to cover the “shared aspects” more comprehensively, such as shared vision (Chiu et al., 2006; Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998), shared goals and shared culture (Inkpen & Tsang, 2005). In the context of the intra-organizational blogging system, participating units usually tag their blogs with some “topics”, which summarize their ideas, interests or stories. Shared topics provide the “sharing of context” between the focal unit and its friends, thus facilitating meaningful communication between employees. Therefore, in our research, we use “shared topics” as our construct for the cognitive social capital dimension and propose the following hypothesis:

H3: The Shared Topics a unit has will have a positive impact on the amount of blogs it reads

in an intra-organizational blogosphere.

## Team Characteristics

As our research is focused on the group level, team characteristics are also considered in the research model. Team size is one factor that may influence blog reading. The findings from related studies (Bakker, Leenders, Gabbay, Kratzer, & van Engelen, 2006; Hoegl, Parboteeah, & Munson, 2003) indicate that the number of team members positively affects the amount of blogs it reads. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5: The size of a team will positively impact the amount of blogs it reads in an intra-organizational blogosphere.

Another important team characteristic is the team’s functional type. The work responsibilities of readers should be taken into consideration in order to better understand the social practice of blogging (E. Baumer et al., 2008), so we propose that participating units with different work responsibilities may have different blog reading behaviors. In this paper, we classify work types as “customer-service oriented”, indicating the teams that directly interact with customers, and “non-customer-service oriented”, indicating those provide back-end

processing and support. We argue that customer-service teams will be more active in blog reading, since they need to seek knowledge exchange more frequently so as to improve the level of customer service quality. Meanwhile, the higher level of sociality of customer-service teams will also strengthen the influences of social capital on blog reading. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H6: Customer-service teams will read more in an intra-organizational blogosphere than non-customer-service teams.

H7a: The influence of Centrality Owned on blog reading is greater for customer-service teams than non-customer service teams.

H7b: The influence of Centrality Accessed on blog reading is greater for customer-service teams than non-customer service teams.

H8a: The influence of Connections Reciprocity on blog reading is greater for customer-service teams than non-customer service teams.

H8b: The influence of Connections Continuity on blog reading is greater for customer-service teams than non-customer service teams.

H9: The influence of Shared Topics on blog reading is greater for customer-service teams than non-customer service teams.

## RESEARCH METHOD

To empirically validate the model proposed above, we tested it using system record data collected from a large telecommunications company’s intra-organizational blogging platform. In the following, we will present our research methods for variable measurement, data preparation, and statistical analysis.

## Research site

The research site is one of the largest telecommunications service providers in China. With about 40,000 employees, this company provides services for over 90 million customers and earns annual revenue of up to 15 billion USD, accounting for roughly a 1/9 share of China’s mobile service market. The company began to deploy an internal blogging system in 2006. The system is team-oriented, which means members within a work team share the same blog. This kind of “group blog” or “collaboration blog” has been widely used in large companies such as IBM (Lee et al., 2006). By the year 2013, a total of 4,000 active group blogs had been established on the platform, generating 4,000 articles plus about 10,000 comments per day.

## Centrality Owned and Centrality Accessed

A number of measures, such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, have been developed for the construct of centrality (Borgatti, 2005). In our research, the construct Centrality Owned reflects how many friends the focal unit gains access to, thus the corresponding variable $C E N \_ O W N _ { i }$ should be measured by the focal unit i’s out-degree centrality. In previous studies, the total number of ties linked to a focal node is also used as the measurement of centrality, which serves as a construct for structural social capital (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005). $C E N \_ O W N _ { i }$ can be calculated as follows:

$$
C E N \_ O W N _ {i} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j}\tag{1}
$$

Meanwhile, the construct Centrality Accessed depicts the “popularity” or “attractiveness” of the focal unit’s friends. Obviously, a popular friend unit will attract a large amount of commenting volume, which will be reflected by the weighted in-degree centrality or in-degree Node Strength by another name. For the focal unit i, if we directly use the sum of the weighted in-degree centrality of all friend units, the effect of friends amount or

$C E N \_ O W N _ { i }$ already defined above is also included, since keeping other factors equal, owning more friends will definitely lead to more Centrality Accessed. To eliminate the interference of friends amount which has been captured by the variable $C E N \_ O W N _ { i }$ , we take the average of the weighted in-degree centrality of all friends as the measurement for Centralities Accessed. Therefore, the corresponding variable $C E N \_ A C E _ { i }$ can be formulated as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} C E N \_ A C E _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \left(\delta_ {i j} \cdot \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {N} W _ {k j}\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j}} \\ C E N \_ A C E _ {i} = 0 \end{array} \quad \left( \begin{array}{l} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} \neq 0 \\ \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} = 0 \end{array} \right)\tag{2}
$$

## Connections Reciprocity and Connections Continuity

Connections Reciprocity, the construct for relational social capital, is defined to reflect the degree of relationships’ “bilaterality” owned by the focal unit and its friends, so we use the number of bilateral relationships as the measurement. Similar to problem when measuring the variable $C E N \_ A C E _ { i }$ , the interference of friends amount also exists if we simply sum up all the bilateral relationships. Therefore, the measurement for the corresponding variable $C N C \_ R E C _ { i }$ is calculated in the average form:

$$
\begin{array}{l} C N C \_ R E C _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \left(\delta_ {i j} \cdot \delta_ {j i}\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j}} \\ C N C \_ R E C _ {i} = 0 \end{array} \quad \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} \neq 0\right) \quad \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} = 0\right)\tag{3}
$$

The construct Connections Continuity reflects the degree to which the focal unit is continuously tied with its friends. Conceptually, continuity is related to “time” and can be measured in different level of “time granularity” such as continuing in the level of day, week, month, year, or even century. To measure continuity, one intuitively method is to count the proportion of periods in which the target event happens. In our research, the target event is whether the commenting relationships exist between the focal unit i and its friends, thus the formula for measuring the corresponding variable \_ CNC CNT in the average form is:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} C N C \_ C N T _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \left(\sum_ {q = 1} ^ {P} \widetilde {\delta} _ {i j q} / P\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j}} & \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} \neq 0\right) \\ C N C \_ C N T _ {i} = 0 & \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} = 0\right) \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

## Shared Topics

As the indicator for cognitive social capital, the construct “shared topics” is used to reflect the degree of “commonality” a focal unit has experienced with other units. Since shared language is the typical construct for cognitive social capital and is most related to our construct of “shared topics”, we carefully consider the “cognition” feature and adopt the logic of measuring “shared language” to develop our measurement of “shared interests”. Three key features, “commonality”, “ego-orientation” and “experience”, are included in this construct. For the feature “commonality”, we use the measurement of shared language as a reference. Traditionally, shared language represents knowledge overlap, and is measured by questionnaire items such as “whether using common terms or jargons” (Chiu et al., 2006). The basic logic here is to use the overlapped items to measure the degree of sharing, thus we use the amount of overlapped topics in blog articles to measure shared interests.

The feature “ego-orientation” implies that, sharing the same “amount” of topics does not necessarily mean the focal unit and its friends have the same “degree” of Shared Topics, in other words, the measurement should capture the cognition of the unit itself and be “asymmetric”. Suppose that two units, unit A and unit B, have certain overlapped topics. The amount of overlapped topics may account for only a small portion of all the blog topics that unit A tags, whereas accounting for a large portion of those that unit B tags. Since many of unit A’s topics are not covered by unit B, it would be difficult for A to communicate with B in these uncovered topics, leading to insufficient cognition to B. On the contrary, it will not be difficult for unit B when communicating with unit A, because A “knows” most of the topics that B tags.

The feature “experience” indicates that the measurement for shared topics should be based on the premise that the focal unit and its friends have developed a relationship on the blogosphere. If the relationship does not exist, the focal unit cannot have “experienced” shared topics with others.

As the construct in the dimension of cognitive social capital, measurement for Shared Topics’ variable $S H A \_ T P C _ { i }$ in the average form should take full consideration of the features discussed above and can be defined as:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} S H A \_ T P C _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \left[ \delta_ {i j} \cdot \left(\sum_ {s = 1} ^ {M} \min \left(T _ {i s} , T _ {j s}\right) / \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {M} T _ {i s}\right) \right]}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j}} & \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} \cdot \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {M} T _ {i s} \neq 0\right) \\ S H A \_ T P C _ {i} = 0 & \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} \delta_ {i j} \cdot \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {M} T _ {i s} = 0\right) \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

In this measurement, we first calculate min $\left( T _ { _ { i s } } , T _ { _ { j s } } \right)$ to satisfy the “commonality” feature. Then we consider the proportion of shared topics to the total topics a focal unit has with its friends $\sum _ { s = 1 } ^ { M } \operatorname* { m i n } \left( T _ { i s } , T _ { j s } \right) \Biggl / \sum _ { s = 1 } ^ { M } T _ { i s }$ to make sure the measurement is “ego-oriented” and “asymmetric”, which accords with our previous discussion. Finally, only the focal unit’s friends, i.e. the unit j with $\delta _ { _ { i j } } = 1$ will be considered, to fulfill the criterion of “experience”. Again, we make the average calculation to eliminate the interference of the amount of friends. The degree of shared interests is defined to zero if the focal unit does not have any friends or does not tag any topics to the blogs.

$\sum _ { s = 1 } ^ { M } \operatorname* { m i n } \left( T _ { i s } , T _ { j s } \right) \Biggl / \sum _ { s = 1 } ^ { M } T _ { i s }$ resembles the formula used to measure the “Niche Overlap” in the discipline of ecology $\sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { M } \omega _ { i k } \mathrm { m i n } \left( x _ { i k } , x _ { j k } \right) \Biggl / \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { M } \omega _ { i k } x _ { i k } \mathrm { ( N o t e : } x _ { i k }$ is the species i’s demand on resource k, $\omega _ { i k }$ is the weighted parameter to reflect how important resource k means to species i ). The niche overlap indicates the level of competition for the same resources among species (Colwell & Futuyma, 1971), which has been adapted into the managerial field to study the inter-organization competition problem (Sohn, 2001). If we regard the topics as resources in the ecology and set the importance of all topics equal (i.e. $\omega _ { i k } = 1 )$ , the measurement of topics overlap should be analogical to that of the niche overlap, thus also support our former deduction on Shared Topics’ measurement.

## Team Size and Work Type

Team size is measured by the number of employees in the unit. The team size for unit i is denoted as \_TEM SIZ . ## Time Windows and Sample Selection

All the independent variables are measured in the time window from 2009/01/01 to 2009/12/31. The dependent variable is measured in the time window from 2010/01/01 to 2010/06/30. Measuring the independent and dependent variables at different time windows can prevent the problem of independent and dependent variables jointly depending on the same message, and therefore allows a stronger claim of causality in the model (McLure-Wasko & Faraj, 2005).

To make sure that the units chosen in our research sample are still “alive”, we require that the qualified unit “reads” other units’ blogs at least once in both time windows defined above. Here, we choose the reading behavior as the criterion because reading is the simplest usage behavior and is most likely to happen if a unit is “alive”. Moreover, as groups are usually defined as “two or more individuals who interact cooperatively and adaptively in pursuit of shared valued objectives” (Cannon-Bowers, Salas, & Converse, 2013), we also require that the qualified unit should have at least 2 members.

In total, from all the 6,050 units in the company, 2,693 units that satisfied our criteria and had no missing values were chosen as research subjects. Among them, 1,482 teams (55%) are customer-service-oriented, and the rest 1,211 (45%) teams are non-customer-service-oriented. The descriptive statistics of these research subjects are shown in Table 1. The correlation matrix is shown in Table 2. Therefore, the social capital between a sample unit and the non-sample units was also considered. In this regard, the sampling did not affect the scale and structure of the overall social network.

Testing Method

We use regression with the ordinary least squares (OLS) method to test the hypotheses. Our dependent variable BLOG\_READ is positive in nature, but using BLOG\_READ directly in the regression may result in a function that possibly produces negative estimation values of the dependent variable. Following the usual way to handle this problem, we make the log transformation of BLOG\_READ. Since the estimated value of log (BLOG\_READ) can be either positive or negative, the function generated by OLS will be reasonable.

Following some common procedures and suggestions for examining the interaction effects in regression models (Aiken & West, 1991; Cindy D. Kam & Robert J. Franzese, 2007; Jaccard, J., and Turrisi, R., 2003), we centered the independent variables involved in the product item, except for the binary variable of work type. Then we tested two regression models, one with the interaction items and the other without. The changes of R square and F value were calculated to reveal the significance of the moderating effects.

Regression Model 1 (without the interaction effects):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {LOG(BLOG\_READ} _ {i}) \square C E N \_ O W N _ {i} + C E N \_ A C E _ {i} + C N C \_ R E C _ {i} + C N C \_ C N T _ {i} + S H A \_ T P C _ {i} + \\ A C E \_ C O N _ {i} + T E M \_ S I Z _ {i} + W O R \_ T Y P _ {i} \end{array}
$$

Regression Model 2 (with the interaction effects)

$$

Based on the Social Capital Theory, we build our research model in order to examine the factors that affect blog reading at the group level. Empirically testing the proposed model yields supports for most of the hypotheses in our research model, as well as meaningful and interesting findings that deepen our understanding of intra-organizational blogging behaviors. In our research model, all constructs of the three types of social capital have significant impacts on blog reading, indicating that they are all important antecedents for explaining group-level blog reading behaviors. In terms of the directions of impacts, all constructs under structural social capital and relational social capital exhibit the same direction as proposed in our hypotheses, while the construct shared topics under the cognitive social capital dimension shows the opposite direction. This finding is very interesting and valuable since it implies that we may have ignored some important aspects when proposing the hypothesis on how shared topics may influence blog reading.

The structural social capital constructs “centrality owned” and “centrality accessed” both have positive effects on the reader units’ blog reading, which is accordant with our hypotheses. What’s worth noticing here is that the influence of “centrality accessed” is significant. Compared with “centrality owned”, which reflects how many friends a focal unit has, “centrality accessed” takes into account how popular the friends are. The significant influence of centrality accessed implies that although establishing more relationships is important in facilitating blog reading, it is equally vital to know more “popular friends” Existing research usually stress more on the “first level” of centrality, but our research has shown that the “second level” of centrality also needs considering when analyzing the impact of structural social capital. Another implication here is that in the intra-organization blogosphere, the “super star” effects, namely the popular units will attract more reading, also exist just like in the public social networks.

The empirical testing results also support the positive influence of “connections reciprocity” and “connections continuity” upon a unit’s blog reading. Some existing studies have reported that reciprocity does not significantly influence the usage behavior (Chang & Chuang, 2011; C.-P. Lin, 2011), but these studies mainly focus on applications in the Internet environment such as instant message software. Our research shows that in an intra-organizational context, users, namely employees, may pay more attention to reciprocity, in order to establish and maintain harmonious relationships at work. It has also been pointed out that compared with using internet blogging system, employees have different expectations when using enterprise blogging systems (Yardi et al., 2009). That is, some employees may hope there are reciprocal reading or writing between each other. Meanwhile, the more “continuous connections” a focal unit has experienced, the more addictive and habitual it will be in the reading behavior, thus boosting the reading amount.

The results regarding the cognitive social capital construct shared interests challenge our previous assumptions. We hold the view that shared interests act as a “sharing context” for communication, but surprisingly they reduce the reading amounts. Two possible reasons may help explain this contradiction. The first is that communication on the blogosphere rarely encounters barriers caused by the lack of shared information (such as interest, knowledge, story, etc). The richness of information on the blogosphere enables employees to obtain various kinds of information, so the reader and writer units can find the “sharing context” easily. The second reason may be that employees like to read from units that are different from themselves. One related factor in reading behavior, “variety seeking”, has been discovered by a recent study indicating that blog readers frequently switch from reading one set of topics to another dynamically (Singh et al., 2014). This implies that employees will pursue more variety during the blog reading process and that too much commonality may discourage their reading.

Accessible Contents are significant in promoting blog reading. This not only evidences the importance of user generated contents (UGC) in the intra-organizational context but also empirically supports the “producing and consuming” analogy stressed by previous studies (E. P. S. Baumer et al., 2011; Y. Huang et al., 2010). The impact of team size is also significant as expected. Larger teams tend to read more on the blogging platforms. This is consistent with previous findings. Regarding the work type factor, our research yields very interesting results as well. Generally, work type can both affect blog reading and moderate the influence of the structural social capital and cognitive social capital, but the interaction between work type and relational social capital is not significant. The casual effect is that the units with customer service oriented work will read more. This can be simply interpreted that those units are more active in blog reading.

#

The moderating effects of work type are relatviely complicated. Non-customer-service teams, such as the units responsible for daily operations, technical support, and finance, are more positively affected by the “centrality owned”, while the customer-service teams receive stronger influence from the “centrality accessed”. As we have discussed previously, the influence of “centrality owned” follows the traditional logic of “the more friends the better”, while the influence of “centrality accessed” stresses “the more popular the better”, indicating the “super star” effect and the social network features. Therefore, it is implied that customer-service units behave more similarly to user groups on the Internet and use the enterprise blogging system “more socially”. This induction is further supported by the moderating effect of work type on shared topics. For customer-service teams, the negative impact of shared topics is strong, showing that they are like Internet users who value their own diversified interests and enjoy variety seeking. Although the non-customer-service teams are also negatively influenced by shared topics, the effect is rather small (the coefficient is only -0.002). These results also imply that units with different work responsibilities utilize intra-organizational social networking platforms to different degrees. Moreover, the non-significant moderating effect of work type on constructs of relational social capital suggests that the nature or quality of relationships developed by two kinds of teams do not differ much, resulting in similar influence on the blog reading behavior.

## MANAGERIAL IMPLICATIONS

The rapid development of social networking platforms has aroused interest as well as confusion among managers. On one hand, these tools can facilitate the intrinsic need for increased communication between employees in today’s complex business world (Bente & Karla, 2009) and potentially increase employees’ job performance (Zhang & Venkatesh, 2013). On the other hand, the balance between work and social uses (Leidner et al., 2010), information security, and cultural barriers (Grossman & McCarthy, 2007) have become significant concerns to managers when companies introduce social networking platforms. Our research results have several useful implications for managers in this information age. Scholars have found that “corporate blogs are not just information transition tools” (Wattal, Racherla, & Mandviwalla, 2010). In our research, we further demonstrate the sociability of the enterprise blog from the view of social capital. The enterprise blog is a social tool rather than a simple communication channel such as telephone or e-mail. Employees who work in a more social manner will make better use of the social aspects of the enterprise blogs. In this case, sociality must be taken into consideration when social networking tools are introduced into the enterprise.

The potential diversification of the organizational culture is worth noticing. The negative influence of shared interests reflects that the enterprise blogs enable employees to seek content that diversifies and arouses their unique interests. This phenomenon, resembling the “Long Tail Effect”, may lead to the diversification of the organizational culture. What has happened on the Internet may replicate itself on enterprise social networking platforms. From Web1.0 to Web2.0, Internet users have shifted from the homogenous “broadcasting” websites to the diversified “social” ones. Their own unique interests are satisfied by the emergence of Web2.0, thus forming the “long tail” culture on the Internet. We believe that a similar long tail culture will also form on intra-organizational social networking platforms.

There may be risk to this kind of culture diversification. When the commonly accepted topics, stories, goals and values are able to attract the most eyeballs, the organizational culture can be cultivated more easily. But when the enterprise blog is introduced, the behavior of seeking variety may cause more trouble for managers to cultivate a unified organizational culture. However, enterprise blogs also provide new opportunities. For managers who hope to shape a more democratic and diversified organizational culture, introducing social networking tools is a good choice. This kind of democratic and diversified organizational culture may facilitate the collision of different ideas, thus encouraging more innovation.

Another notable issue is that the enterprise blogs may introduce potential gaps between different teams. Our research shows that the customer-service teams respond differently than no-customer-service teams to the influence of social capital. These customer-service teams behave more like users on the Internet with more social characteristics, while the other teams behave more traditionally. Gradually, this kind of difference may form gaps between different teams and departments inside the corporation. Those who favor the blog as a social tool will probably develop more sociality oriented logic or ideas while others may still maintain traditional ones. Thus, employees from some teams may have difficulties understanding those from other teams. This is a potential problem for the corporation as a whole.

## CONCLUSION

In our research, we examine how social capital and several other factors can impact a workgroup’s blog reading. A research model based on the social capital theory is proposed and empirically tested. We contribute to the existing literature in several aspects. First, our work is focused on intra-organizational blog reading at the group level, which has not been sufficiently studied in previous research. Although blog reading is a very important behavior that can be considered as the counterpart of blog writing, little research has investigated this particular type of behavior. Meanwhile, existing literature related to blogs focuses mainly at the individual level. Our group level research helps extend the research angles. Second, by employing the social capital theory in our blog reading research, we uncover several findings that bring new insights towards more in-depth understanding of blog reading behavior and contain helpful managerial implications. Third, we contribute to the social capital theory by examining how work type may moderate the influence of social capital. Our research shows that work type has significant moderating effects, which implies that when applying the social capital theory, moderating factors should be considered in order to better understand how social capital influences the other variables in different situations. Moreover, our research utilizes the recorded “objective” data to empirically validate the model. This is a relatively new trend in the IS research and the related measurement may provide some suggestions for future studies.

Ongoing research is aimed at extending our current work to more research sites and incorporating more diversified data sources, such as questionnaire surveys and interviews, so as to obtain a more comprehensive picture for understanding reading behaviors on intra-organizational blogging platforms.
