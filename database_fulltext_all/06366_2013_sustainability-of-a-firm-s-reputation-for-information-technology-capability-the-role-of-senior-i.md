Abst ract : This study investigates the development and sustainability of a firm’s information technology (IT) capability reputation from an IT executive’s standpoint. Building on institutional theory, we argue that IT executives will try to achieve external legitimacy (i.e., project an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders) in the hope that the top management team and board members will reciprocate by elevating the internal legitimacy of IT executives. Firms that develop such a culture of reciprocity with their IT executives are more likely to sustain their IT capability reputation. Econometric results based on panel data for 1,326 large U.S. firms from a wide spectrum of industries over a 13-year period (1997–2009) validate these predictions. More specifically, we find that IT executives with greater structural power (e.g., higher job titles) or IT-related expert power (e.g., IT-related education or experience) are more likely to attract public recognition for their firm’s IT capability. Firms that build such an IT capability reputation are more likely to promote their IT executives, and IT executives who are promoted are more likely to stay longer with their firms. This continuity in IT strategic leadership is positively associated with the firm’s ability to sustain its IT capability reputation. Our findings have important practical implications related to a firm’s IT reputation strategy as well as the motivation and career of IT executives. Firms wanting to develop and sustain their IT capability reputation would do well to foster the creation of a cycle of positive reciprocity with their IT executives. IT executives hoping to increase their power within their firm’s top management team and improve the legitimacy of the firm’s IT organization need to project an image of IT superiority to external stakeholders.

Key w ords and ph rases: external legitimacy, institutional theory, internal legitimacy, IT capability reputation, IT executives, IT strategic leadership, reciprocity, structural power.

Corporat e reput at ion (i.e., the accumulation of public recognition of the quality of a firm’s capabilities and output [79]) is created through an intentional process and is actively managed by firms. Findings from the information technology (IT) strategic leadership research highlight an array of signals (i.e., contributions made by IT executives) that can increase the likelihood of a firm receiving public recognition for its IT capability. For example, IT executives are instrumental in envisioning, aligning, and executing their firm’s IT strategy [16, 30, 84]; promoting innovative and effective use of IT in the firm [6, 56, 57]; and developing IT capabilities [30]. In short, their skills are critical to a firm’s ability to sustain its IT-enabled competitive advantage [26]. Given that (IT capability) reputation is the result of managers’ efforts to persuade external stakeholders of their firms’ (IT capability) excellence [36], and given also that external stakeholders are likely to respond to IT capability-related signals [58], the main objective of this study is to examine the process of building and sustaining an IT capability reputation from an IT executive’s standpoint.

Interestingly, while the contribution of IT executives to a firm’s IT capability reputation is rather explicit among professionals [45, 60], this association is conspicuously absent from the information systems (IS) literature. More importantly, there is no guarantee that IT executives who make such a contribution will be rewarded. Industrywide attitudes regarding the strategic role of IT have fluctuated over the years [86]. Top management teams (TMTs) and board members tend not to pay attention to IT or feel the need to take any actions to support the role of IT in the firm [9, 46]. In many organizations the legitimacy of IT executives has not been fully established [49], and anecdotal evidence corroborates this observation: IT executives have been dismissed or have left their firms at times when they were perceived as being successful at their job [55, 68, 77]. Motivated by the growing interest among executives to build and sustain good corporate reputation [76, 83], this study proposes the following questions: Can IT executives help firms achieve public recognition for their IT capability? Are IT executives likely to be rewarded for their contributions? If firms value this recognition (i.e., are willing to reward their IT executives), does the creation of such an environment of reciprocity contribute to their firm’s ability to sustain its IT capability reputation?

Building on institutional theory, we argue that IT executives will try to achieve external legitimacy (i.e., project an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders) in the hope that firms will respond by elevating the internal legitimacy of the IT organization within the firm by promoting their IT executives. Using panel data for 1,326 large U.S. firms from a wide spectrum of industries over a 13-year period (1997–2009), we examine the association between IT executives and their firm’s IT capability reputation. Estimating a system of linear simultaneous equations to control for endogeneity, we find IT executives with greater structural power (e.g., IT executives with higher job titles) or IT-related expert power (e.g., IT-related education or experience) are more likely to attract public recognition for their firm’s IT capability. Firms that build such an IT capability reputation are more likely to promote their IT executives, and IT executives who are promoted are more likely to stay longer with their firms. This continuity in IT strategic leadership is positively associated with the firm’s ability to sustain its IT capability reputation. Based on this, we conclude that development of a culture of reciprocity between firms and their IT executives leads to sustainable IT capability reputation.

Since organizational legitimacy reflects stakeholder support to the organization, our findings support the argument made by Staw and Epstein [85] that pursuit of (IT) reputation is a vehicle for gaining organizational legitimacy. These findings demonstrate that achieving external legitimacy (IT reputation) leads to internal legitimacy (i.e., reward and appreciation of IT executives) and vice versa. Obviously, this has important practical implications related to a firm’s IT reputation strategy as well as career choices of IT executives.

## Theoretical Foundation and Hypotheses

## Theoretical Foundation and Research Framework

Th e pursuit of good c orporat e reput at ion seems to have become the current mantra of executives [76, 83]. Rationalization of this pursuit can be based on arguments derived economically or institutionally. From an economic standpoint, good corporate reputation has been shown to improve the effectiveness of a firm’s business strategy (e.g., its ability to lower costs or charge a price premium) [25, 29, 36, 37, 79], the attractiveness of its market (e.g., by creating a barrier to entry) [25, 36, 63], and its profitability [80]. Overall, good corporate reputation is a strategic asset with a high degree of causal ambiguity; therefore, it is a source of sustainable competitive advantage [25, 70, 80].

From an institutional standpoint, corporate reputation is a signal to external and internal stakeholders of organizational legitimacy [85, 93]. Once a firm has achieved minimal standards in terms of the quality of its capabilities, executives may continue working for the esteem of external judges such as trade associations, public interest groups, or the business press [85]. For example, IT executives may want their firm to be recognized as among the “best and brightest technology innovators” (InformationWeek 500) or the “most effective IT users” (Computerworld Premier 100); they may seek to have their firm stand out as the one that “demonstrates excellence and achievement in $\mathrm { I T } ^ { \prime \prime }$ (CIO 100) or is the “best place to work in $\mathrm { I T } ^ { \prime \prime }$ (Computerworld 100 Best Places to Work in IT). This paper is based on the premise that certain IT executives will actively pursue this public projection of superior IT capability. Given that (IT capability) reputation is based on stakeholder perception, which is developed over time and from multiple impressions [78, 89], it stands to reason that firms that are successful in projecting an image of superior IT capability over several consecutive years will develop and sustain a positive IT capability reputation.

Understanding that IT capability reputation relies on the projection of a series of positive images to external stakeholders raises the following question: What kind of signals do IT executives need to focus on in order to project images of superior IT capability to external stakeholders? Prior research [58] has used a reverse engineering approach to provide anecdotal support to the argument that the business press seems to be receptive to, among others, the following IT capability-related signals: alignment between business/corporate and IT strategies, IT leadership championing by top executives, the role of IT executives, IT priorities reflected in the IT budget and their execution, and the leveraging of IT to achieve value.

According to the IT strategic leadership literature, the likelihood that a firm will produce such signals is associated with the management power of IT executives (Table 1 summarizes the salient points of this literature). Management power has been defined as the capacity of IT executives to exert their will, and it accrues to top managers who can cope with uncertainty and are uniquely positioned to do so [34]. While there are several sources of management power [34, 35, 64], IS scholars have focused on structural power (formal authority) and expert power (expertise and experience) of IT executives. These attributes have been linked to (1) managerial priorities and perceived effectiveness of IT executives, (2) the role of IT within the firm and perceived effectiveness of IT organization, and (3) financial and market performance of the firm. In this study, we argue that a firm’s IT capability reputation (i.e., the ability of its IT executive to project multiple images of superior IT capability over time) is likely to be linked to that IT executive’s structural power and IT-related expert power.

Projecting an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders is consistent with the career aspirations of IT executives. IT investments are notoriously risky, and there may be a great deal of ambiguity regarding their expected benefits [54]. When board members do not have the IT expertise to evaluate the effectiveness of IT executives, they may rely on signals of external legitimacy as a proxy for the effectiveness of IT executives, rewarding them accordingly [85]. However, this does not constitute a contractual obligation. In other words, there is no certainty that IT executives who achieve external recognition for their firm’s IT capability will be rewarded.

This absence of explicit performance incentives and discretion on employee effort reflects a rational decision, especially when dealing with employees such as IT executives, who are required to multitask and operate in a dynamic environment [32, 33, 53]. Under these conditions, an IT executive who successfully projects an image of superior IT capability may not receive an immediate reward (i.e., a reward above his or her fixed contract payment), but may earn a promotion or reward in the future. However, a firm may be willing to reward an IT executive now in order to increase his or her job satisfaction and job performance in the future. Akerlof [2] describes this norm of reciprocity as a “gift exchange.”

Conditions of incomplete contracts render reciprocity an important part of organizational life [24, 32]. Reciprocity is a new economic theory that complements expectancy theory in the sense that it introduces reciprocity, in addition to self-interest, as a motivation to perform [32, 33]. Reciprocity helps to address practical issues such as timing and form of reward, even when time and form of reward are not explicit [24]. Therefore, consistent with prior literature [32, 33], we speculate that under such conditions of incompletely specified obligations and weak or nonexistent explicit performance incentives, the attitude of IT executives depends on the existence of a cycle of positive reciprocity within the organization. More specifically, we propose the following research framework.

Building on the IT strategic leadership literature, we first argue that the structural and IT-related expert power of IT executives is associated with a greater likelihood that the firm will receive public recognition for the quality of its IT capability. Second, if the TMT and board members value such public recognition, then this will manifest itself in greater job security (lower turnover) and promotion potential for the IT executives. Finally, we argue that if the IT executives value their reward, then they will want to stay with their firm longer. Continuity of successful IT strategic leadership increases the likelihood that the firm will develop and sustain its IT capability reputation. Firms that sustain their reputation are more likely to bestow more rewards and appreciation upon their IT executives. Figure 1 illustrates our research framework and underlines the importance of creating a cycle of positive reciprocity as a condition for sustaining a firm’s IT capability reputation.

## Hypotheses

## IT Executives and Public Recognition of a Firm’s IT Capability

As we have seen in our review of the IT strategic leadership literature, the likelihood of a firm projecting an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders is associated with the structural and IT-related expert power of its IT executives. Structural power is based on an executive’s position in the organizational hierarchy (formal title), and it increases with the number of different titles accrued by him or her [34, 43, 64, 69]. IT executives with greater structural power are more likely to act as entrepreneurs [42], shaping the firm’s organizational mission and vision toward a more strategic use of IT in their capacity as strategist and innovation catalyst [72, 84]. More powerful IT executives are more likely to succeed in promoting their vision among members of the firm’s TMT [18] and in justifying the need for allocation of resources for strategic IT projects [6]. Therefore, IT executives with greater structural power are more likely to achieve internal legitimacy that they can in turn leverage in order to portray an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders.

Hypothesis 1a: Ceteris paribus, IT executives with more structural power are more likely to project an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders (i.e., help their firm achieve public recognition for its IT capability) than IT executives with less structural power.

Expert power is based on the executive’s relevant knowledge, expertise, and experience [34, 64]. Education provides declarative or explicit knowledge. In fact, IT-related explicit knowledge from formal academic work allows IT executives to exhibit IT leadership that better prepares them to cope with complex problems and develop innovative strategic solutions [38, 90, 94]. Since experience is the basis for creating and developing tacit knowledge, IT-related expertise and experience from previous positions form the basis for improved understanding of IT and business problems that, in turn, can help a firm obtain a competitive advantage [10, 11]. Experience in the IT domain indicates a distinctive competence that improves a firm’s ability to achieve competitive technological advantages [11]. By extension, prior employment of IT executives in chief information office (CIO) positions of IT capable firms serves as a signal of the ability of IT executives to implement the firm’s IT strategy [18]. Thus, the accumulated knowledge, expertise, and experience that IT executives bring to their firm materialize in their ability to achieve firm-specific objectives [44, 84]. Given that such IT capability–related signals tend to be valued by the business press [58], we postulate that IT executives with greater expert power will be more likely to portray an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders.

Hypothesis 1b: Ceteris paribus, IT executives with more expert power are more likely to project an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders (i.e., help their firm achieve public recognition for its IT capability) than IT executives with less expert power.

## Public Recognition and Reward of IT Executives

Most organizations will try to signal the alignment between the firm’s internal environment with its external environment in order to gain or improve external legitimacy [93].

On the one hand, if IT executives have been successful in projecting an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders, the TMT and board members will want to raise the internal legitimacy of the IT organization by raising the status of the firm’s IT executives. This is consistent with a general assumption in strategic leadership that appreciation, reward, and the resulting management power of an executive are a reflection of the importance of the resources in the organization that the executives manage [72]. Therefore, if firms value IT reputation, then IT executives who help their firms achieve public recognition for its IT capability are more likely to be retained (lower voluntary or involuntary turnover) and trusted in key decision-making processes (promoted) [16, 71]. On the other hand, IT executives who do not make such a contribution are likely to lose their credibility with the TMT and sometimes their job (higher turnover) [16, 55, 71].

However, academic and anecdotal evidence has produced mixed results regarding the reward of IT executives. The TMT’s and board members’ perceptions regarding the strategic role of IT, captured in news stories reported from 1984 to 2007, has fluctuated over the years [86]. Periods of optimism, when executives see certain “IT fashions” as the key to competitive advantage, alternate with periods of pessimism, when IT is seen as a commodity with no strategic value [86, 93]. This ambiguity means that competing results are likely to emerge. For example, in the case of Tim Stanley, IT executive at Harrah’s (now Caesar’s Entertainment), his efforts to improve the firm’s IT capability reputation were appreciated and he was rewarded.

In an era when far too many CIOs are still struggling to align technology with “the business,” Stanley has already made the two indistinguishable at Harrah’s Entertainment. . . . [Stanley] was hired by Harrah’s Entertainment in 2001 and promoted to CIO in 2003. [45]

However, the recent unceremonious departure of Randy Mott from Hewlett-Packard [77] in spite of a successful record [68] seems to indicate that his contribution to the firm’s IT capability reputation was not appreciated by the firm’s TMT:

Chief Information Officer Randy Mott [is] departing, effective immediately, Hewlett-Packard said. Mott, who joined Hewlett-Packard in 2005 . . .was instrumental in the move . . . to close 84 data centers to save \$1 billion in costs. The company is looking for a new CIO. [77]

Thus, when it comes to whether firms value IT capability–related public recognition— as manifested in the form of reward of (promotion) and signs of appreciation for (lower turnover among) IT executives—we propose the following set of competing hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a: Ceteris paribus, firms that value IT capability–related public recognition will appreciate the contribution of IT executives (or IT executives will feel appreciated).

Hypothesis 2b: Ceteris paribus, firms that value IT capability–related public recognition will reward IT executives for their contribution.

Hypothesis 2a: Ceteris paribus, firms that do not value IT capability–related public recognition will not appreciate the contribution of IT executives (or IT executives will not feel appreciated).

Hypothesis 2b: Ceteris paribus, firms that do not value IT capability–related public recognition will not reward IT executives for their contribution.

## Cycle of Positive Reciprocity and Sustainable IT Reputation

Individuals are motivated because they want to fulfill certain needs such as financial safety and esteem. In fact, if the IT executives of firms that value IT capability reputation feel that their contribution has been properly rewarded and they feel appreciated, they are more likely to be committed and stay longer with their firm [39]. This will ensure continuity in the firm’s IT strategic leadership. A reward will be valued if it satisfies an important extrinsic or intrinsic want or need. Since a promotion (higher title or more titles) is associated with higher compensation [60] and higher structural power [34], it fulfills both the financial and esteem needs of IT executives. Therefore, we postulate that reward and appreciation of IT executives for their contribution to their firm’s ability to achieve public recognition for its IT capability increases the likelihood of continuity in vision and execution of IT strategic leadership:

Hypothesis 3a: Ceteris paribus, firms that receive public recognition for their IT capability and reward their IT executives are more likely to experience a continuity of IT strategic leadership than firms that receive similar recognition but do not reward their IT executives.

A firm’s IT capability image can be created and destroyed relatively quickly. Nevertheless, it takes time to build a good IT capability corporate reputation [40, 80]. A sustainable IT capability reputation is based on stakeholder perceptions, which are developed over time and from multiple images [78, 89]. The observation that sustainable IT capability reputation is a journey rather than a destination underlines the importance of continuity in the firm’s IT strategic leadership and is corroborated by the practices of IT executives such as Timothy Shack at PNC Financial Services Group:

Timothy Shack, in his seventh year as CIO at PNC Financial, also is president and CEO [chief executive officer] of PFPC Worldwide, PNC’s mutual-fund processing subsidiary. . . . [Shack spends] most of his time developing business strategy. . . . [Shack] feels a personal responsibility to provide business-continuity and other services, and to leverage technology across the business. [60]

Given that many firms do not have plans in place to deal with departing IT executives [55], this continuity of a successful IT strategic leadership sets apart firms that develop and sustain their IT capability reputation from firms that develop but do not sustain their reputation. Therefore, we postulate the following:

Hypothesis 3b: Ceteris paribus, firms that have received public recognition for their IT capability (i.e., developed an IT capability reputation) and experience continuity in IT strategic leadership are more likely to sustain their IT capability reputation than firms that have developed a similar reputation but experience discontinuity in IT strategic leadership.

Finally, consistent with our previous argument regarding the value of projecting an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders (H2b), there are two opposing arguments regarding sustainability of IT capability reputation. TMTs and board members that value public recognition associated with their firm’s ability to sustain its IT capability reputation may want to mirror this success in the firm’s internal environment by rewarding their IT executives. But there are two counterarguments explaining why a firm may not want to reward its IT executives for sustaining its IT capability–related reputation. First, the firm may have rewarded the IT executive for the initial success in projecting an image of IT superiority and, therefore, may feel that subsequent recognitions are not as important. Second, the TMT and board members may simply not value either development or sustainability of IT capability reputation. Thus, we propose the following two competing hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3c: Ceteris paribus, firms that value sustainability of their IT capability reputation are more likely to reward their IT executives than firms that develop but do not sustain their IT capability reputation.

or

Hypothesis 3c: Ceteris paribus, firms that do not value sustainability of their IT capability reputation are not more likely to reward their IT executives than firms that develop but do not sustain their IT capability reputation.

Collectively, the hypotheses associated with the third step (H3a–c) establish that a cycle of positive reciprocity can only be developed over time and in the course of interaction between IT executives and firms that value IT capability reputation.

## Methodology

## Data Set

To t est th e proposed h ypoth eses, we used all publicly traded firms that have appeared at least once in the list of InformationWeek 500 (IW500) from 1997 to 2009.<sup>1</sup> To identify IT executives in our sample firms, we searched proxy statements such as 10-Ks and DEF-14A from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. In order to enhance the completeness and accuracy of our data, we also conducted a subsequent manual review of each IT executive’s biographical information via LexisNexis, as well as 12 online information sources.<sup>2</sup> We used accounting and financial data from Compustat.

## Variables

IT Capability Reputation

We treat the annual IW500 list as a proxy for firms that have been able to project an image of superior IT capability to external stakeholders (i.e., achieve public recognition) for that year $( R I T C _ { i t } = 1 )$ . Given that IT reputation is likely to be developed over time and from multiple images [78, 89], we use the evolution of a firm’s recognition in IW500 over four-year rolling windows $( \mathrm { e . g . } , 1 9 9 7 - 2 0 0 0 , 1 9 9 8 - 2 0 0 1 , . . . , 2 0 0 6 - 9 )$ to classify firms in terms of their IT capability reputation. In each window we classify a firm as one that has developed and sustained its IT reputation $( S Y S _ { i t } )$ if it has been recognized in IW500 all years within the four-year rolling window. We classify a firm as one that has achieved but not sustained its IT capability reputation $( O C C _ { i t } )$ if the firm has appeared fewer than four times in IW500 within the four-year rolling window. Finally, we classify a firm as having no IT capability reputation $( N O N _ { i t } )$ if it has not been recognized in any of the four years. The choice of four ${ \mathrm { y e a r s } } ^ { 3 }$ is based on prior research, which has shown that firms that have been able to project such an image of superior IT capability to business press over four consecutive years are more likely to sustain this image in the future due to path dependence [58] and are more likely to recover from losses occurring during an economic downturn [19].

## Structural Power

In the IT strategic leadership literature, structural power has been measured by such proxies as IT executive membership in the firm’s TMT as well as firm reporting structure (see Table 1). Despite the plethora of titles associated with IT executives, there seems to be a consensus among researchers of using the blanket title $\mathrm { ^ { 6 } C I O ^ { 9 } }$ to describe all IT executives [6, 7, 42, 51]. For example, Banker et al. state that the “CIO is defined as the highest level IT executive or manager in a firm or business unit, even if the term CIO may not always be used” [7, p. 489]. Similarly, Grover et al. accept that the title “CIO has been somewhat loosely defined and is often used interchangeably with various titles such as information technology (IT) director, vice president of IS, director of information resources, and director of IS, to describe a senior executive responsible for establishing policy and controlling information resources” [42, p. 108]. In our sample, we manually validate 317 different official titles for IT executives.<sup>4</sup>

However, this convenience-driven simplification goes against the prevailing theoretical insight. IT executives accrue titles over the course of their career, and these titles reflect IT management skills. In the IT business value literature, IT management skills have been linked to a firm’s ability to manage technical and market risks associated with IT investments [12, 62]. They are heterogeneously distributed among competing firms [62] and are a source of sustainable competitive advantage [26]. Consistent with the extant literature [34], in this study we identify IT executives according to their official title as well as the number of titles they hold. In assessing structural power for IT executives, we use the following three classifications:

1. CIOP = 1 represents the IT executive with the formal title of CIO plus additional official title(s). For example, at the time of the completion of our data set, Robert B. Carter had been executive vice president (VP) and CIO at FedEx Corporation since June 2000.

2. CIOJ = 1 represents the IT executive whose title is just CIO. For example, at the time of the completion of our data set, Thaddeus Arroyo had been CIO of AT&T since 2007.

3. Non-CIO = 1 represents the IT executive whose official title does not contain the acronym CIO. For example, at the time of the completion of our data set, Marc Gordon had been the chief technology officer (CTO) for Bank of America since 2004.

## IT-Related Expert Power

IT-related explicit knowledge, expertise, and experience enable IT executives to exhibit IT leadership and to leverage the business value of IT [10, 11]. In this study we consider the following three variables:

1. $A c d = 1$ represents IT executives with an IT-related academic education. For example, Carter, executive VP and CIO at FedEx, earned his bachelor’s degree in computer and information sciences.

2. $I T j o b = 1$ represents IT executives with prior IT-related employment. For example, before becoming CTO for Bank of America, Gordon held various IT management positions with Accenture, West Marine, and Best Buy.

3. $I T f r m = 1$ represents IT executives with IT industry-related practical experience. For example, prior to his appointment as CIO at AT&T, Thaddeus Arroyo worked for Sabre.

Over the years, these individual sources of IT expert power are likely to complement each other to form an IT executive’s IT expert power. Therefore, in this study we concentrate on the IT expert power (ITexp) as the complementary effect of an IT-related academic degree (Acd), prior IT-related employment (ITjob), or IT-related practical experience (ITfrm). Thus, we account for ITexp as an indicator variable if the IT executive is qualified by one or more of these three attributes of IT-related expert power.

## Appreciation, Reward, and Continuity of IT Strategic Leadership

To capture the reciprocity between IT executives and their firms, we use the following three variables: change in the firm’s IT executive (turnover), reward of IT executives (promotion), and continuity in IT strategic leadership. A change in the firm’s IT executive means that either the firm is not satisfied with the performance of the IT executive or the IT executive feels that he or she could be more appreciated in another company.<sup>5</sup> We capture this lack of reciprocity by introducing the variable turnover $( T O _ { i t } )$ as an indicator variable that equals 1 if there is a change in the firm’s IT leadership.

A promotion is a form of reward and appreciation for the contribution of an IT executive to the firm. Promotion is considered in terms of a change in the level in IT executives’ structural power $( P r o | S _ { i t } )$ , and it takes discrete values in the range of –2 to +2. More specifically, we define $P r o \lvert S _ { \boldsymbol { i t } }$ as follows:

•	 Pro $\mid S _ { i t } = + 2$ if the IT executive’s title rises by two levels, that is, from non-CIO to $C I O P ;$

$P r o | S _ { i t } = + 1$ if the title rises by one level, that is, from non-CIO to CIOJ or CIOJ to $C I O P ;$

$P r o { \mid } S _ { i t } { = } 0$ if there is no change in title;

$P r o | S _ { i t } = - 1$ if the title falls one level, that is, from CIOP to CIOJ or CIOJ to non-CIO;

$P r o | S _ { i t } = - 2$ if title of IT executive drops two levels, that is, from CIO Plus to $n o n – C I O .$

The $P r o \lvert S _ { i t }$ variable is calculated only if $S _ { i t } = 1$ , that is, the IT executive has been with the firm for the three previous years. Finally, continuity of IT strategic leadership is captured by $C o n _ { i t }$ , an indicator variable that takes the value of 1 if the same IT executive has been with the firm for four consecutive years.

## Control Variables

Consistent with prior literature [58, 85], we use firm size, firm performance, and overall reputation to account for extraneous sources of variation of IT capability-related reputation. Larger firms (SIZE) are more likely to attract press coverage, and the selection of firms with superior IT capability by industry experts may be influenced by the firm’s past performance (ROA) [85]. A firm’s overall reputation is another factor that may influence the selection of firms with superior IT capability by industry experts. Market-to-book value (MV) has been suggested as a proxy for reputation because it captures tangible and intangible assets [80] as well as the market’s expectations of future economic returns [66]. In addition, we control for tenure $( T e n r _ { i t } )$ of the IT executive, which measures experience in the IT domain as a distinctive competence that improves a firm’s ability to obtain a competitive technological advantage [10, 11].

To control for extraneous sources of executive appreciation/reward, we include concentration ratio $( C R _ { i } )$ and Tobin’s $q ( T Q _ { i t } )$ . The expected effect of $C R _ { i }$ is ambivalent. According to mimetic isomorphism [35, 73], the pressure will be higher to reward IT executives for IT capability reputation when the number of competitors is small $( C R _ { i }$ is high). But high $C R _ { i }$ implies high visibility or peer recognition, which is detrimental to the firm’s ability to sustain its IT-enabled competitive advantage [26], and is thus likely to have a negative effect on appreciation/reward of IT executives. Given that typical IT benefits are intangible, Tobin’s $q \ ( T Q _ { i t } )$ has been used as a performance proxy for examining the effect of IT investment [13, 17, 75], IT synergies [87], and superior IT capability [61]. $T Q _ { i t }$ captures and reflects copresence of such intangibles as good management skills [1] and superior IT capability [61]. Finally, we control for the fixed industry and year effects. The Appendix provides descriptions of all the variables used in our empirical analysis.

## Additional Tests

One of the main findings of this study is that internal promotion leads to sustainability of IT capability reputation, and this effect is channeled through continuity in the firm’s IT strategic leadership. This means that firms that want to develop and sustain their IT capability reputation need to promote successful IT executives so they will be inclined to stay with their firm longer. However, our conceptualization of sustainable IT capability reputation (SYS ) implies that being able to project an image of superior IT capability in year $t ( R I T C _ { i t } = 1 )$ may lead to sustainable IT capability reputation $( S Y S _ { i t } = 1 )$ . This suggests that a firm could increase its likelihood of sustaining its IT capability reputation by simply hiring a new IT executive with high structural power. If this is true, then there is no need to go through the time-consuming process of developing a cycle of positive reciprocity. Contrasting these two opposing results is very important, since prior research has shown that firms seem to value IT executives with IT experience that has been acquired outside the firm more highly than those whose IT experience has been gained internally [65].

## Robustness Checks

To ensure that our results are not driven by the nature of the data set or variable specifications, we conduct several robustness checks.

Excluding Top 100 Ranked Firms. Several recent IT business value studies have focused only on the subset of the top ranking firms [12, 67, 81, 91]. This seems to indicate that unless the firm achieves one of the Top 100 spots, IT executives should not be rewarded for their contribution to their firm’s ability to sustain its reputation, and developing a cycle of reciprocity would not matter. However, even after excluding the Top 100 ranked firms, nontabulated results remain unchanged. This indicates that the perceived benefits of sustainable IT capability reputation are not limited to the Top 100 firms.

Alternative Promotion Specifications. We consider two alternative specifications of promotion. The first one specifies that the IT executive who is rewarded must be the same person who helped the firm achieve its RITC status in the prior year. The second specification for promotion is relatively more generous: an IT executive with the title CIOP or CIOJ who maintains his/her title is treated as if he/she has received a promotion. Nontabulated results based on these robustness checks remain unchanged.

Alternative Rolling Windows. To confirm that our results are not sensitive to the specification of sustainable IT reputation $( S Y S _ { i t } )$ , we replicate our analysis using a classification of SYS firms based on three- and five-year rolling windows. While the classification based on three-year windows produced comparable results, the results based on the five-year window classification of SYS firms maintain the expected signs, but the level of significance tends to be lower $( p < 0 . 1 0$ for almost all tests). Consistent with Lim et al. [58], weaker results with the five-year window were expected, since a firm that sustains its RITC status for four years will be treated as an OCC firm.

Reverse Causality.<sup>9</sup> It is possible that firms that have a good IT capability reputation will be more likely to have IT executives with stronger structural and IT-related expert power. In addition, firms that appreciate and reward their IT executives are more likely to develop a good IT capability reputation because their executives are more motivated. The first econometrics question that arises from the above argument is the following: Are the explanatory variables endogenous with respect to the dependent variable? To anticipate the issue of potential reverse causality, we estimated Equation (1) by forcing $C I O A L L \nu s N o n _ { i t - l }$ and $I T E x p _ { i t - I }$ to enter into Equation (1) as “predetermined” variables at time t through their one-period lagged terms. By construction, these lagged variables are predetermined and thus not endogenous. In the estimation of Equations (2a) and (2b), we use $( R I T C \_ h a t _ { _ { i t } } )$ predicted values of RITC from Equation (1). By construction, $R I T C \_ h a t _ { _ { i t } }$ is “weakly” exogenous. This is akin to using a limited information (or two-stage least-squares) approach in place of full information (or simultaneous equation) approach.

To assess the reasonableness of the above approach, we rerun the estimation of Equation (1) by using the dynamic panel-data system generalized method of moments (system-GMM) estimator proposed by Arellano and Bover [4, 5], which allows the explanatory variables to be potentially endogenous with respect to dependent variables. The resulting equation is estimated in first-differences form with instruments consisting of the explanatory variables lagged up to three periods both in level and in first differences. This estimator is consistent if there is no second-order serial correlation between the error terms of the first-differenced equation. Arellano and Bover [5] propose a test statistic to test $H _ { 0 } { : } E ( \Delta _ { u _ { i t } } \Delta _ { u _ { i t - 2 } } ) = 0 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , lack of second-order serial correlation). The test statistic is $m _ { _ 2 } \sim ^ { \alpha } \quad ( 0 , 1 )$ under $H _ { 0 } .$ . For completeness, we also test for lack of first-order serial correlation using the test statistic $m _ { \scriptscriptstyle 1 } \sim \sp { \alpha } \quad ( 0 , 1 )$ under $H _ { 0 } .$ Moreover, the validity of the instruments can be assessed by Sargan’s [82] test. Results based on the GMM estimation of Equations (1), (2a), and (2b) are qualitatively very similar to those reported in Tables 3 and 4. Furthermore, they passed the $m _ { 1 } , m _ { 2 } ,$ and Sargan’s tests at the 5 percent level of significance, thus confirming the validity of the first-differenced GMM estimated results. This, in turn, suggests that our main results are not likely to be significantly affected by any potential endogeneity biases hypothesized earlier.

Alternative Specifications of Control Variables. We use alternative specifications for SIZE (log of market capitalization [13] or number of employees [74]) and MV (marketto-book value [12] or market capitalization divided by the book value of common equity [22]). These changes have no material impact in our results.

## Discussion

Th e obj ect ive of th is st udy w as t o examine the association between the management power of IT executives and firms’ IT capability reputation. Using institutional theory, more specifically the argument that TMT and board members will try to align the firm’s internal and external environment, we examined whether firms value IT capability reputation. Our evidence shows that there is a positive association between structural and IT-related expert power of IT executives and the likelihood that the firm will project an image of IT superiority to external stakeholders. Firms that achieve external recognition for the quality of their IT capability are more likely to mirror this also internally by recognizing (i.e., rewarding and appreciating) their IT executives. This is important because IT executives who feel rewarded and appreciated are more likely to stay with their firm longer. Continuity of IT strategic leadership is critical to a firm’s ability to sustain its IT capability reputation. Firms that sustain their IT reputation are more likely to promote their IT executives than firms that achieve, but cannot sustain, their IT capability reputation.

The main message in this study, hence, is that developing such a cycle of positive reciprocity represents a non-market-clearing condition. If positive reciprocity is a clearing condition (i.e., all firms reward their IT executives for their contribution to the firm’s ability to develop and sustain a positive IT capability reputation), it confers no competitive advantage to any firm [8, 58]. Given that the reciprocity norm implies a match between the needs/motivation of IT executives and firm goals, there is no shortcut in achieving this match. Also, given prior research showing that perceptions and attitudes of senior executives and board members regarding the strategic role of IT are volatile [86], it is highly unlikely that the majority of firms will be able to create a cycle of positive reciprocity with their IT executives.

The academic contributions of this study are multifaceted. The study extends the IT strategic leadership literature by focusing on how to motivate and reward IT executives. Despite the importance of rewards as a motivating factor, the IT strategic leadership literature is peppered with more references to punishment [3, 18, 55] than rewards. Given that IT executives are personally instrumental in envisioning their firm’s IT strategy and developing IT capabilities, this study points to the strategy of fostering a culture of reciprocity between IT executives and firms that value IT capability reputation as an alternative, possibly more successful, way to motivate IT executives.

Our study contributes to the corporate reputation literature by examining factors that lead to public recognition as well as conditions that sustain this recognition. Prior researchers have argued that IT capability reputation is a strategic asset with high degree of causal ambiguity and is thus a source of sustainable competitive advantage [25, 70, 80]. Our findings provide the empirical support for making the following extension to this argument: IT capability reputation is more likely to be sustained in firms that develop a cycle of positive reciprocity with their IT executives. Therefore, corporate IT reputation is a source of sustained competitive advantage due to causal ambiguity and time compression diseconomies.

This finding has important implications for the literature on IT capabilities and IT business value because IT strategy implies choosing among different options and committing to a long-term path of IT capability development [88]. Our study identifies a foundation upon which IT strategic management can build, maintain, and enhance distinctive and difficult-to-replicate advantages [88]. Our conceptual framework proposes the idea that a cycle of positive reciprocity, the foundation for sustainable IT capability reputation, is an incremental and time-consuming process refined after several iterations. Thus, IT capability reputation exhibits signs of durable heterogeneity due to time compression diseconomies.

In addition to the above, this study makes the following two methodological contributions: First, since prior research has relied either on anecdotal evidence or survey data [20, 50], our understanding of the role of IT executives, as well as antecedents and consequences of IT strategic leadership, remains limited [50]. This can be improved with longitudinal studies [71]. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to use large-scale panel data in order to study antecedents and consequences of IT strategic leadership. Second, this study introduces a new proxy for structural power of IT executives (i.e., title and number of titles, which is consistent with the resource-based view) that is readily available and produces robust results.

The findings of this study highlight several practical implications for the management of the firm’s reputation and human capital. First, our findings show that IT capability reputation is a strategic asset that can contribute to the firm’s competitive position. Its value stems from the fact that while it is relatively easy for a firm to destroy its IT capability reputation, it will take several years to achieve a sustainable IT capability reputation. Our results demonstrate that the effectiveness and adequacy of firms’ IT strategic leadership, as proxied by external legitimacy, provides useful information to the firm (e.g., to executive or board members, compensation committees, etc.) for designing IT executive contracts. Therefore, board members and compensation committees must consider how much the firm wants to achieve and sustain its IT capability reputation when they design IT executive contracts. Second, for firms that simply want to project an image of IT superiority, hiring an IT executive with significant experience or hiring an IT executive with high title matters. However, firms that want to develop and sustain their IT capability reputation will have to foster a culture of reciprocity with their IT executives. Third, the findings of this study have important career implications for IT executives. The main message of this study is that achieving a cycle of positive reciprocity is a win-win scenario for IT executives and firms that value IT capability reputation. IT executives who want to increase their power within their firm’s TMT and improve the legitimacy of their organization can do this by acquiring external legitimacy (i.e., projecting an image of IT superiority to external stakeholders).

Like all studies, there are limitations to our study that must be acknowledged. First, Finkelstein [34] suggests that official title(s), number of titles, and compensation can serve as proxies of structural power. While we used official title and number of titles, compensation was excluded because we were unable to find enough data points to complete a meaningful statistical analysis, though not for want of exhaustive investigation. Compensation data was only available for 2 to 3 percent of the total number of firms in each year. Given this limitation and our interest in treating change in title or number of titles as a proxy for reward, we estimated $C o m p e n s a t i o n _ { i t }$ as a function of $C I O A L L \nu s N o n _ { i t }$ and $C I O P \nu s C I I O J _ { i t } .$ Untabulated results showed that the estimated coefficient of $C I O A L L \nu s N o n _ { i t }$ and $C I O P { \nu } s C I I O J _ { _ { i t } }$ are positive (0.263 and 0.225, respectively) and statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). This means that on average a CIOP or CIOJ earns 26.3 percent more than Non-CIO, and a CIOP earns 22.5 percent more than CIOJ. This is consistent with survey results that have shown that IT executives with multiple titles are among the highest paid IT executives, with compensation packages of \$1 million or more [60]. Thus, we feel confident that this limitation does not compromise the main finding of our study.

Second, we use turnover in the IT executive position (H2b) as a sign that either the firm is not satisfied with the person or the person does not feel appreciated. However, this represents an important simplification; a change may occur for many other reasons. Exploring and accounting for all possible reasons leading to a change in the firm’s IT executive are very important issues, but they are beyond the scope of this study. In the context of our framework, what really matters is continuity in IT strategic leadership. The role of turnover (H2b) in the context of our analysis is more of a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for continuity in the firm’s IT strategic leadership. A possible extension of this study would be to explore the impact of different types of turnover (voluntary or involuntary) on the sustainability of IT capability reputation.

Given the growing interest in corporate reputation, future research should focus on other factors that contribute to the sustainability of IT capability reputation. For example, is there an association between the complementarity of experience and expertise in a firm’s TMT and the likelihood that the firm will foster a culture of reciprocity? Evidence shows that IT capability building is more important in dynamic environments [92], and researchers have argued that in the modern hypercompetitive environment firms use signals to external stakeholders as a proxy for competitive intent [23]. Building on this evidence, future research should investigate the association between the sustainability of IT capability reputation and competitive agility (i.e., ability to launch tactical and strategic movements).

## Conclusion

Consistent with recent academic and professional evidence showing a trend of growing interest in corporate reputation, this study adopts an institutional approach and demonstrates that firms value IT capability reputation. This finding is manifested in the fact that IT executives who succeed in projecting an image of IT capability superiority to external stakeholders are more likely to be rewarded. Creating a cycle of positive reciprocity is essential for firms that want to develop and sustain their IT capability reputation as it ensures the continuity of a successful IT strategic leadership. Given that for many firms there is no provision for such continuity, firms that nurture this continuity of successful IT strategic leadership will enjoy the benefits of sustainable IT capability reputation.
