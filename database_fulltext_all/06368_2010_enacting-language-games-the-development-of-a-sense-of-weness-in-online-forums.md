# Enacting language games: the development of a sense of ‘we-ness’ in online forums

Anne-Laure Fayard\* & Gerardine DeSanctis<sup>†</sup>

\*Polytechnic Institute of New York University, 6 Metro Tech Center, Brooklyn, New York 11201, USA, email: alfayard@poly.edu, and <sup>†</sup>Duke University, 1 Towerview Drive, Box 90120, Durham, North Carolina 27708-0120, USA

Abstract. A sense of ‘we-ness’ – enacted through collective identity and culture – is both crucial in online, remote contexts, and particularly difficult to develop in such settings. Using Wittgenstein’s concept of language games, we examine how participants of two online forums construct collective identity and culture through their discursive practices. We suggest a strong performative interpretation of the notion of language games, i.e. members of a community produce a sense of we-ness through their participation in the language game while also defining their expected behaviours and actions. We illustrate how the notion of language games offers an approach for researching and analysing the emergence of collective identity and culture in online forums.

Keywords: discursive practices, language game, collective identity, culture, online forums, discourse analysis, interpretative research

## INTRODUCTION

Online discussion forums are increasingly regarded as important venues for promoting learn ing across the boundaries of time, space, and formal organization. Online forums sometimes referred to as virtual communities or electronic discussion groups, provide an opportunity for people to exchange information via group discourse around a common topic or theme. The forums are characterized by a discussion structure in which individuals post and respond to questions or commentary that is organized by subject or thread. In-depth conversation and a high diversity of participation are possible because contributors can be located anywhere in the world, pursue discussions for months or even years, and need only to share an interest in a topical area and have access to the internet (Sproull & Faraj, 1995; Jones, 1997; Blanchard & Horan, 1998; Butler, 2001; Gray & Tatar 2004; Herring, 2004).

In order to support participation and knowledge exchange, it is crucial in these online forums to develop a sense of ‘we-ness’, which includes referencing the group as a collective, creating a sense of belonging, and a situated understanding of how to do things, for the same reason as it is central to building virtual organizations (Wiesenfeld et al., 1999; Alvesson, 2002). Yet, this can be difficult to achieve in virtual contexts (Jarvenpaa & Leidner 1999; Smith 1999; Jarvenpaa et al. 2000; Forman et al., 2008) because of the lack of traditional social cues, shared artefacts (Lipnack & Stamps, 1997) and ‘unifying symbols to communicate a sense o oneness’ (Pratt, 1998).

Our aim in this paper is to understand the social processes by which participants in online communities develop a sense of we-ness. Such an understanding is of considerable interes to scholars and practitioners especially as organizations are becoming increasingly involved in cross-organizational arrangements, such as communities of practices in which members share information and discuss problems (e.g. Wasko & Faraj, 2000; Star & Ruhleder, 2001) and in developing communities of customers where the goal is to get a better understanding of their needs, facilitate a loyalty to the brand and facilitate transactions (e.g. Hagel & Armstrong, 1997; Jarvenpaa et al., 2000; Palmer et al., 2000; Muñiz & O’Guinn, 2001).

A sense of ‘we-ness’ involves the development of a collective identity and culture, which are in many ways synonyms (Hatch & Schultz, 1997; Van Maanen, 2005) or at the very least, closely intertwined. Collective identity, which we define as a label associated with a set of shared attributes meaningful to the group, helps to create and preserve a ‘system of meaning that binds people together (Albert et al., 2000). This is particularly important in social groups that are informally structured around shared interests by individuals whose membership is voluntary (Friedman & McAdam, 1992). Kogut & Zander (1996) and Moreland & Levine (2002) note that identification with a group can act as an intrinsic motivator for individuals to contribute and not simply ‘free ride’.

Following Hardy et al. (2005), we take a discursive approach to the notions of collective identity and culture. We consider language less as a tool for describing and reporting on reality and more as a tool with which to accomplish things (e.g. Boje et al., 2004). We adopt a performative analysis of the language seen in online forums, where it is indeed the main locus of interactions (Rheingold, 1993; Jones, 1997; Wilson & Peterson, 2002; Herring, 2004), and where language is ‘doing’ (Austin, 1962) in the truest performative sense (Kolko, 1995; Herring, 2004). According to the discursive perspective, ‘a collective identity exists as a discursive object produced through conversations, rather than as a cognitively held belief (Hardy et al., 2005, p. 61), and culture consists of symbols and meanings produced through discursive practices (Alvesson, 2000).

We more specifically draw upon Wittgenstein’s concept of language games (Wittgenstein, 1953) to study the emergence of collective identity and culture in online forums. We sugges that language games offer a powerful lens through which to analyse collective identity and culture because language games are ‘systems’ (Wittgenstein, 1969, p. 81) that include not only words, but also actors and ‘the actions with which [language] is interwoven’ (Wittgenstein, 1953, I, sec 7). Hence, language games are more than linguistic patterns of use – they also include social interactions. Indeed, ‘the speaking of a language is a part of [an] activity, or form of life’ (Wittgenstein, 1953, p. 10). Wittgenstein emphasizes the link between words and actions, i.e. words provide the script for actions (Shotter, 1996). Each language game defines a set of discursive practices and activities (Mauws & Phillips, 1995) that are embedded in the ‘situated action of our immediate communities’ (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995).

As a result, by participating in the language game of specific online forums, contributors constitute the ‘reality’ within which they find themselves (Wittgenstein, 1953; Ford & Ford, 1995; Rindova et al., 2004), i.e. they produce the culture of the group and its identity. More specifically, participants construct a discursive object that refers to himself or herself as a form of the collective (Hardy et al., 2005) and they develop discursive practices that define ways of interacting that other members and newcomers reproduce. Through the enactment of the language games, collective identity and shared culture are produced.

In this paper, we illustrate how a language game approach can be used to understand the emergence of a sense of we-ness by investigating the first 9 months of two public online forums dedicated to knowledge management. Our discourse analysis illustrates how, as they engage in the language game, participants produce a set of five discursive practices that define and enact their sense of we-ness.

Our paper proceeds as follows. First, we provide an overview of the notion of language games and of the discursive approach to collective identity and culture. Second, we describe the research setting and how we collected and analysed the data. Third, we describe and illustrate the five practices enacted in the two forums. Finally, we explore the variations in their enactment across the two forums and suggest implications for future research.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Language games

There are two approaches to language in contemporary philosophy. The first, known as the logicist approach, focuses on the meaning of sentences (whether they are true or false; whether they describe a state of affairs that exists), and assumes that words (that picture objects or states of affairs) and meaning are unique and stable. The second – the pragmatist or discursive approach (we use the second term) – focuses on the use of expressions in speech situations. This approach implies that discourse organizes experience and reality (Wittgenstein, 1953; Austin, 1962). It assumes that language is not only a tool to report and describe reality, but is also a tool to create a context within which we ‘know’ reality and orient our actions (Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Feldman & Pentland, 2003).

In this paper, we take a discursive approach, and consider that meaning emerges from the interactions and constant realignment between different participants of the community. We suggest that language games offer a powerful lens through which to analyse collective identity and culture, as language games are ‘systems’ (Wittgenstein, 1969, p. 81) that include not only words but also actors and ‘the actions with which [language] is interwoven’ (Wittgenstein, 1953, I, sec. 7). Language games are intrinsically situated in communities and emerge from a constant realignment between their members, and can thus be interpreted as socially sustained boundaries: ‘When human beings agree in a language they speak, they agree in a form of life’ (Wittgenstein, 1953: sec. 231). As a result, by participating in the language game of specific online forums, contributors become members of a community (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995), developing a sense of what is appropriate or not within their group, and thus defining the boundaries of the group and its identity.

Wittgenstein (1953) argues that language is not universal or standard in its usages or meaning; rather, language is created and enacted by speakers as they generate discussion around ideas or objects of common interest. Language games are routines of discourse that constitute everyday life, giving form, function, and meaning to human interaction; shaping the very structure and function of social settings (Koppl & Langlois, 2001); and enabling members of a group or community to pragmatically coordinate their activities (Shotter, 1996). Language games provide a practical guide for actions: ‘To understand a language means to be a master of a technique’ (Wittgenstein, 1953, p. 68). The meaning of words is defined through their use in social interactions – therefore, words are tools (Wittgenstein, 1953, sec. 291) that not only name things, but also presuppose that appropriate actions should be taken toward said objects (Perry, 1994). As a simple example, Wittgenstein (1969, p. 81) describes the game of building. A builder shouts the words, ‘cube’, ‘column’, ‘slab’, or ‘beam’, and in response, a helper passes the builder a stone selected in reply to the shout. This simple language game is more than jus a communication exercise. When the builder calls out a stone, he performs a certain type of act, with certain success conditions. If he calls out beam'. the success condition is that the assistant passes him a beam, demonstrating that he understands the meaning of the word (for a more detailed analysis of this example, see Perry (1994). Understanding the meaning of the words therefore means knowing what to do in a given context. Language games provide scripts for interactions and actions. For example, in online forums, do you perform greetings and closings? Do you refer to specific individuals (e.g. ‘Dear Jeanne and Noah’) or to the group (e.g. ‘Dear online forum members’)? Can you ask a question without introducing yourself, or should you always introduce yourself before posting?

The builder language game also highlights the importance of the material context for language games. As the builder and his assistant interact with ‘blocks,’ ‘beams,’ and ‘stones, they not only refer to them, but also point to them, move them, bring them. Language games as ‘form of life’ have to be understood not only as discursive practices that are socially enacted as well as accomplished within a material context.

## Collective identity and culture

As noted earlier, the notions of collective identity and culture are closely related and almos synonymous (Van Maanen, 2005). Cultural elements such as artefacts, logos, and organizational dress codes can indeed be powerful symbols of shared identity within an organization (Trice & Beyer, 1993; Pratt and Rafaeli, 1997). Both collective identity and culture address ‘the “we-ness” of a group, stressing the similarities or shared attributes around which group members coalesce’ (Cerulo, 1997, p. 386).

In this paper, we take a discursive approach to collective identity and culture as we argue that both are produced through the enactment of discursive practices. The discursive approach situates ‘collective identity in the language in use among members’ (Hardy et al., 2005) and focuses on the constructive effects of interactions where members describe themselves as a collective. Our position, inspired by Martin (2002), is that culture includes a broad range of ideational and material manifestations of culture. Hence, studies analysing organizational cultural must not only focus on the beliefs and claimed values of participants, but researchers should also analyse the way people dress, the jokes told, seating arrangements at meetings, the ‘rite’ of firing people, how people greet each other, what employees complain about, and so on. (e.g. Boje, 1991; Trice and Beyer, 1984; Weeks (2004). Organizational culture studies highlight the importance of interactions and practices in the emergence of culture and we focus our paper on these elements.

The language game approach provides us a powerful lens to analyse the development of collective identity and culture because it allows us to shift our analysis from attitudes to observable practices and offers a means of understanding how language and action are situated and intertwined. This perspective enables us to illuminate how the language games of a forum provide participants with a set of expected and ‘appropriate’ behaviours that are linked to words and rules and to how members in a community create a collective identity and culture through their language games.

While discursive approaches often take a differentiation perspective on collective identity and culture (Martin, 2002) and, in fact, are often inconsistently or ambiguously interpreted, our analysis in this paper tends to focus on the development of shared practices and understand ings. This does not mean that we are suggesting that all members have a clear and unique interpretation of the cultural practices developed and of their collective identity in an online forum, or that there is no room for ambiguity or conflicts. The individual’s interpretations of the collective identity and the definition of the community vary and are often not identical (Monod & Boland, 2007). Culture, more than a single entity, is composed of motley subcultures (Van Maanen, 2005).

The language game perspective also highlights the multiplicity and constant change that members of a community imbue their own languages games (Wittgenstein, 1953). Yet, communication requires some overlap in the interpretations and the development of shared practices in the context of a specific language game (Wittgenstein, 1953; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). More specifically, in the context of the online forums we studied, the participants’ aim through ‘communicative action’ (Habermas, 1984) is to develop shared interpretations that ‘represent the mechanism for coordinating action’ (Habermas, 1984, p. 101). In this paper, our main interest is to understand how members of online forums develop a sense of we-ness that allow them to interact, coordinate, and share knowledge. Therefore, we focus our analysis on how participants implicitly negotiate shared interpretations of the discursive practices and activities that define their forum language game.

## RESEARCH SETTINGS AND METHOD

We direct our analysis to online forums with high potential for cross-organizational information-sharing among managers or other professionals (Sproull & Faraj, 1995; Blanchard & Horan, 1998; Butler, 2001). These forums are not established to produce a product or service, but merely to share information. We undertake a qualitative and interpretative (Walsham, 1993; 2006) examination of two online forums devoted to the same theme: knowledge management

## The settings

To provide a grounded understanding of the construction of collective identity and culture in online forums, we analysed the discourse of two online forums that we will call KM Forum and KM Chapter. We selected forums with the same topical theme in order to hold constant basic variations in subject matter, as well as in the kinds of participants. The two forums were chosen to be reasonably typical and comparable, while exhibiting some contrast, as well. Both forums have similar founding conditions, are not part of a portal, and are public forums accessible on the web. Yet, they provide a contrast in terms of the contributor retention and the high-volume contributors, which are two objective measures that have been suggested by researchers as important in profiling online forums (e.g. Smith, 1999; Butler, 2001) (see Table 1). The two forums also vary in their eventual number of contributors, message contributions per person, and other basic communication patterns (see Table 2).<sup>2</sup>

Although contributors could be located anywhere in the world, they appear to be from a dominant geographic locale. Contributors to KM Forum and KM Chapter are dominantly located in India and Australia respectively, and all messages are written in English. To protect the anonymity of the contributors, the forums and contributors are referred to by pseudonyms.

KM Forum focuses on knowledge management in India. Some of the participants have met in person and work in the same company; others seem to know each other by reputation, even if they have not met. The founder, Gopal, plays a key role. He is the most active participant, and moderates as well as facilitates the forum. KM Chapter is based in Australia and is dedicated to knowledge management in the public sector. The contributors are largely from Sydney and belong to a professional group that sponsors face-to-face meetings, focus groups, and the like. The founder, Sam, is one of the most active contributors. He moderates the forum and facilitates discussion, but shares those activities evenly with a core of active members. There is strong evidence of group identity in this forum. There is a helping atmosphere, a willingness to debate, and references throughout to live meetings that are open for anyone to attend. The discourse style is friendly and informal. At times the discourse has the feeling of a synchronous chat, a conversation of ‘pop-up’ messages with a few lines or words and no need to introduce oneself or set the context.

## Analytical approach

Our general research approach in this paper is interpretative (Walsham, 1993; 2006). From an epistemological perspective (Becker & Niehaves, 2007), we are not taking a strong ontological stance, instead we are adopting a more pragmatic one (Barley, 2004). Thus, we can be said to be social constructivists in the sense that we claim that collective identity and culture in online forums are the products of participants’ actions and interpretations. However, as Barley notes, ‘to say that something is socially constructed is not equivalent to saying that it has no facticity (Barley, 2004, p. 79). Hence, it is not enough to say that the sense of we-ness in the forum is socially constructed and interpreted because it also includes facticity, e.g. technology and the specific geographies of the participants. One might argue that technology is also socially constructed. While we do not deny the social construction of the technology – how it is designed, interpreted and used – we believe that technology has a certain number of affordances and material properties that cannot be ignored (Barley, 2004; Fayard & Weeks, 2007; Orlikowski, 2007). In fact, we argue, following Orlikowski (2007), that we need to consider the social and material as constitutively entangled with each other. Our analysis does not focus on the materiality of the practices per se; in fact, we presuppose their materiality, i.e. the messages, which are referred to or forwarded, the attachments, the functionalities of the forums such as the calendar, and the reference to face-to-face meetings and physical locations.

Lastly, our analysis focuses on the processes underlying the emergence of a sense of we-ness, i.e. our goal is not to describe the language game of knowledge management per se, but rather to study how a sense of we-ness emerges through the enactment of a shared language game. Therefore, while most researchers using the language games framework focus on the development of a ‘topical’ language game, i.e. the language game of management, of organization science, of war, and so on (e.g. Astley & Zammuto, 1992; Rindova et al., 2004), our emphasis is procedural, akin to Wittgenstein’s (1969) analysis of the language game of builders (and not of the buildings) or Grover’s (1993) analysis of the language game of project management (and not of software or other products of project management).

## Data analysis

We limit our analysis to the first nine months of content in the two forums, starting with their founding, in order to provide sufficient data to document the development of the language game in a critical period of the forum’s life, while still being manageable for an in-depth case study. In all, the dataset of the two forums includes 782 messages over 9 months for each (August 2000 to May 2000 for KM Forum; December 1999 to November 2000 for KM Chapter).

The data were gathered as part of a larger project on knowledge-sharing in online forums; our orientation to data analysis was exploratory in nature. First, we developed notes for each case, which we then clarified and further expounded to yield the final case study for each forum. Then, in keeping with Glaser & Strauss (1967), we iteratively and qualitatively analysed the discourse of the complete text of the 782 messages that composed the two forums. (Numbers in the text refer to the number of the message in the forum.) Analysis consisted of multiple readings of all the messages by the first author, with regular discussions with the second author to adjust and refine the dimensions. In these first phases of the analysis, we were surprised by the frequent references to a collective, which created a sense of we-ness. We decided to explore how this sense of we-ness was enacted by the participants – beyond the expressions of collective identity (e.g. use of the collective ‘we’, references to the group). We compared and contrasted specific messages and built theoretical categories that served as the basis for the language-game analysis. For example, we noticed that participants often refer to previous messages, forward messages, or synthesize previous messages, and that all these activities create a sense of shared history, which plays a role in the construction of collective identity. This led us to define the practice of ‘building a shared history’.

In both forums, we found that participants enacted a sense of we-ness through the discursive practices defining their forum’s language game. Because the enactment of the five practices constitutes the language game, we were interested in how the forum members learn the rules, although while rarely discussing them.

## The language game’s practices

Five practices were common in both settings: (1) self-referring; (2) building a shared history; (3) expressing legitimacy and authority; (4) developing a consistent linguistic style; (5) managing relationships. Table 4 presents additional evidence of how each practice was enacted in each setting.

The self-referring practice includes several activities. Identity can be found in surfacelanguage features that convey intimacy with others, such as reference to ‘we’, ‘us’, ‘our group’, (Weiner & Mehrabian, 1968; Ashforth & Mael, 1989); in references to a common, larger community (‘our KM professional community’); or in references to a locale, such as one’s workplace, homeland, or geographic region (see Kogut & Zander, 1996).

KM Forum contributors share a strong feeling of being part of a community – ‘a group like this serves as solace, sounding board (virtual friend, philosopher, and guide)’ (159) – borne ou by their heavy use of collective language (e.g. ‘I am raising some issues that we might need to discuss in a serious way’ (352)).

Participants frequently refer to the group (‘Dear KM Forum members’ (189), ‘the people of this community’ (134)). They perform general greetings (e.g. ‘Hi all’ (86), ‘Hi everyone’ (4), or ‘Hello friends’ (508)). They also refer from time to time to the broader knowledge-management community: e.g. ‘What should a KM enthusiast do?’ (48). Participants (mostly those based in India) often refer to their Indian context, either through cultural references such as the celebration of Diwali, an Indian festival (116); references to cities such as Pune, Hyderabad, Mumbai, Delhi, Bangalore (187); or more specific references to knowledge management in India: ‘to see if they are suitable for the Indian context’ (153), ‘a network of India thinkers’ (202).

A strong sense of community pervades KM Chapter from the beginning, through general greetings, references to the group (e.g. ‘it’d probably be useful resource for the group as a whole . . .’ (254)), and use of ‘we’ (e.g. ‘If we are a group of people interested. . . .’ (23)). Participants also refer to the larger knowledge-management community, asking for names of ‘leading experts of KM’ in Boston before going on a trip there (221), mentioning knowledge management conferences, or just mentioning the community (e.g. ‘I have been fascinated by the willingness of many people to share in the KM community’ (260)).

References to the Australian context are frequent, as most of the members are based in Australia. There are references to cities such as Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne, Canberra, and Perth (e.g. ‘as you can see I have returned from the long drive to Perth and back’ (58)).

The self-referring practice enacted by participants in the two forums is an important means for people to feel part of a group, particularly in online forums where participation is voluntary, and where, because of the online context, there are few, if any, unifying symbols (Pratt & Rafaeli, 1997; Pratt, 1998). The self-referring practice creates a label, ‘our group’, which is a key unifying symbol. It is also interesting to note that the self-referring practice includes many expressions of commitment and enthusiasm toward the group, such as ‘We should believe in ourselves’ (46) in KM Forum, and ‘it is great to belong to a group that can provide such a wide range of interesting speakers’ (312) in KM Chapter. The expression of enthusiasm related to self-referring is important because it increases the attraction to the group and the tendency toward agreement (Fulk, 1993). Moreover, these frequent expressions of commitment in the first few months of the forum seem to be a variation of the swift trust in virtual teams (which is defined within context of the temporary team as the measure of trust initially present in the formation of a zero-history team) described by Jarvenpaa & Leidner (1999). Self-referring activities and the expressions of enthusiastic commitment associated with them are therefore central to the construction of a collective identity in online contexts.

## Building a shared history

How does one create a sense of belonging in an online environment where the other members of the forum are invisible, and where the only shared context is the archived messages? It is a sense of shared history that differentiates groups from each other and endows a given group with a definite identity (White, 1992). It is also an important element in the construction of a culture (Frost et al. 1985). Although none of the participants refer to the founding messages (see Table 4), participants develop a discursive practice to develop a shared history through a series of activities, such as linking – i.e. they refer explicitly to the content of a previous message in their reply (Baym, 1995); quoting – i.e. copying portions of a previous message in one’s reply (Herring, 2001); forwarding messages; and weaving – summarizing the points discussed in a series of messages (Feenberg. 1989)

In KM Forum, contributors often perform linking activities. The contributor, by referring to another message (e.g. ‘This is in continuation to Gopal’s reply to Vibha’s query’ (16)), shows that her message is connected to the forum’s discussions, and also shows that she is part of the group, and aware of discussions taking place in the forum (e.g. This has reference to the discussion which happened yesterday and the subject matter raised by Gopal today’ (42)). Participants also practice quoting, although less frequently. Quoting creates the impression of adjacency, as it juxtaposes two turns (or portions of turns) in the same message (Herring, 2001). Moreover, participants often forward others’ messages – either messages that were posted in another forum which the participant wants to share in KM Forum (4, 12, 24, 129, 132), or replies to a message that were sent only to the poster (e.g. 25, 126). This forwarding activity, which brings side discussions back to the group, extends the space of the forum and gives a ‘let’s all share what we have’ feeling of community. Gopal often weaves messages, i.e. summarizes different points made in a discussion and provides a synthesis:

## Hi everyone,

I sense that somewhere the discussions that happened took most people by surprise. So would like to take a few steps back so that some understandings are clarified (especially for those members who have joined out of interest about KM and have no experience in implementation as a discrete activity)

for the whole group (We are 79 now!). (. . .) Any more thoughts anybody?

## Gopal (29)

Only a few other members perform weaving. By referring to previous messages, this weaving activity participates in the building of a shared history.

In KM Chapter, members practice linking as well, referring to previous messages in their responses. For example, one member writes: ‘Sam’s most recent comments have left one concern that I think is worth discussing . . .’ (86). In some cases, members are very specific: ‘Last week I suggested . . .’ writes John, and then discusses Phil’s reply to his comment, saying ‘Phil made the point . . .’ (95). In other cases, participants refer to a general discussion (‘Recent contributions to the discussion seem to imply’ (120)). Participants also forward some emails that they receive or that they have read on other forums (e.g. 12, 16, 59, 76). Weaving activities are also performed in KM Chapter by various other participants, such as Tim:

I’d like to summarise the responses here, but first, here’s an interesting extract from a book called ‘The New Office’ by Francis Duffy. It’s three pages long so I thought it might be better to attach it as an RTF: (See attached file: The New Office extract.rtf)

Duffy also talks about the importance of actually designing circulation spaces, not treating them as a necessary evil and trying to minimise them. (. . .) Now to the summary of responses (. . .) (81)

Having a shared history is crucial to the emergence of a sense of we-ness among participants in online forums. First, it creates a collective library of quotes and opinions to which members can refer. More specifically, new members can read previous messages to get a sense of the topics discussed. Hence, a new member in KM Chapter notes: ‘Hi, as a relatively new participant I went back through the messages particularly KM snips’ (139). This also exemplifies the expected behaviours of the forum members.

## Expressing legitimacy

The cultural perspective emphasizes ‘what people must learn to become fully functioning and accepted organizational members’ (Van Maanen, 2005, p. 57). Showing that one is an authentic member of the community is particularly crucial in online settings, where people most of the time have not met. Therefore, contributors to the online forum present themselves so as to be accepted and establish influence, and in doing so define themselves as legitimate members. Galegher et al. (1998, p. 499) discuss legitimacy in online support groups, observing that ‘to obtain direct support and information from others in the group one must demonstrate legitimacy – that his or her concerns are genuine and justified. Legitimate membership, or the right to contribute, is signalled by reference to relevant experience; by reference to shared history, such as reading earlier contributions to the online forum; by statements that signal worthiness of participation in the topical discussion (e.g. ‘I have been practicing KM in my company for years’); and by disclosing personal information that states who and/or why one is present in the online forum. First-time contributors sometimes do this by referring to behaviours (‘I have been lurking here for a few months’), or by stating a legitimate topic and noting its worthiness for discussion by the group.

In KM Forum, newcomers often present themselves when they first post as having been lurking for a while: ‘Hello this is just to introduce myself. I have joined KM Forum sometime back. I am with the Institute of . . . in the HR/OB area. KM is something of interest to me and I would like to learn more from these egroup discussions . . .’ (141). They also sometimes refer to their experience: ‘I actually used this technique quite successfully’ (341). New members often mention that they are pleased to be part of this group, e.g. ‘I am happy to be part of this group’ (5), and by expressing their membership, they do become members.

In KM Chapter, a few newcomers refer to their membership. When they introduce themselves, they often refer to how long they have been involved: ‘Hi all, I have just returned from my Christmas break and being a member of this group since 22 of December 99, I thought would take this opportunity to introduce myself’ (26). Participants refer to their experiences, either to indicate that their questions are legitimate – ‘our (my) job is educating the senio management, and I would love to share in some of your success stories about that . . .’ (242), or that their replies are worth reading – e.g. ‘This is exactly what a number of us are trying to do in our organization . . .’ (7).

By expressing legitimacy, participants in the two forums claim membership, thus implicitl affirming and defining what it is to be a member of that specific online forum.

## Developing a consistent linguistic style

The distinct linguistic traditions of a group – stylized vocabulary and communication routines – are partly the symbols of identity and culture. These traditions play an even more important role in online contexts, where people have no other symbolic artefacts with which to identify. After reading a few messages in a forum, one might observe some ‘orderliness’ (White, 1992), which partially comes from and which is reflected in the consistent linguistic style developed by its members.

Members of KM Forum use a pleasant and informal style of interaction, nearly always performing greetings, closings (‘cheers’, ‘best regards’), and expressions of politeness and gratitude (‘Please forward this announcement’, ‘I would be grateful . . .’, ‘Thanks’). Contributors make heayy use of parentheses to express thoughts and feelings. Parentheses are used to embellish the text, and create an oral style. For example, in the following quote, the poster uses a parenthesis to make an aside comment, as he would do orally: ‘We must learn to visualise how the same km system would look 25 years from now. I am sure that we would be laughing at own remarks, the way we look at ELISA the first computer. (Would this egroup survive the time?).’ (352). Oral style is also reinforced by the use of punctuation (‘Etc. Etc.!!! . . . quite a lengthy topic, eh??’ (15), ‘Hi all!!!1’ (275)) and of emoticons such as smileys (‘right ‘ (158), ‘pompuousness apart;-)’ (295)). These different linguistic activities are developed from the beginning of the forum, and are consistent during the 9 months we studied.

Participants in KM Chapter also develop a consistent style. As in KM Forum, participants typically post friendly, chat-like, informal messages with no introduction and very little relationship management. Most of the time, they perform greetings (either general ‘Hello’, ‘Hi friends’, or personal addresses) and closings (‘cheers’, ‘best regards’, ‘ciao’). Smiles and capital letters are used to express emotions (whether gratitude or tensions). Contributors also use parentheses to make extra comments, although less heavily than in KM Forum.

However, KM Chapter’s style differs in several ways. First, the discourse at times has the feeling of a synchronous chat, a conversation of ‘pop-up’ messages with a few lines or words and no need to introduce oneself or set the context. For example, people reply immediately or use very time-specific greetings such as ‘good morning’. Immediacy is reinforced through the informal tone of speech (e.g. ‘I am only just able to get to the keyboard, I just fell off my chair with hysterical laughter . . .’ (135)), as well as through the use of capital letters, punctuation, and emoticons to express emotions (e.g. ‘FINALLY !!!! Someone who agrees with me! (134)). Moreover, many messages reflect some intimacy between the participants, through frequent personal addresses and references to specific contextual elements, such as, ‘BTW, it’s great to be back’ (193), or ‘We must catch up for lunch soon’ (299). While in KM Forum, members tend to address their messages to the whole group; in KM Chapter, they tend to address it to specific individuals.

We also note messages with the appearance of a conversational flow, as a result of a smooth transition from one message to another (Orlikowski & Yates, 1994). For example, John posts a message on a philosopher who made an interesting point. As often happens in the forum, he does not use any greetings and just signs ‘Cheers, John Henry’ (116). Eva replies smoothly with no greeting or linking, ‘This means there are three challenges for organizations and society . . .’ (117). She signs without closing. A third participant continues the conversation, posting a very short message: ‘I’d be inclined to reverse the order: work out what it is you want to do first and this will guide the rest of the process. Dan Stevens’ (118). This feature is specific to KM Chapter and does not occur in KM Forum.

In synchronous voice communication, paralinguistic speech devices – such as run-on sentences, ellipses, parenthetical remarks, exclamatories, and first- and second-persona pronouns – are used to approximate a conversational style and a sense of informality. In online settings, people have created new conventions unique to the electronic context, such as common abbreviations and emoticons (e.g. the smiley), contractions and informal spellings, and substitutions of symbols for letters (see Ferrara et al., 1990). While culture in face-to-face contexts is displayed through dress codes or communicative practices (Van Maanen, 1991; Pratt & Rafaeli, 1997), shared identity in an online context is displayed through the enactment of a consistent discursive practice. This practice also creates a governance of speech inside the forums, defining what is acceptable or expected of players as they participate in the game (Grover, 1993; Myrsiades, 1998; Topp, 2000).

## Managing relationships

Developing a sense of we-ness involves emotions and the building of relationships. Relationship-building is more difficult in online settings, where people miss informal interac tions (e.g. Kraut et al., 1988) and non-verbal cues (Short et al., 1976; Sproull & Kiesler, 1991). In the two forums, participants are involved in three main activities of relationship maintenance: thanking people, giving feedback (i.e. saying how interesting and/or useful they found prior messages), and ‘helping’ (replying and offering the information requested when someone asks a question). Because they create a positive atmosphere with a lot of support, these activities produce the feeling of being part of a community.

In KM Forum, participants often thank other members for their contribution: ‘I agree with TV (180), ‘Good idea from Gopal’ (191), ‘Shiva, that was a valid point’ (153). They also provide a lot of positive feedback, e.g. ‘Some really good insight from Sophia Dixie’ (3410), ‘I completely agree with Mr Kumar’s views’ (343).

When providing information, contributors (often Gopal, the founder, and a few frequent contributors) note that it might be interesting for others and ask for comments, creating a sense that they are invested in others’ opinions

Hello friends! I came across this site and found it to be pretty interesting. The Demo and RO calculations are very very interesting . . . maybe you might find it good to! Please do post your comments. (323)

Here is a phenomenal link on storytelling from FastCompany . . . some would find it quite interesting ciao. (326)

Participants also help each other, providing replies in a timely manner in several cases. Consider, for example, a discussion of 14 messages over 2 days, that started with a question posted by a newcomer, Rhodit Gaba. He introduces himself, ‘Hi all, I have just joined the group. I work as a knowledge manager at XYZ,’ and asks, ‘How many companies really implemented KM in India, and who are the major players in KM? Thanks. Rhodit’ (272). Another participant (a newcomer too) says that he has a similar question. Then follows a series of replies such as:

KM has been experimented in various hues by various organizations in India. But in my knowledge there are two home grown initiatives I am aware of (. . .) Infosys (. . .) another is my organization (. . .). Some other examples are CGEY, Microsoft, PwC, McKinsey, Accenture. (274)

It is interesting to note that one of the replies refers to the shared history of the group: ‘To find out how Satyam is doing it, please check the earliest KM Forum messages’ (274). Rhodi concludes with a very warm and thankful message: ‘Hi Pals: Hey (. . .) This is wonderful (. . .) I like the quick responses (. . .) Thanks everyone for the response (. . .)’ (288). In this exchange, participants try to help the two new members by providing them with a rich and useful replies. They help by responding (which is an action in itself), by providing links to websites and white papers (277), by sharing their own experiences (274), by summarizing presentations they went to (279), or by referring to specific experts (282).

Participants in KM Chapter are nearly always thanking other participants, often in an enthusiastic manner:

I have just jumped in on this conversation and wanted to support Tom’s delineation (. . .) thanks of letting me in on this great conversation (52) or

Jim, THANK YOU. As someone who sat on the edge of this conversation, it is fantastic to have this summary as a future reference tool. Thank you. (100)

Most of the time, they provide feedback: ‘Paul, this is an excellent idea’ (24) or ‘I am delighted to see this question raised’ (103). These two activities create a friendly and caring atmosphere, which is also reinforced by the requests for comments: ‘Your thoughts are welcome’ (302), ‘Look forward to the replies’ (217), and so on.

KM Chapter members also help each other whenever they can. For example, when Kim asks for ‘names of leading experts on KM (academics or practitioners) or companies that are leading examples of knowledge organizations in Washington and Boston’, as a senior executive in her organization is ‘traveling there and would like to meet up with some experts’ (221), she receives five replies in the next few hours (223, 230, 234, 236, 243) that provide either direct contacts or resources. Kim thanks everyone for their replies (245). Sharon, another member, has a similar experience. There was a lecture in her city by a famous speaker which she could not attend. Sharon asks if anyone went to the lecture and could summarize the main points of the presentation. She receives four replies, and one member sends her his typed notes. Sharon replies and thanks everyone: ‘Thanks for taking the time to summarise the S lecture’ (279). In both cases, participants in the forums aim to help the poster by performing a certain number of actions: first, by replying in a timely manner, and second, by sharing the information they have – providing literature references, URLs, names, asking other people (e.g. ‘I will try to go and ask Tom D. tomorrow’ (243)), and summarizing their notes (as in the case of the lecture).

Relationship management is important in any group or organization, and crucial in building a sense of belonging and commitment. This becomes even more important when people do not see each other and have no opportunities for informal interactions, relying entirely on written interactions with very few opportunities to exhibit friendly behaviours or support (even simple things such as saying hi in the elevator, holding the door, etc.) In both forums, participants develop practices to manage their relationships, thereby creating a friendly and helpful culture, which in turn fosters commitment and a feeling of belonging. Relationship management implies more than just thanking others, giving positive feedback, and sharing information (references, names, contacts), but also summarizing knowledge, presenting and interviewing others who might have even more expertise.

This repertoire of practices constituting language games is not proposed as exclusive. On the contrary, the practices and the activities it involves should be understood as interdependent and intersecting throughout the specific activities performed by participants. For example, the referring activity is a way of expressing legitimacy. When a participant refers to a previous post, she is indicating that she is aware of the discussion in the forum and that therefore her post is grounded and legitimate. Yet, she is also building a shared history by referring to previous discussions. The concept of language games interpreted as a form of life suggests that members of a group engaged in a language game develop shared discursive practices and norms for behaviours within the context of their interactions. Our analysis shows that by participating in the forum language game and enacting its discursive practices, members construct their forum's culture

## Learning the rules of the game

How do participants who are not collocated and often have not met learn how to participate in the language game through which their we-ness emerges? Language games are learnt through social practices, and rules are typically not explicit. They may be discussed, or even codified, but the actual language game is the enacted process of dialogue; rules are established and maintained as players imitate and react to one another. New participants are taught the rules through examples and by practice. In participating in the language game of the forum, and in learning and following the (implicit) rules for interacting and behaving, participants become members of the forum, producing a collective identity and culture. For example, a newcomer joining the forum by reading a set of messages will learn about the basic interaction norms: Do you use greetings and closings? Do you introduce yourself? Do you ask short questions, or do you give some context? It is interesting to note that both forums possess consistent styles, although there is no early posting that presents these stylistic guidelines. Indeed, we did not observe any cases of newcomers posting messages that did not reflect the style of the other messages. Most of the time, forum interactions are grounded on implicit rules that are discussed only when one of the members ‘misbehaves’.

In KM Forum, rules and norms are mostly implicit, except for a few technical rules, e.g. ‘I would request all of you to delete the earlier mails when you reply to the group’ (103) or ‘I hope I am not violating any group norms in sending this attachment to all members’ (177). Appro priate behaviours are conveyed through the practices of Gopal and the active contributors in the first 3 months.

However, rules are discussed and stated once a participant exhibits a behaviour that is considered inappropriate by the other participants. For example, a newcomer posts a message indicating that he feels lost and disappointed by the exchanges he reads on the forum, stating, ‘My experience over the last 3 months has left me more confused and ignorant than before . . (152)’. One participant replies to this message with an aggressive message:

Hi Mandi, firstly it is a fabulous analysis but sadly incorrect! (. . .) but this mail of yours is discouraging a healthy discussion (. . .) Secondly I feel one should contribute positively to the group and not discourage discussion. (158)

This post receives several replies from other participants who see it as an inappropriate way to reply. It leads to a discussion on appropriate messages and the definition of the forum. Participants agree on the norms for behaviours (being open-minded and polite), and discuss the role of the moderator. Hari, a participant, notes that a moderator is needed to prevent this kind of interaction and suggests Gopal. Pushan replies, ‘I think the group has enough maturity to take some amount of criticism in its stride, that’s why Gopal stopped moderating it’. This post suggests that the group’s identity is strong, but also that criticism is allowed in its culture.

Apart from this explicit discussion, participants in the forum also enforce norms by ignoring certain messages or deviant behaviour. For example, in the first two months, there are four messages in which participants do not introducing themselves, do not thank people in advance, and simply ask for a resource or for a reply to a survey. Nobody replies to these posts, which do not exhibit the appropriate style described earlier; this type of post disappears in the following months. A similar example is illustrated in the following story. Pascal Reit, a consultant, starts posting in January (fifth month), stating that he would like to start building a knowledge-management group in India (202). Reit performs a distinctive set of discursive practices, which are not those usually performed in KM Forum: he posts long, complex, and theoretical messages in the form of pronouncements, without seeking feedback. At one point, he posts several aggressive messages arguing with another well-known consultant. Apart from the other well-know consultant who replies twice, all the other participants completely ignore Reit (who eventually stops posting in the eleventh month). This example illustrates how, because he does not enact the forum language game and therefore does not ‘fit’ the group culture, Reit is ignored.

While in KM Forum rules are only discussed when they are broken, in KM Chapter, a discussion about rules and netiquette is started by Sam Carpenter (after 4 months). However, it does not foster a lot of participation and has no influence on behaviours:

Another idea from our experienced virtual community members is to establish a code of conduct or netiquette for our group. If anyone was examples or ideas we should include in our code of conduct I would love to hear from you. The purpose is to encourage participation by creating a safe place for people to air ideas. Cheers, Sam. (218)

Only one participant replies, providing a list of websites and suggesting that ‘rather than re-invent the wheel, we might want to re-purpose something similar off other KM-related online discussion groups – are any of the members of the list able to help here?’ (226). Based on the resources provided by Andy (226), Sam proposes a list of rules and announces that he will create a poll (249), which he opens in message 252. The results appear by posting 326 with only seven replies (all positive), which seems to indicate that there is little interest in formalizing rules.

The only discussion about a practice considered annoying takes place in July, when two people reply to a reminder for a monthly meeting (messages 357 and 358) saying that they will not be able to attend because of the flu. A participant replies briskly: ‘Do list members really need to be receiving these sort of e-mails? Is there any way list recipients can avoid them? Regards, Matt’. Unlike the discussion on behaviours in KM Forum, this discussion is short and does not lead people to discuss appropriate behaviours more generally. Sam, who immediately replies, closes the discussion: ‘Mark has a good point. It is great to receive people’s apologies but could I ask you to send apologies directly to Anita (anita@d . . .). It is important to remember that when you reply to an email from KM Chapter, you are emailing everyone in the groups (165 people)’ (359). Except for this occurrence in KM Chapter, the rules and norms of behaviours are generally implicit and followed by all the participants.

Because of our interest in the development of a sense of we-ness and of the forums language games, we paid particular attention to the founding message and the first interactions that followed, as a language game can emerge from the initial ways in which one person reacts to another, with these first interactions defining a certain ‘form of life’ between them (Wittgenstein, 1953, p. 218). In KM Forum, the style of the founding message (see Table 5) and of the first interactions replying to this message illustrate well the linguistic style developed in the forum: polite, friendly and calling for future interactions. In the founding message, Gopal, the founder, uses an intimate (referring to two participants, Samba and Anil) and enthusiastic tone (‘We are already three of us’, ‘Looking forward to valuable interaction!’). Anil replies a few minutes later thanking Gopal for his invitation. Two days later, another person, Jyoti, posts saying that she’s happy to be part of this forum. Like Gopal, both start their posts with a greeting (‘Hi’) and finish with a looking forward: ‘looking forward to interesting interactions’ (2), ‘looking forward to know the state of KM in Indian organizations’ (5). These first interactions illustrate well the style of the interactions in the forum.

In KM Chapter, as in KM Forum, a distinctive style emerges in the first few interactions. The informal tone and the feeling of a conversation taking place are enacted in the first message posted after the founding message (posted by Sam Carpenter, see Table 5). The post has no greeting and no closing, and seems to be a follow-up to Sam’s post: ‘It looks like this particular site also enables us to post events in the calendar so that none of us actually forgets. Or if we do, helps us manage our knowledge (. . .) seriously though I have one question (. . .)’. These informal messages, with no introduction and very little relationship-management, that some times feel like chat, are typical of KM Chapter style.

Language games are developed through the interactions of their participants; as soon as the first interactions are initiated, distinctive practices and expected behaviours emerge without rules being explicitly defined. Participants therefore learn by doing and replicating previous interactions, just as the builder in Wittgenstein’s language game teaches the apprentice. When they do not follow the implicit rules, they are notified either explicitly (and this is sometimes th case in KM Forum, regarding a collective discussion and agreement) or implicitly (by being ignored). Therefore, through participation in the language game (i.e. the enactment of a set of discursive practices) participants constitute a shared identity and culture that together define a set of expected behaviours.

## VARIATIONS IN ENACTING THE LANGUAGE GAME

In understanding how participants in the forum develop a collective identity and culture through the enactment of a language game, we focused on the use of five common practices. Ye members in the forum perform these practices in distinct ways. Our analysis highlights two major differences: the organizational structure of the forum, and its embodiment (i.e. do participants meet face-to-face from time to time?).

## Organizational structure and roles

In both KM Forum and KM Chapter, the participants engage in a series of discursive practices that provide scripts for action (particularly for newcomers), thus implicitly defining appropriate behaviours. In both forums, the practices that emerge in the first interactions persist throughout the life of the group. Yet, the two forums differ in how these practices are enacted and the difference in structure and roles explains some of these variations.

In KM Forum, there is no reference to an organizational structure, apart from references to Gopal, the founder, and his active involvement in the forum’s life. The founder plays a key role in this forum. Several times he signs ‘Gopal, Owner and Moderator – KM Forum of India’. He is the most active participant, and he moderates as well as facilitates the forum over the nine months we studied. He takes on the moderator role in the founding message (see Table 5) in which he provides practical information on how to post on the forum, celebrates growth, and asks for ‘valuable interactions’. He has an important role in building up the community, and regularly celebrates growth, an important activity in the self-referring practice. It is interesting to note that others recognize the leadership role enacted by Gopal. For example, when the previously described discussion on proper behaviours follows an aggressive email, a partici pant expresses the need for a moderator (158) and a second participant agrees and suggests Gopal, ‘our most prolific contributor’ (159).

He posts reflective messages defining the nature of the forum and its activities:

(. . .) if you and other members of this egroup have been thinking that it will be easy to take a few defined concepts and find out what KM is (. . .) then I regret to say that this need wil not be fulfilled at this forum (. . .)I believe that sense making is the actual knowledge creation process for the community (. . .) (155)

In this post, Gopal lets other participants know what they should expect of their participation in the forum, and implicitly defines the types of interactions they might have.

Despite Gopal’s central role, he shares his facilitator role with a small core group of active participants, which he refers to as ‘a core of regulars [who] keeps the pot boiling’ (190). They post information asking for the opinions of other members, aimed at stimulating a discussion, or reply to messages, providing feedback. Three members are highly active in the first three months (i.e. post more than five messages) but stop contributing in the last 3 months. These three early members are involved in enacting the language game and creating a lively ‘atmosphere’ and ‘setting the stage.’ Once this is done, these early members disappear and others enter, joining the core group of players. The majority of forum contributors do not participate consistently – they post one or two messages once in a while, and yet the practices are enacted consistently. It seems that the active early members and Gopal have enacted a language game strong and simple enough to allow participants to enter the forum and quickly grasp how to behave and play its particular language game.

In KM Chapter, the founder Sam is one of the most active contributors. He moderates the forum and facilitates discussion. As a moderator, he is in charge of calendar issues, starts discussions on matters such as the forum name and netiquette, creates polls, welcomes new members and manages the group repository. He also plays an important role in facilitating the discussion: e.g. ‘I would love to hear about any references you come across’ (60), ‘your thoughts are welcome’ (302). He is perceived as a key actor in the forum as this posting attests: ‘Hi Sam, congratulations on the continuing success of the KM Chapter group ’ (220).

Although Sam dominates in sheer number of messages, interactions and roles seem to be quite evenly distributed among a core group of participants. Apart from the founder, seven participants contribute in all 9 months, and 22 during at least 6 months (while in KM Forum, four participants, without including Gopal, posted in all months and only 12 in 6 months). These members are highly active, both from an organizational perspective (e.g. sending information regarding technology, meetings and events) as well as from a content perspective (stimulating lively discussions by giving a lot of feedback and asking other members for their opinions). During the first 3 months, Tom, a consultant, regularly posts the snips of the day, takes the role of a KM expert, and is referred to by two contributors as such. At one point, Sam asks for volunteers to present at the monthly meetings and receives many positive replies, suggesting the commitment of the contributors.

References to several roles and division of labour (e.g. the role of moderator, organizing committee) highlight the existence of structure. Sam refers to the coordinating committee (143), to the executive members (308), and to Anita ‘our secretary [who] manages the list (156). In the fourth month, the organizational structure emerges more clearly. Messages star to be posted by an ‘administrative’ address and roles are clearly evident; there is reference to the executive members (308), and Anita, who was already active in the management of the meetings in the first three months, is even more active. She sends reminders and complementary information (e.g. venue or changes in time) for the meetings. Two other members, Patrick and Ben, also play a role in the management of the forum.

In the two forums, participants take on different roles. These roles are either implicit (e.g. facilitators) or explicit (e.g. founder, moderator). Active members implicitly determine the forum’s rules and practices. Hence, when Gopal enacts the five practices of the language game, he teaches other members of KM Forum the rules of their own language game, just as the builder ‘teaches’ the apprentice how to build a wall. In KM Chapter, a similar learning process takes place, but it is performed and shared through a group of participants.

## Embodiment

Each forum has some degree of geographic identity, although they are open to anyone. In both forums, there are many references to geography. Moreover, participants are interested in knowledge management in general, but also in specific issues related to their respective countries, India and Australia. Yet, there is a major difference between the two forums as members in KM Chapter often meet offline, while they do not in KM Forum (in KM Forum, there are merely references to face-to-face meetings). We call this phenomenon – the link between the forum and the sharing of physical space – embodiment of the language game.

In KM Forum, some of the participants appear to know each other by reputation, even if they have not met; a few of them mention that they are working in the same company. In the fourth month, there are two offline meetings mentioned (four messages in total): a course on KM offered by Bhala in Mumbai and a meeting in Hyderabad organized by Gopal. None of the two emails received replies on the forum (although people may have sent emails directly to Bhala or Gopal), and there are no other postings mentioning these offline meetings in the next 5 months we studied.

In KM Chapter, there is no reference to the geography in the founding message, and the focus is more on the domain (public sector) than on the national context. As one participant notes, ‘[the] online community obviously is unconstrained by geographical boundaries’ (251). However, the community is strongly embodied, and face-to-face meetings are key in structuring KM Chapter. Frequent references to physical locations and to face-to-face meetings (announcements, reminders, asking for speakers, providing logistic information) convey a strong sense of identity, as noted by Kogut & Zander (1996). Members have a monthly meeting, There are several references to this meeting in the first three months, and it becomes central in the last 6 months we studied. Many postings refer to offline meetings, either future meetings (e.g. ‘Looking forward to catching up at the May meeting’ (193), ‘we could do this at our next meeting’ (216)), or referring to what happened in a previous meeting (e.g. ‘We discussed the issue at the last meeting (May)’ (251)). Many contributors invite other members to contact them offline as well.

The intertwined relationship between face-to-face meetings and online discussions is well described and analysed by Sam in a message from the fifth month of the forum’s existence:

building a healthy vibrant online discussion requires over 400 members; and conducting stimulating and engaging face to face meetings requires less than 25 members. Whilst building the online community we must continue our f2f monthly meetings. These meetings are typically the source of many great conversations and new relationships. (. . .) We just need to build the online community so we all benefit from great discussions online (251).

Sam insists on the importance of the face-to-face meetings for building relationships and trust, but also shows how these meetings can support the growth of the online community.

Embodiment is a key characteristic of KM Chapter, and, as highlighted by Sam in the previous message, it aids in the building of trust and relationships, and thus also in the enactment of a collective identity. Our observations on the importance of face-to-face interac tion for the development of a collective identity are consistent with the observations of Wenge et al. (2002), who highlight the importance of embodiment for distributed communities of practice inside organizations, and of O’Mahony and Ferraro (2007), who found that face-toface meetings were crucial in defining membership in open-source communities. They are also in line with the findings of experimental studies showing that a single face-to-face can lead to more trustworthy behaviour online (Rocco. 1998). or that when group members communicate face-to-face instead of electronically, they tend to like each other more (Weisband & Atwater, 1999).

We might expect a common locale to aid social identity in an otherwise distributed group, such that KM professionals from the same geographic region would more readily identify with one another than with people distributed around the world. Commonality of culture and experience are more likely to promote social solidarity (Markovsky & Chaffe, 1995), identity synergy (Pratt & Foreman, 2000), and thus commonality in a group’s language game. Yet, it is interesting to note that participants in KM Forum were able to develop their group’s language game and a sense of shared identity without meeting face-to-face. However, if there are only a few references to face-to-face meetings in KM Forum, the forum’s geographic identity in India bounds its scope and provides a common ground for those who participate. Moreover, as we noted, there are a few attempts to start meeting face-to-face. It might be that references to face-to-face meetings, which are nearly non-existent in the first 9 months, become more prevalent in the following months, after the forum develops.

In different ways, both forums illustrate the importance of a locale in the construction of collective identity. The role of the locale is more obvious in KM Chapter because of the structuring role of face-to-face meetings. This confirms findings that suggest that contributors who share encounters outside of the forum boundaries, such as face-to-face meetings or attending the same conferences, may be more likely to overcome the drawbacks of distance, and have greater opportunity to form a common identity (Cummings et al., 2002).

## DISCUSSION

Our paper provides a thick description of how participants in two online forums enact a sense of we-ness. It suggests that participants in KM Forum and KM Chapter construct their forums collective identity and culture by participating in their respective forums’ language game, more specifically by enacting five practices: self-referring, building a shared history, expressing legitimacy, developing a consistent style and managing relationships.

Our paper takes a discursive approach and therefore allows us to focus on the communicative practices of the participants, which is the essence of interactions in online forums as wel as to focus on the processual aspect of the emergence of a sense of we-ness. Some studies show that groups in online contexts can develop a strong and meaningful sense of collective identity through interaction (e.g. Postmes et al., 1999) and that social identity influences behaviours and social norms in computer-mediated communication (e.g. Postmes et al., 1999), but very little is known about how groups develop a sense of identity in online contexts (Baym, 1995; McLaughin et al., 1995; Postmes et al., 2000). Our paper extends and complements previous research on collective identity in online forums, using qualitative data and interpretative methods.

While it has been suggested by Kolko (1995) and Herring (2004) that language in online settings is truly performative (Austin, 1962), and while there is an entire field of research focusing on the linguistic style developed in online settings (e.g. Ferrara et al., 1990; Collet &

Bellmore 1996; Crystal, 2001; Herring, 2002), little is known about the performative nature of language in online forums – i.e. how language stimulates certain behaviours and actions (Ashforth & Humphry, 1997). This paper contributes to this literature by illustrating the performative nature of discourse in online forums. It describes how participants in online forums, through their discursive practices within the forum’s language game, produce a sense of we-ness.

Our paper also extends the studies that take a discursive approach to organizational discourse (Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Phillips & Hardy, 2002; Boje et al., 2004) showing how such an approach is relevant to the understanding of online forums and virtual organizations. A strong definition of performative discourse suggests that language does not only construct reality, but also leads to and/or affects actions (e.g. Rindova et al., 2004). Ou research takes another step in that direction by introducing the concept of the language game.

Although the concept of language game has been discussed extensively in the philosophy of language (Smart, 1957; Perry, 1994), it has been used in a rather limited fashion by organizational scholars (e.g. Astley & Zammuto, 1992; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Rindova et al., 2004). Its applicability to online groups is still under-explored, except for the theoretical work of Boland & Tenkasi (1995), which suggests the relevance of the language-game perspective for the design of electronic communication systems to support communities of knowing in knowledge-intensive firms. Our empirical analysis extends the language-game approach to the electronic environment, where discourse is dynamic, ‘truly performative’, and occurs over long time periods. Our findings show that, through the enactment of the forum language game, participants enact a ‘form of life’, or culture. This form of life emerges through the interaction of the participants in the forum, and is implicitly reproduced by new members, who are able to learn the ‘rules of the game’ enacted in previous postings. Therefore, even when the core group is very small and the turnover high, as in KM Forum, participants know how to behave, and develop a sense of belonging. The notion of the language game allows us to concentrate on discursive practices, taking into account not only the conversations in which participants describe themselves as a collective (Hardy et al., 2005), but also the effects of these practices on their interactions and behaviours.

As noted in the introduction, the construction of a sense of we-ness is crucial to support participation in online forums and collaboration across organizations. Our research suggests that through the development of language games, managers could trigger and support the development of a collective identity and a shared culture in online communities and reinforce the positive effects associated with collective identity (Dutton et al. 1994; Mael & Ashforth, 1992) and culture (Alvesson, 2000; Van Maanen, 2005). Previous research (e.g. Orlikowski & Yates, 1994) has studied how specific tasks such as product development can be accomplished online through language. Our study extends this research to communities where the ‘task’ is more ambiguous and provides interesting insights for research on online communities of customers (e.g. Hagel & Armstrong 1997; Palmer et al., 2000; Jarvenpaa et al. 2000; Muñiz & O’Guinn, 2001) or extra-organizational communities (Star & Ruhleder, 2001; Wasko and Faraj, 2000; Faraj and Wasko, 2005).

## Implications for the design and management of online communities

With the limitations of our analysis in mind, i.e. our analysis is limited to analysis of only two online forums and we examined the discourse for only the first 9 months, we offer some tentative implications for design and management of online communities. Some of these guidelines reinforce those suggested in other studies (e.g. Mynatt et al., 1998; Preece, 2000; Wenger et al. 2002; Fayard & DeSanctis 2008; Silva et al., 2008) while others provide new insights.

## Create a ‘context’

As noted earlier, developing a sense of we-ness is key for active participation and knowledge exchange in online forums (e.g. Kogut & Zander, 1996; Moreland & Levine, 2002). Yet, while our analysis shows how through the enactment of discursive practices participants can produce a sense of we-ness, it is important to keep in mind that it is difficult to formalize these practices, as they are generally implicit and vary with the context. Indeed, individuals create knowledge by interacting and participating in the practices within their local communities (Lave and Wenger, 1991; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). This suggests that managers need to pay attention to local specificities such as geography and culture.

Moreover, instead of developing a set of formal rules and roles, we suggest that it is important to create ‘a context’ (see Powazek, 2001; Preece, 2000) in which the discursive practices and activities enacted by the participants can enable a sense of we-ness to develop. Two types of participants or ‘roles’ can support the process of creating such a context (see Fayard & DeSanctis, 2005). First, the facilitator/moderator, who welcomes newcomers, stirs up the discussion, and sets the pattern for the discursive practices, is instrumental in building the stage for lively interactions. Second, having a small core group of very active members who engage in a lively pattern of friendly discourse in the forum is also essential.

## Get it right as early as possible

Moreover, we note that in both forums, the language game took hold quickly, in a matter of months. In line with the findings of Jarvenpaa and Leidner (1999) on the importance of developing trust from the beginning in virtual teams, this observation suggests that one should ensure that a positive language game is developed right from the start, as it will shape future interactions and be difficult to change. Therefore, efforts have to be put forth at the beginning to set the context and behaviours that will become the basis for the forum’s language game long into its life. This is as important, if not more so, as getting the discussion going.

## Embodiment matters

Our analysis highlights the importance of embodiment and of locale for both forums. This finding suggests that forum managers might want to develop sub-communities or local chapters in order to support the emergence of a collective identity and a shared culture among members. Managers might also want to develop functionalities such as the possibility to attach pictures and/or videos or to create a profile with personal details in order to embody the different actors and their postings. The research also suggests that managers might want to organize face-to-face meetings for the participants of these communities (customers, suppli ers, organizational members).

## Limitations

Our study is limited in a number of important ways. As noted earlier, we examined only two forums, focusing on the first 9 months of their lives, and on a single topical domain: knowledge management. However, the common practices enacted in the two forums give us confidence that our observations may be generalizable. Although this repertoire is not exhaustive, and should be seen as open-ended, we believe that the five particular practices presented in this paper offer important insights into why they are particularly relevant to the construction of collective identity and culture. More longitudinal studies of forums in different domains would allow us to verify how general our findings might be.

We did not anticipate the regional connections we found in the two forums. We noted some variations between the two, such as the roles and the organizational structure. In KM Forum, Gopal is very active, and defines the rules and the interaction style with only three other active members. In KM Chapter, Sam is similarly influential, but more participants are active and perform the same sort of moderating and facilitating activities mostly performed by Gopal in KV Forum. Culture might shed some light on the differing structures. Indeed, power distance is higher in India than in Australia (Hofstede, 1991), and that might explain why the founder has a heavier hand in KM Forum, while the participation pattern is more distributed in KM Chapter. Future studies could look more specifically at the impact of culture in the activities developed in language games.

Our focus in this paper was on the emergence of shared understandings and a sense of we-ness that are both crucial for knowledge sharing. However, as previously mentioned, we are aware that this shared sense of we-ness is not as ‘simple’ and shared as it may seem. In future analyses, we would like to focus on the ambiguities and differences in interpretations between different groups in the forum and study the different emerging subcultures. Lastly, we argue that the language game approach was interesting, not only because of its discursive twist, but also and primarily because it provides a situated perspective. Language games are forms of life and are deeply embedded in the social and material context as illustrated by the language game of the builder. While our analysis presupposed the materiality of the practices involved in the language games, it did not analyse them in detail, nor did it focus on the entanglement of the social and the material in language games. In our future work, we are planning to focus more on the material aspects of the practices and explore how socio-material practices in online communities are constitutively intertwined.

## CONCLUSION

Drawing upon ideas from philosophy of language and sociology, this paper develops a grounded theoretical understanding of how collective identity and culture are enacted in online contexts. The concept of the language game, which frames our analysis, suggests tha language and action interact to construct a shared identity and culture, and that language can shape specific practices and behaviours that correspond to the group culture. Moreover, it suggests that by attending to the performative nature of language, researchers can better understand how individuals in remote contexts and virtual organizations can develop practices, routines and behaviours that might trigger and support the development of a sense of we-ness.

## Author Biographies

Anne-Laure Fayard is Assistant Professor of Management of the Polytechnic Institute of New York University Prior to moving to New York, she was a faculty member at INSEAD, both in Singapore and in Fontainebleau. Her research interests involve organizational communication (including discourse analysis, discursive practices and technology-mediated communication) and socio-materia practices (including the affordances of material artefacts and the influence of space). She holds a BA and an MA in Philosophy from Paris – La Sorbonne, an MA in Cognitive

Science from Ecole Polytechnique (Paris) and a PhD in Cognitive Science from the Ecole des Hautes-Etudes en Sciences Sociales (Paris).

Gerardine DeSanctis (1954–2005) was Thomas F. Keller Professor of Business Administration in the Fuqua School of Business at Duke University. Prior to that she was at the University of Minnesota and held visiting posi tions at Queen’s University, Technische Universiteit Delft, Erasmus University, INSEAD (Singapore and Fontaineb leau), and Nanyang Technological University, Singapore. Her research interests were in the general areas of computer-mediated work organization design distributec teams, online communities, and management of informa: tion technology. Most of her research examined the impacts of electronic communication systems on teams and organizations. She served as a Senior Editor for both Organization Science and MIS Quarterly and an Associate Editor for Management Science, a member of the advisory boards for JA/S and Information Systems Research, and a member of the editorial board for the Journal of Organiza tional Behavior.
