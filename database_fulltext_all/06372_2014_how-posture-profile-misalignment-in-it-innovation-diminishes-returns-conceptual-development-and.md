# How Posture–Profile Misalignment in IT Innovation Diminishes Returns: Conceptual Development and Empirical Demonstration

Ro ber t G. Fichman and Nige l P. Me lvi lle

He received a B.S. and M.S. in industrial and operations engineering from the University of Michigan and a Ph.D. in information technology from the MIT Sloan School of Management. His main research interests concern digital innovation, including the factors that affect the diffusion and assimilation of new IT, and the transformative effects that digital innovations have on individuals, organizations, markets, and society. The common theme of his professional background was innovative application of information systems to generate new sources of value in organizations, which is the focus of his research. Abstr act: We conceive of information technology (IT) innovation posture–profile misalignment as a condition that exists when a firm’s innovation posture (the extent to which a firm leads with IT innovation) does not match up with its innovation resource profile (the firm’s stock of resources conducive to effective innovation). We theorize that firms with a posture–profile misalignment will see diminished returns from IT adoption because they will be less likely to possess (and be less effective at exploiting) crucial innovation resources when they need them most. We demonstrate how misalignment conditions the link between IT innovation adoption and organizational performance using a data set comprising electronic networking technologies in over 25,000 U.S. manufacturing plants. Productivity regression estimations reveal a consistent pattern that the association between IT innovation adoption and productivity is substantially diminished among misaligned firms. These empirical results provide initial confirmation of the theoretical value of innovation posture, innovation resource profile, and innovation posture–profile misalignment. We consider the implications for research on business value and innovation as well as for the practice of management.

Key wor ds and p hr ases: innovation, innovation outcomes, innovation with information technology, IT adoption, IT business value, IT innovation.

Inf or mation technolog ies (IT ) incr easing ly f or m the f oundation for improved organizational processes and products. They transform the way people work, the nature of competition, and even how markets evolve. It is through the process of IT innovation adoption that technologies that are new to the firm get acquired, implemented, and assimilated into the ongoing work life of the firm [36, 73].

Nevertheless, IT innovation adoption and assimilation is not easy, as exhibited by the very mixed track record of organizations in practice. Firms such as Starbucks and Walmart provide positive examples of firms that have used IT innovation to create and sustain a competitive advantage [42, 64]. On the other side are firms such as Fox-Meyer and Rich-Con Steel that drove their operations into the ground through failed deployments of new IT  [56, 75]. Looking beyond the anecdotal, evidence suggests that half or more of firms that adopt technologies such as enterprise resource planning (ERP) and customer relationship management (CRM) fail to achieve expected benefits [10, 72].

While IT innovation is never easy, the level of challenge presented to an organization is a function of its adoption timing, that is, how early in the overall diffusion cycle the organization engages with IT innovation. Early adopters must navigate a more difficult process, because early-cycle technologies tend to be relatively immature and unproven, lack complementary products and services, and lack accumulated community wisdom about where and how to best deploy the technology to generate business value [3, 6].

The firm’s general pattern of engagement with IT innovation can be thought of as its IT innovation posture. Those firms that engage early and often have the aggressive posture of an IT innovation leader, while those that engage late and infrequently have the conservative posture of a laggard. Because firms with an aggressive innovation posture face greater innovation challenges, it is especially important for such firms to possess a relatively generous stock of human and organizational resources conducive to effective innovation, such as greater slack, more technically skilled staff, greater professionalism, and so on [36]. This collection of resources constitutes the firm’s IT innovation resource profile.

While many firms have a good fit between their pattern of engagement with IT innovation (IT innovation posture) and the level of their innovation resources (IT innovation resource profile), some do not. In other words, some firms have the aggressive innovation posture of an IT innovation leader, but the sparse resource profile more characteristic of an innovation laggard (or vice versa). We refer to this situation as an IT innovation posture–profile misalignment.

In this research, we develop the notion of IT innovation posture–profile misalignment, theorize about the impact of misalignment on returns to IT innovation, and conduct an empirical demonstration that confirms (tentatively) our theorizing. We investigate the following research question:

## RQ: What is IT innovation posture–profile misalignment and how does such misalignment condition the returns to IT innovation?

We theorize that misaligned firms will see diminished productivity benefits from any given level of innovation because they will be less likely to have an appropriate mix of innovation resources upon which to draw, and also because they will be less likely to put whatever resources they do have to appropriate use. To provide an empirical basis for our theorizing, we use data on the adoption of innovative electronic networking technologies in over 25,000 U.S. manufacturing plants. We find that, consistent with our fundamental thesis, posture–profile misalignment negatively moderates the relationship between IT innovation and plant productivity. Overall, the labor productivity reduction (total value of shipments to total employment) associated with any given level of IT adoption in the presence of posture–profile misalignment ranges from 50 to 75 percent. In other words, our results suggest that the productivity boost associated with IT drops by more than half in the presence of IT innovation posture– profile misalignment.

In sum, this study makes three primary contributions to the literature: (1) development of the concepts of IT innovation posture, profile, and posture-profile misalignment; (2) theoretical development explaining the effects that misalignment should have on the innovation–performance link; and (3) presentation of the first empirical evidence consistent with the prediction that misalignment diminishes the returns to IT innovation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first develop the ideas of IT innovation posture, profile, and posture–profile misalignment. Next, we theorize about the link between misalignment and IT innovation returns and develop our propositions. Then, we discuss our research methodology and present results of a two-stage estimation procedure comprising a Poisson count adoption model and instrumental variables regression productivity model. Finally, we discuss implications of our research for theory and practice.

## Theoretical Development

## IT Innovation Posture

In an org anizational context, an innovation is defined as any idea, practice, or material artifact that is perceived as new by the adopting unit [73, 88]. Therefore, what matters is not newness to the world but, rather, newness to the prospective adopter. As a result, when examining organizational decisions, the adoption of a technology “counts” as an innovation even for laggard organizations that adopt well after the technology has reached the mainstream.

The organizational decision processes surrounding IT innovation adoption play out at two main levels. First, there is an innovation-specific level wherein managers go through some variant of a four-stage process consisting of (1) comprehension (making sense of a particular innovation), (2) adoption (developing a rationale about whether and when to adopt), (3) implementation (managing the physical installation and organizational transition), and (4) assimilation (absorbing the innovation into the work life of the firm) [79]. The majority of prior empirical work on IT innovation has been positioned at this innovation-specific level, with both case studies and quantitative empirical studies enriching our understanding of the adoption and assimilation of particular innovations (e.g., [22, 54, 69]).

However, innovation processes can also play out at the firm level, wherein managers determine what their firm’s general posture toward IT innovation is and ought to be. It is at this second level where managers come to an understanding (implicit or explicit) about the firm’s usual or taken-for-granted stance toward IT innovation. In our field work we have heard managers make statements such as “we try to be leading edge, but not bleeding edge” or “around here, we like to say ‘you can know the technology pioneers by the arrows in their backs.’” These are the layperson’s way of articulating a firm’s posture toward IT innovation.

Interestingly, this more general level of innovation engagement has received little conceptual attention in prior work, although that appears to be changing. In fact, Chen et al. [18] put IT innovation front and center in their approach to defining a firm’s overall information systems (IS) strategy. In particular, they operationalize IS strategy “as the degree to which the organization has a shared perspective to seek innovation through IS” [18, p. 233]. They classify firms as having the strategy of an IS innovator (characterized by the desire to be among the first in their industry to explore and exploit innovative opportunities) or an IS conservative (characterized by the desire to exploit IS innovations only after they have been thoroughly vetted and proven effective by others). They also recognize the possibility of an undefined strategy (characterized by an absence of clear long-term goals for IS in the organization and an inconsistent pattern of IS innovation behavior). Based on data from 174 matched pairs of chief information officers (CIOs) and business executives, Chen et al. find that measures for the three categories exhibit good convergent and discriminant validity, and that CIOs and executives were consistent in their assessments of the firm’s IS strategy.

While IT innovation is likely only one component of a firm’s overall IS strategy, Chen et al.’s [18] research provides empirical support for the notion that firms may have such a thing as a general posture toward IT innovation. Therefore, following Chen et al., we define a firm’s IT innovation posture as reflecting the extent to which a firm generally seeks to take a leading role when innovating through IT. This leading role could pertain to both earliness of adoption and the speed/extent of implementation. Firms can have the aggressive innovation posture of leader, the conservative posture of a laggard, or somewhere in between. For example, evidence from the business press strongly suggests that firms such as UPS [51] and YCH Group [81] have an avowedly aggressive innovation posture. One key point of differentiation is while Chen et al. emphasize the importance of a shared perspective among managers for an IS strategy to exist, we assert that a firm possesses an aggressive or conservative innovation posture based on its actual pattern of innovation behaviors, and so there does not always need to be a shared perspective among managers for a posture to exist [17].

## IT Innovation Resource Profile

In contrast to the firm’s innovation posture, which as we have defined it refers to its historical pattern of actual innovation behavior, we conceive a firm’s IT innovation resource profile as reflecting its stock of human and organizational resources conducive to efficient and effective innovation with IT. A firm can have the generous resource profile seen most often among innovator leaders, the sparse profile seen most often among laggards, or somewhere in between. For example, based on its documented innovation capabilities [51] UPS would appear to have a generous resource profile. As we will argue below, some firms—particularly those that do not innovate mindfully— can have a large discrepancy between their posture and profile. In other words, they can have the aggressive posture of an innovation leader but the sparse resource profile of an innovation laggard, or vice versa. Examining the case of Kmart’s response to Walmart’s aggressive use of IT innovation for supply chain management suggests that Kmart had an aggressive posture but inadequate resource profile [64].

How then to determine which factors constitute the resource profile of innovation leaders? Several approaches are possible. One approach is to reason through the question theoretically, although it would be difficult to draw clean conceptual boundaries regarding which sorts of factors to consider given the tremendous variety of potentially relevant resources. Another approach would be to survey organizations to determine their perceptions of what resources matter most, although such a survey would likely suffer from standard survey limitations such as common method bias. A third approach is to leverage the substantial accumulated knowledge from the hundreds of prior studies of IT innovation in organizations about what the resource profile of IT innovators tends to look like, and then to include, from among those factors, the ones that have a clear rationale linking the variable to a firm’s propensity to derive value from being an innovation leader.

Along these lines, prior research has demonstrated that while there is much variation in the circumstances associated with earlier and more extensive innovation by organizations, there is also much regularity. For example, a meta-analysis by Damanpour [23] showed that the most innovative organizations tended to be large, professional, formalized, highly endowed with expertise and skill, run by senior managers who were more open to new ideas, and so forth. In the IT domain, Swanson [78], having observed certain regularities in prior empirical results, developed a general theory of IT innovation in which early adoption is predicted to be more likely among organizations that are larger and more diversified, more professionally oriented, possesses slack resources, and are part of a host organization where IS is more strategically relevant. In a later review of scores of studies of IT adoption in organizations, Jeyaraj et al. [49] found that organizational size, top management support, professionalism, use of external information sources, and external pressure were among the most consistent positive correlates of innovation adoption. Fichman [38] discussed the idea of the innovation profile, and noted the economic-rationalistic orientation of most profile characteristics examined in prior studies.

Building on this stream of results, we assert that the generosity of a firm’s innovation resource profile can be assessed based on a set of general resource characteristics that (1) have been commonly observed among innovators in a wide range of studies, and (2) possess straightforward rationales that link the presence of these characteristics to more efficient or effective innovation. Depending on the degree to which it ranks higher or lower on an index of such characteristics, a firm can have the innovation resource profile typical of innovation leaders, or innovation laggards, or somewhere in between.

For a characteristic to be included as part of the innovation resource profile, we suggest that it must meet both criteria in the above definition, meaning that narrow or arcane characteristics, or ones that do not have a clear linkage to effective innovation, would be excluded. We note that the generosity of a firm’s innovation resource profile is not cast in stone; rather, managers can act to enhance their resource profiles over time. While some innovation resources, such as size and slack, are not highly amenable to managerial control, other “softer” innovation resources, such as top management support and a culture that encourages and rewards innovation, can evolve over time through a concerted managerial effort. That said, managers in firms with lesser size and slack may find that they have fewer degrees of freedom when it comes to developing softer innovation resources.

## IT Innovation Posture–Profile Misalignment

In any given population there will be some firms that have a strong alignment between their IT innovation posture and resource profile, and others with a large deviation between posture and profile. More formally, we define IT innovation posture–profile misalignment as the level of deviation between the relative aggressiveness of a firm’s innovation behavior and the relative abundance of its innovation-related sources. A large misalignment happens when a firm has a pattern of taking the lead with innovation even though it has the sparse resource profile of a laggard, or vice versa.

While some organizations could achieve a state of alignment by accident, it is more likely that the extent of alignment will result from a firm’s pattern of managerial decision making. We see this pattern of decisions playing out at two levels: a strategic/ organizational level that transcends any particular innovation initiative and a tactical/ innovation level that pertains to particular innovation initiatives. Both levels of decisions serve to produce and reproduce the firm’s overall state of alignment over time (the variable we have actually measured).

At the strategic/organizational level, we can expect that some firms will take conscious steps to ensure their profile and posture become and remain aligned over time. Organizations that have decided that their innovation posture should be (generally) more aggressive in the future might first set about enhancing their innovation resource profile. Such a firm might change its hiring and training policies, or make it known to executives that innovation is taking a higher priority and should therefore receive greater attention and support from them. For example, Starbucks is well known for its aggressive posture toward IT-enabled innovation (most recently manifested in its pioneering use of social media as a means to connect with customers) as well as for a valuable innovation resource, namely, its technology-savvy chief executive officer [42]. Conversely, a firm that recognizes that its resource profile is sparse might send out the word that the organization should as a general rule avoid being on the leading edge when it comes innovation.

Alignment is also enacted at the innovation level, through a firm’s pattern of tactical decisions surrounding the particular IT innovations it encounters. For example, a firm with sparse resources that is considering early adoption of a promising innovation can reflect on whether it makes sense, given the state of its current resource profile, to undertake the additional risk and expense of being an innovation leader [79], and may choose instead to take things slowly.<sup>1</sup>

Conversely, some firms may fail to consider these sorts of issues—either at the organizational or innovation-specific levels. Rather, the innovation posture of the firm (perhaps not even explicitly recognized) may arise from a series of innovation encounters that are driven by other factors—such as serendipity, popularity of the innovation in question, imitation of competitors, the whims of particular managers—that have little to do with the resource profile of the firm. In fact, there are a number of causal mechanisms that could lead to a significant incidence of innovation posture–profile misalignments in a group of firms. These include agency conflicts (the tendency of some managers to prefer actions that benefit them personally at the cost of the firm [33]), information cascades (the tendency of decision makers to base their choices on observations of others’ choices more so than private information [11, 84]), and managerial fashions (the tendency of organizations to adopt innovations that are the most prominent topics of discourse [1, 86]). Although the mechanisms differ, these perspectives all provide explanations for why some firms might go along with the pack without giving much attention to their innovation resource profile as a factor in their ongoing adoption decisions.

## Research Model and Propositions

positive linkage between IT and organizational performance is well established [52, 60]. As Dehning et al. note, the question for IT researchers and practitioners today is not “do investments in IT payoff?” but, rather, “under what conditions do investments in IT pay off?” [24, p. 628]. In line with this large body of literature, our conceptual model not only posits a direct effect of IT innovation on organizational performance but also examines the moderating effect of innovation posture–profile misalignment. In particular, we posit that misalignment negatively moderates the link between IT innovation and organizational performance. We now explicate key constructs and theoretical propositions.

## Innovation Posture and Organizational Performance

As described above, a firm’s IT innovation posture reflects the extent to which it generally seeks to take a leading role when innovating through IT. Firms with a more aggressive posture will tend to adopt a given set of innovations earlier and more extensively. The rationale for a positive association between IT innovation and organizational performance is based on theoretical considerations as well as a large body of prior research.

At the most basic level, the propensity to adopt and use innovative IT can be viewed as creating a particular kind of IT capital. According to microeconomic theory, a rational firm should invest in any particular form of capital just to the point where the last unit of investment produces no more value than its costs. Since costs of IT capital are positive, the gross marginal productivity contribution of IT should likewise be positive [43]. Many studies have verified a positive association between IT capital and productivity, both when IT is defined as aggregated IT investment [25] and when it is defined as the use of particular technologies [9, 44, 63]. Although additional arguments can be developed for why the propensity to adopt and use IT innovations should improve productivity (e.g., IT innovations might enable a more efficient organization of production), the interpretation of IT innovation as producing a specialized form of capital suffices for our current purpose.<sup>2</sup> Therefore we propose:

Proposition 1: A more aggressive IT innovation posture leads to higher organizational performance, ceteris paribus.

## Moderating Effect of Misalignment

In this section, we provide our logic for why IT innovation posture–profile misalignment should lead to diminished returns from any given level of IT innovation. We provide two interrelated arguments. First, we argue that misaligned firms are less likely to have generous innovation resources when they need them most. Second, we argue that misaligned firms are less likely to put whatever innovation resources they do possess to effective use. Either way, the quality of innovation implementations should suffer, which implies that the extent of benefits to be derived from any given level of innovation use will also be diminished.

On the first point, let us consider those firms that engage in a matching process to align the aggressiveness of their innovation posture with the abundance of their innovation resources. Such firms tend to lead only when they have the generous innovation resources of innovators (greater slack, more technically skilled staff, greater professionalism, etc.). By contrast, some firms—perhaps lacking the bulwark (against bandwagon pressures) of contextually differentiated reasoning that mindful firms possess [40]—will be more prone to exhibit a pattern of early adoption even when innovation resources are sparse. Such firms are apt to be especially susceptible to the forces of managerial fads and fashions [1] and to uncritically accept the universalistic and noncontingent way in which an innovation’s benefits are portrayed by innovation promoters [79]. This may lead firms into a “too aggressive” form of misalignment, where their posture is too innovative given the state of their resources.

Having an abundance of innovation resources is especially crucial among firms with an aggressive innovation posture because adoption barriers are typically much higher when innovations are new [3, 29]. In the beginning of the diffusion cycle, the commercial products that embody IT innovations are immature and possess a narrower feature set. There is a relatively thin availability of external experts, complementary products, and accumulated community wisdom about how best to deploy and use the technology. Firms that take an aggressive innovation posture despite a sparse set of innovation resources are less likely to be able to navigate these extra challenges as effectively relative to those that have more generous resources. They should also do worse than sparsely endowed firms that choose to delay adoption until these challenges have been moderated by accumulated investments and knowledge pertaining to the innovation. As a result, misaligned firms should be prone to experience lower-quality implementations, which diminishes the value-producing potential of the innovation.

In addition, a second rationale for misalignment as a negative moderator becomes apparent when we consider that innovation mindfulness [79] is apt to be inversely correlated with innovation posture–profile misalignment. This provides a rationale for why firms with a “too conservative” form of misalignment might also experience difficulties adopting technology in a way that produces value. More specifically, we posit that firms with a large misalignment are likely to be less mindful than those with a small misalignment, and so will be prone to make less effective use of the innovation resources they do possess. Our argument proceeds as follows. Regarding the link between mindfulness and IT posture–profile misalignment, Fiol and O’Connor [40] posited that a lack of mindfulness accounts for the propensity of firms to jump on innovation bandwagons without taking into account the extent to which the innovation should be beneficial given their organization’s specific situation. In the IT context, Swanson and Ramiller classify an organization as innovating mindfully when it “attends to innovation with reasoning grounded in its own facts and specifics” [79, p. 559] and further explain that these situational specifics “can be quite complex, including, among other issues, the innovation’s ramifications for operational efficiencies and strategic advantage; the organization’s preparedness for the change involved; the quality and availability of complementary resources needed; implications for various common and conflicting interests, both internally and in inter-firm relationships;

and the effects of adoption on the firm’s legitimacy with outside constituencies” [79, p. 559]. A less-mindful organization, by contrast, makes adoption decisions without a reasoning grounded in situational specifics.

These definitions suggest a strong linkage between innovation mindfulness and innovation posture–profile misalignment. In particular, we should expect that mindful organizations will be more likely to consciously seek a state where there is a good fit between their actual patterns of innovation adoption (leader or laggard) and their innovation profile characteristics (generous or sparse), and will hence be less prone to experience inadvertent innovation posture–profile misalignments. By contrast, the less-mindful organization should be highly prone to such misalignments. Thus, while we do not have a measure for mindfulness in this study, we assume there will exist a close relationship between this construct and alignment.

If it is true that innovation mindlessness and innovation posture–profile misalignment often go hand in hand, this suggests a second channel for misalignment to negatively moderate the IT innovation-to-performance linkage. Unmindful organizations will likely make less effective use of whatever resources they do possess, because unmindful firms lack the preoccupation with failure, the reluctance to simplify interpretations, and the reliance on expertise over formal authority [87] that characterizes mindful engagement.

Preoccupation with failure means managers are always cognizant of—or even obsessed with [79]—the possibility of failure, and so are always on the lookout for conditions or pitfalls that could forestall success of particular initiatives. Managers who lack this preoccupation, by contrast, are less likely to perceive the salience of the many pitfalls that litter the path from adoption to assimilation and then on to captured business value. These pitfalls include choosing the wrong instance of the technology, or improperly configuring it, or installing it badly, or finding that intended users resist it, or failing to make crucial complementary changes to the surrounding organization [16, 60]. If less-mindful managers are less likely to perceive the salience of these pitfalls, it follows that they will be less likely to allocate the right resources or to take actions to avoid these pitfalls and thereby achieve more effective implementation at reasonable cost.

An absence of two of the other qualities of mindful managers—the reluctance to simplify interpretations and the reliance on expertise over formal authority—can be seen as exacerbating the ill-effects just described. For example, managers who simplify interpretations may be prone to think things such as “if this package is from a good vendor, then it will probably be well suited to our needs” (when in reality it is not), or may think “this package embeds ‘best practices’ we ought to adopt” without consideration of how well those practices actually synchronize with other elements of the firm’s competencies or strategies. Likewise, managers who do not value expertise over formal authority may be reluctant to give proper credence to the warnings of the experts actually doing the implementation when pitfalls arise that require a significant change of course. They may be prone to discount the importance of expertise when assigning people to key positions on the implementation project, perhaps favoring instead people who are more senior or politically powerful.

Therefore, based on the above two arguments (i.e., that misaligned firms of the “too aggressive” variety will less likely to possess the kinds of resources necessary to succeed with the level of innovation they have chosen, and that all misaligned firms will be less likely to make best use of the resources they do possess), we propose:<sup>3</sup>

Proposition 2: The IT posture–profile misalignment diminishes the strength of the positive association between aggressiveness of innovation posture and organizational performance.

Empirical Illustration—Methodology

Empirical Model

The intuition of our emp ir ical model is to regress the extent of a plant’s IT innovation on measures of its innovation resources and use the residuals as a proxy for posture–profile misalignment. The larger a given residual, the more likely there will be a misalignment for that particular plant. In a second-stage performance regression, we use first-stage residuals to examine whether misalignment moderates the association between IT innovation and productivity. This two-stage approach builds on prior research. In the IT innovation domain, Fichman [38] suggested the use of residuals for this purpose. Beyond IT, other research streams have demonstrated the validity of using residuals in similar approaches [26, 47, 62]. For example, Ittner et al. [47] use a multivariate regression predicting activity-based costing (ABC ) adoption to compute two separate measures of deviations using positive and negative residuals. These measures were subsequently used as predictors in manufacturing plant performance regressions. Similarly, Dewar and Werbel [26] use the absolute value of residuals derived from regressions of structure and control variables on the routineness of technology as measures of deviation. We use a single measure (greater than one standard deviation above or below the mean of the residuals) as a dichotomous measure of misalignment. As we discuss below, we use a common set of control variables in both the first-stage and second-stage regressions.

To demonstrate that there is no a priori reason for our misalignment operationalization to moderate the association between IT innovation and productivity, we conducted an extensive set of simulations. We constructed stage 1 regressors using standard data-generating processes (multivariate normal data). The dependent variable (INNOVATION ) is computed as a weighted sum of the regressors, all of which conformed to the general characteristics of the data in our sample (means, standard deviations, correlations). The stage 1 simulation regression takes the following general form, where different sets of betas were chosen in different simulation experiments and Gaussian noise was added in all experiments:

$$
I N N O V A T I O N = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} x _ {1} + \beta_ {2} x _ {2} + \beta_ {3} x _ {3} + \beta_ {4} x _ {4} + \beta_ {5} x _ {5}.
$$

We used the residuals from this regression to construct our misalignment variable as zero if within plus or minus one standard deviation of the mean, and 1 otherwise.

We then constructed stage 2 regressors using IT innovation and two variables from stage 1 to compute a labor productivity variable (again, to match our actual specification as closely as possible). Two additional variables in stage 2 were generated from a multivariate normal density. The simulation specification for the stage 2 performance regression for generating the regressand (performance variable) thus takes the following form, with an added term of white noise:

$$
P E R F O R M A N C E = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I N N O V A T I O N + \alpha_ {4} z _ {4} + \alpha_ {5} z _ {5} + \alpha_ {6} x _ {3} + \alpha_ {7} x _ {4}.
$$

In the last step, we estimated the full interaction model and examined whether the interaction term was significant over a large number of replications:

$$
\begin{array}{c} P E R F O R M A N C E = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} I N N O V A T I O N + \alpha_ {2} D E V + \alpha_ {3} I N N O V A T I O N ^ {*} D E V \\ + \alpha_ {4} z _ {4} + \alpha_ {5} z _ {5} + \alpha_ {6} x _ {3} + \alpha_ {7} x _ {4}. \end{array}
$$

If there is something about the nature of our empirical modeling specification that yields a high likelihood of a moderating effect being significant, this will show up as a large number of replications containing p-values of less than 0.05.

To illustrate the overall pattern of results, we plotted histograms of the main effect (DEV ) and interaction effect (INNOVATION\*DEV) over 1,000 replications—both for estimates and p-values—across various experimental runs. Results across all variations of parameters exhibited the same pattern (Figure 1): it is extremely unlikely that misalignment significantly moderating the IT innovation–performance link will occur randomly using our empirical estimation approach. The interaction term is centered on zero and is roughly normally distributed; the p-value approximates a uniform distribution centered on 0.5 (kernel density and normal density are overlaid for comparison). The implication is that in very few cases does misalignment significantly moderate the link between IT innovation and performance on a random basis. (The R code used to perform simulation experiments is available upon request from authors).

## Construct Operationalization

## Innovation Posture and IT Innovation Extent

We operationalize a plant’s innovation posture using the variable IT innovation extent, which we measure as the sum of the number of electronic networking technologies reported to be in use at the plant (see Table 1). One advantage of aggregating over this set of technologies is that they are diverse in terms of their positions in the overall diffusion cycle as of 1999. In other words, some of these technologies were later in the diffusion cycle (e.g., LAN [local area network], EDI) while others were much earlier in the cycle (e.g., intranets and extranets). As a result, our aggregate measure can capture greater extremes in conservative behavior (i.e., plants that are not yet reporting “in use” for late-cycle innovations) as well as extremes of aggressive behavior (i.e., plants that are already reporting “in use” for early-cycle innovations).<sup>6</sup>

That said, we acknowledge that an ideal measure of innovation posture would capture more fine-grained information about timing of adoption and extent of implementation for these technologies. However, we note that the number of innovations adopted in a set of innovations as of a particular date tends to be positively correlated with finegrained measures, such as average earliness of adoption and the average extent of assimilation for innovations in the set [37, 91].<sup>7</sup> The reason for these correlations is that if a firm tends to adopt early, then there is a greater chance for those adoptions to have occurred prior to any given point in time, which will lead to a larger number of innovations reported as in use when surveyed. Also, a firm that has adopted innovations earlier will have had more time to assimilate and use those innovations more extensively by the time of the survey date. As a result, we feel comfortable that our count measure of technologies in use as of 1999 represents a reasonable proxy for our underlying concept of innovation posture.

## Innovation Posture–Profile Misalignment

To construct our measure of innovation posture–profile misalignment, we compare the plant’s extent of innovation (our operational measure of innovation posture) to an aggregate measure of the plant’s innovation resources (our operational measure for innovation resource profile). We selected measures for innovation resources by inspecting the variables available in our secondary data set, mapping them to variables that have featured prominently in prior conceptual and empirical work in IT innovation, and then reflecting on whether clear rationales exist that link the variables to more efficient or effective innovation. This resulted in the selection of four characteristics: plant size, plant age, multiplant status, and employee skill.

Plant Size. Size is operationalized as total employees at the plant (see Table 2). Size of the adopting unit has been found to be perhaps the most consistent positive correlate of innovation in general [23] and IT innovation in particular [49]. Size features prominently in Swanson’s [78] model of IT innovation. As argued by Tornatzky and Fleischer [83, p. 162], size per se has no clear theoretical relationship with innovation but, rather, serves as a proxy for several other variables that do theoretically relate to innovation, including scale, slack resources, professionalism, and specialization. Most of these other variables have clear conceptual linkages to more efficient and effective innovation. For example, Fichman and Kemerer [39] have argued that a larger scale of activities in which the innovation can be used should promote the ability to amortize fixed innovation costs. This, in turn, can smooth the way to greater investment in mechanisms—training, consultants, experiential learning—to enhance the implementation process. Similarly, slack resources allow an organization to bear the costs of instituting innovations and to absorb setbacks along the way [23, 78]. Large organizations tend to be more professional, which, as described below, has positive implications for innovation.

Plant Age. Age is operationalized as the time since the plant opened, in years. The age of the adopting unit has appeared in a large number of studies on technology innovation in manufacturing [5, 20, 31].<sup>8</sup> Greater youth has been hypothesized to promote more efficient innovation in this domain, in that new plants have the opportunity to invest in the latest technology without incurring costs associated with switching from older technologies implemented in prior years. Also, younger plants have had less time to acquire entrenched structures and routines that might hamper effective innovation and change.

Multiplant Status. We view a plant’s status as being part of a multiplant firm (or not) as tapping into three mechanisms that are conducive to more efficient and effective innovation with IT. First, multiplant firms are more likely than single-plant firms to have a corporate staff of professional managers [5] and other corporate-level resources (such as programmers who can be loaned from one unit to another [41]). Professionalism of the adopting unit has been consistently linked to organizational innovation in general [23], and also features prominently as a positive determinant of IT innovation in Swanson’s model [78] and prior work on IT innovation [90]. Greater professionalism increases boundary-spanning activity, self-confidence, and a commitment to move beyond the status quo [23]. As a result, increased professionalism should increase the innovative abilities of an organization.

Second, multiplant firms tend to have a larger headquarters staff, and among them there may be people who are wholly devoted to innovation-related activities such as scanning for new technologies, evaluating commercial products, hiring consultants, managing innovation projects, and sharing lessons across the firm.

Third, multiplant firms have the opportunity to spread the fixed costs of innovation [39] across more than one plant-level implementation, and also have enhanced learning opportunities, as the lessons from one plant-level implementation can be folded into the next one [31].

Employee Skill. IT innovation is difficult, and requires skilled employees to execute successfully. Skilled employees are required to manage a successful implementation process; skill also promotes the ability of each user to learn about and adapt to new technologies and routines. Also, evidence exists that IT has greater benefits for skilled employees, which should increase their willingness to accept the innovation [5, 14]. Employee skill is operationalized as the ratio of nonproduction labor to total labor [5, 21, 28].

Putting the above characteristics together, plants that are large, young, and part of multiplant firms, and that have employees that are more skilled, have the generous innovation resource profile of an innovator. By contrast, plants that are small and old and not part of a multiplant firm, and have less skilled employees, have the sparse profile of a laggard. Of course, there is a continuum between these profiles, and organizations with a mixture of characteristics would fall somewhere in between these two extremes.

One approach to aggregating these characteristics into a measure of the extent of innovation resources would be to create a simple unweighted average of standardized characteristics (with age reverse scored). However, this approach rests on the highly unrealistic assumption that all these characteristics are equally important.

An alternative approach is to weight each characteristic by its importance, as determined by its extent of association with innovation adoption. To do that, we employ an innovation adoption prediction model and use the model coefficients to weight the factors (as in the simulation experiment discussed earlier). More specifically, we use each firm’s fitted score from the adoption prediction model to comprise its extent of innovation resources. We treat organizations with a high fitted score as having the generous resource profile of an innovator, while we treat those with a low fitted score as having the sparse resource profile of a laggard.

The use of a fitted score to identify each firm’s resource profile position suggests an obvious measure for innovation posture–profile deviation, which is the absolute value of residuals from the innovation adoption model. Firms that have an aggressive innovation posture (i.e., extensive innovation) together with a sparse resource profile (smaller, older, etc.) will have large positive residuals, while those that have a conservative innovation posture together with a generous resource profile will have large negative residuals.

We employ a plant-level model to estimate the predictors of innovation adoption [22, 34, 71]. Our adoption prediction model is expected to explain a significant amount of variance in INNOVATION given the arguments presented earlier and the findings of prior research. The model is given by

$$
I N N O V A T I O N = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S K I L L + \beta_ {2} A G E + \beta_ {3} S I Z E + \beta_ {4} M P + C O N T R O L + \varepsilon .\tag{1}
$$

We include industry sector dummies as controls (see Table 2) because IT investment intensity varies with economic sector [59, 77] and recent studies have emphasized the important role of industry in studies of IS adoption [85]. For example, Chiasson and Davidson [19] suggest that certain industries lag in their adoption of IT, possibly due to the effect of the industry environment.

After estimation, we use the residuals to determine innovation posture–profile deviation (DEV ): 1 if residuals are in the top or bottom 25 percentile, and 0 otherwise. Analogously, we compute two variants of the deviation, one to identify firms with a deviation suggestive of an overly aggressive posture (DEV‑A) (plants in top 25 percentile of residuals) and those with a deviation suggestive of an overly conservative posture (DEV‑C ) (plants in the bottom 25 percentile of residuals). Statistical inference is insensitive to the precise choice of residual cutoffs, as discussed below.

## Organizational Performance

To test our propositions, we estimate a plant-level performance model that uses labor productivity as the performance measure, includes innovation extent (our measure of innovation posture) as a direct predictor and innovation posture–profile deviation (our measure of misalignment) as a moderator of the effect of innovation extent on performance.

Improving productivity has been an important goal of innovative activity associated with IS [12, 14, 60, 65], so we use labor productivity (LP) as our measure of plant performance. Similar to prior research examining the productivity implications of IT  [5, 12], we predict labor productivity using the capital-labor ratio (KL), materials-labor ratio (ML), and controls for size and industry (CONTROL). To test our propositions, we also include an interactive term of posture–profile deviation (DEV ) and electronic networking technologies in use (INNOVATION ) as well as main effects of each. The plant performance model is presented as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} L P = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} I N N O V A T I O N + \beta_ {2} D E V + \beta_ {3} I N N O V A T I O N \times D E V \\ + \beta_ {4} K L + \beta_ {5} M L + C O N T R O L + \varepsilon . \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

In Equation (2), the estimated coefficient on the interactive term $( { \mathfrak { P } } _ { 3 } )$ is used to assess Proposition 2, while ${ \partial L P / \partial I N N O V A T I O N = \ B { \beta } _ { 1 } + \ B { \beta } _ { 3 } \times D E V }$ is used to assess Proposition 1. Analogous estimation models are employed that use DEV‑A and DEV‑C. As described earlier, simulation results demonstrate the validity of the two-stage approach described above in that both the deviation and the interaction terms are statistically nonsignificant in the absence of an underlying causal mechanism.

It is possible that more productive plants are more likely to adopt IT innovations, which may lead to endogeneity and simultaneity bias. Use of instrumental variables is one approach to account for unobserved heterogeneity that gets included in the disturbance term and can cause biased estimations. To correct for this possibility, we reestimated our split sample productivity formulation by instrumenting INNOVATION with other technology variables that do not affect productivity directly (due to low reach and range), yet are correlated with INNOVATION (due to being technology complements): computer-aided design and online access to product catalogs.<sup>9</sup> The last two columns of Table 4 indicate that when controlling for potential endogeneity, Propositions 1 and 2 remain supported. We assessed the validity of our choice of instruments by examining first-stage IV estimate results, which reveals a high F-statistic and R<sup>2</sup> in stage 1. We also estimated the Sargan statistic, which regresses the residuals from IV stage 1 on included and excluded exogenous variables and tests the hypothesis that all coefficients are jointly zero. Our test statistic $( p > 0 . 1 )$ supports retention of the null that all are zero. Taken together, first-stage IV regression results combined with the support for our choice of instruments provide further confidence in our posture–profile deviation IV estimation results supporting Propositions 1 and 2.

## Sensitivity Analysis and Robustness Checks

We conducted additional analyses to examine whether our results are sensitive to modeling assumptions. First, we used alternative cutoff levels for our posture–profile deviation computations, including 20 percentile and 1 standard deviation.<sup>11</sup> Rerunning productivity regression estimations using posture–profile deviation measures based on these alternative measures resulted in no change in signs or significance in coefficient estimates, which rules out the possibility that our results are sensitive to the particular choice of residual cutoff parameter. Second, we found the same signs and significance in stage 1 when using different subsets of adoption predictors in stage 1 (e.g., leaving out size or multiplant), which helps to rule out the possibility that our results are driven by a particular set of adoption predictors. Third, to check the sensitivity of the results to the choice of dependent variable in the productivity models we reran all productivity specifications using labor productivity based on value added rather than total value of shipments. This resulted in a similar pattern of results to the pattern of results using shipments. Fourth, given that posture-profile deviation categories differ slightly across the OLS and Poisson specifications, we reran all productivity models using posture-profile deviation variables generated from our logged (INNOVATION ) OLS specification. No change in the overall pattern of results was observed. Fifth, we examined alternatives to heteroskedasticity-corrected standard errors.<sup>12</sup> Given the high number of single-plant firms (roughly 30 percent) and the low average number of plants in multiplant firms, clustering standard errors at the firm level was not necessary [74]. Also, dichotomizing the residuals in stage 1 and using this variable in stage 2 is not likely to be a problem as we are not using a continuous measure [67] and given our large sample size. However, we did rerun all the specifications using bootstrapped standard errors, given that assumptions of computed standard errors may be violated. The significance levels were maintained in all cases. Sixth, our 21 NAICS3 (three-digit NAICS) industry groupings may not provide sufficient granularity to control for industry differences. Given the need to control for unobserved heterogeneity in industry-level demand for technology that may bias results, we reran all models using NAICS4 (four-digit NAICS). Significance levels were maintained in all cases.<sup>13</sup>

Finally, earlier we analyzed the sensitivity of our results to the potential of one form of endogeneity. We reported results based on OLS and IV regression. The latter assumes that we used good instruments, which is consistent with our Sargan test results. Also, IV estimation does not necessarily solve potential endogeneity problems, although we included it for completeness. Furthermore, we cannot know for sure (without running an experiment) the extent to which our chosen instruments satisfy all assumptions, despite our choosing the best available instruments of which we are aware. Thus, we conducted a simulation analysis to condition our results to the extent to which endogeneity (if present) may be biasing our estimations and inferences. Our approach follows guidelines from prior research on endogeneity [53]. The procedure involved generating data with similar properties to ours and adding an endogenous regressor that is correlated with INNOVATION. We then estimated the productivity model regression with and without inclusion of the unobserved correlated variable and plotted the estimation bias of the term of interest (DEV × INNOVATION). We also computed inferential agreement. We varied the magnitude of correlation and noted the value that meets a 90 percent inferential agreement: values of correlation on the range [–0.33, 0.31]. We thus condition our results to two things: Either (1) our chosen instruments are good instruments or (2) there does not exist an omitted variable that is correlated with INNOVATION greater than 0.31 or less than –0.33.

## Discussion

In this p ap er we develop ed a theor etical arg ument for why the degree of misalignment between an organization’s innovation posture and its innovation profile should negatively moderate the link between IT innovation posture and performance. Consistent with our fundamental thesis, empirical results reveal a statistically significant performance penalty associated with large innovation posture–profile deviations. We now briefly consider implications of these results for research and practice.

## Implications for Research

Our empirical results provide initial confirmation of the theoretical value and managerial relevance of the new concepts introduced here, namely, innovation posture, innovation resource profile, and innovation posture–profile misalignment. Previous studies have urged researchers to examine problems at the intersection of IT innovation and IT business value [38, 50] and to analyze organizational factors that affect the returns to IT investment [16, 60]. This study incorporates both of these suggestions to develop new knowledge at the nexus of both research streams.

More broadly, prior studies have variously argued for (1) a direct link between IT and performance [15], (2) an indirect link operating through mediating variables such as capabilities [7], and (3) a contingent link that depends on other factors such as the presence of complementarities [14, 89]. Our study provides new evidence for a contingent link between IT and performance, and suggests that posture–profile misalignment holds promise as a new variable in explaining the complex linkage between IT innovation and performance. In this way, we refine prior findings that IT adoption has a direct correlation with firm-level profit: Our results suggest a more complex situation, where the extent to which “more is better” depends on the degree to which a firm has aligned the aggressiveness of its innovation posture with the level of its innovation resources.

Our approach overlaps with the idea of complementarities, but also differs. Regarding overlap, one of the characteristics comprising the innovation profile in this study— skill—has been shown to complement IT  [14]. Also complementarities, just as misalignment, are theorized to moderate the IT–performance relationship. Nevertheless, our approach is different in important respects. The complementarities approach has no direct measurement of the extent of matching or alignment.<sup>14</sup> Innovation complements are presumed to magnify returns regardless of whether a firm has matched its level of innovation to the level of its complements.

Looking at this another way, the complementarities framework does not incorporate time as it relates to innovation returns. It does not matter whether the firm is innovating early in the overall diffusion cycle or late in the cycle. The effect of complementarities in amplifying the impact of IT is supposed to be the same either way. Our conceptual framework suggests a more complex formulation between innovation resources and performance. We argued that generous innovation resources are especially crucial early in the innovation cycle when adoption barriers are highest and that they may be worth little unless paired with a mindful process that allows the firm to appropriately exploit them. These arguments provide an explanation for what otherwise would be a puzzling finding in our empirical results. Specifically, we found that laggard firms with an apparent “too conservative” type of misalignment paid a performance penalty relative to laggard firms that had little or no misalignment—even though the former have more generous innovation resources. If a “too conservative” misalignment is viewed as reflecting the absence of mindfulness, this result makes sense. In sum, although innovation resources are not the same as complementarities, we believe that if innovation resources operate differently depending on the level of alignment, then perhaps complementarities may as well. Finally, because the complementarities framework provides no direct measure of misalignment or deviation, it does not provide any basis to investigate a more fundamental question: Why do some firms have a more or less mindful process of aligning innovation posture and profile? We take up this question in our discussion of future research.

## Implications for Practice

For innovation adopters, our results reinforce the importance of engaging in a process of mindfully aligning the firm’s innovation posture and innovation resource profile. Our results suggest that there may be a substantial performance penalty for mindlessly jumping on an innovation bandwagon just because a technology happens to be highly fashionable or for some other reason divorced from the innovation resources of the firm.

Rather, firms would do well, in the short term, to condition their innovation posture based on the extent of their innovation resources. In general, firms with generous innovation resources should take the lead, while those with a sparse endowment of innovation resources should wait until adoption barriers have been lowered. Over the longer term, firms have the option to evolve their innovation resources to suit a more or less aggressive innovation posture. For example, a firm seeking a more aggressive posture can institute hiring and training policies to increase the level of professionalism and skill. It can encourage executives to give stronger support to IT innovation initiatives. It can work toward building an organizational culture that encourages and rewards innovation.

Interestingly, our results suggest that firms with a “too conservative” misalignment have negatively moderated returns to IT innovation. This gives some credence to our argument that misalignment proxies for a general lack of innovation mindfulness in such firms. As previously noted, unmindful organizations will be less likely to make effective use of the innovation resources they do possess because they lack the preoccupation with failure, the reluctance to simplify interpretations, and the reliance on expertise over formal authority [87] that is seen in mindful organizations. This suggests that they will be less attuned to the myriad implementation pitfalls that can mean the difference between success and failure on IT innovation projects—even among relative laggards [76].

Finally, our results also have implications for innovation proponents and vendors. While these actors have always been interested in the profile of innovation leaders in order to find the most likely adopters, our research suggests that those with the resource profile of a leader are also more likely to have beneficial innovation outcomes when they adopt early. The implication is that attempting to push early adoption on firms that do not have the resource profile of an innovator can hold dangers going beyond wasted marketing resources: It can increase the incidence of early adopting firms with low economic returns. Such firms might well serve as negative opinion leaders that discourage later adoptions.

## Limitations and Future Work

This study is subject to four main limitations. First, because of limitations in our secondary data set, we had to employ relatively indirect measures of a fairly narrow set of innovation profile resources. The use of measures that tap more directly into variables such as professionalism and skill, and the use of measures of other important innovation resources, such as top management support and a culture that encourages creativity and innovation, would be an important refinement. However, our use of secondary data is also a strength, in that it has given us the ability to examine our research question using a very large, rigorously gathered data set. We believe that being able to show that even easily measured and widely available characteristics yield estimated models that behave as hypothesized provides some solid preliminary evidence for the validity of our constructs and theorizing. One possible reason for the good performance of our model is that the “softer” resources that our data set lacks (e.g., management support, culture) will, arguably, tend to have a positive correlations with the “harder” resources our data set includes (e.g., size and skill). For example, Rai et al. [70] found that in the context of electronic procurement adoption, top management support had a 0.35 correlation with “financial resources” and a 0.4 correlation with “IT sophistication.” One explanation could be that larger organizations with greater financial resources are more prone to make investments in hiring innovative people, sending them to technical conferences, and so forth, which in turn promotes an innovative culture and reinforces support for innovation among managers.

Second, our measure of innovation posture does not reflect potential shifts in posture over time. In other words, some firms might exhibit aggressive innovative behavior in one period and conservative behavior in another. This could average out to an intermediate classification on our measure even though this pattern suggests a different dynamic than for firms that are consistently intermediate in their posture. Another concern is that our measure might capture only one of these extremes of behavior during the data collection time window, and so such a firm might be misclassified as highly aggressive or conservative when in general it is not. We think the main result of these sorts of problems are to add noise to the measurement of innovation posture.

Third, while we argue that (un)mindfulness is likely to go hand in hand with (mis)alignment, and posit an important theoretical role for mindfulness in explaining the association between misalignment and innovation returns, we do not have any measures of mindfulness (or the related decision processes at the strategic/firm or tactical/innovation levels) available in our data set.

Finally, our study might be criticized for only applying to a particular type of IT innovation (electronic networking technologies) in a particular context (U.S. manufacturing firms). We emphasize that this choice was deliberate as we sought to capture a large proportion of economic activity (more than 10 percent of the gross domestic product) in the midst of rapid technological change (e-business transformation in the late 1990s). At the same time, it is unclear the extent to which our results may generalize to other IT innovations and sectors. Future research is needed to refine and extend our results to other industry sectors, other IT innovations, and other time periods. For example, it would be interesting to determine whether the impact of a lack of posture–profile alignment on IT innovation returns varied in magnitude across industry sectors.

Beyond addressing the potential limitations just mentioned, we suggest some additional avenues for future work. In particular, there is the question of what kinds of conditions lead to greater or lesser IT posture–profile misalignment in practice. We see four possibilities: (1) a lack of mindfulness, (2) agency conflicts, (3) susceptibility to management fashions, and (4) institutional isomorphism.

The first possibility, already mentioned above, is that managers who innovate mindfully will be less prone to posture–profile misalignments because they are more likely to make adoption decisions that take into account the specifics of their organization and its context [79]. If so, we should expect that mindful organizations will be more likely to consciously seek a state where there is a good fit between their actual patterns of innovation adoption (leader or laggard) and their innovation profile characteristics (generous or sparse), and will hence be less prone to experience inadvertent innovation posture–profile misalignments. By contrast, the less-mindful organization should be prone to such misalignments.

A second possibility is the existence of agency conflicts. Agency conflicts arise when the goals of agents (managers) are misaligned with the goals of principals (firms) and there is an absence of effective mechanisms (monitoring, incentives, cultural norms) to prevent opportunistic behavior on the part of agents [30, 33]. It has been argued that some managers may be more interested in IT as signal of competence [35, 82] than as a tool to improve organizational functioning. This raises the possibility that managers in some firms will push for a more aggressive innovation posture without much concern for what level of innovation resources the firm may possess. Recent research on IT fashions reveals a pattern consistent with this possibility. In particular, Wang [86] found a strong positive association between executive salaries and the early adoption of fashionable innovations, but only a weak positive association between early adoption and firm performance. This suggests that the benefits to managers of an aggressive innovation posture can be somewhat disconnected from the benefits to the firm. Therefore, future researchers might examine whether posture–profile misalignment is more prevalent in firms that are prone to agency conflicts among managers involved with IT innovation initiation decisions.

Wang’s [86] results also suggest attention to managerial fashions themselves as a third potential cause of posture–profile misalignments. Management fashions have been proposed as a key driver of social bandwagon dynamics wherein firms rush to adopt an emerging IT owing to its fashionability rather than an assessment of benefits of the technology based on situational specifics [40]. Therefore, future researchers might examine whether misalignments are more prevalent in firms whose managers are more susceptible to managerial fashions.

A fourth possibility arises from institutional theory, which seeks to explain why organizations come to resemble each other more than they should from an instrumental standpoint (institutional isomorphism) [27, 80]. This suggests that the desire for legitimacy with outside stakeholders might drive some firms to adopt popular innovations earlier than those firms should (based strictly on their innovation resource profile), or conversely, could cause firms to defer the adoption of unpopular innovations longer than they should. Unlike the first three causal mechanisms, this fourth candidate raises the intriguing possibility that strategic benefits arising from greater legitimacy could in some cases outweigh the benefits of posture–profile alignment. As with the above three, future research is needed to disentangle these potentially competing forces.

## Summary and Conclusions

We examined how a misalig nment between an organization’s IT innovation posture and its IT innovation resource profile conditions the link between IT innovation and organizational performance. We argued that innovation posture–profile deviation— which happens when a firm has a pattern of taking the lead with innovation even though it has the sparse resource profile of a laggard, or vice versa—constitutes a useful measure for misalignment. Based on this conceptual foundation, we theorized that firms with large misalignments experience diminished returns from IT adoption because they will be less likely to possess crucial innovation resources when they need them most, and because they will be less likely to put whatever resources they do have to productive use.

Using a data set comprising measures of the adoption of electronic networking technologies in over 25,000 U.S. manufacturing plants, we found that the productivity benefits of any given level of IT adoption were about 50–75 percent lower (depending on the specification) among plants with high posture–profile deviations compared to those with low deviations. Our empirical results provide initial confirmation of the theoretical value of innovation posture, innovation resource profile, and innovation posture–profile misalignment. We provide new evidence for a contingent link between IT and performance, in which the returns to IT innovation depend on the degree to which a firm has aligned the aggressiveness of its innovation posture with the level of its innovation resources. Our results have implications for future work on innovation complements, which are presumed to magnify returns regardless of whether a firm has matched its level of innovation to the level of complements. Our results suggest a more complex formulation whereby those complements that are part of the innovation resource profile may be less influential in misaligned firms. For managers, our results suggest a substantial performance penalty for jumping on an innovation bandwagon for reasons divorced from the innovation resources of the firm. Rather, managers should engage in a process of mindfully aligning the firm’s innovation posture and innovation resource profile.
