# In the Backrooms of Data Science

Elena Parmiggiani<sup>1</sup>, Thomas Østerlie<sup>2</sup>, Petter Grytten Almklov<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computer Science, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Norway, parmiggi@ntnu.no <sup>2</sup>Department of Computer Science, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Norway, thomas.osterlie@ntnu.no <sup>3</sup>Department of Sociology and Political Science, Norwegian University of Science and Technology, Norway, petter.almklov@ntnu.no

## Abstract

Much information systems research on data science treats data as preexisting objects and focuses on how these objects are analyzed. Such a view, however, overlooks the work involved in finding and preparing the data in the first place, such that they are available to be analyzed. In this paper, we draw on a longitudinal study of data management in the oil and gas industry to shed light on this backroom data work. We find that this type of work is qualitatively different from the front-stage data analytics in the realm of data science but is also deeply interwoven with it. We show that this work is unstable and bidirectional. That is, the work practices are constantly changing and must simultaneously take into account what data might be possible to access as well as the potential future uses of the data. It is also a collaborative endeavor involving cross-disciplinary expertise that seeks to establish control over data and is shaped by the epistemological orientation of the oil and gas domain.

Keywords: Data Work, Data Science, Practice, Performative Perspective

Matthew Jones was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on January 10, 2020 and underwent two revisions.

## 1 Introduction

With the surge in big data and datafication, data science is now recognized as a central discipline in the design and implementation of digital transformation strategies throughout organizations and society (Abbasi et al. 2016; Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Galliers et al., 2017). Therefore, different professional roles have coalesced into organizational groups responsible for handling the discovery, transformation, modeling, and reporting of large and complex data sets in enterprises under titles such as “data scientist” or “data analyst” (Davenport & Patil, 2012; Kandel et al., 2012).

Researchers in information systems (IS) and related fields have taken a number of perspectives on data science, including technical (Liebman, Saar-Tsechansky, & Stone, 2019; Shi et al., 2016; van der Aalst, 2016), management-oriented (Baesens et al., 2016; Dhar, 2013; Kane, 2014; Mikalef et al., 2020;

Saar-Tsechansky, 2015), and performative (Aversa et al., 2018; Jones, 2019; Østerlie & Monteiro 2020; Monteiro & Parmiggiani 2019). Although all three perspectives acknowledge that the work of data science requires a combination of analytical skills and domain-specific knowledge, the first two tend to assume that data remain ontologically separate from the practices that produce and manage them (Provost & Fawcett, 2013, p. 52). Researchers taking these perspectives are inclined to espouse—often implicitly—what Kitchin (2014) refers to as a prefactual take on data—that is, a view on data as “meaningful independent of format, medium, language, producer, and context [and] a key element in the constitution of … facts, evidence, information, and knowledge” (p. 3; see also Abbasi et al., 2016). The consequence of this take on data is that the actual work tasks carried out by individuals to curate and set up the data are typically downplayed (Carter & Sholler, 2016). However, ongoing datafication makes a case for a more nuanced characterization of such tasks. As larger data sets become available, organizations and scholars are pressured to better understand how the value of data crucially depends on the data work to handle their variety and quality (Leonelli, 2019; Passi & Jackson, 2018).

Sociologists of science and technology highlighted in the 1980s and 1990s that a lot of work is performed in the backrooms of science to, for example, set up instrumentation and clean, curate, and retrieve the data (Barley & Bechky, 1994; cf. Shapin, 1989). This backstage work is largely invisible, yet it is fundamental for the front-stage work of conducting experiments. This remains true for data science today (Müller et al., 2019; Plantin, 2019). Whereas the majority of the IS literature focuses on the front-stage analytics, we unearth what happens backstage. To this end, we ask: What is the backstage work involved in data science?

Drawing on a performative approach, we pursue one possible answer to this question. We bring to the forefront the extensive, complex, and ongoing work involved in finding and curating digital data that happens before, during, and after data science analytics. The performative literature puts forth a perspective on data as the product of a long process of theory-building and instrumentation (Barad, 2007) where the data collection instruments’ “materiality plays an integral part in creating, not simply representing, the materiality of the physical world” (Østerlie et al., 2012, p. 85). In this perspective, data do not have value in and of themselves, but as emerging from the coevolution of technological and organizational practices (Jones, 2019; Moser & Law, 2006; Østerlie et al., 2012). A performative lens allows us to bring to the forefront a wide array of technological and organizational tasks that constitute data science.

We ground our analysis on a longitudinal case study of data management in data science in oil and gas (petroleum) operations in Norway. As the hydrocarbons forming oil and natural gas reservoirs reside several kilometers below the seabed, they are accessible only through sensor networks and historical data sets accumulated over time. Finding new oil or natural gas depends on meticulous work to handle the available data about the subsurface structures. As new data stream in, they need to be reconciled with old data, including existing interpretations. This is a back-and-forth movement where the new data are interpreted in light of the old data and the old data might be reinterpreted in light of the new data (Almklov 2008; Parmiggiani et al. 2016). This work is undertaken by a variety of professionals, a growing number of whom have been hired or designated internally as data managers (DMs), but also by geoscientists (geologists and geophysicists) and, less often, by petroleum engineers.

We contribute to the IS literature and the discourse on data science in IS by providing in-depth empirical insight into the intersection among work practices, organizational models, and technology (Abbasi et al., 2016; Günther et al., 2017) in the backrooms of data science. Data work is qualitatively different from the analytical aspect of data science. It consists of complex sets of practices of data discovery and preparation, which do not run sequentially, as technical and managerial perspectives on data science would acknowledge, but are unstable (that is, dealing with high variability in terms of inputs and outputs) and bidirectional (that is, always concerned with the historical trajectories of the available data, as well as their future potential or actual uses). This work is also ongoing, because not only must the data always be put into context to make sense but the context and the questions asked also evolve continuously.

Our discussion of the backrooms of data science speaks to theories of digitalization in IS by emphasizing the importance of data work in data science in contexts where work is saturated with digital data (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). The rapid technological changes seen today necessitate study not only of organizational models and strategies but also of how they affect work (cf. Barley & Kunda, 2001; Stein et al., 2013). Shedding light on data work in the backrooms of data science, we believe, contributes to understanding the division of labor between humans and technologies and, in particular, how digitalization does not replace but surfaces human work tasks (cf. Autor, 2015). Data work is a complex and ongoing effort to solve problems that requires substantial adaptability and learning flexibility. Unpacking the actual practices of data work associated with digitalization, and data science in particular, is helpful to reveal deep changes in work and organizing and trace how new professional roles emerge (Huysman, 2020).

## 2 Foregrounding Data Work in Data Science

Data science was famously baptized the “sexiest job of the 21st century” (Davenport & Patil, 2012) and is characterized as the work of applying a set of various principles and algorithms, such as big data analytics, to analyze data and extract actionable insights into business problems in different domains (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Kelleher & Tierney, 2018). Different lenses have been adopted to magnify aspects of the discipline.

Technical perspectives tend to emphasize data science as a decontextualized discipline, defined as “an amalgamation of classical disciplines like statistics, data mining, databases, and distributed systems” (van der Aalst, 2016, p. 3). Scholars taking this perspective often conceptualize data as raw materials out of which data scientists extract actionable insights with powerful software tools (Provost & Fawcett, 2013), with no need for guiding theories and assumptions as long as large enough data sets are available (Anderson, 2008). Examples in IS range from how using finegrained consumer behavior data can improve predictive analytics (Martens et al., 2016) to adopting topic modeling for calculating business proximity (Shi et al., 2016) and frameworks for automating the recommendations on music playlists (Liebman et al., 2019). These studies have the merit of discussing the analytical skills and software tools required to conduct data science work today. However, they do not problematize the work involved in cleaning, preparing, and putting together the training and test data sets that are fed into algorithms, assuming that this work will increasingly be conducted by machine learning algorithms in the future (Provost & Fawcett, 2013).

Despite hopes for automation, the data preparation work performed by humans is still central to data science. Management-oriented perspectives on data science remark that there is a significant human component mediating the relationship between data and analytics (Mikalef et al., 2020). Much “janitor work” goes into data science but tends to remain invisible (Lohr, 2014). For example, airlines, such as KLM, rely on careful human work to handle vast amounts of social media streams to improve customer experiences, especially during big service disruptions (Kane, 2014). Such perspectives have focused mostly on business analytics, defined as data science applied in an organizational context (Baesens et al., 2016). The focus is on data as assets for firm managers to develop a firm’s specific capabilities and measure competitive performance (Mikalef et al., 2020). From this angle, researchers also consider the professional context and the importance of domain-specific knowledge for data scientists to be able to assess the feasibility of their models in a given domain (cf. Dhar, 2013). In spite of this, management-oriented perspectives still tend to package data work in data science as a bounded task that happens during a specific phase of an input-output data flow where the surrounding context (Pettigrew, 1990) is represented by an external stimulus (Abbasi et al., 2016; Mikalef et al., 2020).

Scholars adopting a performative perspective problematize pre-factual takes on data (Kitchin, 2014) as givens that are fed into algorithms or managerial decisions. For example, works in science and technology studies (STS) and related fields have demonstrated that data are deeply entangled with the means and setting of their production and, over time, are attached to several loci and practices (Baker et al. 2016; Edwards, 2010; Leonelli, 2016). In a study of information sharing in healthcare work, Moser and Law (2006, p. 68) observe: “Information does not simply flow. It does not stay stable ... it is open, uncertain, in process, both complete and incomplete in ways that are themselves open and uncertain.” In other words, data are constantly (re)assembled and sustained over time through processes of decision-making and interpretation that happen in addition to what technical and managerial perspectives on data science in IS mostly focus on.

Some scholars in IS have taken stock of the insights above and begun to shed light on data work in data science-related arenas. Studies of data infrastructures in different empirical domains have convincingly shown that data work is not one definite set of tasks in a chain of bounded phases but is continuous and cannot be decoupled from the use of specific data tools. For example, the value of data in digital ecosystems is dependent on data-processing mechanisms (Aaltonen & Tempini, 2014). Within the genetics domain, the processes through which the data are generated, controlled, and curated generate tensions among different actors who pursue or restrict data openness (Vassilakopoulou et al., 2018). Tensions also emerge as different actors have nonoverlapping understandings and perspectives concerning the data flow. A substantial part of data work often consists of ongoing coordination among the actors’ multiple temporal rhythms (Ribes & Finholt, 2009; Venters et al., 2014). In a domain similar to the one we examine in this paper, Almklov et al., (2014) showed how the data interpretation work practices of engineers in the petroleum industry are entangled with the available information systems, sensors, and the data they produce. Data work can become so prominent in some sectors that the backstage work becomes a front-stage profession. This is what Waardenburg et al. (2018) discuss in the case of crime prediction in police work, where human information officers have a fundamental role in influencing the production and presentation of data.

## 3 A Practice Perspective on Data Work in Data Science

In line with a performative lens, we focus on backstage data work practices. We view practice as the “recurrent, materially bounded and situated action engaged in by members of a community” (Orlikowski, 2002, p. 256). Nicolini (2012, p. 2) states that a performative lens

resonate[s] with the contemporary experience that our world is increasingly in flux and interconnected, a world where social entities appear as the result of ongoing work and complex machinations, and in which boundaries around social entities are increasingly difficult to draw.

To overcome a pre-factual understanding of data, this understanding of practice is relevant, because it reminds us that the objects of interest in data science— the data—are inherently undefined and acquire meaning through the practices of working with them. Knorr Cetina (1997, pp. 12–13) calls them objects of knowledge, or epistemic objects:

Objects of knowledge ... are more like open drawers filled with folders extending indefinitely into the depths of a dark closet. Since objects of knowledge are always in the process of being materially defined, they continually acquire new properties and change the ones they have.

We do not mean to say that data do not exist per se, but that they are necessarily shaped and become relevant and meaningful as part of heterogeneous sociotechnical practices (Østerlie et al., 2012). Data qua epistemic objects promote and mediate relationships among experts: “Because of this openended nature, they ... generate intimate attachment that creates social bonds” (Knorr Cetina, 1997, as cited in Nicolini et al., 2012). Although formulated more than two decades ago, IS scholars today still emphasize this conceptualization of data because data science is concerned with digital artifacts whose value is not static but “contingent on shifting webs of functional relations with other artifacts across specific contexts and organizations” (Kallinikos et al., 2013, p. 354).

Accordingly, we understand data science as a collaborative and “sociomaterial practice (Orlikowski, 2007) in which human and technical forms of work intertwine in specific, significant, and mutually shaping ways” (Passi & Jackson, 2018, p. 136). We contend that this perspective is important, because, as Passi and Jackson observe, scholars tend to imagine data science as if it were a “clean room” where data scientists sit “interacting around reliable and widely shared tools, norms, and conventions” (Passi & Jackson, 2018, p. 136:21). However, the authors found that data science practices are inherently collaborative and heterogeneous. They do not happen in clean rooms but involve various activities, negotiations, and actors beyond the analysts, such as project managers and product designers. This is especially the case in corporations like the ones we address in this paper.

From this perspective, the backrooms of data science are hardly constituted by universal methods or algorithms. Instead, these backrooms are deeply rooted in evolving institutional organizing principles and epistemic practices of scientific domains (Bailey & Barley, 2019; Ribes et al., 2019) that are not clearly visible externally and are influenced by, for example, positivistic (Forsythe, 2001) or hermeneutic (Frodeman, 1995) views of the world. A practiceinformed focus on data science helps us unearth the work of discovering, preparing, maintaining, and assembling data, in addition to analyzing them, which is performed by multiple different actors with their own epistemological assumptions (Mikalsen & Monteiro, 2018). By not shedding light on the work carried out on the backstage of data science (cf. Barley & Bechky, 1994), scholars still tend to underemphasize the practical and theoretical consequences of such work for the advancement of data science:

Work is involved at each stage. This work may in some circumstances be automated, but work will still be needed in the initial design of the requisite algorithms … Too often, however, data are discussed as if they simply exist with no need to consider either their provenance or their accessibility. (Jones, 2019, p. 13)

To summarize our theoretical background and framework, pre-factual takes on data assume that data are separate from the work to produce and curate them. This is problematic because, in so doing, we risk bracketing the role of data work as a confined phase that precedes data analytics along a one-directional data trajectory. Evidence from an emerging strand of research on data work in IS and neighboring fields that draws on a performative perspective suggests, however, that the actual practices of producing and curating data in data science-related domains matter. They are emergent, ongoing, and context dependent. Our case setting, in which data science professionals deal with epistemologically uncertain data that approximate an inaccessible reality (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019; Oreskes et al., 1994), represents an interesting testbed for shedding further light on the backstage data work that makes data science possible.

## 4 Case Context: Data Work in Petroleum Operations

The business problems that data science in oil and gas deals with are broad. They range from exploration for new resources and field development to drilling exploration and/or production wells and production, until the depleted field is decommissioned. Formal workflows in organizations, for accountability reasons, tend to represent data work as a set of finite tasks in a linear value chain (Figure 1). According to these accounts, data work consists of receiving data requests from petroleum professionals, identifying the relevant sources (whether they are historical databases or thirdparty service companies’ real-time data streams), and connecting them with applications where petroleum professionals access the data. Along the way, various forms of quality assurance are carried out on the data.

These accounts, however, do not match the reality of data work. When investigating actual practices, a much more faceted and often overlooked reality emerges. We focus on the exploration and production phases. The types of data involved are very heterogeneous, although they can be sorted into several broad categories, as outlined in Table A1, Appendix A. Data management work in these contexts is carried out by slightly different groups of professional roles whose configurations typically depend on the size of the company and include DMs, geoscientists (geologists, geophysicists), and sometimes petroleum engineers. With the term “data managers,” we emphasize not primarily the “scientific” or “analytical” aspects of their work—though they are clearly there—but that their role is often more hands-on. The professionals performing data work are rarely formally organized as a professional unit or department. Instead, their role varies. In the oil and gas companies that we investigated, these professionals are attached to specific focus areas (such as exploration or production) or to specific fields during field development and production. Geoscientists and petroleum engineers usually focus on data analysis-related tasks in data science but also perform tasks related to, for example, data cleaning and quality checking. The specific organizational role of DMs is new and has emerged as a response to the increasing workload represented by data management-related tasks on the shoulders of the other professional groups. For example, in the largest oil and gas company we examined in Norway, the professional figure of the DM was formalized in the mid-1990s to enable geoscientists involved in exploration to concentrate on finding more hydrocarbons. Therefore, the role of DMs, in this case, is to ensure that the other petroleum professionals have access to all relevant data available of good enough quality to perform their tasks, “providing users with the right data, with the right quality, at the right time, in the right database,” as one DM put it. Their professional backgrounds tend to vary as well, and there is no formal training for this role. Smaller companies tend to not have formally designated DMs, so that their work is conducted by other geoscientists and petroleum engineers, sometimes as part of their analysis tasks.

## 5 Research Methods

## 5.1 Research Design and Access to Case

We draw on an interpretive case study (Walsham, 2006) of data management activities in the oil and gas sector in Norway. The unit of analysis consists of the practices of handling the data in the domains of exploration and production. We chose this research design because we are interested in developing an in-depth understanding of the work processes that enable the work on which the majority of the data science literature focuses.

We conducted the case study against the background of a longitudinal engagement with the oil and gas industry. Our research group has studied the oil and gas business using ethnographically inspired methods for 20 years. The authors of this paper and the other members of our research group have investigated data management and analytics in different domains in the oil and gas sector (Almklov 2008; Monteiro & Parmiggiani 2019; Østerlie & Monteiro, 2020; Parmiggiani et al., 2015). Although our group engagement with the domain of petroleum production started in the early 2000s, our interest in exploration is more recent and started in 2016.

Against this backdrop, our access to the case was driven by pragmatic concerns and facilitated by our participation in a national center for research-based innovation on digitalization in the oil and gas industry. We had access to one small and two large petroleum companies operating on the Norwegian continental shelf, a large international service company providing digital platforms for data visualization and management to petroleum operators, and a group of university geoscientists.

The data that inform this study consist of interviews, participant observations during workshops, and software tools and documentation and were collected in the period 2016– 2019. Semistructured interviews represented the primary data source. We conducted 17 interviews lasting between 1.0 and 1.5 hours each. These interviews were important for two reasons. First, given our interest in uncovering the practices of handling data that are often invisible in accounts of data science, the interviews allowed us to “see that which is not ordinarily on view and examine that which looked at but seldom seen” (Rubin & Rubin, 2011, p. xv; see also Myers & Newman, 2007). Second, the interviews enabled us to identify and discuss patterns in users’ actions over time (Schultze & Avital, 2011). Because of our experience studying the oil and gas sector, we were aware of the emerging role of DMs in large oil companies. Thus, these professionals were the first informants we approached. Using a snowballing strategy, we identified additional professionals responsible for data work in the same companies and in other organizations.

Taken at face value, interviews are probably the secondbest choice for investigating practices (Nicolini, 2009). To provide a more nuanced account of work practices, we supplemented the necessarily limited nature of this data collection approach in two ways.

First, we designed the interviews following the storytelling technique rooted in the ethnographic tradition (Lutters & Seaman, 2007) to elicit accounts of actual data work practices by seeking to capture our informants perspectives on their work reality by preserving their daily language and foregrounding the contextual details of their day-to-day activities. We wanted to obtain “contextually rich narratives” (Lutters & Seaman, p. 577) of the relationship between the professionals working with the data, the data, and the information systems, as well as the evolving context. To facilitate this, we asked the informants to focus on one or more concrete examples of their work tasks. Some began drawing sketches of an oil reservoir by hand on a piece of paper to explain how they managed and analyzed the data based on their (changing) understanding of the evolution of that reservoir over time (Monteiro et al., 2018). These sketches became a source for further questions. A strength of this approach was that it gave our informants a voice to raise issues from their point of view and an opportunity to reflect in depth on their professional role within the domain of data science and within the oil and gas industry (cf. Langley & Meziani, 2020; Myers & Newman, 2007). For this reason, we were also invited to present and validate our findings as part of two sessions arranged by a group of DMs. These sessions were organized as fora where the DMs reflected on the status and future of data management and data science in the oil and gas sector.

Second, we supplemented the insights from the interviews with sensitivity we developed during previous ethnographic studies of the work of exploration and production. This was further strengthened by the third author’s training in geology before he began conducting extensive ethnographic fieldwork as a social scientist among subsurface professionals. All authors’ extensive experience in different parts of the industry and familiarity with the technical issues and main challenges of the industry in terms of data management and analysis, enabled us, in the interviews, workshops, and analysis, to challenge the simplified descriptions of practice often provided in interviews. We may not have known every detail of our informants’ specific practices, but we had ample experience of the nature of such details—how different vendors use different data formats, how to navigate between versions of temporary interpretations, how sensor data are often unreliable, and how extrapolations and interpolations are at the crux of knowing in the subsurface disciplines (Almklov et al. 2014), and the different epistemic cultures of geologists and geophysicists. We knew what to ask to engage in reflexive discussions of practice.

The second data source that we adopted to complement the interviews was participant observations with DMs and geoscientists conducted during four co-design workshops (Dahl et al., 2014), divided into 11 sessions in total. The first two authors organized the workshops. The purpose was to study and contribute in order to improve the existing information systems available for data management and data science tasks. In line with the aim of co-design approaches, we used the workshops to propose new functions and prototypes, so that they could better match the actual nature of data work in oil and gas operations and the professionals’ knowledge by including them directly in the process. The detailed nature of the prototypes is beyond the scope of this paper. However, the recordings of the workshops were crucial to acquire a more nuanced characterization of actual data work tasks in collaborative settings and to flesh out the professionals concerns. Through the co-design workshops, we were allowed to partially access information systems in use or under development at the companies. This was important to get a clearer understanding of the actual technologies available to data work.<sup>1</sup>

The third data source was documentation. We relied on the trust established with the informants at the companies investigated and collected internal documentation, email threads, internal memos, sketches drawn during interviews, and PowerPoint presentations. We also collected public documents, consisting of regulations, policies, and white papers issued by national and international institutions. Documentation was important for our practice focus, to get a deeper understanding of the contextual conditions under which data science in oil and gas operations is understood, and specifically to investigate the tension between formal accounts of data science versus actual practices.

## 5.3 Data Analysis

To analyze the data, we used an interpretive research paradigm (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 2006) inspired by a hermeneutic approach to make sense of data work in data science as a complex whole that we sought to understand by alternating between our and the informants’ perspectives (Klein & Myers, 1999). We jointly conducted the analysis and overlapped it with the data collection through the deductive-inductive strategy in three phases, iterating between theory and data (Eisenhardt, 1989). The third author has a background in geosciences and experience from similar studies in other parts of the industry and regularly challenged the analysis results. This process strengthened the veracity of our account.

In the first phase, we scoped the analytical focus, driven by our long-standing attention to data work in different contexts. We open-coded the transcriptions and field notes manually, using colored highlighters and sticky notes, and developed descriptive codes following Emerson et al.’s (2011) guidelines. We focused on the informants’ concerns related to working with data and the practices they devised to deal with such concerns in practice. We developed codes describing the informants’ perspectives, adopting the terminology they used as closely as possible.

Working inductively, we were struck by the mismatch between the organizational workflows describing data management in the value chain of a well lifecycle (Figure 1) and the informants’ accounts of their daily data work. According to our informants, dealing with data requires intimate and extensive knowledge of, for example, different systems, data formats, and typical errors to look for, as well as knowledge of how to fix and mitigate poor data quality. One DM defined her profession as that of a “data sewer.” Elaborating on her statement, she explained that it referred to the sense of someone responsible for constantly trimming and stitching together data packages and data sources, but also the less romantic yet fundamental role of managing the data by draining, treating, cleaning, and transporting them back and forth. Working with the codes, we realized that few people other than the professionals actually performing that work are aware of what happens in the backrooms of data science, namely, the familiarity with the data and data formats required to perform apparently mundane tasks.

In the second phase, we became interested in characterizing the activities performed in the backrooms of data science and identifying sets of practices adopted to handle the emerging concerns. Therefore, we clustered the codes recursively. At this stage, we realized that data work in oil and gas data science is a collective accomplishment that professionals learn through daily practice. For example, the DMs in production are often concerned with their “perceived isolation at work.” As we discovered, the DMs’ strategy for solving this concern involved establishing a network of contacts and sharing a set of possible solutions to recurring problems (grouped under the label “informal networks”). During this phase, we also validated the emerging findings and constructs by presenting them to the informants in petroleum and digital platform vendor companies during interviews and workshops. The result of this phase was the detailing of the data work necessary to make analytic sense of the data in an effort to ensure the stewardship of the data. The interpretive table (Table B1 in Appendix B) lists the three identified main categories and subcategories. The first main category characterizes the context in which backstage data work unfolds (“data stewardship context”), and the other two describe the set of practices enacted to discover the data (“getting the right data”) and prepare them (“getting the data right”) for analytic work. We supplement this overview with samples from the data (excerpts from field notes and interview quotes). Given that our discussions and interviews often focused on examples of specific fields or technologies, some data samples are generalizations based on these issues. We then organized the three categories and their relationship into an orthogonal context (stewardship) cross-cutting the two main processes of discovery and preparation. This conceptual structure is reflected in the presentation of our findings below. In specific instances, we simplified the excerpts from field notes and quotes from interviews used to support our findings to omit the technical complexity of geological and geophysical processes that are beyond the scope of this paper.

In the third and last phase, we compared the themes with theoretical concepts from the literature (Suddaby, 2006). Given our interest in the nature of data work in the data science domain, we sought to further specify and operationalize Jones’s (2019) concept of data in practice; that is, to understand how data emerge from situated practices of production and (re)use. Working inductively, we were struck by the way in which data work is unstable—that is, it varies greatly in terms of what data were to be used and produced based on the specific context. Data work is also a bidirectional activity, in which knowledge of the data’s provenance and of what the data will be used for is crucial for its completion. As we elaborate in the theoretical background section, these aspects contrast with assumptions in the dominant discourse on data science in IS, where data are often assumed to be pre-factual, which leads the ongoing and complex practices to produce and curate them to be downplayed. In the discussion section, we theoretically elaborate on the nature of data work in terms of its unstable and bidirectional nature and what this implies for the establishment of control over the data flow, the sociality of this type of work, and the epistemological orientation of its context.

## 6 Empirical Findings: Ensuring Data Stewardship

Data management tasks vary across groups, departments, and companies. Some of this variation is attributable to the different professional domains that constitute the oil and gas value chain. The production phase, for instance, has a much faster pace compared to exploration. Analyzing geological data and developing a hypothesis of the presence of an oil reservoir in exploration might take months or years, but an operational well produces an array of different types of sensor data every second. Despite domain differences, what emerges from the accounts of the DMs and geoscientists involved in this study is the idea of data sets as a “chain of evidence” (DM, production, oil company) that must be (re)assembled and maintained over time. In the interviews and workshops, several DMs and geoscientists performing comparable tasks in small companies described themselves as custodians or stewards of the data flow. Investigating what goes into this definition, we identified an array of extensive, complex, and ongoing tasks involved in finding and curating the digital data—what we refer to as data work—that happens before and during data science analytics in exploration and production. We conceptualize this effort of ensuring the stewardship of the data in terms of two practices—data discovery (getting the right data) and data preparation (getting the data right). These practices and the work they entail unfold against a sociotechnical contextual background that is (1) constantly evolving, (2) epistemologically oriented, and (3) grounded in informal networks.

## 6.1 Data Stewardship Context

## 6.1.1 Constantly Evolving Sociotechnical Context

The Norwegian oil and gas industry has collected and accumulated subsurface data for more than 40 years. Oil and gas companies store much data in-house, but the data also stretch outside organizational boundaries. Companies must also draw on publicly available data from all wells and all surveys on the Norwegian continental shelf that have been uploaded since the 1980s to a national public repository called Diskos.<sup>2</sup> Although the Diskos database is extensive, not all forms of potentially relevant data are typically uploaded there. For example, a DM commented while discussing a tool displaying the Diskos data with another DM:

These geological formation tops are only the official ones from the Petroleum Directorate. We can often have ten years of detailed interpretation [that were made] after that, but we need to go into another system [to find them].

## Her colleague supports her:

We see that very often, such programs are much more suited for newcomers, that is, small companies that haven’t worked as long as us. We have drilled a lot, done a lot, and we want something that includes that knowledge too.

Data stewardship appears to be dependent on modes of ordering the data—that is, classifying, organizing, and (re)structuring—that unfold against an evolving context of social routines and technical systems in a given company or domain. Petroleum companies’ data tend to be spread across archives and databases. The two largest oil companies we investigated, for example, have acquired and retired numerous digital systems. Database retiring is particularly important, because it usually entails closing the database to new entries but not phasing it out completely. Although no longer maintained, old databases are kept running because they contain data not found elsewhere. Furthermore, other companies’ databases are acquired during acquisitions and mergers, introducing further complexity to the sociotechnical context.

## 6.1.2 Epistemological Orientation

Work in production and exploration depends on the way that human knowledge is built and managed in domains that are concerned with a reality that is physically inaccessible to humans, i.e., it is epistemologically oriented. The orientation of these domains influences the way that professionals perform data work. In the exploration domain, data work is typically organized so that exploration geoscientists can search for new hydrocarbon deposits and thus identify and/or model potential oil and gas fields. Therefore, data have differing relevance for the professionals involved in the analytical work.

In the production domain, data work is crucially linked to drilling projects planned in a particular field. Engineers and geoscientists have developed an understanding of the main structures of the field over time and, in contrast to the case of exploration, many relevant data from neighboring wells are (potentially) at hand. This is not to say that this is simple or straightforward or that the uncertainty is low. Since geoscientists, especially for older fields, are looking for remaining pockets of oil, the targets are smaller, and the analyses require more detail. In addition, in production, the data from currently producing wells and reservoir simulations provide more information but also make the analyses more complex: “We have a group of geoscientists whose sole responsibility is to update the subsurface model” (Lead Geologist, field note excerpt).

For subsurface specialists searching for new subsurface resources in exploration, data are tools for exploring underground. Geoscientists in exploration use data in different ways based on the hypotheses and concrete issues that are unique to each exploration project. However, the historical well data they use were typically produced primarily to support the drilling of a specific well and for a specific project, not for future exploration. One geologist commented:

Historically one used to drill to look for the rotated fault blocks from the Jura era [where most hydrocarbons are found in the Norwegian continental shelf]. It was down there one was aiming but, in the process, one drilled through a lot of interesting things above that.

These “interesting things” were not at the forefront during the older projects, however, meaning that the quality of the data from these zones may vary.

In exploration, the available data and the possible relevance of the data are not as straightforward as in production. Geoscientists and DMs are presented with much more open questions and use data to respond to the questions in numerous ways that vary based on the hypotheses of the exploration project. DMs, when present, support geoscientists who search for and assess subsurface areas for new hydrocarbon deposits. All subsurface petroleum activities are characterized by large epistemic uncertainty (Monteiro et al., 2018). For exploration projects, this is particularly true; around 70% of exploration wells turn out to be dry. Exploration projects emerge based on different theories or ideas about possible histories that have led to hydrocarbons being developed at some point in geological history, migrating through different formations, and being captured by different forms of structural traps.<sup>3</sup> Thus, the process of developing a project from the idea phase to a concrete drilling project involves searching in the existing data for clues that might support or contradict different hypotheses. Which data are relevant for each hypothesis vary, and the relevant data do not necessarily come from the area in question. Relevance is not based only on proximity. The relevance of the subsurface data depends on the concept or idea that the geoscientists developed behind the prospect. Therefore, DMs might need to look at data from more distant areas with geological similarities to support new ideas. Thus, data work is a matter of providing not only the data that best represent the reservoir (whether they even exist is questionable) but also the data that are most suited for the ongoing (and ever-changing) analyses of different hypotheses of where oil may have migrated during the geological history of the area.

Thus, the role of DMs in exploration—or those performing DM work in small companies—is fundamental to enabling investigative work. The DMs collect and organize data for geoscientists’ analyses. In production, in contrast, the majority of the data used are from the assigned field, namely, produced and organized by the corresponding department. However, DMs sometimes resort to investigating data from other areas or data types outside the typical data sets to pursue novel ideas and concepts.

## 6.1.3 Informal Networks

We found that the problem-solving heuristics developed by professionals investigating data often rely on informal social networks. This is particularly true for DMs, who belong to a new profession in the large case companies but tend to be isolated, especially in exploration. DMs continuously mobilize an informal network of contacts on whom they know they can rely. Such informal networks are an important instrument for DMs to feel part of a distinct professional group, which contributes to their strong sense of disciplinary identity. These networks also have practical reasons for existing. First, they are an important instrument for solving data problems—in particular, locating the data for which DMs have been asked:

The first thing I would do is to search across our internal hard drives; to see if there is any information. And then I would have read the reports to see if they had run VSP [a particular kind of test run during drilling]. Did they run a VSP, and if so, is it available somewhere? … In the end, I’d call data managers in other companies to ask if they have any files they can send me. Over time, you build a good relationship with other data managers [across companies] that you can ask about these things. But this comes down to goodwill and acquaintances. (DM, exploration oil company)

Second, based on the experience acquired in sharing knowledge with colleagues, many DMs we interviewed invest time and energy to set up and share informal knowledge bases that include menus of recurring issues and possible solutions. In a large company, DMs share wiki-like repertoires of solutions where they post and search for approaches to dealing with recurring questions and errors, using for instance ad hoc TeamSites on Windows:

There is a lot that is experience-based in our work. So, we have a TeamSite where we store online descriptions of how to handle issues that we might have encountered on the way. This constitutes a true repertoire of routines that contains a lot of good things. This tool is also a way to overcome the DMs’ isolation, something that is particularly challenging in the production domain where one DM is usually associated with a group of petroleum engineers. (DM, production, oil company)

## 6.2 Data Discovery: Getting the Right Data

To conduct analytical work, geoscientists and engineers usually request seismic surveys or well data but may also request archived exploration projects, reports, and even physical samples (Table A1). The representation of the tasks for managing those data in the companies’ workflows (simplified in Figure 1) is based on an understanding of data as prepackaged and self-contained entities. This ideal picture, however, contrasts with the data work practices adopted for making the right data available to the requesting engineer or geoscientist.

## 6.2.1 Knowing the Data Ecosystem

At the beginning of one interview, a DM (exploration, oil company) defined her profession as “almost a librarian or an archiver.” Then she said, “every data type has its own home,” stressing the importance of proper data-archiving procedures. She pointed to one characteristic of the oil and gas sector: The available data sources are numerous and heterogeneous. When we inquired into this aspect, however, what emerged was a much more nuanced picture—the opposite of the tidy metaphor of “librarian.” Not all data necessarily have their own home or a single home. Consequently, many data are data only in principle, not in practice. Physical core samples illustrate this point (see Table A1). Core sample pictures may be uploaded to the national data repository (see Section 6.1.1) but it is not a regulatory requirement. Furthermore, although there is a separate national repository for storing physical core samples taken during drilling, companies are not required to share these samples. Nevertheless, the professionals performing data work can find themselves in the position of searching for physical samples:

Another issue is whether or not the well core has been sampled. If so, are well core data available or not? … If the data are not, the core sample itself can be stored somewhere. But they may be anywhere, so sometimes we basically have to look through old drawers and filing cabinets to track them down. (DM, oil company)

A challenge in the process of looking in old drawers and cabinets is that the data might not be accessible to anyone other than the professional in charge of managing the data for that area. Similarly, data about an area may exist but might not be publicly available or accessible to particular companies. One DM in exploration said that the only way to solve this challenge relies on “people know[ing] each other” (see Section 6.1.3). As a result, if people need data from an area, they must know and contact the person in charge of the data. Having an extended group of colleagues outside and inside the company is part of getting the right data.

Two DMs from one of the major operators on the Norwegian continental shelf discussed retrieving physical samples of sidewall cores. The first one commented: “A special thing about sidewall cores is that we know that they were taken, but there is no one that knows where they are today or if they exist at all, maybe in a cupboard somewhere.” The other DM agrees: “Yes, if they are available, it’s a plus.” The first concludes: “So the fact that it says ‘yes’ here [in this system] doesn’t mean that it exists.” He then explains that ordinary cores are required according to the Petroleum Directorate but that these additional sidewall samples are not systematically stored. “So we need to ask [name] to look for them.” (DM, exploration, oil company)

## 6.2.2 Knowing Historical Data-Ordering Modes

The drawer-and-cabinet-search metaphor above illustrates well that discovering data in this context appears to be dependent on modes of ordering the data over time (see Section 6.1.1). Usually, subsurface data are stored and further processed in specialized tools and applications, in addition to being stored in dedicated databases. Different professions use different specialized tools and applications running on different operating platforms. Some are desktop based, others are server based; some run on Microsoft Windows, others on various Unix flavors. For example, a popular system used by geoscientists to analyze reservoirs is Unix based. Moving the data across different application environments and adapting the data accordingly is very cumbersome, especially when the data transfer is not in the same direction as the company’s formal workflows. Some of the DMs we interviewed focus almost entirely on this issue. A production DM at an oil company stated:

One challenge is the moving of data between X [a Windows-based reservoir analysis proprietary system] and Y [an open-source Linux-based system used by geoscientists]. This is two-way movement. For transferring data from Y into X, plugins are available from the vendor of X. In this direction, the data transfer works well because it follows the same direction of the workflow ... Unfortunately, however, Y is not able to read the data from X. Moving the data from X to Y is a challenge, there is no one “button” that the geoscientists can press. So everything is manual in this direction.

One of the reasons for this is that the application environments change over time. New and better tools emerge, but most large corporations also seek to avoid lock-in to one particular vendor and change application environments aperiodically. Over time, however, corporate policy changes, and the corporations we examined embrace a heterogeneous operating environment of Windows- and Unix-based specialized tools and applications. Corporate mergers and acquisitions further increase system heterogeneity. Furthermore, there is no one-to-one mapping of data representation formats between two similar systems, such as a reservoir modeling tool. Each application has been designed for specific purposes. Some can, for example, be designed to give overviews of large areas, sacrificing detail and alternate data, while others are oriented toward uncertainty and facilitate the inspection of alternative data sources. Moving data between application formats, therefore, entails a degree of transformation, where something is lost (or made more cumbersome to include) and, possibly, something is gained.

Against this continuously unfolding sociotechnical context, there is no single order where “every data type has a home” that holds up over time. Instead, there are multiple and overlapping orderings of data. Responding to data requests involves locating the right data across the myriad internal and shared systems in use within the company and its collaboration partners. To deduce where relevant data may be found, DMs and their colleagues develop a combination of an extensive overview of available databases and historical knowledge of the different modes of ordering data. Talking about how naming is central to organizing data content, a DM in a major operator on the Norwegian continental shelf commented: “We need to keep track of and map the different ways we have named [geological] formations in the past, and the changing ways of naming formations in the future.”

Length of service also plays a role. Several oil and gas professionals in this study were involved in setting up the initial tape-based national repository in the 1980s with data management in various petroleum-related enterprises. Others, however, are younger and do not have such experience. Given the unfolding character of the system environment, these DMs do not have a wideranging understanding of the many systems and varying modes of ordering the data. Therefore, several DMs referred to themselves as “detectives” because they perceive that their work consists of gathering and organizing a chain of evidence that can solve the queries by other petroleum professionals.

## 6.2.3 Data Usage Understanding

However, getting the right data is not merely a matter of locating the data about an area designated by the requesting petroleum professional. Our informants were reflective about the effort it takes to understand what kind of data the geologists and engineers are looking for (see Section 6.1.2). Such problem-solving tasks are far from trivial and require knowledge and experience in different domains: “DMs have to know about seismics, geophysics, and geology to know why you need different data on logging,” one DM in exploration explained.

Seismic data, for example, must be processed and refined in one way or another, and to different degrees. This processing is done for specific purposes—for instance, to achieve the best resolution at a certain depth, to highlight a specific phenomenon, or to cancel out a specific form of noise. Whether these refined data can be used for new projects depends on the problem the geoscientists are seeking to solve. In companies that employ DMs, answering this question relies on constant interaction between DMs and geoscientists. However, as one DM commented: “The problem is that sometimes the geoscientists don’t know what to ask for … because they don’t know what data are actually available and where.” Thus, the professionals handling the data set need to learn to assess how far back in the processing or interpretation chain they have to go to obtain data (see Section 6.3.3). Raw, unprocessed data sets are extremely large (in the order of terabytes for seismic data) and may be found in one kind of database, while the newest official interpretation of an area is found elsewhere. We discuss this point further in Section 6.3.4. An aspect of data usage understanding that emerges from this story is determining from which physical area to provide data. This is particularly relevant in exploration. An example of such discussions is a well log acquired from a nearby old well. Is it too old? Is it too far away? Does it penetrate relevant geological layers? Was the sensor technology and execution of the logging project reliable? Thus, instead of merely collecting as much data as possible, petroleum professionals learn to assess the relevance and usefulness of the data based on their understanding of the ongoing project and the context of the different data.

## 6.3 Data Preparation: Getting the Data Right

Formal accounts of data management work in oil and gas companies (Figure 1) tend to assume that this work is merely a matter of assuring the quality once and importing the data into relevant specialized tools and applications once the data have been located and retrieved. However, this belies the complexity and actual work involved in getting the data right for use by the requesting petroleum professionals. For example, since all exploration data collected on the Norwegian continental shelf must be stored in the Diskos national centralized repository, it might appear straightforward to think that all the available data are stored in a sanitized format. The reality is very different.

## 6.3.1 Ensuring File Formatting Consistency

The format of the data in oil and gas exploration and production depends on where, when, why, how, and by whom the data were collected and stored. Therefore, an aspect of getting the data right is ensuring the formatting consistency across data files before sending them to the requesting petroleum professional.

The DMs we interviewed remarked that geoscientists in the company often talk about ensuring file formatting as a matter of fixing file headers. However, this work is not so simple. Available data are collected by different service companies with differing technological setups that vary over time. File formats differ over time and different companies use varying data conventions and stick to standards to greater and lesser degrees. For instance, one company may provide the depth of its well measurements based on the sea surface, but another may correct the depth, basing it on the standard convention of meters from the seabed instead.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, once the right data are identified, the DMs say that an important part of the work is to standardize the file headers. This is, for instance, crucial for seismic files as they are analyzed and filtered. One DM in production commented, “It must look right, so that you understand it!” We became curious about his use of the term “right.” He explained that this is something that professionals working with the data learn to assess based on experience: “You learn that it is important to know how the seismic data have been collected and processed to be able to check their header.” As a result, a file format being right or not depends on work performed by others (DMs, geoscientists, engineers) on that data set thus far. A DM in production specialized in seismic data reflects on her recurrent practices to ensure that files are error free:

When I want to check the internal consistency of the seismic data, I open them in [a Unix-based system] to ensure that they are complete. I do some random slicing and crosscuts just to make sure the data look ok. I feel like it is more a matter of sensing that something looks off and then checking more, but sometimes it is also a matter of stumbling across errors. Both in seismic data and elsewhere. For example, I worked with well data that had been released to partners too early. I discovered a mistake by accident in Diskos, where [a company] had set a wrong completion date. I contacted the company, they apologized and uploaded new metadata.

## 6.3.2 Ensuring Cross-Platform Formatting Consistency

As illustrated above, data files tend to be scattered across many different software systems. An example is the national database Diskos. The data in Diskos are rarely clean and ready for use in digital format. There are always integration issues, as data sets must be assembled by combining what is available in Diskos with what can be found in the sociotechnical infrastructure on which oil operations rely. This infrastructure consists of several internal and external systems and services (see Section 6.1) as well as the knowledge of experts who might have worked with and interpreted those data sets. One DM (production department, oil company) told us that this situation made her “feel like an octopus.”

To deal with this situation, the DMs and their colleagues are concerned with constantly ensuring formatting consistency across different platforms because the same file does not necessarily have the same format on all platforms. This includes the well logs that are streamed in by vendors on a regular basis. According to one DM, well data files are more problematic to handle compared to seismic data. The work of ensuring file consistency across platforms is considerable in terms of converting file formats and investigating the origin of the data set:

Seismic data are typically quite straightforward. Well data are instead much more complex. The first step is to get the well data from Diskos into [the company database]. It is the well data [DMs] work most with. [He then goes through all the possible file formats that the well data can be available in] The “.las” files are in ASCII. The “.lis” format is older, is found in Diskos but needs to be re-converted back to “.las”. Then there is the “.tif” format which refers to the old tape formats. These too are converted into “.las”. A [great] deal of work is to check that the data match the reference value—for example, the correspondence between the drill floor and the water level [across the various platforms in which the data are found]. This is important because there are often [vendors] who perform the logging during the drilling phase. It also depends on where the data are generated, if in the British or in the Norwegian continental shelf, for example. (DM, exploration, oil company)

The work of ensuring cross-platform consistency is important because the process of setting parameters varies considerably across platforms. Therefore, moving files across platforms is far from trivial. Some systems that the disciplines in this study use for exploration reside in, for example, either Windows or Linux only. However, the work of geoscientists interpreting the subsurface is dependent on the data flow across the two platforms. One DM told us that a crucial challenge when moving files across platforms is that data sets are like black boxes that must always be opened to understand what “lies beneath it,” namely, what data are actually there and in what format. This includes understanding, for example, the context in which they were produced, when they were produced, and the technology, purpose, and metadata available (see also Section 6.2.3). Once again, the history of a data set is important for how it can be handled today.

## 6.3.3 Assessing Data Provenance

Assessing the quality of the data is a very broad concern with different facets. As a starting point, quality means slightly different things for the different professionals involved in data work in this study. For instance, geoscientists are interested in the quality of the data in the sense of the data’s consistency with reference to the physical world—that is, the subsurface reservoir. DMs focus instead on quality assurance in the sense of ensuring formatting consistency. We pointed above to the importance of considering the historical context of a data set’s production to ensure the data set’s internal or external consistency. We elaborate further on this issue. All the professionals in this study are very aware that a data set can make sense only with reference to the work that has been done to produce and sustain it. One geologist acting as DM described his job as being about “establishing a conscious relation with the data” (exploration, oil company). Interested in this statement, we asked him and other informants what they thought about it during one of the co-design workshops that we ran.

During this workshop, we introduced a web-based portal for presenting exploration data where a quality flag had been associated with each data set. We then asked a group of geoscientists and DMs what they thought about it. Interestingly, none of them accepted the flag at face value. They asked questions about it, wondering where it came from. For instance, one commented, “What does 100% quality mean? And for whom? I am always skeptical towards [Quality control] flags. What I usually do is to download the data and see if there are problems.” (Excerpt from field notes)

In this vignette, quality emerges as a relational property of a data set, which depends on its provenance—namely, who has done what and how with a data set, and when. One reason for the skepticism about pre-set data quality flags was that the degree to which the data have been checked and quality-assured before archiving varies greatly, according to our informants. This is why a central task and concern for DMs is ensuring internal consistency within individual data sets and across different data sources, as illustrated above.

The DMs continued discussing the webbased portal. They find it useful to know tha there is a quality issue, so an indicator [a percentage] might work fine for that, but they need to know more about exactly what kind of issue it is. They give several examples of different quality issues such an indicator may point to, issues that they need to be aware of. One of them explains how well logs taken after drilling, arguably the best logs, also have quality issues due to the fact that they are based on sensors measuring through the concrete casing of the log. (Excerpt from field notes)

This conversation about data quality illustrates that the work done to assess the provenance of well data largely translates into acquiring additional knowledge about the way the well logging process was performed in general and for the specific well.

## 6.3.4 Data Gatekeeping

The octopus metaphor mentioned above is a colorful reminder that data work often consists of stretching across a maze of systems and people who might be related to the data being searched for, and then filtering, cleaning, and massaging the data into an acceptable format before they are handed over to petroleum engineers and geoscientists. These professionals sometimes come back with additional requests based on the new insights from the analysis of the first data set. The interactions between the petroleum professionals involved in data work are not only important for iteratively finding the right data but also are an important aspect of the work of gatekeeping data, where what data sets will reach the requesting geoscientist or engineer is negotiated. One aspect of gatekeeping, indicated by all the DMs we interviewed, is to never provide a colleague with data deemed incomplete or too low in quality. A second aspect of data gatekeeping deals with learning to provide colleagues with only the data that they actually need, instead of the data they ask for:

When geoscientists say, “I want all these data,” I will reply, “Ok, but tell me what you have to do with them.” The geoscientists will specify more, so I will reply, “Ok, then you do not need all this! You need only this and that.” (DM, exploration, oil company)

Gatekeeping practices, in sum, exhibit a deep understanding of the professions served regarding their work activities and data needs.

## 7 Discussion: Turning the Lights on in the Backrooms of Data Science

Knorr Cetina (1997) observes that a key characteristic of modernity is that work comes to increasingly center on epistemic objects. She develops the notion of epistemic objects as continuously unfolding, and having an ambivalent ontology (Kallinikos et al., 2013) to explain the emergent and open-ended character of scientific work. Central to Knorr Cetina’s knowledge work argument is that contemporary forms of work come to share many of the characteristics and practices of scientific work. This motivates further research into what Bechky and Barley (1994) refer to as “the backrooms of science,” regarding the extensive yet invisible work of lab technicians in preparing, handling, and curating objects of scientific inquiry (see also Shapin, 1989).

Data science is an illustrative instance of a transition toward knowledge work that uses digital representations as focal epistemic objects. A performative perspective on data has taught us that data come to matter in the context of specific practices (Østerlie et al., 2012) that are often overlooked in official accounts. The challenge for IS is to unpack how the data come to matter for analysis in data science through data management practices. We contribute to the IS discourse on data science by drawing attention to the backstage work involved in data science and by investigating what happens behind the scenes in data analytics. In what follows, we discuss four key characteristics of data work on the backstage of data science in the context of oil and gas exploration and production.

First, data work is unstable and bidirectional. Our findings indicate that data work is hardly a specific step in the workflow but unfolds dynamically through the interplay between the data stewardship context and the recurring practices that shape and are shaped by that context (cf. Almklov et al., 2014; Jones, 2019; Moser & Law, 2006). We identified two main sets of practices used to discover (i.e., getting the right data) and prepare (i.e., getting the data right) the data. The context is not stable, however. In this study, for example, data work depends on the constant variations that characterize data stewardship in oil and gas and, more generally, in geology.

Nevertheless, all work is messy if one just examines it closely enough. So, too, with data science analytics. For instance, in discussing the implications of different (inductive/deductive) approaches to data science analytics, Mikalef et al. (2020) draw attention to how analysts at different points in the analysis process may have to backtrack, revisit assumptions to address confirmation bias, and possibly begin the analysis anew. While the instability of data analytics is episodic (i.e., confined to the data analysis process), the instability of data work is longitudinal, as the data are maintained through continuous work that balances heterogeneous technical, temporal, spatial, and social concerns over time. In the case of exploration and production, data work is unstable because it unfolds within a constantly evolving context of systems, formats, regulations, and practices that have evolved and changed over time. Not only were different systems operable in different time periods and supplied by different vendors, but the data sets stored in different databases also vary in terms of quality and format. Furthermore, regulations change across country borders and, crucially, the interpretation of the historical evolution of a geological area is evolves continuously. As a result, data work tasks cannot be predefined because they vary in response to this shifting context.

The episodic character of data science analytics can be regarded as an effect of data work. A key function of data work performed by professionals such as DMs is to establish the references that make the data appear sufficiently stable for subsequent analysis. Thus, data work is qualitatively different from data analytics: It is a necessary precursor to analytics work because it enacts the conditions for the episodic character of analysis by insulating it from the longitudinal context of evolving systems, formats, regulations, and practices. This means that data work is also bidirectional. To perform it, DMs and their colleagues must have a good enough understanding of not only the journeys that the data have undergone (cf. Leonelli, 2016) but also the potential applications of the data for subsequent analytics. Never isolated from the practices in which data are enacted, they are held together by ad hoc combinations of coordination acts, tacit knowledge, personal experience, system integration (or lack thereof), and informal conversations (cf. Moser & Law, 2006). In this study, DMs play a crucial role in building and sustaining this itinerary for the data and their meaning. For example, the data involved in an exploration project are decontextualized representations drawn from a vast variety of contexts. The data are produced by different sensor types with different characteristics and peculiarities, at different times, by different companies, and from different areas. An important task of data workers is to resolve all the different references to each context and assess the relevance that these references may have when the data are (re)assembled for a new project. Compared to technical accounts of data science (Provost & Fawcett, 2013), we zoom out of a specific analytics system (cf. for example Shi et al., 2016) and show that the itinerary and the future use of the data are not always well supported by the analytics tool set available; often, significant extra work is required. We illustrated this in the case of the data transfer across Windows and

Unix systems, where data are stored for different aims: Although data discovery requires a bidirectional data transfer across the two operating systems, the actual implementation of the data analytics tools is such that only direct data transfer from Unix to Windows is possible. As a result, considerable effort is required to transfer the data in the opposite direction, so that data analytics can be performed.

Data quality is a key concern for data analytics. Large data sets may be more robust for varying degrees of data quality, but “the quality and veracity of data is important because they underpin the extent to which one can trust the conclusions drawn from analysis” (Kitchin, 2014, p. 152). Studies of data science often perceive data quality as control over the various stages of the data flow, from data generation through processing to finished data products, where a lack of control over the data flow leads to a loss of data quality (Abbasi et al., 2016; Monteiro et al., 2018). Chapman et al.’s cross-industry standard process for data mining (CRISP-DM), a widely used analytics model (Chapman et al., 2000, as cited in Kelleher & Tierney, 2018), for instance, relocates data quality to one of several activities in the early stages of a formal data science pipeline. Within analytics, in general, it is assumed that control can be quantified into acceptable margins of error (Baesens et al., 2016). The instability and bidirectionality of data work, however, show that much work lies behind the percentages describing acceptable margins of error. Therefore, the second characteristic of data work is that it involves establishing some degree of control over the data flow in practice.

Think, for instance, of the DM referring to a data search as an effort of going through drawers and cabinets, trying to make some degree of order by acting as an “archiver” of the data within a project. Data work is often quite messy. Nevertheless, as Mol (2002) points out, this might not necessarily be a problem. DMs try to temporarily orchestrate control (Monteiro & Hepsø, 2002; see also Müller et al., 2019) within a project to ensure that the data are of sufficient quality and quantity for the subsequent analytics. We have shown that data do not necessarily cohere in practice across disciplinary, spatial, and temporal boundaries. But when data are made to cohere in a project or a particular setting, this implies constant work to change, clean, and massage the data so that an itinerary is temporarily established along which data may travel and be adapted from one site and situation to another. Thus, control is performed by combining systems, people, and regulations, based on the specific context through emerging practices (see also Parmiggiani & Grisot 2020). This effort was nicely described by the DM who described herself as feeling like “an octopus.” Thinking of the DM like an octopus is a powerful simile, because it foregrounds the flexible and polymorphic intelligence (Ciborra, 2004) that DMs rely on to exert sufficient control over the data flow. One example of this is the practice of assessing the quality control flags associated with a data set. To assess quality, the first thing that the DM asks for is the journey (work) that the data have undergone and who performed that work. This is also a vivid illustration of the relational nature of quality in data science (cf. Star & Ruhleder, 1996), where quality is an emergent property that is contingent on the specific data stewardship context and practices that produced the data.

Third, this study emphasizes the importance of the sociality of data work in data science. We draw upon a picture of data science as an inherently collaborative endeavor (Passi & Jackson, 2018) centered on and mediated by the data qua epistemic objects (Knorr Cetina, 1997). Whereas imaginaries of the solitary data scientist who can “make discoveries while swimming in data” fascinate scholars (Davenport & Patil, 2012), we illustrate that data work in data science in oil and gas exploration and production involves different disciplines with porous boundaries between them, often blending with those involved in analytics. We illustrated, for example, that data work is not only performed by those formally assigned to it, such as DMs, but is also a concerted effort involving their peers and other disciplines in the domain, such as geoscientists and petroleum engineers. In addition, the professionals working with the data informally rely on colleagues to solve problems and answer questions about the data and to negotiate what data are needed and why. Even when DMs are not formally organized in separate departments, across exploration and production, DMs maintain a disciplinary identity to tackle the highly dynamic nature of their tasks by forming informal networks. The sense of belonging to a network is strong. However, mirroring the tendency to focus primarily on front-stage data analytics, these networking activities are not visible in most of the IS literature or in official accounts of oil and gas exploration and production. Together with the sociotechnical arrangements their work is part of, DMs have, to a certain extent, sunk into the background of the organization’s core business tasks (cf. Star & Ruhleder, 1996). Playing a supportive role for core tasks, the infrastructural character of data work is evinced by its lack of recognition as a profession in its own right. This is not always a negative thing, however. As illustrated in Plantin’s (2019) study of data curators in the social sciences, the same work practices can implement visibility and invisibility. The other side of DMs invisibility is the polymorphism that characterizes their professional role, which can adapt based on the nature of the data flow to make sense of the data and find a solution to recurring problems.

An additional factor that might partially explain the invisibility of DMs is the current absence of established formal training at the university level. Managerial perspectives on IS point to data science expertise as a type of capability that must be formalized by firm managers to gain competitive advantage (Mikalef et al., 2020). Although this is certainly the case for data analytics, we show that there is no finite set of skills that can be easily formalized into a set of capabilities that a data worker must acquire. Following the informal networks of DM practice illustrated that the work of establishing references in the data for specific projects is not something that DMs know from the start. They learn to use the available systems and recognize the data types by constantly interacting with other colleagues to solve emerging problems and new queries sent by geoscientists and petroleum engineers. Data work skills are constructed over time and must constantly adapt to new challenges.

Finally, data work is shaped by the epistemological orientation of the domain. Like other domains, oil and gas is characterized by modes of acquiring knowledge that influence how work is performed (Ribes et al., 2019). Frodeman (1995, p. 936) describes geology as a hermeneutic science: “The geologist picks upon the clues of past events and processes in a way analogous to how the physician interprets the signs of illness or the detective builds a circumstantial case against a defendant.” In this interpretive work, geologists move recursively from seeing details in the context of large structures and structures in the context of details. They search for patterns and clues on different scales, and they refine, discard, and strengthen theories as they interrogate various data. As geologists’ theories of how hydrocarbons moved and became trapped somewhere in the past change, so too do their data requirements. Although vast amounts of data exist, phenomena such as subsea structures cannot be seen or accessed directly, and searches for patterns are also largely based on these hermeneutic movements between parts and wholes, in which data scientists play an important role.

## 8 Conclusions and Implications

In this paper, we sought to open the backrooms of data science to inspection. Taking a performative perspective, we illustrate that data work is both integral to and a necessary precursor of data analytics. We are aware that the boundary conditions (Suddaby, 2010) of this study are empirical, as we primarily drew on a study in the oil and gas sector. Because of the inaccessibility of oil and gas formations, this sector relies entirely on big data generated by sensors. Although we do not claim that we can transfer these findings to other domains as is, we believe that our discussion of data work has relevance for other domains characterized by large open sets of sensor-generated data that are, by definition, inherently underdetermined. This is, for instance, the case in earth sciences (Oreskes et al., 1994), climate modeling (Edwards, 2010), and the energy industry (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019).

Moreover, data science today is part of the backbone of several data-driven domains, such as the banking and insurance sectors (Passi & Jackson, 2018), the airline industry (Kane, 2014), and, importantly, healthcare (Grisot et al., 2019). In these domains, unpacking data work invites researchers to reconsider the dominant visions of algorithm adoption and the rhetoric of automation and “to study the multilevel sociotechnical conditions and stakeholders involved in the design, use, and consequences of algorithms in organizations” (Markus, 2017, p. 233). Data-driven decision-making processes are not happening only in isolated front-stage “managerial cockpits” based on visualized data (Abbasi et al., 2016). This is only the tip of the iceberg. The rest of the iceberg below the water surface comprises backroom data work practices. These are not routinized but entail continuous decision-making processes. For instance, Amazon’s Alexa vocal assistant’s ability to makes sense of and respond to human inquiries relies on continuous human work to prepare the training data sets to feed its neural network (Statt, 2019). Taking into serious consideration the day-to-day decisions made as part of backroom data work practices is fundamental to ensuring the sustainability of digitalization initiatives.

Finally, these findings have significant implications for teaching data science at the university and executive education levels. Data science is often taught as a business school or computer science subject, where either business-related concerns or programming and statistics dominate (West & Portenoy, 2016). We show that data science also involves learning data work-related abilities over time that are highly cross-disciplinary. Interdisciplinary educational programs at the intersection between computer science, sociotechnical studies, and the “background” scientific fields where the data originate (such as the geosciences, in the cases studied) would seem needed if best use is to be made of the burgeoning data resources that are becoming available in many domains.

## About the Authors

Elena Parmiggiani. is an associate professor of digital collaboration at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU), where she coordinates the Digital Enterprise Research Area at the Faculty of Information Technology and Electrical Engineering. She holds a PhD in information technology from NTNU and an MSc in computer engineering from the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia. She studies data work in data science, as well as the ongoing digitalization of work processes associated with the implementation and maintenance of digita platforms and information infrastructures. She is also interested in the methodological stakes of studying distributed and long-term arrangements. Her work is based on an interdisciplinary lens across information systems and computersupported cooperative work (CSCW) influenced by science and technology studies. Empirically, she has focused on environmental monitoring, the oil industry, and welfare. She currently serves as associate editor at Computer Supported Cooperative Work and has published in outlets such as MIS Quarterly, Computer Supported Cooperative Work, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, and Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems.

Thomas Østerlie. is an associate professor of management of information systems at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU). He currently serves as research program manager for Industrial Digital Transformation in SFI Sirius, a national center for research-based innovation on digitalization in the oil and gas industry. Østerlie holds a PhD in information technology and an MSc in computer science, both from NTNU. His main research interest is digitalization and the transformation of contemporary work and organizing, with particular focus on the role and function of digital representations. He pursues these research interests through longitudinal and indepth empirical studies in close collaboration with practitioners and key stakeholders in mature industries such as oil and gas production, offshore infrastructure construction, and the onshore construction industry.

Petter Grytten Almklov. is a professor of sociology at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) within the research areas of work, organization, and digitalization. He received an MSc in geological engineering before he turned to the social sciences. His main research orientations can be summarized as representations of work practice and the use of representations in practice, the latter primarily focusing on expert work such as subsurface geology. He is currently, among other things, conducting research on emergency management and societal safety, including control room studies in emergency call centers. His work has been published in a variety of outlets such as Social Studies of Science, Information and Organization, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Computer Supported Cooperative Work, Public Administration, Marine Policy, and Safety Science.
