# Extending Unbounded Systems Thinking with agent-oriented modeling: conceptualizing a multiple perspective decision-making support system

Dianne Hall<sup>a,\*</sup>, Yi Guo<sup>b,1</sup>, Robert A. Davis<sup>c,2</sup>, Casey Cegielski<sup>a,3</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, Auburn University, 401 Lowder Business Building, Auburn, AL 36849-5241, United States <sup>b</sup>Department of Information and Operations Management, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843-4217, United States <sup>c</sup>Department of Computer Information Systems, Texas State University-San Marcos, San Marcos, TX 78666-4616, United States

Received 19 June 2003; received in revised form 18 June 2004; accepted 24 June 2004 Available online 4 August 2004

## Abstract

Organizations today face complex decision-making environments in which multiple perspectives must be considered. Mitroff and Linstone [The Unbounded Mind: Breaking the Chains of Traditional Business Thinking (1993) Oxford University Press, New York] developed Unbounded Systems Thinking (UST), of which multiple perspectives are an integral part. They suggest that, while the theory of applying multiple perspectives to decision-making and knowledge creation is sound and well researched, the implementation of multiple perspectives is difficult. It is maintained here that agent-oriented modeling can be used to design a system supporting multiple perspectives and to extend UST. This paper applies the Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) model of agents to UST to demonstrate the use of agent-oriented modeling to facilitate multiple perspective decision support. <sup>D</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Multiple perspectives; Decision-making; Agents; BDI model

## 1. Introduction

Research on decision-making with its various components (information acquisition, problem formulation, information modeling, etc.) is prevalent in the literature. Much of the literature focuses on enabling decision-making by categorizing or focusing knowledge and information into uni-perspective packages ripe for choosing the <sup>b</sup>right<sup>Q</sup> or <sup>b</sup>best<sup>Q</sup> alternative. Very often, the perspective chosen is one that is widespread within the group or organization, or one that is associated with an influential individual within the group. According to Nonaka and Takeuchi [33], individuals are not passive in their reception of new information or knowledge and must interpret it to fit their own situation and perspective. This interpretation is tenuous at best; change in interpretation can occur simply through filtered diffusion of information or knowledge from a singular perspective. Only by employing multiple perspectives during information acquisition and diffusion can an organization provide <sup>b</sup>. . .employees with a conceptual framework that helps them make sense of their own experience<sup>Q</sup> [33, p.15]. This necessitates a system design that is cognizant of the use and benefits of multiple perspectives. System design, irrespective of methodology, is the first critical step toward an efficiently working implementation.

This paper begins the process by examining agentoriented system design implications for multiple perspective system support and proposing that Unbounded Systems Thinking (UST) can logically be extended by applying the Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) model. A cooperative agent system is exemplified, indicating that agent-oriented modeling is an appropriate methodology for designing such a system, and that agent technology may be an appropriate implementation tool for multiple perspective decision-making systems.

## 2. Systems design for multiple perspectives

Many researchers have investigated the need for organizations to support multiple perspectives. Hine and Goul [22] stress the need for organizations to engage in interpretive learning, during which members develop their own perspective of the organizational environment and work toward forming an organization-wide perspective based on these multiple interpretations. Argyris and Sch<sup>f</sup>n [1] and DiBella and Nevis [10] suggest that information stagnation may be reduced and organizational learning improved if groups are formed with individuals of different backgrounds (hence different perspectives) who are encouraged to interact and share their views during information acquisition, problem formulation and/or decision-making. A recent study by Kim et al. [28] shows that groups that utilize a higher number of perspectives to refine hypotheses regarding problematic situations are more likely to correctly identify problems.

Churchman [6] approached the problems and benefits of multiple perspectives in systems in his book The Design of Inquiring Systems. Of particular importance is his Singerian inquirer, based on the writings of E. A. Singer, Jr. The Singerian inquirer is the most complex of the inquirers discussed by Churchman [6], both in concept and in the fact that it incorporates other inquirers into its environment. This inquirer can be thought of as a continuous process that includes establishment of measures, replication, challenge of assumptions, and growth of organizational memory through knowledge growth by individual employees [7]. The goal of the Singerian inquirer is to create verified common knowledge, <sup>b</sup>sweeping in<sup>Q</sup> variables such as rules, heuristics, and perspectives from any verified source until equivocality in the system has been reduced. If equivocality cannot be reduced using existing measures, the <sup>b</sup>sweeping in<sup>Q</sup> process is employed. This process is often accomplished by encouraging divergent thinking; that is, encouraging users to consider alternative perspectives to mediate disagreements. When users routinely use such a process to expand how they collect and interpret information and how they act on such information, they have increased their natural image (worldview) [37]. When employees are encouraged to broaden their natural image during activities such as information acquisition, information discovery, or decision-making, it is probable that consensus will be achieved.

Achieving consensus may be a critical success factor in organizational decision-making, which is usually accomplished in a group setting. This setting forces social interaction to the forefront. Group interaction is an integral part of decision-support and problem solving, and yet is often overlooked in the literature. Kwok et al. [30] propose that a multiperson, multicriteria decision model may increase the probability of reaching consensus by encouraging communication from involved individuals. This communication gives rise to a mutual understanding among decision makers who view information and consider alternatives from multiple perspectives.

Consensus may be difficult to achieve with traditional decision-making models. Naturalistic and classic decision models disagree in their approaches to the decision-making process and may not be adequate for sustainable decision-making [20]. Sustainable decision scenarios very often impact many diverse individuals or groups, making it difficult not only to achieve consensus on a solution, but also on the pool of alternatives from which that solution may be chosen. A wider decision context requires an extension of the traditional organizational thinking process and may be necessary to generate appropriate alternatives. Mitroff and Linstone [32] proposed such an extension.

Using Singer’s philosophical basis as a background, Mitroff and Linstone developed Unbounded Systems Thinking (UST). While Singer often considered perspectives to come from (particularly scientific) expertise, Churchman [6] pointed out that human-based organizations must pay particular attention to the social perspective and must guard against the loss of individual perspective. UST promotes the use of technical (scientific), organizational (societal), and personal perspectives.

Each of these perspectives has distinct underlying assumptions and values and constructs unique insights to a given problem. The technical perspective embodies the scientific worldview and prefers to function with logic and rationality. Its goal is to analyze situations in a way that produces a series of possible rational problem solutions. Choice or implementation of a solution is secondary to the analysis. The technical perspective is a common one in organizations, and works well under structured problems. It is not effective in situations where the situation is complex or where there is little or no background information.

The organizational perspective has a societal worldview and prefers abstract thought. An organizational perspective does not imply that it exists only in an organization. It may be embodied in either a formal or informal grouping, as large as a worldwide religion to as small as a family unit [32, p. 99]. This perspective pays particular attention to societal impact and to organizational policy, and considers the culture and myths that are the foundation of the group.

The personal perspective has an individualistic worldview and is concerned with values and morality. The goal of the personal perspective is individual gain of power or influence. When an individual has a strong personal perspective and is in a situation to provide leadership within a group, the personal perspective of that individual may serve to expand the context of a decision. The personal perspective is a function of the individual’s experiences and values, and thus is unique for each individual.

UST maintains that no problem can be adequately analyzed without attention being paid to all of these perspectives. According to Mitroff and Linstone, none of the perspectives by itself <sup>b</sup>suffices to deal with a complex system, but together they give a richer base for decision and action<sup>Q</sup> [32, p. 101]. They suggest guidelines for integrating these perspectives, such as maintaining a balance among them. Importantly for decision-making, it must be recognized that each perspective may recognize, gather, and perceive as important vastly different types of information for the same decision context. The worldview of each perspective determines the <sup>b</sup>lens<sup>Q</sup> through which a problem scenario is viewed and on which action is taken. This lens influences not only the generation of alternative solutions (goal), but also the search for and acceptance of relevant information (mode of inquiry). Each perspective limits the amount of information deemed relevant in any situation by further segregating information according to other dimensions, such as the ethical basis, planning horizon, and other characteristics of the perspective such as communication style.

It is generally not human nature to embody multiple perspectives, even when one has been made aware of alternative perspectives. This may be a particular problem in a group situation if any group member has real or imagined influence over the others. Individuals reacting from UST’s personal perspective may affect problem reformulation, validation, and information seeking/sharing. Cross et al. [8] find that the social context of a group is relevant not only to information seeking, but also to problem structuring and decision validation. This is particularly important in this era of corporate social responsibility, where decisions made within an organization must not only be evaluated for their impact on the organization, but also for their impact on society [4,7,17,23,35,38].

Utilizing one perspective gives an individual the ability to focus on what the problem is whereas multiple perspectives requires the ability to focus on how that problem is being viewed. When multiple perspectives are utilized, the what of the problem often changes and the solution may become more evident. This process requires the support of a system that can embody beliefs and can work cooperatively toward multi-perspective problem formulation and decisionmaking.

Development of systems that support multiple perspectives has not been historically evident in the literature (an exception is literature on cooperative work and group decision-making) but is becoming more prevalent. Janssen and Sage [24] introduce a support system designed to help an organization process information received from a number of viewpoints. Mark [31] discusses the use of conventions (a means to merge multiple perspectives) both between and within groups. Stolze [39] and Finkelstein et al. [14] suggest that uni-perspective approaches in systems development are potentially dangerous. Davies and Mabin [9] suggest that multiple perspectives may be successfully merged when framing techniques are properly applied and that the synthesis of the perspectives leads to greater insight and knowledge.

Both Churchman [6] and Mitroff and Linstone [32] suggest that, while the theory of applying multiple perspectives to decision-making and knowledge creation is sound and well researched, particularly in the area of cognition and psychology, the implementation of multiple perspectives is difficult. It is maintained here that a system can be designed to support multiple perspectives by employing agent technology. To demonstrate the concepts outlined in this research, an artifact (the multi-agent admissions review system) has been created to alleviate a business problem (balancing the admission of qualified applicants with resource constraints and diversity concerns), and it has been tested in a controlled simulation environment with data from actual applicants. This follows the guidelines for IS design research suggested by Hevner et al. [21].

## 3. Facilitating multiple perspective systems with agent technology

Constructing complex information systems using software agents is not new. For instance, Elofson et al. [12] illustrate how using intelligent agents during information acquisition can facilitate knowledge sharing. Chang and Chen [5] discuss using autonomous agents for information management. Bradshaw et al. [2] indicate that agent technology provides potential for many applications and begin development of tools designed to allow agent technology to be used by non-experts in the field.

Fowler [15] discusses the application of AI technology to a framework based on knowledge creation and business processes that support knowledge management. Bui and Lee [3] propose a framework for a cooperative decision support system that incorporates cooperating agents (either human or not). Based on their description of an Internet-based DSS being a form of Cooperative Information System, their framework relates primarily to situations in which the task, although possibly complex, is likely pre-determined. While this framework is appropriate for that context, a multi-agent framework is necessary to support a comprehensive knowledge management system faced with uncertainty, complexity, and illstructured problems.

While application of agent technology in information technology is a current and active research stream, much of that research does not directly address the problem of multiple perspectives. A framework for the incorporation of agent technology into a multiple perspectives system is outlined below after a brief introduction of related agent concepts.

A software agent (<sup>b</sup>agent<sup>Q</sup>) is a self-contained program capable of controlling its own decisionmaking and acting, based on its perception of its environment, in pursuit of one or more objectives [26]. In particular, agents enjoy properties of autonomy, responsiveness, pro-activeness, and social ability, which make an agent a "computer system, situated in some environment, which is capable of flexible autonomous action in order to meet its design objectives<sup>Q</sup> [18, p. 276]. Two key distinguishing characteristics of agents are (1) that relatively high level tasks can be delegated to them, and (2) that they exist in an environment that can dynamically affect their problem-solving behavior and strategy.

Many researchers agree that agents and multi-agent systems represent a new way of analyzing, designing, and implementing complex software systems. The agent-based and agent-oriented views offer a powerful repertoire of metaphors, tools, and techniques that have the potential to considerably improve the way people conceptualize and implement many types of software, including that designed for decision-making and information management.

With special relevance, multi-agent systems, where the system is designed and implemented as groups of interacting agents, are <sup>b</sup>ideally suited to representing problems that have multiple problem solving methods, multiple perspectives and/or multiple problem solving entities.<sup>Q</sup> [25, p. 277]. This is primarily because, in addition to the traditional advantages of distributed and concurrent problem solving, agent technology also has advantages of sophisticated patterns of interaction (cooperation, coordination, and negotiation). However, there is little literature on how to implement these more complex, multi-leveled systems. A cooperativeagent framework is proposed herein as an implementation tool for complex systems.

## 4. A cooperative-agent framework for multiple perspective decision-making

The Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) model of agents, developed by the Australian Artificial Intelligence Institute, has become one of the best known and most studied models for agents [34]. An agent’s practical reasoning process involves repeatedly updating beliefs from information in the environment, deciding what options are available, filtering these options to determine new intentions, and acting on the basis of these intentions. In some cases, this reasoning may also include an effort to coordinate with other agents in the system in order to synthesize beliefs and desires into an achievable, consensual plan of action. In a similar fashion, agent-oriented programming [36] is a multi-agent programming model in which agents are explicitly programmed in terms of mentalistic notions. In a multi-agent system, agents have different beliefs and goals. Multiple perspectives are implicitly embedded in a given agent’s beliefs; these beliefs include the agent’s worldview. Therefore, it is natural and reasonable to use agents, particularly within the context of the BDI [34] model, to express multiple perspectives and to facilitate the entire process of decision-making, from information acquisition through action (solution) selection.

The mental structure of a BDI agent is comprised of three components; they are beliefs, desires, and intentions. Beliefs are the informative component of the agent’s mental state. When perceiving the world and collecting data, agents acting under different perspectives each pay unique attention to various information items in the environment according to that perspective. The desire component represents the motivational state of a certain perspective, thus representing the goals of an agent. The intention component is used to capture the currently chosen course of action (the plan) or the output of the last selection. It is the deliberative component.

When applying BDI structure to UST dimensions, an agent’s beliefs can be partitioned into two parts. The first contains the fundamentals of the particular perspective or axioms base (worldview) on which the agent is to act. These are meta-beliefs. General characteristics of each perspective are also embodied in their meta-beliefs. The other belief component contains facts that the agent collects from the environment, which are interpreted through the lens of the meta-beliefs (i.e., in terms of UST, the perspectives). Beliefs are parallel to the concept of a mental model. That is, they are continuously updated as the environment changes, yet the underlying foundations often remain unchanged over time. Desires are the goals or motivation of the agent. In order to fulfill its desire, an agent must choose a course of action based on certain decision criteria, which also reflect the perspective of an agent. The decision criteria of perspectives are the expected utility defining deliberate functions. The output of these deliberating and selecting activities is the intention component. In a dynamic environment, each entity with the capacity to make decisions and to carry out acts will face dilemmas between reconsidering the choice of action at each step based on newly perceived information, which can be costly, and unconditional commitment to chosen actions, which can lead to failure. Thus, the agent adopts the best intention/plan for the specific context. Table 2 maps the BDI model to the dimensions of the UST perspectives.

For example, looking for cause and effect relationships is one of the goals in the technical perspective; however, information or facts about causes and effects must be expressed and stored as beliefs. More importantly, this mapping is not meant to be comprehensive. Some characteristics listed cannot be easily mapped to any of the mental attitudes comprising the BDI model.

The table is a meta-level structure. When applied to a real situation, meta-information should be substituted with contextual information. For example, solving a specific problem or designing a particular product will be the goal or desire. An agent can be used to represent each stakeholder or groups of stakeholders with individual value systems, personally defined challenges and responses, and so on. Agents working from a technical, organizational, or personal perspective can represent different worldviews as they interact not only with each other, but also with individuals.

Consider the example of the agent-oriented air traffic management system (OASIS) [34] in which a given aircraft agent tries to fly along a certain flight path provided by the coordinates of a sequence of waypoints. Naturally, such an agent pays attention only to facts related to its job—taking off, flying, and landing safely. Those facts include wind and trajectories. It also follows rules regarding speed and altitude. On the other hand, a sequencer agent has different goal of landing all aircraft safely in an optimal sequence; it is concerned with aircraft performance, desired separation time, wind field, and runway traffic and condition. These two types of agents have different intentions and desires, thus different perceptions of the world (beliefs). In other words, they have different perspectives. An additional example is one of e-commerce agents in a market, engaging in negotiations to maximize their utility [27]. Utility is a factor of the perspective from which one views an event and thus how one interprets and reacts to that event. In the following section, an example is given to illustrate the internal model and interactions among agents representing the technical, organizational, and individual perspectives.

## 4.1. Extending the UST model with agent technology

To model a multi-agent system, both external and internal views are necessary. The internal view is the internal structure of an agent; the external view is the interaction among agents. According to Kinny et al. [29], using BDI architecture allows an agent to be <sup>b</sup>completely specified by the events that it can perceive, the actions it may perform, the beliefs it may hold, the goals it may adopt, and the plans that give rise to its intentions.<sup>Q</sup> Therefore, the internal structure for a BDI agent should at least be composed of a belief model, a goal model, and a plan model in order to capture beliefs, desires, and intentions, respectively. Hall et al. [19] proposed a generic internal agent structure based on the BDI architecture and UST dimensions. As shown in Fig. 1 below, the belief component represents the worldview of the UST dimension, the intention generator represents the goals and desires, and the plan repository represents those actions that must be taken to achieve the goals.

As discussed previously, a perspective-based agent has two types of beliefs which are meta-beliefs and environmental interpretation. Meta-beliefs influence how the agent perceives the world; that is, what to see and how to see. An agent’s beliefs may be affected by its perception and interpretation of events occurring in the environment. A belief model contains a belief set and one or more belief states [29]. A belief set describes possible information/beliefs an agent can have about its environment and other internal states and properties of these beliefs. A belief state of an agent (local knowledge or worldview) is an instance of a belief set. Depending on the implementation techniques, a belief set may be considered a <sup>b</sup>table structure<sup>Q</sup> without data while beliefs are manifested as populated tuples (rows) within the table (data). In object-oriented terminology, a belief set can be defined as a class with attributes; once an object is instantiated from the class, the attributes have been populated and the collection of them is the belief state of that object. For example, if a pilot agent believes wind speed is 15 knots, it translates this into a fact in predicate format, such as Belief(pilot, wind speed(15)). This fact can then be stored as a tuple (wind speed: 15) or as an attribute wind speed=15. A pilot agent may have dozens of these facts, all of which are predefined by its perspective and updated regularly.

Both perspective and the role an agent plays determine what beliefs the agent holds. There are facts all agents need to acknowledge, such as wind speed and wind direction. Some facts are determined to be necessary by the role, whereas other facts may be considered irrelevant. A pilot agent pays attention to its own waypoint data and ignores that of the other pilots. A sequencer agent must respond to the waypoint data of multiple pilot agents. Each pilot must work with the sequencer agent to navigate safely; when a disagreement occurs, the sequencer agent has the ultimate authority.

The plan repository (see Fig. 1) contains both predefined plans of action and social laws representing speech act coordination rules. The plan model describes the plans available to the agent. It consists of a plan set that defines individual plans in terms of properties and control structure. A plan can be described using a plan diagram [29]. The plan diagram specifies for each plan the activation event or action, the activity formula, and the outcome of the plan (either pass or fail). Each activity in the plan body can be subgoals. In addition to regular plans, a social component (based on speech acts) can be added [11] to make a BDI agent more socially aware. Available plans are also determined by meta-beliefs.

Goals of an agent can be expressed in a separate module or embedded in its Intention Generator. The intention generator (see Fig. 1) uses information stored in beliefs and the plan repository components along with embedded algorithms to generate appropriate plans of action to achieve its goals. The goal component describes the goal that an agent may have according to its beliefs and the events to which it can respond. A data structure called a goal set is used define the goal and its associated event domains.

An agent has a sensor through which it perceives the world and an effector to carry out the chosen plan. The internal structure is the same for all agents, although each agent’s perspective affects the content of the structure. Contents can be similar among agents but will be interpreted differently (for instance, prioritizing). Likewise, social plans may include a social network of cooperative agents. This network would, of course, be different for each agent type. These concepts are illustrated in the following example.

## 5. Illustrating a cooperative agent implementation of the UST model

All universities have established admission guidelines, but the admissions procedure itself is conducted by individuals with different ideas of what information is important to a student’s future success. Hence, although quantitative models can be and are applied during the admissions process, qualitative information is often important. The following example is patterned after the doctoral candidate admissions process for a Management Information Systems department at a large Southeastern university. Determining whether a particular applicant should be admitted can easily be seen from the three perspectives as described in the UST model.

A technical perspective focuses on models and quantitative data, striving to admit only the most highly qualified candidates. An organizational perspective strives to balance the needs of the department (e.g., diversity) and constraints imposed by the department (e.g., financial aid available, number of students for whom places are available) against the desire for highly qualified candidates. In doing so, the organizational perspective must review and consider not only the quantitative data, but also the qualitative data. The personal perspective focuses on the individual applicant’s overall picture, weighing qualitative data more heavily than quantitative, with its goal to admit as many as possible. All perspectives will examine the same information about an applicant, but each will determine what information is most relevant in vastly different ways. Thus, this combination of meta-beliefs and environmental interpretation populates the belief component of the agent’s internal structure (see Fig. 1) that then influences the agent’s plans. ## 5.1. Building the admissions agent system

In the context of this example, there are three major agents that represent the admissions process from the technical, organizational, and personal perspectives, and one coordinating agent. In a full implementation, there may be significantly more agents; for instance, an information agent that provides additional information on request and an interface agent to bridge between the user and the system. The number of agents is limited in this example in order to provide a reasonably complex demonstration of a multi-agent system while focusing on the basics underlying the DSS.

Further, information collected from each applicant does not necessarily reflect all potential measures of acceptability. The data chosen for analysis were determined by the responses to a survey of the current professors in the department. This survey asked that several factors be ranked according to their importance to the professor in determining an applicant’s suitability. The factors chosen for use in this simulation include quantitative measures (e.g., admission test scores, previous GPA) and qualitative measures (e.g., need for financial aid, gender, personal knowledge of applicant).

The factors collected by the survey were also used to construct the belief sets of the admission agents. Interestingly, the survey results clearly showed each of the perspectives. For instance, one professor indicated that test results are the true indicator of success, ranking both GPA and GMAT results mostly highly with little consideration of other factors. This clearly represents the technical perspective. One professor indicated that it was difficult to rank the criteria because <sup>b</sup>each applicant must be considered individually and subjectively,<sup>Q</sup> representing the personal perspective. The organizational perspective was represented by those who ranked quantitative qualifications fairly high, but considered qualitative factors when confronted with the issue of resource constraints.

Using the Java Agent Development Framework (JADE), an admissions system was constructed with the three admissions agents described above and a coordinating agent that facilitates the conversation. The belief set of each of the admissions agents was populated according to their characteristics as defined by the UST model (Table 3) and the results of the survey by coding heuristics to support each agent’s analysis of applicant data.

Each agent has specific predefined plans in its internal structure that correspond to its worldview, goals, desires, and intentions. Additionally, each agent has social plans designed to facilitate its interaction with other agents. The initial predefined plan for each admission agent is to examine an applicant based on their interpretation of the applicant’s credentials and to respond accordingly. Secondary predefined plans are activated based on the results of the initial plan. For example, if analysis indicates that the applicant is acceptable, the secondary plan for the admission agents is to recommend admission to the coordinating agent. A secondary plan for the coordinating agent is to compare the recommendations and sort the applicants accordingly. Social plans are activated when conversation is required between the agents. Examples of initial, secondary, and social plans involved in consideration of one applicant are shown in Table 4.

Plans such as those outlined in Table 4 are affected by the agents’ beliefs. Accordingly, each interprets the environment (admission application) differently. The technical agent focuses primarily on test score data that can be modeled while the organizational agent looks at all information and attempts to assess the social situation and implications of admitting the applicant. The personal agent looks at the life score qualifications and places the highest weight on that score. Because each agent will interpret the results of the model and the weight of the qualitative information differently, full cooperation will require some element of negotiation to arrive at a consensus. When multi-agent systems achieve consensus through negotiation, they are considered negotiating systems, a subset of cooperating systems [16].

It is important to note that consensus does not imply that all agents must agree—only that all must accept the decision. In the context of the admission example, it is known that the technical agent will not approve the application of someone who fails to pass the model, whereas the personal agent will prioritize applicants with career and/or research experience. Either, however, may agree to accept the other’s recommendation. The organizational agent will investigate further to see if admission can be recommended within the confines of departmental needs and constraints. Ultimately, however, consensus must be reached on each applicant’s admissibility.

In the first decision round, each applicant’s information is passed to the technical and personal admission agents (hereinafter referred to as T and P, respectively) from the coordinating agent (hereinafter referred to as C). The organizational admission agent (hereinafter referred to as O) is situated between T and P in its beliefs and therefore does not participate in the initial decision. Its social plan is to accept the admission list from T and P and analyze it from a departmental viewpoint. Thus, the most efficient use of O as a resource is to perform final admissions analysis. After receiving an applicant’s data, T and P analyze the applicant against their initial admissions criteria. The criteria for initial acceptance and rejection for each agent are listed in Table 5; the initial weights used in negotiation are also listed.

If the applicant fails to satisfy the minimum qualifications set by the agent, a rejection recommendation is sent to C. If the applicant satisfies the ideal qualifications of the agent, an admit recommendation is sent. If the applicant falls between those two extremes, a recommendation to analyze further is sent. When C receives a recommendation from both agents, it sorts the students into three different files: Conditional Admit, Reject, and Analyze Further.

At this point, the admission agents begin the process of negotiating with each other in an attempt to reach consensus regarding the applicants who have not been admitted or rejected. P is the leader in the discussion (that is, begins the proposal sequence) because its goal is to admit individuals that T, by virtue of its more stringent beliefs, does not want to admit. The outcome of this negotiation can take only two forms—T agrees with P on an applicant’s recommendation for admission, or T and P cannot agree. This is translated in agent technology to an affirmation conversation between the two agents [13, p. 337]. Fig. 2 illustrates an affirmation episode in which T and P both argue their sides until an agreement can be reached (T acquiesces) or it becomes apparent that no agreement can be reached.

In this figure, the personal agent (P) is trying to convince the technical agent (T) that the original recommendation for the case (rejection) is incorrect. P’s goal is to have T agree to recommend admission. P first sends an assertion request with a pertinent argument to T which, upon receiving the request, reconsiders the argument. If the argument received from P is in keeping with its own beliefs (such as the strength of the experience credential is high and the test scores are not significantly low), T sends an acquiesce message back to acknowledge the agreement to P and at the same time updates its own knowledge base. An example of a knowledge base insertion might be <sup>b</sup>If GMAT and GPA are low, but experience is high, student is acceptable.<sup>Q</sup> The relativity of <sup>b</sup>high<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>low<sup>Q</sup> will be context dependent, generally defined within the constraints of the current applicant base. If the argument is not in keeping with T’s beliefs (for instance, GMAT, GPA, and experience are all low), it sends a refusal back to P. In response to the refusal, P must invoke its resources (inference engine, plan repository, knowledge base, etc.) to construct other arguments designed to convince T.

The two agents exchange requests and responses until either of two situations occurs. This first situation occurs when T has been persuaded to recommend admission, and P achieves a satisfactory state. The second situation is one in which T reaches a threshold and sends an end request to P to stop further discussion, which results an unsatisfactory situation for P. If T and P have reached an agreement, the applicant will receive a conditional recommendation. If the conversation ended without agreement, the applicant will receive a rejection recommendation. Other conversations can be diagrammed in a similar manner.

In the system developed here, the negotiation takes place by having each agent (T and P) weigh the applicants listed as needing further analysis according to each agent’s priority list weights, and then comparing those lists to see whether they agree. Starting at the top of the list, P recommends admission for the applicant in the top spot. If this applicant is also ranked at the top by T, T acquiesces; if not, T sends a refusal to P. P responds by requesting a secondary weight ranking for the applicant. P will send the ranking order of the applicant to T following the secondary weight analysis, and the process continues. If there has still not been agreement after the secondary weight analysis, T will send a refusal and a request to stop negotiation on this applicant to P. The applicant will then be rejected by both T and P. This process continues until the entire list has been examined. After the negotiating rounds are completed, C is informed by each agent of the outcome of each applicant’s analysis to ensure that the list is true and complete. C then compiles the list of ranked, acceptable applicants, forwards that list to O, and dismisses T and P.

O has a social plan of accepting the decisions made by T and P and the additional responsibility of taking those applicants marked as conditionally accepted and selecting those who fit best with departmental needs. This requires that O rank the applicants with conditional acceptances in a way that best suits the constraints set by the department, such as diversity and available aid. Once all available places have been filled, O informs C of the outcome. O is then dismissed by C, who generates the lists of accepted and rejected applicants.

## 5.2. Testing the admissions agent system

To provide a demonstration of the system, applicant information was analyzed. While the data set is small (n=9), it is actual applicant data received by the aforementioned department for admission into the Fall 2004 doctoral class. The analysis occurred immediately prior to the actual admissions process being completed; the admission committee was unaware of the simulation or its results. The results of the simulation and the admissions committee process are discussed following analysis of the test. The data for each applicant and the results of each decision round are listed in Table 6.

Applicant information was entered into a text file readable by the system. After the system was coded to interact with the text file, the simulation run was started. After the first decision round, only one applicant (4) received a conditional admission by exceeding the initial admission criteria for both T and P. Although one applicant scored higher than applicant 4 on the GMAT and had a higher GPA, and one applicant tied both the GMAT and GPA scores of applicant 4, neither of these applicants had sufficient work and research experience to be conditionally admitted by P. None of the applicants was rejected in this round; those candidates not admitted were compiled for further analysis (all except applicant 4).

During the first round of negotiation, P and T failed to agree on the results of only two candidates (applicants 2 and 3). Applicants 8, 5, and 7 were ranked first through third respectively, while appli cants 1, 6, and 9 were ranked sixth through eighth, respectively. Because each of these candidates entered into the consensus set, no rejections were necessary. The outcome of the second round of negotiation of applicants 2 and 3 was also agreement on the rankings, with applicant 2 in the fourth position and applicant 3 in the fifth. Thus, in this simulation, the ranked list of eight applicants who initially required further analysis was passed back to O for consideration with no candidates rejected.

Because any applicants given conditional admission during the first decision round are inserted in the top of the ranked list; O received a ranked listed of all nine candidates with applicant 4 in the first spot and the others shifting down one position each. These rankings are shown in Table 6. O then begins the analysis of the applicants based on three departmental criteria. The department has five positions available each fall, with three of those positions funded. There are also some diversity criteria. First, at least 40% of the entering class must be female if possible. Second, at least 40% of the admitted applicants must possess degrees outside the MIS discipline, and third, there must be a mix of experience in the entering class. Obviously, it is possible that there are no (or few) females in the applicant pool, that all applicants have associated degrees, etc. O must balance the structure of the entering class against the ranked applicant pool received. O must also attempt to respect the ranking of candidates made by T and P, and strive to balance the admissible candidates by accepting the highest ranked candidate who satisfies particular criteria.

The second and fourth ranked applicants (8 and 7, respectively) are both female; they are chosen for admission. The final entering class consists of three females, two non-degree students, and two students with experience.

The last step that O must undertake is administration of the available financial aid. The least controversial method is to assign financial aid to those students who requested it and who are in the top three positions of applicants on a quantitative scale based on test scores. In the simulation, this aid was assigned accordingly to applicants 8, 5, and 4.

When comparing the simulation results to the admission letters from the departments, a striking similarity is noted in those who were offered positions. However, during the meeting to select applicants, the admission committee elected to admit only three applicants, all with aid. Two additional candidates were identified as alternates given the level of competition among schools for qualified applicants and assuming that not all the top candidates would accept admission. The top five candidates selected were the same as those of the simulation (applicants 2, 4, 5, 7, 8). The three selected were similar to those selected to receive aid in the simulation (applicants 4, 5, and 7). On analysis of the physical vote, it was found that personal knowledge of the applicant (which was true for all the committee-selected applicants) drove the final vote. This action showed that the reported value of knowing an applicant, which was ranked relatively low by most surveyed, carried a higher weight during actual decision-making.

This simulation shows that, overall, the system performed similarly to that of the physical departmental vote. However, while the simulation considered whether an applicant was known during its decision-making, it did not place as high a weight on that value as the <sup>b</sup>human<sup>Q</sup> system did. In this respect, it might be concluded that a system such as that designed here is more capable of objective decisionmaking while being cognizant of qualitative factors.

This successful simulation indicates that it is feasible to facilitate multiple-perspective decisionmaking with agent technology in this and likely in other situations and contexts. Additionally, such a system can systematically capture and store existing perspectives, facilitate the discovery of hidden perspectives, and emphasize incorporation of other useful but perhaps not popular perspectives in a given situation. Agent-oriented analysis using the BDI model provides a good discussion platform for individuals with different perspectives. A more detailed and formal methodology could be a future research endeavor.

## 6. Conclusion

The admissions example discussed here illustrates the application of cooperative agents within the threeperspective framework of the UST model. Because specific plans and facts for each agent are domain specific, it is clear that this cooperative multi-agent system could be applied to other decision settings where consideration of multiple perspectives is important. Furthermore, the relationships among the agents may be determined by the actual context of the problem.

The need for organizations to support multiple perspectives in decision-making has been cited frequently in the literature. Consideration of multiple perspectives helps decision makers by representing information in a variety of ways. Viewing information from perspectives other than the one that is natural for that decision maker helps determine the most appropriate decision within the context of the problem.

Based on the writings of Singer [37] and Churchman [6], Mitroff and Linstone [32] developed the concept of Unbounded Systems Thinking (UST). In this model, technical (scientific), organizational (societal), and personal perspectives are employed. Each of these perspectives has underlying assumptions, values, and insights to a given problem situation. It is argued that no problem can be adequately analyzed without consideration of all three perspectives. While the theory and concept of applying multiple perspectives are well documented, the actual implementation is much more difficult.

This paper proposes the application of agentoriented modeling and cooperative multi-agent technology to facilitate the implementation of multiple perspective systems. Specifically, the UST model is extended by use of the Belief–Desire–Intention (BDI) model of agents. Agents can be created to represent each stakeholder with its individual value system and set of characteristics. Agents working from the technical, organizational, or personal perspective can represent different worldviews as they interact among themselves and with human agents. Interaction among agents provides a means to expand and synthesize worldviews.

The university admission process is provided as an example of a cooperative multi-agent implementation of the UST model. In this decision context, several agents are proposed, each with its own perspective or function. In addition to the technical, organizational, and personal agents representing different perspectives, several other agents could be utilized to perform coordination, information gathering, and other interface functions.

It has always been a difficult task to encourage multiple-perspective consideration, particularly those perspectives that support a societal theme, but the need to consider the human aspects of business is widely recognized. The globalization of the economy and the disintegration of physical boundaries imply that organizations generally need to move beyond their cultural perspective and begin the process of expanding their use of alternative perspectives to facilitate decision-making. By adopting technology that can begin to promote the use of multiple perspectives, organizations can begin to migrate toward a multiple perspective orientation. The cooperation among those with different perspectives (as may be evident in a cooperative multi-agent system) is the key to creating an improved decision-making environment.
