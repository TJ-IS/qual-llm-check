# Action in context and context in action: Modelling complexity in multimedia systems development

## Abstract

Whereas multimedia systems development (MSD) is inherently complex, and increasingly so, little is known about how multimedia designers manage or cope with this complexity. In particular, little is known about complexity beyond technical and process levels, and how such complexity impacts design activity. In this paper we construct a model of MSD based on the formulation of the broader concept of context complexity. This concept, which subsumes but also transcends technical and process complexity, is used to explain design strategies and their consequences. We identify four design contexts that explain, and are also explained by, complexity. These contexts map out the ‘landscape for action’ over which design activity takes place. They shape the design environment and the responses taken to it. We use a grounded theory approach to study what designers actually do (as opposed to theorising what they should do). We argue that a better understanding of context determined and context determining strategies better informs interventions aimed at improving MSD design practice. Our main conclusion is that interventions should be directed to those situations where there is a mismatch between the level of complexity in the design context and the level of complexity in the design strategy taken in response to that context. We call these situations ‘breakdown’ contexts and examine their causes and consequences in greater detail. Our claimed contribution is to broaden the concept of design breakdowns through a specific understanding of the role and impact of (context) complexity in MSD.

## Introduction

M <sup>ultimedia</sup> <sup>is</sup> <sup>inherently</sup> <sup>complex</sup> <sup>because</sup> <sup>it</sup> <sup>seeks</sup> <sup>to</sup>emulate the human mind in knowledge organisa- emulate the human mind in knowledge organisation, it challenges the limits of existing models and metaphors (Palmquist, 1996) and it tends towards spaghetti structures (Lang, 2003). This complexity poses problems for users in visualising knowledge structures, and in locating, navigating and managing content; and for developers at the level of individual cognition, in communication processes and consensus seeking. A number of technical solutions are on offer, such as 3D hypermedia mapping techniques (Kahn and Lenk, 2001) but there is evidence that these are not widely used in practice, or are not considered useful (Lang, 2003).

The communication and management problems of multimedia development (Latchem et al., 1993; Dospisil and Polgar, 1994; Garzotto et al., 1995; Mulhauser and Effelsberg, 1996; Gallagher and Webb, 1997, 2000; Lang, 2003; Webb and Gallagher, 2006) are increasingly the problems of information systems (IS) development and software design, as systems become more media rich, more interactive and more complex (Jayaratna, 1994; Avison et al., 1998; Gasson, 1999; Fitzgerald, Russo and O’kane, 2003; Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 2004).

While most responses to increasing technical and process complexity have been to suggest new methods, or metamethods that prescribe what designers should do, we argue that it is first necessary to understand design activity at a higher level of abstraction, and to describe what designers actually do. In this we follow Van Gigch and Pipino’s (1986) model of inquiring systems (locating ourselves firmly at the ‘Science of IS’ level), its application within the field of IS in general (Hirschheim, 1985; Farhoomand, 1987; Banville and Landry, 1989; Hirschheim and Klein, 1989; Iivari, 1991; Shneiderman, 1997) and multimedia in particular (Gallagher and Webb, 1997, 2000; Lang, 2003; Webb and Gallagher, 2006). In doing so, we go beyond technical and process complexity to formulate the concept of ‘context complexity’, which subsumes but also transcends these more commonly used concepts.

Specifically, we wish to answer the following three research questions: How do multimedia designers manage (or cope with) complexity in the multimedia design context? [R1]. What impact does complexity in the design context have on design strategies, and vice versa? [R2]. How does knowing these things (answering research questions one and two) help us to make better interventions to improve multimedia design theory and practice? [R3].

We present a model of multimedia design activity based on a grounded theory study of 16 multimedia designers. The purpose of the model is to describe, not prescribe, design activity. No attempt is made to advocate a particular design approach, or method. Rather we map out patterns of design activity, an understanding of which, we argue, better informs interventions to improve design practice. Our study is located within the broader IS research context of studies into the role of context (Kautz, 2004; Gasson, 2005; Madsen et al., 2006) and the impact of communication breakdowns (Winograd and Flores, 1987; Curtis et al., 1988; Hofmann and Lehner, 2001; Benyon-Davies and Holmes, 2002; Teasley et al., 2002; Patel et al., 2006).

Our inductive model identifies four design scenarios (design contexts and associated response strategies) and suggests interventions to improve design practice. In particular, interventions are suggested in two identified ‘problem’ contexts (or scenarios), wherein the design strategy is not aligned with the prevailing design context. Suggested interventions are in the development and choice of strategies, which must be (or become) ‘fit for purpose’ to the design context and in the education and training of designers aimed at increasing awareness of context determined and context determining actions. The importance of path dependency, flexibility and contingency in design is emphasised.

The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section we briefly review the literature on the role of context in design. Then we set out the research method used and follow that with a discussion of our main findings. We construct a model of observed design activity, identifying four distinct design scenarios. These scenarios map out the ‘landscape of action’ within and across which designers design. We explain further two particular design scenarios that were identified as problematic. These are when the strategy taken is inappropriate or incommensurate with the level of complexity found in the design context. The implications of our research for design theory and practice are then discussed.

## The role of context in design

The role of context in design is addressed in several streams in the IS literature. For example, in the notion of adaptive design (Blum, 1996), a feature of which is a desire to maintain flexibility in response to the prevailing environment by means of morphology (Lawrence, 1981), contingency (Flynn, 1992), cognitive fit (Vessey and Glass, 1994), design rationales (Carroll et al., 1994), Agile Software Development (Aoyana, 1998) or configurable processes (Cameron, 2002). Communication breakdowns in large software design processes were studied by Curtis et al. (1988). Gasson (2005) has studied context as part of the sense-making process, while Kautz (2004) has studied the impact of context on multimedia methods in particular and Madsen et al. (2006) has studied the impact of context on IS development methods in general.

Our interest was the relationship between context and complexity. Specifically, we were interested in what happens when the complexity of the design context is not matched by an appropriate level (of complexity) in the strategies taken to manage or cope with that context. In our model we postulate two such ‘breakdown’ contexts. We therefore draw on theories of communication breakdowns to contextualise our study, and briefly present these here to better ground our results in the relevant literature. It should be noted however, that – in keeping with the grounded theory method we used – no attempt was made to formulate theories or extensively cover the literature a priori.

In their seminal work on communication breakdowns, Winograd and Flores (1987) rejected Newell and Simon’s (1972) rational decision-making model (except in narrow domains with well-defined problem spaces) arguing that it failed to take sufficient account of context (even the concept of bounded rationality is deficient in this respect). Actual decision making is frequently irrational. One cannot avoid actions, one is thrown into them and even doing nothing has consequences. The crucial part of problem solving lies in the formulation of the problem and

‘much of what is called problem solving does not deal with situations of irresolution but takes place within a normal state of resolution y resolution concerns the exploitation of the situation, not the application of habitual means’ (Winograd and Flores, 1987: 150)

Design is not the result of individual cognitive operations in an objective world but participation in mutual behaviour in which language is essential. Here language is much more than description, it is a social act in which commitments are sought and given and is therefore essential to human discourse (1987: 176). Language and thought are built on social interaction and knowledge is socially constructed (1987: 78);

‘every manager (designer) is primarily concerned with generating and maintaining a network of conversations for action, conversations in which requests and commitments lead to the successful completion of work (Winograd and Flores, 1987: 144)

However breakdowns in communication are inevitable, designers need to understand breakdowns, try to anticipate them and benefit from them in the form of self-reflection. Breakdowns – along with language (shared commitments) pre-understanding, knowledge, experience and interpretation – shape the design context

‘The analysis of a human context of activity can begin with an analysis of the domains of breakdowns, and that in turn can be used to generate the objects, properties and actions that make up the domain’ (Winograd and Flores, 1987: 17)

A number of themes emerge from an analysis of this, and cognate, literature. These are – the limitations of traditional problem-solving theory, a move from prescription to description, an increased emphasis on empirical studies, a shift from product to process focus, emphasis on situated or socially mediated design, the concept of design breakdowns, the importance of context and responsiveness to it. None of this is unique to software design. On the contrary, a review of the general literature on design quickly confirms that such issues have much wider resonance. Indeed that their identification in software design is merely an instantiation of a general class of design problem. For example, the paradigm models put forward by Blum (1996) and Winograd and Flores (1987) are based on the philosophy of Martin Heiddeger that underpins Schon’s (1983) seminal observations of design practice. Meanwhile, the view of software design as a trial and error process (found, e.g., in Hartson, 2003) can be identified in the general design studies of Rowe (1987) and Runco (1994).

Whereas the processural nature of software design has long been recognised (e.g., Budgen, 1994) it has been traditionally described as a series of steps such as requirements, build, test, implement (see, e.g., Sommerville, 1992). However, evidence of the activity of design (including in this study) suggests that design is not linear but progressive and rather a pattern of responses to prevailing conditions. It is purposeful and non-purposeful, characteristised by flexibility, expediency and opportunistic behaviour. Strauss and Corbin (1990: 152) refer to such an activity as ‘non-progressive movement’ where there are ‘purposeful altercations or changes in action/interaction in responses to conditions’ but that movement ‘does not necessarily occur in stages or phases’. Therefore, the idea of design as contingent on the prevailing design context fits also with Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) concept of process, or change.

In the next section, we shall describe our implementation of Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) grounded theory method. Then the results, and our model, will be presented. This is followed by a discussion of its significance, again informed by relevant literature.

## Data collection and analysis

Our purpose was to understand and describe what designers do when they design, based – as far as possible –on the experiences and narratives of designers themselves. We sought to avoid explicit theories and concepts formulated a priori; therefore, we chose the grounded theory research method (Glaser and Strauss, 1967). The version of grounded theory chosen was that of Strauss and Corbin (1990). Strauss and Corbin’s version of grounded theory has, itself, been criticised for being too prescriptive, in a debate that has taken place largely outside the field of IS (but see, Hughes and Howcroft (2000) and the exchange between Urquhart (2001, 2002) and Bryant (2002), for a continuation of the debate inside the IS field). However, the Strauss and Corbin (1990) version of grounded theory has the advantage of being relatively easy to follow. The coding process is rigorous (Baskerville and Pries-Heje, 2004) and more transparent than the original (Glaser and Strauss, 1967) method, since their objective was to ‘spell out the procedures and techniques in greatest detail’ and ‘in a step by step fashion’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990: 8).

Our sample consisted of multimedia companies located in the greater Belfast Metropolitan area, Northern Ireland. The companies selected were small and generally young, reflecting the nature, size and location of the industry sector. The sample included a mixture of new and old media enterprises, public and private sector organisations, production and service firms. All interviews were conducted over a 6-month period, on site, excluding some follow up telephone interviews. In total, 16 companies were visited (no two interviews were conducted in same company).

Interviewees were selected on the basis of purposive/ judgement sampling and snowballing sampling (Babbie, 1995). So, an initial list of interviews was generated via local knowledge and contacts and then enlarged based on personal recommendations. The purposive/judgement sampling method and the snowballing method necessarily involve some degree of theoretical sensitivity and sampling (wherein the next interview is chosen to obtain data for comparison with that already collected) and are fully consistent with the grounded theory approach (Strauss and Corbin, 1990). In total, 16 interview transcripts were analysed.

Five interviewees were Computer Science graduates, five Graphic Design graduates, two held a primary degree in Visual Communication, one had studied Computing and Design, two had Postgraduate Masters in Computer Science, and one had a Higher National Diploma in Computing. Experience ranged from 1 to 24 years. Those with the least experience overall tend to be those with experience only in the multimedia field. Although most interviews fell readily enough into one or other of the two major design communities involved in MSD – software engineering and graphic design – it was not our purpose to examine differences of approach between these groupings. This has been done elsewhere (e.g., by Gallagher and Webb, 2000; Lang, 2003; Webb and Gallagher, 2006) and is highlighted as an area of future research (in this paper). However, when identification of a source as either a software engineer or a graphic designer is important to a general point being made about design strategies, this is done (e.g., in all the interview extracts used in this paper).

The interviews were semi-structured, and an interview format consisting of a series of open-ended questions was used (see Annex A for the list of questions). The advantages of this approach – in particular, the greater clarity and control, which it offered – were deemed to outweigh the disadvantages (the danger that it may lead respondents towards answers that they feel is ‘correct’). The approach also permitted a copy of the interview schedule to be sent to interviewees prior to the interview. A number of benefits accrued from this. It permitted the interviewees to consider in advance how they may respond and thereby reduced any anxiety experienced by respondents. It also facilitated short, or shorter, interviews. These benefits were observed during the interviews. Many respondents had prepared written notes in advance of the interview with the result that their responses were much more free-flowing. Also the willingness of the majority of interviewees to allow the interview to be recorded was, at least in part, attributable to the fact that they had had the interview schedule beforehand.

Two further benefits of semi-structured interviews became clear during the data analysis stage. Firstly, the format allowed triangulation of the data requested through multiple questions on the same, or broadly similar, topic. For example, designers were asked ‘to define design’ and ‘to describe what you do when you design’. Secondly, semistructured interviews were much easier and quicker to code than unstructured interviews as the coder does not have to search through the entire transcript when looking for themes and concepts (because relevant (sought) data is more readily identified where the transcript is already segemented). This was particularly useful when multiple readings of a transcript were required.

Each interview lasted between 60 and 120 min. During the interview, interviewees were encouraged to be as expansive as possible but to base their responses to specific questions on one or two recent projects. This helped ‘anchor’ the discussion on the participant’s recent experience and therefore helped improve the clarity and relevance of the response. Although formal visualisation techniques were not used, an A4 pad was made available and a number of interviewees drew diagrams or scribbled notes to clarify particular points.

After the interview, the transcript was sent to the participant to allow him or her to review the material for accuracy. This is recommended practice in such approaches (Walsham, 1995). Clear instructions were given not to arbitrarily amend content but to raise any concerns with the researcher. Minor amendments, including annotations relating to style and emphasis, were permitted. In the event, only two interviews were annotated in this manner, and these involved minor changes.

Walsham (1995: 78) advocates making interviews the ‘primary data source’ since they provide the researcher with the ‘best access to the interpretations that interviewees have regarding the actions and events which have or are taking place’. In this research, triangulation of the data was achieving by requesting company documentation. Also, during the interviews, the researcher was frequently shown designs, sketches and storyboards. Where appropriate, such information was included with the transcript, either as annotations on the main text or as supplementary material.

Each transcript was read and re-read and concepts (words, phrases or paragraphs) that seemed important (i.e., descriptive of the phenomenon, relevant to the purpose of the study) were extracted. An entry was created in a database by cutting and pasting the original text or by keying a summary of what was said. Each entry was given a label or name, its exact location in the transcript was identified and three keywords recorded. In addition, a memo was written further describing the concept (what Strauss and Corbin call an ‘operational memo’), or developing it analytically and conceptually (a ‘theoretical memo’) and this was referenced to the concept (database) record. Thus, while the concept records themselves were descriptive, each fostered analytical memos within which the original concept was further developed. Moreover, the three keywords per concept provided a quick and easy way to compare concepts and an initial lead to categorisation. In total, 160 concepts were coded.

While this number of concepts is low relative to the number and length of the interviews transcripts, this is explained by the process of data analysis undertaken. This process was not purely, or even mostly, inductive. Rather it was quasi-inductive as concepts identified from earlier transcripts were sought in the remaining transcripts. As the analysis proceeded, new concepts were still being generated but at a much reduced rate. This process is consistent with the grounded theory technique of theoretical sampling which is ‘sampling on the basis of proven theoretical relevance’ where theoretical relevance indicates that certain concepts are deemed significant because they are repeatedly present or noticeably absent when comparing incident after incident (Strauss and Corbin, 1990: 176). As a general rule of thumb, where a concept was simply repeated (within the same source or elsewhere) and did not add anything new to what was already coded, it was ignored.

When a transcript had been coded for concepts, these were organised into categories. Categories are higher level and more abstract entities that subsume two or more concepts. This was rarely successful at the individual transcript level but became more significant at higher levels of granularity. Categories were further developed in terms of their properties (and the values of the dimensions of each property). This was an important step as ‘properties and dimensions form the basis for making relationships between categories’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990: 70). In practice, this step was easier (and quicker) than first anticipated as the major categories expanded to subsume weaker categories as sub-categories or properties. This proved effective in reducing the total number of categories to be considered, albeit simultaneously adding to the complexity of each remaining category, and to the relationships between categories. Finally, 27 categories were developed.

Relating categories by means of their properties was an inductive–deductive process. A relationship was proposed on the basis of some data. Further evidence of this relationship was sought elsewhere in the data. In particular negative or invalidating data was looked for. If the relationship was corroborated (or at least not refuted), it became a propositional rule (to be further tested elsewhere). Otherwise the relationship was revised, thus adding richness and variation to the analysis, or rejected outright.

The development of the ‘core’ category of context complexity illustrates this process. Early in the analysis it became clear that, in many situations, the interaction of multiple conditions created a complex design environment, characterised by uncertainty and volatility. However, later analysis of other interview transcripts showed that this was not always the case, that other design scenarios existed that were non-complex, or at least less complex, due to the interaction of a different set of dimensional values attached to the same properties of the category. This context was found in engineering or ‘back end’ design projects and provided a significant and timely qualification to the emerging general category.

Strauss and Corbin recommend the development of a ‘core category’ (1990: 119) in order to synthesise and integrate the analysis. This core category must be broad enough to relate as subsidiary categories, all other categories – ‘the core category must be the sun, standing in orderly systematic relationships to its planets’ (1990: 124). In fact, in this study, we identified two core categories, even though ‘to fully develop two core categories, then to integrate the two, and to write about them with precision and clarity is difficult’ (1990: 121). Our task was made easier because the two core categories we selected – context complexity and action/interaction strategy – were closely coupled in our analysis, and the object of further study was the relationship between the categories rather than the categories themselves. This is illustrated by identifying some of the properties and their dimensional values as in Table 1.

The relationship between context complexity and action/ interaction strategies was used to develop a model with which to understand how designers design. The development of this model is outlined in the next sections. Firstly, the core categories (of context complexity and action/ interaction strategies) are discussed.

## Context complexity

Many of the conditions that shape context complexity emanate from the nature of the design artefact itself but many do not. The role and impact of the designer’s education or professional experience in the design process, for example, is not primarily determined by the nature of the design artefact but by multiple influences operating at the individual and organisational levels. As expected we found that experienced designers were better than inexperienced designers in managing complex design projects. But we also observed some significant variations in performance levels related to the same (or similar) design task, which did not appear to be attributable to differences in qualifications, training or experience.

Thus, our concept of context complexity is much broader than Brook’s (1987, 1995) notion of software complexity. Whereas, Brooks does address some contextual factors – most notably he later cites Herzberg’s work on motivation (1995: 210) – these are not the focus of his study. Yet even the broader concept of context complexity does not alone explain design activity. Rather we found, consistent with traditional problem-solving theory (Newell and Simon, 1972), that complexity in design is best understood by reference to the phenomenon and the response to the phenomenon. Therefore, we coupled context complexity with the strategies framed and implemented in response to context complexity, but which also influence it.

Client requirements are often ill-defined and subject to frequent change, designers bring to the design a range of personal and professional motivations and prejudices, communication between designers and clients and between designers and designers can be problematic. This creates a design context that is uncertain, volatile and complex. ‘Everything is constantly in flux’ and ‘you don’t really know where you are’. It is difficult to scope a design; there is little agreement as to when a design begins and when it ends. ‘You are always designing because everything is always changing’. The outcome of the design process is controversial and evaluations of design remain highly subjective. Design is ‘an ongoing process’ and tends to be quite ‘woolly’. Technical complexity is increasing due, in part, to the increased functionality (richness and interaction) at the interface.

‘I think that it is not so much that the interface and the functionality can’t be separated, it’s more a case of the interface becoming much more richer and there ends up being more interaction and complexity in the interface. With conventional software, we don’t design a new keyboard every time we design a new system’ [graphic designer]

However, a significant exception to this design context was observed. There are occasions when the design context is well defined, stable and the technology is better understood. User requirements are fixed, or at least fixed earlier, changes are less frequent, and communication is better. This context is most reflective of engineering type approaches to software design, for example, ‘back end’ server design. This ‘contra’ context is accounted for by a different set of values attached to the (same) properties pertaining to the (identified) causal conditions. This variation of contexts (and design approaches) suggested that complexity was a key attribute in determining contexts, strategies and outcomes.

We distinguish between low context complexity and high context complexity as described using a range of conditions (and the differing value of properties attached to the same conditions). These critical differences can also be attributed to, and reconciled with, the level of structure in the problem task. That is, where there is a high level of structure in the problem task, the design context tends to be less complex (as with engineering or ‘back end’ design). Where there is a low level of structure in the design task, the design context tends to be more complex (as in ‘front end’ design). This is possible to acknowledge without accepting the supremacy of the rational decision-making model (Newell and Simon, 1972) in explaining design activity. On the contrary, as we shall argue in our account of strategies taken in response to complexity in the design context, greater insights into how designers design may be gleaned from those situations where the rational decision-making clearly model breaks down.

## Action/interaction strategies

Strategies were classified as either action strategies or interaction strategies. Action strategies are manifested as a series of action processes, such as sketching, drawing, diagramming and flowcharting. Interaction strategies are defined as ‘people doing things together or with respect to one another in regards to a phenomenon and the action, talk and thought processes that accompany the doing of those things’ (Strauss and Corbin, 1990: 158). Interaction strategies in the context of multimedia design include communication, negotiation, compromise and collaboration. Also noted were sequences of actions, of interactions, and of actions and interactions and the impact of context in determining which type of strategy was employed and when.

Prototyping is a good example of this process. Based on an initial explanation of user requirements [interaction], the designer will build a prototype [action]. This prototype will be shown to the users who will comment on its features [interaction] and the designer will return to modify the prototype [action] in line with these comments. This sequence of action and interaction continues until the prototype is acceptable to the client. The process is further illustrated in the following protocol

‘What we will try to do is develop a proposal, which we will have discussed with the client – we try to nail down as much as possible. From there we try to move towards a more formal, tighter functional specification. The coding team will then go on to code based on that functional specification. If the customer won’t sign the functional specification, then we will not code it’ [software engineer]

Here discussions with the client [interaction] lead to the development of a proposal [action] that is discussed with the client – ‘we try to nail down as much as possible’ – [interaction]. As a result of this, a functional specification is written [action] which is discussed with the client [interaction]. If the client agrees with this specification by signing it off [action] then coding begins [action].

Of course, action strategies are not only (or even normally) individual strategies. They frequently involve others. Sketching, drawing, diagramming, flowcharting and coding may also be a group activity. In a design team, prototyping is rarely done alone but involves frequent interactions with others, designers as well as clients. Therefore, design strategies are better viewed on a continuum of interaction with action at one end of the continuum, and interaction at the other. In practice, all design will involve some interaction but the level of interaction will differ according to design context, and will change over time. So, despite weaknesses in Strauss and Corbin’s definition of action and interaction (1990: 169), we used them, not as two separate and discrete entities, but as polarities on a spectrum of interaction along which more subtle and changing combinations of each are acknowledged and valued.

Because action/interaction take place in sequence, it is possible to observe them over time and to account for process or change. Thus, we can comment not only on current action/interaction but on how previous action/ interaction influenced this and how current action/ interaction may influence action/interaction in the future. The action/interaction strategy re-use encapsulates this process. Faced with a design problem, the designer may decide that this problem is sufficiently similar to a previous problem that existing expertise, experience, methods, tools or techniques can be applied to its resolution. Similarly, after the completion of the design task (successfully or otherwise), the expertise, experience, methods, tools or techniques developed may be brought to bear on a future design problem. This is not to argue that all design projects are the same but to point out that re-use is an important design strategy that links past, present and future design efforts. Certainly what works is likely to be repeated.

Re-use is made of code, methods, tools, techniques and experience – and also the design itself. Code re-use may be ‘haphazard’, possible only because in a small team ‘it’s in peoples heads’ or, in one case, when as a result of a quality assurance initiative ‘a library of routines which are well and truly tested’ had been built up. Alternatively, code is re-used on an individual basis

‘I would just be trying out different bits of code based on past experience – I know that this is similar to something else so I would plug that bit in. I may have another bit of code that I’ve used elsewhere so I’ll reuse that’ [software engineer]

Knowing what and when to re-use is regarded as an important design skill, comparable to generalisation, abstraction and de-bugging. Good design can be re-used whereas bad design is ‘ad-hoc, you can’t repeat or re-use or stand over it’.

So, each design strategy consists of action and interaction. In every strategy there is some element of interaction (at minimum, self-reflection) and some element of action (e.g., talking or thinking). A strategy is therefore defined by a set of actions and interactions directed at solving the design problem. But each strategy consists of action and interaction in unequal measure. Some strategies will be primarily action-based whereas others will be primarily interaction-based. For example, the action strategies of drawing and flowcharting also serve important interaction (communication) functions. The relative importance of action and interaction in a given strategy is determined by context. In practice, a designer will use both action and interaction strategies in each design effort – and move quickly and seamlessly between them as context changes.

## A model of complexity in MSD

Strauss and Corbin (1990: 131) point out that context is an arrangement ‘of properties of the general phenomenon, ordered in various combinations, along their dimensional range to form patterns’. We identified two design contexts along a property dimension of the general category of context complexity from complex to non-complex. We then used this dimensional scale to relate the specific category of context complexity to the response to that context, instantiated as the level of interaction (or the balance of action and interaction) in design strategies.

Figure 1 identifies four design scenarios created through the juxtaposition of context complexity (level of complex ity) and action/interaction (level of interaction).

This model was developed to explain (and is also explained by) the data. However, not all scenarios are equally found in the data. Whereas three of the four scenarios were generated primarily from an analysis of the interview transcripts, one scenario (low context complexity/high interaction) is deduced primarily from the other scenarios. That is, a high context complexity/low interaction context must also permit a low context complexity/ high interaction context. There was limited support for this context in the data. Nevertheless, it is included here for the sake of completeness.

We now outline each design context but pay particular attention to those (two) design scenarios where there is a clear misalignment between the level of context complexity and the level of interaction in the (action/interaction) strategy taken in response to that context.

## The ‘Inside Out’ design context

Where the context is less complex, strategies will require less interaction (beyond any interaction with the design itself). In multimedia design such a context can be observed in traditional software engineering problems, normally associated with the ‘back end’ of the application where problems of algorithms, coding and testing are usually well defined. In ‘black box’ design, the designer seeks to specify a set of pre-conditions, a set of post-conditions and the transformations necessary to turn one into the other.

‘I have a black box model of design; if I have a requirement for a piece of design then I will put in the elements that I know will enable a designer to make the design. Then they do what they need to do to produce the design and they hand it back to me in a suitable format. That is all I need to know, and it is all that I want to know’ [software engineer]

‘If its the backend – the server constraint – I would hand you a set of preconditions and post-conditions; a set of white box tests and black box tests. That would be given to the coders and they would be told to go and code a solution in JAVA that meets this functional requirement that passes those sets of tests. I or someone else will produce that design and you or someone else will go off and deliver that design.’ [software engineer]

At the front end of the application – the user interface – this context is much less likely due to the necessary involvement of users (and therefore reliance on extensive and intensive interaction). Converting a product catalogue to a web site, where the structure and content of the printed catalogue is to be maintained is one example.

Figure 1 A model of complexity in MSD.

‘I mean a lot of designers are simply ‘‘OK lets turn the handle and we’ve got the design’’y if you have got 800 pages of material you wanted to put on the Web – that’s the spec’ [graphic designer]

Even where the context is stable (less complex) iteration and refinement still goes on

‘sometimes the server design will be fixed beforehand, sometimes the front-end design will be fixed beforehand (but) usually there will be several iterations of refinement’

We label this design context ‘Inside Out’ to reflect the systems focused (as opposed to user focused) nature of the design effort, usually – but not always – carried out by software engineers at the ‘back end’ of the design product.

## The ‘Outside In’ design context

Where the context is complex, a high degree of interaction is necessary to frame or bound the problem. This interaction is with clients and other designers and involves communication and collaboration – the primary purpose of which is to clarify ill-defined and shifting requirements and to deliver a design that is sufficiently satisfying to the client. So, ‘designers must know their user and put themselves in the users place’ and ‘design is about meeting the clients needs. It’s about coming up with ways to meet user requirements, within constraints’.

Interaction between designers is also important, for example, to define and agree tasks, negotiate roles and responsibilities or obtain resources. There is some evidence that both software engineers and graphic designers are becoming more comfortable with this level of interaction, as illustrated by the following comments

‘I have come to take on board more of their approaches and processes’ [software engineer]

‘I have noticed the software developers that I have worked with – the longer we have worked together the more changes I notice in each of us y Now we are working very well together and we are constantly exchanging information and ideas’. [graphic designer]

Interaction with the materials of design also increases with increases in complexity as the designer tries to understand, and reflects on, the design context. Design is often a long and difficult process, with no quick fix, but rather a period of ‘wrestling with the problem’ until a satisfactory solution emerges. Knowledge is often tacit and sometimes it is difficult for the designer to explain how or why a solution is reached.

‘When a designer gets to this point it is difficult to explain why he or she is at that point, but they themselves will just know. This is a problem that most students face – it takes them a while before they realise that they have actually solved the problem. After a certain period of design time a designer will know that it is right’ [software engineer].

One designer likened design to driving a car ‘once you learn you never really think how you do it’. Another used a bike-riding analogy observing that aspects of design (in this case the technique of separating out buttons on the interface) ‘might be really tedious, but if you’ve done it once you can do it again’.

We label this design context ‘Outside In’ to reflect the user focused (as opposed to systems focused) nature of the design effort, usually – but not always – carried out by graphic designers.

The previous strategies are compatible with context. That is, given a complex context we may expect to find strategies that are primarily based on interaction and where the context is less complex we may expect to find strategies that are less based on interaction. However, the juxtaposition of context and strategies throws up two other scenarios that are less expected and more difficult to explain.

## The (‘Inside Out’) and the (‘Outside In’) design contexts

Here the prevailing context is not met with an appropriate, or expected, response. Strategies that are characterised by low levels of interaction are pursued in contexts that are complex. Strategies that are characterised by high levels of interaction are pursued in contexts that are less complex. What causes these anomalies? The answer can be again found in the context.

In both scenarios, the designer is using an inappropriate strategy to deal with the design context. Either, they have failed sufficiently to understand the problem or having recognised it, are unable to implement an appropriate strategy – due to inadequate education, training and experience, or perhaps because the designer is bound by the in-house design culture, environment or methodology. The designer may be ‘locked in’ to an approach or way of thinking about the design by the design itself – through the pulls of complexity, technology or aesthetics. In this sense, multimedia can seduce the designer into ‘making arbitrarily complex things’ evident in the use of ‘non-relevant technology’ (such as flash technology or lens flare). Such ‘indulgence’ can lead to poor design – design that is ‘overly complicated’, ‘cluttered’, ‘non-intuitive’ or ‘unnatural’. This is indicative of the application of ‘Inside Out’ design when an ‘Outside In’ approach is a better fit. A systems or technology-driven approach is used when a user-driven approach is more appropriate.

Users may force the response. Many designers praised prototypes and storyboards but others used them reluctantly or avoided them altogether. In a given design effort, users’ enthusiasm or client expectation for such devices may force a level of interaction inappropriate to the level of context complexity. A high level of interaction is not always a good thing. Where it is inappropriate to the design context and peripheral or unnecessary to the design outcomes, it is wasteful of resources. Too much interaction may result in an inferior design, ‘It’s bad communication if every time you do something more and show it to the client they tell you it is not really what they had in mind’. This is an example of the inappropriate application of the ‘Outside In’ approach. The approach is user-driven and highly interactive when what is needed is an approach that is systems-driven and less interactive. Of course, this strategy may still be effective but it is certainly inefficient, and its sustainability in a commercial environment is doubtful.

Another explanation may be found in process. The design context changes – becomes more or less complex – and the response has yet to catch up. An exponential increase in context complexity may mean that existing strategies do not work, and cannot be calibrated to meet the new demands. It is conditions that account for this mismatch between context and strategy and potentially any intervening condition or combination thereof may be culpable. Such delays can be fatal. If the response to a changing context is not made in good time – even to the extent of anticipating further increases or decreases in context complexity – the outcomes may be unsuccessful. Moreover, this may impact future responses to continuing or intermittent changes in context complexity. Of course, even where the response is timely and successful, context complexity will be modified and future strategies changed accordingly.

‘Lack of time’ is a significant design constraint. The following comments are representative of the problem faced. ‘There will always be a problem with deadlines’. ‘I think that there is a problem of allocating enough time for the design process’. ‘Depending on the budget and time that whole process can be telescoped into a very short period of time’. ‘I only have a certain amount of time to convince the customer to buy this service so I need to get something that is concrete as quickly as possible’.

There is a feeling that users and managers do not appreciate how long it takes to do design. That ‘designers just sit and doodle and that they can come up with something overnight’, whereas ‘trying to work up a coherent set of designs for something like an icon family will take a significant length of time’. In addition, because design is a process of refinement and trial and error – it is often difficult to estimate the amount of time that a design may take. ‘I know that it will take a long time, but it is difficult to gauge this in terms of hours, days, or weeks’.

Communication problems arise between designers and the materials of design, between designers and users and between designers and designers. One example of this is the different interpretations of the word ‘structure’. When software engineers talk about the structure of the system they are invariably referring to data structures and algorithms, the ‘back end’ of the functionality. In contrast, when graphic designers talk about structure they are usually referring to the content, the information structure or the structure of the interface (the ‘front end’). One designer noted ‘the trouble with multimedia is that we don’t have the language and we are crashing along at a fast pace with little in the way of common language – we need this’. Here the potential for the misappropriation and misapplication of ‘Inside Out’ and ‘Outside In’ design approaches is increased because of the presence of communications problems.

Any of these conditions or a combination thereof can lead to a mismatch between design context and design strategy. We denote the general class of such mismatches in our model by parenthesising the (otherwise legitimate but now inappropriately applied) strategy. Hence we have (‘Inside Out’) and (‘Outside In’) design contexts. However, as we shall point out in the next section, such contexts do not necessarily result a negative outcome.

## Implications for theory and practice

Our model identifies two design scenarios wherein the design strategy does not fit the design context. In each, the level of interaction in the design strategy is incommensurate (too high or too low) to the level of complexity in the design context. Interventions intended to improve the quality of the response should then be directed at increasing or decreasing the level of interaction as appropriate. While increasing context complexity is hardly recommended, a number of steps can be taken to reduce it, at least over the long term. For example, less complex tasks may be undertaken, as with the engineering type ‘back end’ tasks identified in the study. With such tasks greater structure and transparency may be brought to their solution and the demands on the designer are simplified, or at least better understood. However, the option of reducing context complexity by avoiding complex, highly interactive tasks is not one that is often open to designers operating in the frequently volatile and complex arena of multimedia development.

The desired outcomes of interventions are clear. Where the level of interaction in design strategies is too high or too low then this is to be reduced or increased to a level that is commensurate with design context. The desired direction of the interventions is indicated by the arrows in Figure 2. These are achieved primarily through the selection and implementation of suitable strategies. Where the previous strategy has been insufficiently interactive in a more complex environment, a more interactive strategy is tried (intervention [1]). Where the strategy has been previously too interactive in a less complex context, a strategy that relies less on interaction is employed (intervention [2]).

This has implications for designers and for method developers. Designers need to be aware of the fit between context and strategy, to be flexible in their approach to problem solving and to be ready to calibrate their approaches according to the changing nature of the design context. This requires education and training in design practice beyond that tied to specific methodologies, and the use of a range of methodologies, or none, as determined by the prevailing context. Methods developers need to develop methods that permit such flexibility, and tools and techniques that support it.

One explanation of design breakdowns lies in an inflexibility or inability to adapt to changing circumstances. The model of design presented in this paper is consistent with this analysis, predicated as it is on the importance of design context and responses to that context. However, two important and inter-related questions remain to be addressed: (a) what is the nature of the breakdowns reported? and (b) how well does the model explain such breakdowns?

Firstly, the description of design breakdowns given applies to both interactions with others and to interaction with the materials of design. Although the analysis of breakdowns presented has emphasised breakdowns in communication (with other designers and/or with users), breakdowns between context and strategy may equally occur with the materials of design, analogous to Schon’s (1983) concept of ‘reflection in action’. Here the designer interacts with the design materials – the notations, prototypes and other artefacts identified at the action level. As a result of this interaction, both the design and the designer’s perception of the design change. Schon refers to this dialectic relationship as ‘reflection on action’ but the idea of changing self as a result of interaction with work can be traced back directly to the writings of Hegel and Marx. The causes of such self-reflection were not specifically studied in this research but other researchers (e.g., Winograd and Flores, 1987) have suggested that it occurs when there is a breakdown of some sort in the normal activity of design.

Secondly, the type of design breakdown has been, at least implicitly, negative. Whether a breakdown in communication with others or a breakdown with the design itself the imperative has been to ‘repair’, ‘recover’ or ‘get back’ to the situation before the breakdown, which is considered the normal state. Yet it is clear from the literature that design breakdowns can also be positive. Schon and Bennet (1996: 173) observe that breakdowns occasion reflection in action which is ‘thinking about what she is doing while doing it, in such a way as to influence further doing’ or reflection on action which is ‘pausing to think back over what she has done in a project, exploring the understanding that she has brought to the handling of the task’.

Winograd and Flores (1987) draw on the philosophy of Martin Heidegger (as does Schon) and apply his concepts of ‘being-in-the-world’, ‘throwness’, ‘present-at-hand’ and ‘readiness-at-hand’ to the design of computing systems. Breakdowns are fundamental in shaping the design context and outcomes.

‘Breakdowns serve an extremely important cognitive function, revealing to us the nature of our practices and equipment, making them ‘‘present-to-hand’’ to us, perhaps for the first time. In this sense they function in a positive rather than a negative way’ (1986:78)

‘A breakdown is not a negative situation to be avoided, but a situation of non obviousness, in which recognition that something is missing leads to unconcealing (generating through declarations) some aspect of the network of tools that we are engaged in using. A breakdown reveals the nexus of relations necessary for us to accomplish our task. This creates a clear objective for design – to anticipate the forms of breakdown and provide a space of possibilities for action when they occur.’ (1986:165)

Thus, the breakdowns identified in this paper may be positive as well as negative and apply to interaction with the materials of design as well as to interaction with others.

The second question – how well does the model explain such breakdowns? – may be addressed through further reference to the Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) paradigm model. Our model identifies four design scenarios or contexts, two of which are labelled ‘breakdown’ contexts. For each of these contexts, it is possible to use the paradigm model to investigate the causes, conditions and consequences of such breakdowns. Furthermore, Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) conditional matrix (de facto, an extension of the coding process used to develop the paradigm model) may be used to extend this analysis to multiple levels that impact on the breakdown including personal/individual, sub-organisational/group, organisational, supra-organisational levels. A conditional path can be traced for specific events or incidents, linking causal conditions, other attendant conditions, and consequences. Such extensions were not included in the analyses reported in this paper but we expect them to be fruitful avenues of further research.

The outcomes of our reported research are similar in many ways to the conclusions reached by Schon (1983) and Winograd and Flores (1987). A rational decision-making model of design has been rejected, the role of context in determining action is emphasised, action/interaction is at the centre of the design process (for Winograd and Flores these are ‘speech acts’) and design is explained – at least in part – by the concept of ‘breakdowns’. One point of departure is the different emphasis on the context–breakdown relationship. For Winograd and Flores (and to a lesser extent Schon), breakdowns causes context. In the model developed in this paper, context causes breakdowns. In practice, both are interdependent and the difference is one of emphasis. We give emphasis to the role of context complexity in creating design breakdowns, but we report little support for positive outcomes from breakdown contexts.

In our research, the objective has been on developing, from first order concepts, a holistic picture of software design (within which breakdowns are one, albeit important, feature) rather than on developing existing theories. The actual employment of pseudo- or quasi-inductive methods as reported, and the subsequent ‘grounding’ of our outcomes in the existing literature, although legitimate and necessary, should not obscure the inductive imperative of the grounded theory research method, the advantages of this an approach and the opportunities it affords for further research in this field.

## Suggestions for further research

Other researchers are invited to examine software design using the same, or a similar, method. We suggest three areas wherein such effort may be rewarded. Firstly, the application of specific qualitative analysis techniques to measure the validity of reported outcomes. The use of a technique such as multidimensional scaling (Butterfield, 1998) could address a major shortcoming of our research – the absence of any validation of final outcomes by practitioners themselves. This would also open up the data for further interpretation by more accurately accounting for differences between designers on certain issues. Although differences between software engineers and graphic designers in MSD have been well reported in the literature (e.g., see Gallagher and Webb, 2000; Lang, 2003; Webb and Gallagher, 2006), there has been no specific focus on their differing approaches to complexity in the design context. In our research, we focused on context complexity in MSD but not specifically on commonalities and differences between the two major design players.

Secondly, the testing and development of the core relationships between context, complexity and breakdowns. In our research, we suggest that these relationships are critical in shaping the process and outcomes of design. But what are the implications of our understanding of these relationships for designers, method builders and managers? We have alluded to some of the implications in this paper – for example, that all must become more flexible – but have not dealt with the necessary changes for any of these parties in any detail. With a better understanding of the commonalities and differences in software engineering and graphic design approaches to context complexity (as in our recommendation above), we could use this knowledge to assist in the evaluation, categorisation and selection of methods used to manage or cope with context complexity (following, e.g., any of the approaches put forward by Jayaratna, 1994; Ter Hofstede and Verhoef, 1997; Yoon et al., 2001; Lycett et al., 2003; Fitzgerald et al., 2003).

Thirdly, we suggest moving from concerns over internal validation to issues of external validation, or generalisability. Although notoriously problematical in qualitative research (see Lee and Baskerville, 2003, for a critique), it would be interesting to compare and contrast the findings of this research with research into design in other fields. Are multimedia designers better at coping with context complexity than other designers in other fields, for example, in the general field of software design, or in cognate design disciplines such as architecture or engineering? We suggest that MSD is particularly complex and increasingly so, largely because of the pace of technological development and the immaturity of the emerging design discipline. But is MSD so different from other design fields, contexts and scenarios? What can we learn from elsewhere? What can they learn from us? Answers to these questions are surely worth pursuing.

## Conclusions

This paper used the grounded theory method to develop a model of multimedia systems development. This model proposed that complexity arises from a number of contextual factors, which in turn determine the chosen design strategies [R1]. A particular design strategy consists of actions by individual designers and interactions between designers at group/organisational level [R1]. It was theorised that the appropriate level of action/ interaction in a design strategy is commensurate with the level of complexity in the prevailing design context [R2]. Two design scenarios were identified as problematic. In both, the level of interaction in the design strategy was incommensurate with the level of complexity in the design context. Either there was too much interaction in a design context of low complexity or there is too little interaction in a design context of high complexity. These scenarios were referred to as the breakdown contexts [R2].

We claim a contribution to the broader understanding of design breakdowns. We examined context complexity rather than technical or process complexity and proposed a causal relationship between design context, complexity and breakdowns. Our concept of context complexity is a construct generated from the data (on what designers do when they design) but is also informed by theories of rational decision making and problem solving, problem framing, adaptive design, contingency fit and situated action. Thus, we reject any notion of a dichotomy of design approaches, instead acknowledging that in any given design strategy there will be multiple influences at work, at multiple levels in the design environment, and that these will change over time. This means that there is no one best strategy to cover all – or even most – situations, rather design strategies must reflect and respond to the prevailing design context [R1]. The implications of this for the development and use of design methods were considered and areas for future research identified.

Our main conclusions suggest that interventions aimed at improving design practice should be focused on better explaining how and why mismatches between design context and design strategy occur [R3]. This would require basic, descriptive but explanatory research into a much broader range of factors than it was possible to include in this study, for example, the attitudes and motivations of individual designers, the value of specific co-ordination and collaboration activities, and the evaluation of MSD methods already designed (at least ostensibly) to address these issues.
