# Knowledge Transfers in Alliances: Exploring the Facilitating Role of Information Technology

T. Ravichandran,<sup>a</sup> Simona Ileana Giura<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Strategic alliances have become popular organizational forms in the last two decades. Prior research suggests alliances promote knowledge sharing between the partners. In this research, using patent data, we study the impact of information technology (IT) intensity on knowledge flows in alliances. Research has shown that prealliance knowledge stocks of the partners facilitate knowledge flows to the focal firm. We find that knowledge flows to the focal firm are enhanced when the focal firm and its partner have high IT intensity. We find support for our theorizing that IT is a necessary complementary resource that facilitates knowledge flows among partners in alliances.

Keywords: knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows • alliances • learning • IT investments • IT value • patents • innovation

## Introduction

Alliances are a learning mechanism comprising voluntary arrangements between firms pertaining to exchanging, sharing, or codevelopment of products, technologies, or services (Gulati 1998). Alliances allow firms to capture market opportunities in a timely manner by being able to tap into complementary knowledge and resources that a partner might possess (Teece 1986, Hagedoorn 1993, Anand and Khanna 2000, Dyer et al. 2001) that otherwise would not be available to them. By forming an alliance, the partners are able to learn from their differences and from approaching issues in new ways that are less likely in either of the partner firms. Consistent with the literature that alliances are a learning mechanism, we can observe that some of the most innovative companies have been forming an increasing number of alliances. For example, Apple has formed 240 alliances since its first alliance in 1987, and IBM has formed 1,508 alliances since its first alliance in 1984.<sup>1</sup>

However, despite increased interest in learning, there is limited research that has systematically examined how knowledge transfers between partners in alliances and the conditions that enable or inhibit such knowledge flows. Although recent studies have attempted to measure knowledge flows using cross-citations of patents by alliance partners, this research stream has just begun addressing questions pertaining to factors that influence knowledge flows. Issues such as the characteristics of the knowledge (Szulanski 1996, Simonin 1999, Chen 2004), the interactive dynamics between the partners (Easterby-

Smith et al. 2008), and alliance governance structures (Mowery et al. 1996, Oxley and Wada 2009) have received attention. But, how the resources of the partners in an alliance impact knowledge flows has received less attention. These factors are worth examining because alliances are an attractive but uncertain mechanism for transferring knowledge, with some alliances leading to more knowledge flows than others (Kumar and Nti 1998). Owing to the competitive tension that arises between the partners and because of appropriation hazards, some firms may not be willing to share their knowledge, leading to the alliance not achieving its learning scope. However, even when the firms are willing to collaborate, knowledge sharing does not happen successfully because knowledge is causally ambiguous (Lippman and Rumelt 1982), tacit (Polanyi 1967), sticky (Szulanski 2000), or the receiver does not have the absorptive capacity to identify and assimilate new knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Because there are various barriers to effective knowledge transfer, it becomes particularly important to understand the determinants of knowledge flows in alliances. In this study, we argue that although most alliances offer the potential to learn, the extent to which this potential is realized depends on firm resources, especially the knowledge stock and the information technology (IT) resources of the partners in an alliance.

It is established in the literature that IT creates value for firms (Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000, Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Mithas et al. 2012) by reducing coordination and transaction costs within and across organizational boundaries (Malone et al. 1987, Clemons et al. 1993). However, research on the impact of IT on firm innovation has been limited, with some studies finding IT investments complement research and development (R&D) investments, leading to greater innovation by mitigating the diminishing returns to R&D (Ravichandran et al. 2017), and others finding that IT plays a significant role in innovation production (Kleis et al. 2012). Although recent work has shifted from an internal focus to examining the role of IT in external partnerships, such as in supply chains (Sambamurthy et al. 2003), not much work has focused on understanding its role in facilitating value creation in strategic alliances. With the exception of a few studies (Yu and Ravichandran 2015 Chi et al. 2010, Tafti et al. 2013), research on the role on IT in interfirm collaboration still remains limited, especially with respect to innovation and knowledge flows.

In this study, we examine how the resource endowments of the partnering firms in alliances influence knowledge flows. Past research has argued that the knowledge stocks of the partnering firms are likely to influence knowledge flows to the focal firm in an alliance. In this paper, we posit that examining knowledge flows in contemporary alliances is incomplete without considering the IT intensity of the partnering firms. We reason that IT intensity can enhance a firm’s capacity to exploit knowledge resources from within and outside by providing the infrastructure for creating multiple links between alliance partners, enabling weak ties, increasing communication and social interaction, decreasing the knowledge dispersion inside an organization, and by increasing knowledge transparency. Hence, we posit that IT intensity increases the potential for knowledge sharing in an alliance and will positively moderate the effects of knowledge stocks of the partners on the knowledge flows to the focal firm.

We explore these relationships using a sample of 272 U.S. firms from various industries, involved in alliances formed between the years 1991 and 2001. By linking these concepts, we hope to add to the understanding of the drivers of knowledge sharing in an alliance. We also develop theoretical and empirical insights about why and how IT can be a complementary resource that could enhance alliance value creation. Collectively, we expect these findings to contribute to both the information systems and the strategy literatures.

## Theory and Hypotheses

Acquiring external knowledge in the market is extremely difficult owing to hold-up problems and knowledge barriers. Because of the reciprocal nature of alliance agreements, there is increased incentive alignment and reduced opportunism between the alliance partners as compared with market transactions. As a result, alliances are a better option for acquiring new knowledge that cannot be developed inside the firm. Alliances, however, are an uncertain mechanism, with some alliances leading to more knowledge flows than others and some failing to allow any transfer of knowledge. Although there has been a long debate in the literature about whether alliances promote knowledge flows, Gomes-Casseres et al. (2006) find that alliances indeed promote more knowledge flows than market transactions.

Alliance Knowledge Stocks and Knowledge Flows Although firms enter an alliance with the “intent to learn” (Hamel 1991), and most alliances offer the participating firms the potential to learn from their partners, the degree to which this potential is realized is a function of numerous factors. Below we discuss how partners’ knowledge stocks impact the potential to learn in alliances.

The ability to learn and acquire knowledge from outside sources and therefore from alliances is largely a function of prior resources, operationalized in this paper as the patent stocks of the partners, and thus their prior knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). We refer to knowledge flows as the process through which one firm is affected by the knowledge of another (Argote and Ingram 2000), and we capture these flows as the difference in patent cross-citations to the partner, after the alliance as compared with before the alliance.

Having exposure to external knowledge does not automatically lead to knowledge inflows. To be able to transfer knowledge in an alliance, firms must be able to identify the needed knowledge from their partner (Grant 1996), and existing knowledge is the starting point for such learning (Nelson and Winter 1982, Dierickx and Cool 1989). Not only have firms with knowledge stocks developed a set of skills necessary to create new knowledge; they rely on their extant knowledge as a reference point to recognize relevant outside knowledge that is valuable and useful.

Absorptive capacity is critical in a firm’s ability to appropriate resources from its partner in an alliance (Mowery et al. 1996). A firm that has a greater stock of knowledge can have higher absorptive capacity (Dushnitsky and Lenox 2005) if they know what they know and tap into this knowledge when needed (Penner-Hahn and Shaver 2005). Naturally, these firms are better positioned to identify and use knowledge from their partners and are more cognizant of important incoming knowledge. Moreover, existing knowledge broadens the scope for complementarities to exploit (Cassiman and Veugelers 2002). Because knowledge tends to be complex, and overlaps across multiple domains, a higher stock of knowledge gives more opportunities to the firm for knowledge inflows: firms have been found to learn from their partners in alliances in a spontaneous, “whatever it takes” fashion (Oxley and Wada 2009).

Collaborations with firms that have high knowledge resources present the most feasible opportunities to learn for the focal firms because these have the most skilled innovators, offer the highest knowledge exposure, and offer a higher likelihood of finding complementary knowledge. High knowledge exposure can increase the absorptive capacity of the focal firm by broadening and enhancing the understanding of various technological and market developments. Participating in alliances with partners that have high stocks of knowledge “expands the horizons of the firm’s personnel and increases the awareness of additional projects that might be undertaken” (Powell et al. 1996, p. 120). As a result, the more knowledge the partner has, the greater the potential for the focal firm to learn through the alliance.

Thus, alliance knowledge stock, which is the cumulative knowledge stocks of the partners in an alliance, will have a positive effect on the knowledge flows in an alliance. However, a firm may not realize the full potential to learn in an alliance because of various barriers. Knowledge attributes influence whether the transfer in alliances will be successful. Explicit knowledge is easier to transfer (Chen 2004) whereas causally ambiguous (Simonin 1999), and sticky knowledge (Szulanski 1996) is more difficult to transfer. Knowledge flows are also impacted by the interactive dynamics between the partners (Lyles and Salk 1996, Easterby-Smith et al. 2008), trust (Chen 2004), commitment to the partner (Muthusamy and White 2005), social ties (Bell and Zaheer 2007), alliance structure (Mowery et al. 1996, Chen 2004), and mechanisms such as the provision of training, technology, and managerial assistance (Lyles and Salk 1996). Moreover, receptivity of the focal firm, which has been argued to be partner specific (Larsson et al. 1998), inhibits learning. In part, this could be because of the degree of knowledge transparency in the partner, which in turn can be driven by the level of knowledge codification by and the communication capacity of the partner (Larsson et. al.1998).

Thus, the full potential of knowledge transfer in alliances can be realized only if appropriate facilitating mechanisms are put in place. In the next section, we explore how IT can enhance this potential by reducing some of the barriers identified above.

Moderating Effects of Information Technology Knowledge transfer inside and across organizations has changed over time, especially because of IT.

Hence, examining knowledge flows to a firm in an alliance is incomplete without taking into consideration the IT intensity of the focal firm and its partner. IT has been argued to increase a firm’s innovation through effective management of knowledge (Ravichandran et al. 2017), the innovation production process (Kleis et al. 2012) and through external coordination (Thomke 2006). In this research, we argue that IT is a complementary resource, which facilitates knowledge transfers between alliance partners. We theorize that the IT intensity of the firm and its partner in an alliance will positively moderate the effects of the alliance knowledge stock on the knowledge flows to a firm.

Extant literature suggests that alliance knowledge stocks will have a positive influence on the knowledge the firm receives (Ireland et al. 2002). At higher levels of IT intensity of a firm and its partner in an alliance, this positive impact of alliance knowledge stocks on knowledge flows is even stronger, for three reasons. First, the IT intensity of a firm can increase its absorptive capacity by providing ways to easily access and integrate prior knowledge and deploy the knowledge as and where needed. Second, the IT intensity of the partner enhances the knowledge transparency in the firm’s partner in an alliance, thereby enhancing the potential for knowledge sharing. Third, the IT intensity of both firms in an alliance provides an effective infrastructure that enhances the conduits through which knowledge can flow to the focal firm from its partner.

IT can increase the transfer of explicit knowledge, and transferability allows knowledge sharing among different groups within the firm and thereby enables knowledge production (Zander and Kogut 1995). Further, IT facilitates the conversion of some of the tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge (Sambamurthy et al. 2003) by enhancing the process of dialogue and collaboration among individuals in the firm (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). Once the tacit knowledge is made explicit, it becomes the basis for future incremental learning. By being able to record some of the tacit knowledge, IT increases a firm’s awareness of its own knowledge, making it easier to know where the knowledge resides and therefore making it easier to access the knowledge.

By being present in the minds of various individuals, knowledge is dispersed in the organization (Weick and Roberts 1993). Dispersed knowledge is more difficult to detect and therefore is more difficult to use as a basis for future knowledge creation (Galunic and Rodan 1998). Moreover, dispersed knowl edge may not be available where needed. For example, if a firm has accumulated knowledge about managing relationships in a marketing alliance, such knowledge might reside among the business development groups within the firm and may not be easily accessible to, say R&D managers, when they try to structure relationships in an R&D alliance. IT artifacts such as knowledge management systems offer the possibility for individuals across the organization to access each other’s knowledge without temporal or spatial restrictions (Yu and Ravichandran 2015). Recent empirical studies have found that knowledge management systems enable firms to effectively learn from prior alliance experiences and use such learning for effectively managing subsequent alliances (Yu and Ravichandran 2015). In other words, IT intensity reduces knowledge dispersion inside the organization, ultimately leading to more knowledge sharing within the firm. Such sharing enhances the absorptive capacity of the firm. Thus, it is reasonable to expect that firms with higher IT intensity might enhance their absorptive capacity because of their capacity to make tacit knowledge explicit, share such knowledge, and recombine it to create new knowledge. Increased absorptive capacity by providing a shared understanding and an appropriate setting could be expected to influence the ability of the firm to learn from its partners.

IT intensity of the firm and its partner, by providing an enabling infrastructure that allows for easy socialization of individuals and groups across the partner firms, fosters weak ties and thereby enhances knowledge flows. Knowledge transfers occur from repeated and intense interactions between the partnering firms (Ariño and de la Torre 1998, Tiwana 2008). To transfer knowledge effectively and achieve their common goal, alliances make use of both formal and informal mechanisms (Inkpen 1998, Rosenkopf and Almeida 2003, Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009). “Formal mechanisms” refers to the managerial mechanisms governing the alliance, as well as the collaborative roles each partner adopts as the alliance evolves (Kumar and Nti 1998). Informal interaction allows employees to learn from each other’s experiences and knowledge through social contact (Kogut and Zander 1992, Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). However, enabling such informal interaction could be challenging because of temporal and spatial distance between the partners and because of the general time demands that employees have that get in the way of informal interactions.

IT can provide a flexible infrastructure that sustains the numerous informal interactions between the members of the partnering firms beyond the meetings and structured interactions. Additional conversations can happen at any time, in a very flexible manner, in online forums and via portals, to support routine information exchange as part of executing the alliance. These informal conversations create opportunities for sharing knowledge across the firms without significant cost and allow employees to learn from each other’s experiences through social contact (Kogut and Zander 1992, Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995, Hoetker and Mellewigt 2009). As individuals in the alliancing firms start interacting through these ITenabled channels, knowledge flows in various areas could be enhanced.

Alliances typically have a functional focus (i.e., a focus on marketing, manufacturing, R&D, etc.), and it is common that people in the respective functiona groups in the partnering firms interact. This, while facilitating knowledge flows among these groups, can also hinder exposure to the broader knowledge that a partner has because such knowledge might reside in other functional areas. Integration across groups is critical for the knowledge flows to be higher, because members of the same functional area tend to have overlapping knowledge bases, and members from different functional areas have a higher chance of possessing novel knowledge elements. Other organizational structures and mechanisms that support the efficiency of coordination between alliance partners, such as the frequent use of certain communication patterns, may also create rigidities because they could keep organization members from obtaining and integrating new knowledge that comes through other communication patterns (Levitt and March 1988, Leonard-Barton 1992).

These rigidities can be overcome with the use of information technologies that can enable flexible or fluid interactions leading to various expected or unexpected connections among alliance partners, beyond typical communication channels and functiona boundaries. Weak ties can be established among members of the partnering firms, which provide access to novel information by bridging otherwise disconnected groups (Granovetter 1973). The existence of IT-enabled networks encourages members of the partnering firms to find others with needed knowledge by creating links between people who otherwise would not have shared knowledge (Feldman 1987). For example, Wasko and Faraj (2005) find that users of electronic networks of practice are typically strangers who share knowledge and information. Because it is easier for firms in an alliance with high IT intensity to maintain weak ties between each other, the scope for serendipitous knowledge discovery and identification of common interest areas could be enhanced, thereby creating “more conduits” though which knowledge can flow.

Together, these mechanisms enhance the capacity of a firm to leverage its knowledge resources and those of its partner in learning. Because IT allows a firm to tap into a wider variety of its knowledge stock, the scope for it to identify potentially useful knowledge from its partner is enhanced. Additionally, because IT enhances absorptive capacity by enabling a firm to link its dispersed knowledge, the novelty of its knowledge base could be higher, and this allows it to recognize opportunities to learn from its partner. Thus, the focal firm has more “antennae” and more fine-tuned “antennae” that enhance its learning even during the routine interactions with its partners as part of executing the alliance.

Knowledge flows to the focal firm will be influenced both by the receptivity of the focal firm and the knowledge transparency of its partner in an alliance (Larsson et al. 1998). Knowledge transparency in the partner is likely to be influenced by the IT intensity of the partner. To the extent the partner firm has invested in IT to codify and make explicit its knowledge, it will be more accessible to the focal firm. Moreover, the extent to which the partner firm has linked its knowledge resources impacts the extent to which the focal firm can identify potential synergies and serendipitously discover opportunities to learn from its partner in an alliance.

Past studies have found that when IT provides the capacity, firms are motivated to choose options that utilize that capacity. For example, Dewan et al. (1998) found that higher IT intensity creates the coordination capacity that allows firms to expand in scope through diversification. Similarly, we argue that in an alliance, when both the focal firm and its partner have high IT intensities, they are likely to choose IT-enabled interalliance coordination mechanisms because they are cheaper, scalable, and allow for richer information exchange. When a firm finds that its partner does not have corresponding IT-enabled capacity, then a mismatch occurs, and this could lead to dysfunctions that impede knowledge flows. Therefore, we expect that the IT intensity of both firms in an alliance will influence the knowledge flows to the focal firm. Hence, we propose the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. Information technology intensity of the focal firm will positively moderate the effects of the alliance knowledge stock on the knowledge flow to a focal firm in an alliance.

Hypothesis 2. Information technology intensity of the partner firm will positively moderate the effects of the alliance knowledge stock on the knowledge flow to a focal firm in an alliance.

## Empirical Analysis Data and Sample

Our data set is constructed from four main sources: the Securities Data Company (SDC) Database on Joint Ventures and Alliances, the National Bureau of

Economic Research (NBER) patent database (Hall et al 2001), InformationWeek IT investments survey, and Compustat. The SDC database reports information on all types of alliances and is compiled from publicly available sources, including U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission filings, industry and trade publications, and news reports.<sup>2</sup> Our sample consists of all alliances that occurred between 1991 and 2001 For simplicity, we restricted our sample to two partner alliances. All firms in our sample are public U.S.-based firms. The U.S. firm restriction is important to maintain consistency and reliability in the patent data, because patenting standards, the system of granting patents, and the value of protection granted differ across nations. Moreover, SDC data are more complete for U.S. firms than for non-U.S. firms. Similar to Schilling and Phelps (2007), who argue that learning and knowledge sharing occur in all types of partnerships, we include all types of alliances. However, because transfer of knowledge might be higher in R&D alliances, we include controls for alliances in which the explicit purpose is research and development.

Table 1 summarizes the alliances in our sample. Most of the alliances in our sample are in the high technology industries, such as Prepackaged Software (25.0%), Semiconductors & Related Devices (9.56%), Computer Integrated System Design (8.82%), Computers & Computer Peripheral Equipment (8.82%), and Telephone and Telegraph Apparatus (5.15%).

We collected patent data from the NBER patent database. This database has detailed information on patented innovations, such as the technological area to which these innovations belong, information about the inventors, the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) assignee names, patents cited, etc.<sup>3</sup> Our final step in compiling our data set was to match the sample with the IT intensity data from InformationWeek. We measured IT intensity by using the annual IT spending data from the surveys conducted by InformationWeek during the period of 1991–2001.<sup>4</sup> The aggregate IT spending data include hardware and software investments in multiple categories (personal computers, workstations, servers, mainframes, peripheral devices, software, local and wide area networks, and telecommunications). Using IT spending to compute IT intensity has been conventional in past information systems (IS) studies (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Bharadwaj 2000, Santhanam and Hartono 2003, Tafti et al. 2013).

Our final sample comprises of 272 firm-observations. Because firms do not always assign patents to the subsidiaries in which innovations take place, looking at corporate firm-level portfolios is particularly important. ## Measures

All U.S. patents have to cite all existing patents that are relevant to that technology. Thus, patent citations become an appropriate proxy for tracing an organization’s knowledge flows (Mowery et al. 1996, Gomes-Casseres et al. 2006, Oxley and Wada 2009). Previous literature has argued that patent citations and citations in academic articles are similar because both are believed to indicate previous work on which the current work builds on (Gomes-Casseres et al. 2006). However, patent citations have an advantage over academic citations in that they are verified by an examiner. Examiners are experts “able to identify relevant prior art that the applicant misses or conceals” and make sure that the firm is not strategically hiding relevant knowledge (Hall et al. 2001) or that unnecessary citations to friends and colleagues are included (Jaffe and Tratjenberg 2002).

A central concern with patent data is the impact of examiner-added citations, which typically account for more than two-thirds of the citations of a patent (Alcacer and Gittelman´ 2006), on the results of studies. We do not believe that examiner-added citations bias our results for two reasons. First, as mentioned above, examiners are experts who make sure firms and their scientists are not strategically hiding relevant knowledge (Hall et al. 2001). Firms have incentives to not cite their partners to hide any knowledge flow asymmetries in an alliance. Under these circumstances, examiner-added citations clarify prior knowledge and would improve the quality of citations as a measure of knowledge transfers (inflows and outflows) in alliances.<sup>5</sup> Second, our dependent variable is computed as a difference in citations before and after the alliance. Therefore, systematic addition by the examiner would cancel out when taking the difference.

## Dependent Variable

Focal Firm Knowledge Flow is measured as the difference between the number of firm citations to the partner after the alliance and the number of firm citations to the partner before the alliance. As Firm acquires knowledge from its partner in the alliance, Firm<sub>j</sub>, there should be a higher rate of citation of Firm ’s patents in the new patents applied for by Firm (Mowery et al. 1996). In constructing this measure, we counted the total number of partner patent citations in the four-year window before the alliance (Stuart and Podolny 1996, Ahuja 2000), with a one-year lag (for example, for a firm that participated in an alliance in 1999, we looked at all the patents applied for and eventually granted to the firm between 1995 and 1998). Further, we counted postalliance citations from Firm to Firm in patents applied for four years after the alliance, with a one-year lag (for example for a firm that participated in an alliance in 1999, we looked at all the patents applied for and eventually granted between 2000 and 2004). We choose a one-year lag because of the potential contemporaneous relationship between R&D efforts and patenting (Hausman et al. 1984). In addition, we looked at the date when the patents were applied for (rather than when the patent was granted) because this date is the earliest time when a new technology can be identified (Rosenkopf and Almeida 2003). The last step in computing the knowledge flow measure was to take the difference between partner citations after the alliance and partner citations before the alliance. This difference represents our measure for the Focal Firm Knowledge Flow. This measure is aligned to an alliance and assesses the “abnormal” knowledge flow to a firm because of an alliance.

## Independent Variables

Firm IT Intensity is measured as the ratio of total information technology expenses to the total revenue of the firm (Bharadwaj et al. 1999). We measured Partner’s IT Intensity in a similar manner

We measured Firm’s Knowledge Stock as the total number of firm prealliance patents. Similar to Sampson (2007), we captured this measure by simply counting the total number of patents that a firm has applied for, four years before the alliance, with a one-year lag. Similarly, we measured the Partner’s Knowledge Stock as the total number of partner prealliance patents. Consistent with previous literature, this represents the total number of patents that the partner of a firm has applied for, four years before the alliance, with a one-year lag. By adding these two variables, we obtained our measure of Alliance Knowledge Stock.

## Control Variables

An essential control variable when using patent data is the Prealliance Citations from Firm to Partner. This control accounts for the previous size of citation activity to the partner by the focal firm. Similarly, the Prealliance Citations from Partner to Firm controls for the overall size of the citing activity of the partner to the firm. We include both variables in our analysis.

We control for Firm Alliance Experience (Hoang and Rothaermel 2005) because it could impact the transfer of knowledge: more experienced firms could be better at extracting knowledge from their partners. Similarly, we also control for Partner Alliance Experience, because a more experienced partner could better protect its knowledge leakage. Both Firm and Partner Alliance Experience are measured as the number of alliances, as listed in SDC, that a firm has been part of since its first alliance up to the current alliance.

Firm R&D Intensity is the ratio of R&D expenditures to total net sales in the year before the alliance. We measured Partner R&D Intensity in a similar manner. We controlled for both variables in our analysis.

We controlled for Firm Age to account for any advantages that more established firms may have in the innovation process (Balasubramanian and Lee 2008). We collected age data from Lexis Nexis and 10-Ks reports for the year when the firm was engaged in an alliance. Similarly, we also collected Partner Age data and controlled for it in our analysis.

Relative Size is measured as the ratio of the total assets of the smaller firm to the total assets of the larger firm in the alliance. We measured Firm Performance and Partner Performance using average return on assets for the three years before the alliance and controlled for these in our analysis.

Firm Technological Diversification is a measure of how diversified the patent portfolio of the focal firm is. Previous literature has argued that the diversity of a firm’s patent portfolio is an indicator of the firm’s breadth of technological resources (Miller 2004). We measured this by creating a Herfindahl index of the patent classes in which the firm has patented. We used a similar approach to measure Partner Technological Diversification.

We used the measure of Technology Overlap<sup>6</sup> developed by Jaffe (1986) on the basis of the angular separation of the patent class distribution vectors of the partner firms in the five years previous to the alliance announcement date. The firms’ distribution vectors (F , F ) are defined over the approximately 500 patent classes defined by the USPTO (Jaffe and Tratjenberg 2002). Past research has used 118 technology classes as defined by the International Patent Classification System to construct their technology diversity measure (Oxley and Wada 2009), whereas others have used 49 aggregated technological subcategories (Jaffe 1986). By using all the 500 classes provided by the USPTO, we developed a more finegrained measure of Technology Overlap.

Horizontal Alliance denotes those alliances that link competitors. Whether the alliance is horizontal or vertical impacts the dynamics of learning. Partners in horizontal alliances have more similar knowledge, and these alliances involve a lower degree of tacitness, ambiguity, and complexity when transferring knowledge (Rothaermel and Deeds 2006). SDC provides the primary Standard Industrial Classifications (SIC) codes of the alliance partners. This variable is a dummy set to 1 if partnering firms have the same primary industry four-digit SIC code, and 0 otherwise.

R&D Alliance. Similar to Schilling and Phelps (2007), we included all types of alliances in our sample. However, it is important to control for R&D alliances because these are more directly related to firm patenting. Therefore, R&D Alliance is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the alliance is an R&D alliance, and 0 otherwise.

Joint Venture. Because previous literature has made the point that knowledge sharing is enhanced in joint ventures (Mowery et al. 1996, Oxley and Wada 2009), we included a control for whether the alliance is organized as a joint venture or as a contract-based alliance by using a dummy variable.

Partners’ Trust can have an impact on the knowledge flows because it limits opportunistic behavior. Therefore, we controlled for Partners’ Trust using a dummy variable, equal to 1 if the partners have previously formed an alliance, and equal to 0 if the partners have not formed a previous alliance.

Industry Dummies. Because firms in different industries may have different patenting propensities due to differences in the importance of patent protection, technological advancement, and the tendency of the industry to keep trade secretes rather than patenting, it is important to control for the industry of the alliance, which we measure using the one-digit SIC classification.

Year Dummies. Because the propensity to patent may also vary across time owing to cost of patenting (Pavitt 1984), market fluctuations, or other reasons, we controlled for the year when the alliance was announced.

## Empirical Analysis

Before we did our main analyses, we analyzed the magnitude of firm citations in allied versus nonallied pairs of firms, to establish the baseline premise in our theorizing that alliancing influences knowledge flows.<sup>7</sup> Similar to Gomes-Casseres et al. (2006) and Mowery et al. (1996), we generated a random sample of nonallied pairs of firms. We formed pairs randomly among the different nonallied firms from the initial sample. This randomization creates a control group for which knowledge transfers serve as a benchmark and occur purely owing to arm’s length spillovers from the external environment (Gomes-Casseres et al. 2006). For these random pairs, we constructed the same measures as for our initial sample. Using our sample, we conducted t-tests to examine whether indeed firm citations to their partners in alliance pairs are significantly greater than firm citations to a randomly assigned partner in nonallied pairs. The mean value for post-alliance cross-citations from the firm to the partner in alliances is 146.09. This is higher than the mean value of 90.84 cross-citations in nonallied pairs. The t-tests suggest that indeed cross-citations in alliance pairs are significantly greater than crosscitations in random pairs and imply that alliances indeed promote higher knowledge flows (t-value of 1.4667, p = 0.07). In addition, we compared the difference in the absolute citation rate before the alliance in allied pairs with that in nonallied pairs. Before the alliance, we found no difference in the citation rate in firms that will form an alliance and firms that will not form an alliance, which further reinforces the notion that alliancing influences knowledge flows. These results are presented in Table 2.

Having established the baseline premise that alliancing influences knowledge flows, we now lay out our analytical approach for testing our hypotheses. Given the scope for both selection bias and endogeneity in our model, we chose an analytical approach that allows us to deal with both. We used a 3SLS to simultaneously control for the sample selection and endogeneity issues. Our analytical approach follows the work of Mroz (1987) that is widely used in the econometrics literature (Wooldridge 2012) and is gaining traction in the IS literature (Bharadwaj et al. 2007).

The decision to ally may not be random, and it is necessary to eliminate the effects of this selection bias explicitly and adjust the estimation of parameters in the structural equation for the selection effect. This is done by including the Inverse Mills Ratio<sup>8</sup> calculated in the first stage, which reflects how the variables included in this stage are related to the selection of the sample, in the structural equation. Accounting for self-selection is important because factors such as resource complementarities, firm capabilities to manage alliances, or industry differences in alliancing patterns could account for both the decision to form an alliance and the knowledge flows between two firms.

In the first stage, we estimated a probit model depicted below to assess the effects of Firm Alliance Experience, Industry Propensity to Ally, and Technological Overlap on the decision to form an alliance.

Probit (Decision to Ally) = f (Firm Alliance Experience, Industry Propensity to Ally, Technological Overlap). . .. . .. Stage 1

Decision to ally is a dummy variable, equal to 1 if the firms have formed an alliance, and equal to 0 if the firms are nonallied firms. On the basis of past findings in the literature, we expect the likelihood to form alliances to be higher for firms with higher alliance experience (Gulati 1999), firms in industries with higher propensity for alliance formation (Garcia-Pont and Nohria 2002) and between firms with higher resource complementarities.

In the second stage, we addressed the problem of endogeneity because alliance knowledge stocks and IT intensity of the focal firm (respectively, alliance knowledge stock and IT intensity of the partner)

might be endogenously determined in our model. Instrument variables are typically used to predict the endogenous variable. To predict the IT intensity of the focal firm (partner), we used an instrument variable that captures the level IT intensity for the industry in which the firm (partner) participates. IT spending is an endogenous variable in the model, because firms may choose their IT spending depending on their alliancing activity. Similar to past studies (Ray et al. 2013), we used data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis to compute the IT intensity of the threedigit North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) industry that a firm participates in. Industry-level IT intensity is calculated as the ratio of the IT spending in the industry (computers, peripheral equipment, and software) to the total number of fulltime employees in the industry. The endogenous variable is first regressed on the instruments, and then the estimated value of the endogenous variable is included in the last stage, in the structural equation instead of the actual value of the endogenous variable. In this second stage, IT intensity of the focal firm and that of the partner are estimated for our sample on the basis of the reduced form equation, which contains the instrument Industry IT Intensity and the Inverse Mills Ratio from stage 1.

Focal Firm IT Intensity = f (Industry IT Intensity, Inverse Mills Ratio from stage 1 to account for selection bias). . . . .. . ..Stage 2

Partner Firm IT Intensity = f (Industry IT Intensity, Inverse Mills Ratio from stage 1 to account for selection bias). . .. . .. . .. Stage 2

In stage 3, the structural equation is estimated. In this stage, the dependent variable is the knowledge flows to the focal firm. The predicted value of the endogenous variables, Focal Firm IT Intensity and Partner Firm IT Intensity from stage 2, the Inverse Mills Ratio from stage 1, our independent variables, interaction terms of interest, and all of our control variables are included in the regression. By following these three steps we have accounted for both endogeneity and selection bias at the same time.

## Results

We present our main empirical results in Table 5. Model 1 contains only the main effects (Alliance Knowledge Stock) and the control variables, whereas model 2 represents the full model with all the control and independent variables as also the interaction terms. The coefficient estimate of Alliance Knowledge Stock is positive and significant, indicating that the alliance knowledge stock will increase the knowledge received by the firm from its partner in the alliance. The coefficients in Table 5 cannot be interpreted straightforwardly because of the logged dependent variable. The coefficient estimate of Alliance Knowledge Stock of 0.001 indicates that an increase of 1 unit in the Alliance’s Knowledge Stock will result in a 0.1% increase in knowledge inflows ((exp (0.001) <sup>−</sup> 1) × 100). At the mean value for Alliance Knowledge Stock of 1,677.22, this means an increase in knowledge flows of approximately 167.722.

Examining the interaction effects, we find that the coefficient estimate of the interaction term between Firm IT Intensity and Alliance Knowledge Stock is positive and significant as predicted, thus supporting Hypothesis 1. To determine the nature of the significant interactions, we plotted the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable for values of Firm IT Intensity set at the mean and one standard deviation above and below the mean, as suggested by Cohen and Cohen (1983). The plot in Figure 2 shows that when Firm IT Intensity is at high levels, there is a strong positive relationship between Alliance Knowledge Stock and Knowledge Flows to the focal firm. This plot shows overall support for our Hypothesis 1. Thus, as Firm IT Intensity increases, the focal firm is better able to make use of the prealliance knowledge stock, and this has a more enhanced impact on the knowledge flows to the focal firm.

IT spending of the partner, by providing the appropriate communication channels, enabling weak ties and enhancing knowledge transparency, enhances the impact of the alliance knowledge stock on knowledge flows to the focal firm.

To rule out the possibility that Firm IT Intensity and Partner IT Intensity are not both moderators and mediators in our model, we did a split sample analysis.<sup>10</sup> We created three subsamples, one in which Firm IT Intensity is less than one standard deviation below the mean (IT Low), one with IT intensity within one standard deviation around the mean (IT Medium), and one with IT intensity more one standard deviation above the mean (IT High). We ran our full model on each of the three subsamples and estimated the coefficients for the interaction terms. We then tested the hypotheses that the coefficients of the interaction terms are equal across the subsamples. We did a similar analysis with Partner IT Intensity by creating three subsamples.

## Discussion

Researchers in strategic management have long been interested in alliances as a mechanism for transferring knowledge. However, there has been limited work aimed at understanding how the assets of the partnering firms in an alliance and their complementarities influence the knowledge sharing and learning that occurs. The limited empirical evidence about the characteristics of the alliance partners that influence knowledge transfers has prevented researchers from understanding which alliances contribute to more knowledge transfers. In this study, we posit that the knowledge stocks of the partnering firms will influence knowledge flows to the focal firm in an alliance. Recent IS literature has found IT spending to be an important resource in developing firm capabilities (Mithas et al. 2011), in enabling firms to learn from their alliance experience (Yu and Ravichandran 2015), and in enhancing a firm’s absorptive capacity (Heart et al. 2011). Building on these studies, we argued that IT spending is a complementary resource that enables firms to enhance their absorptive capacity, provide a flexible infrastructure to support cross-partner interaction and socialization, and enhance knowledge transparency, and thereby influences their learning in alliances.

On the basis of the data compiled from alliances in the U.S. public firms, we find strong support for both our hypotheses. We find that firms receive more knowledge from alliances if the prealliance knowledge stock is high. We show that these knowledge flows are enhanced in the presence of high IT spending of both firms in an alliance.

Although a large body of work has examined the value of IT spending to firms, research has largely focused on the efficiency gains and coordination benefits provided by IT. While recent work has started examining the role of IT in external partnerships (Tafti and Krishnan 2007), including alliances (Yu and Ravichandran 2015, Chi et al. 2010), research on the role of IT in interfirm collaboration still remains limited, especially with respect to knowledge flows. It is hoped that the empirical results presented here and the theorizing will set the stage for further research exploring the role of IT in alliance success and value creation.

Our results have important practical implications. Choice of alliance partners has been an important consideration for firms and has been found to be critical in influencing alliance successes. In fact, firms through repeated alliance experience fine tune their skills in partner selection. Our results indicate that two important criteria in selecting partners are their knowledge stock and IT intensity. Given that the knowledge stock of a potential partner can be identified from external sources such as patent databases, profiling of potential alliance partners can easily be part of the due diligence process. Similarly, the availability of IT intensity data makes it possible to include this as part of the due diligence process as well. Given that one of the key objectives in most alliances involves knowledge transfer and new competence development, explicit focus on knowledge flows is indeed warranted but missing in most due diligence processes. In fact, in most pre-deal analysis the focus is much more on the complementary capabilities of the partner and the cultural proximity of the partner. Although these remain important considerations in partner selection, our findings highlight the importance and the potential value in examining the knowledge stocks and the IT intensity of the partners.

Traditionally, IT spending in firms is justified according to the efficiency gains and business model innovations that they enable. Our results clearly highlight that firms do realize intangible benefits, such as knowledge flows, because of their IT spending. The extent to which firms learn from external sources depends on the firm resources, especially their IT resources. In fact, learning goals in alliances could be set after due consideration of the firm’s IT intensity. Serial alliancing firms, such as Cisco and P&G, have realized the importance of IT in driving value gains in alliances and have proactively sought to invest in an IT infrastructure that enables them to harness alliance experience as well as link with their partner firms. Therefore, although it is important to look at the characteristics of the partner, firms also have to take into account their own IT spending when establishing goals in terms of learning from alliances.

## Limitations and Future Research

Although this study provides useful insights, it has its limitations. Firms in our sample are limited to those for which we have IT spending data as provided by InformationWeek. Therefore, care must be exercised when generalizing these results to other firms. A limitation of this research is that is does not look at different types of IT spending, such as spending on communications, training, hardware, etc., because of lack of availability of such granular data. Although the measure of IT intensity has been previously used in the literature, we recommend future studies to test the relationships posited in his research using more fine-grained measures. A small sample of dyadic firms can be examined over time to understand whether and how the knowledge flows between them were impacted by the IT intensities of the firms. Such time series data offer the advantage of controlling for unobserved heterogeneity (first differences technique) and provide rich insights into how knowledge flows may be impacted by changes in the types of IT spending.

One of the challenges of the patent data is that the commercial importance of patents and the propensity to patent are different across industries. We tried to account for these differences by including industry controls in the regressions. Despite these limitations, patent citations remain the most accepted indicator of knowledge flows in part because of the easy access to patent data. Using other measures of knowledge flows, although interesting, requires methodologies that involve primary data collection or longitudinal approaches that examine product introductions and new market entry over time. We hope future studies explore such approaches and thereby enrich the body of knowledge in this area of research.

By establishing the role of IT as a facilitator of knowledge flows, our research opens new research questions. In this research, we only looked at bilatera alliances. However, when there are more than two partners, coordination becomes even more challenging. It is likely that the role of IT in influencing knowledge flows and eventually the success of such alliances could be significant. Examining such effects would be an important direction that can add to our understanding of the factors that influence learning and knowledge flows in alliances.

Our supplemental analyses suggest that in non-R&D alliances learning from partners is more challenging because easy exposure to the technological knowledge may not happen when the alliance focus is on other functional areas. To the extent both partners have the IT infrastructure that enables easy socialization, knowledge flows will be enhanced. Future research should explore whether and to what extent the parity or discrepancies in the IT spending levels between the partner firms in an alliance influences knowledge flows in any manner, and if so, why. Future studies should also explore using more finegrained measures of IT-enabled capacities in the firm and its partner in an alliance to enable a deeper investigation of the specific pathways through which knowledge flows are enhanced. Studies could also discriminate between different types of knowledge flows, such as intended and unintended knowledge transfers, and different types of knowledge stocks, such as functional knowledge and strategic insights into markets, and examine whether IT spending impacts differently these different types of knowledge flows. Such studies could shed light on the broader question of who gains more from an alliance, and why. Although knowledge appropriation questions have been investigated, the focus has been more on the governance of the alliance and not on the internal firm capabilities and resources. By focusing on IT spending, future research can add to this growing body of knowledge aimed at understanding value appropriation in alliances. Finally, although we focused on alliances in this paper, other forms of interorganizational initiatives, such as mergers and acquisitions, are also common. A logical extension of our study would be to theorize and empirically examine whether and why IT spending of the acquiring and the acquired firms impact post–merger and acquisition innovations.

## Concluding Remarks

Our goal in this study was to understand how information technology enhances knowledge flows in alliances. We argued and found that IT intensity of both firms in an alliance enhances knowledge flows in the presence of high knowledge stocks of the partnering firms. This study makes important contributions to research in both information systems and strategic management. The findings also provide practical advice for managers to enhance alliance value creation and in terms of key aspects to be considered in the due diligence process in choosing alliance partners, to enhance learning outcomes.
