# Contextualizing Team Adaptation for Fostering Creative Outcomes in Multicultural Virtual Teams: A Mixed Methods Approach

Anuragini Shirish,<sup>1</sup> Shirish C. Srivastava,<sup>2</sup> Imed Boughzala<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Université Paris-Saclay, Univ Evry, IMT-BS, LITEM, France, anuragini.shirish@imt-bs.eu <sup>2</sup>HEC Paris, France, srivastava@hec.fr

## Abstract

Rapid developments in real-time collaborative technologies, coupled with the quest for innovation and creativity, have made global virtual teams (GVTs) a viable workplace collaboration option that many companies are turning to. Although diverse team member perspectives in GVTs are expected to foster creativity, cultural diversity within GVTs also poses significant challenges related to knowledge exchange and integration among team members. Grounding our work in the team adaptation and cultural intelligence (CQ) literatures, we suggest CQ as a plausible modality for cultural adaptation in GVTs. Specifically, we propose a nomological network comprising CQ dimensions (motivation, cognition, metacognition, and behavior) serving as a cultural adaptation mechanism for fostering creativity in GVT outcomes. We contextualize and extend CQ theory, which has previously focused on face-to-face contexts, to the virtual collaborative GVT environment. For this, we conceptualize the significant role of deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior (role structure adaptation) in GVTs—in addition to surface-level explicitly displayed adaptative behavior (CQ behavior). We tested the proposed model through a sequential mixed methods approach that integrated the results from a quantitative two-wave survey study with findings from a qualitative study comprising expert interviews, to arrive at rich and robust inferences and metainferences. The proposed CQ-for-GVT framework, along with delineated boundary conditions and associated propositions, explicates an integrative model explaining the role of CQ for GVT creativity performance. The delineated model not only has theoretical implications but also provides useful directions for GVT practitioners.

Keywords: Virtual Team Performance, Cultural Intelligence, Global Virtual Team, Team Creativity, Mixed Methods, Deep-Level Negotiated Adaptative Behavior, Surface-Level Explicit Adaptative Behavior, Role Structure Adaptation, Team Adaptation, Cultural Differences

## 1 Introduction

With advances in real-time collaborative technologies and the emergent need for enhancing innovation and creativity in business outcomes, we are witnessing a growth in the use of geographically dispersed global virtual teams (GVTs) as new units for work collaboration (Jimenez et al., 2017; Windeler et al., 2015). Given the diversity of expertise and perspectives that GVTs can offer (Carte & Chidambaram, 2004; Gibson & Gibbs, 2006; O'Leary & Mortensen, 2010), such teams are generally tasked with nonroutine, complex, and creative collaborative tasks (Espinosa et al., 2015; Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014) for which cultural diversity is a clear strength (Asatiani et al., 2021; Gilson et al., 2015). Though in the current business context, where innovation and creativity are crucial expectations from GVTs (SHRM, 2019; Tippmann et al., 2021), facilitating creative outcomes is tough—as it requires an efficient knowledge exchange among culturally diverse GVT members (Driskell et al., 2018; Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017). Hence, while on the one hand, cultural diversity in GVTs provides diverse perspectives, which are prerequisites for creative outcomes—on the other hand—cultural diversity poses a significant communication challenge that can impede knowledge exchange and integration among team members (Hinds & Bailey, 2003; Kankanhalli et al., 2006; Watson‐Manheim et al., 2012). Recent studies have echoed this sentiment by indicating that though 76% of virtual team executives acknowledge the value of cultural diversity in GVTs, only 23% capitalize on it (RW3 CultureWizard, 2016; 2018). Our work is motivated by this perceptible dilemma and seeks to improve the understanding of the mechanisms facilitating cultural adaptation in GVTs for fostering creative outcomes, which we believe will be of value to both research and practice.

Clearly, effective GVT collaboration entails the management of different technology, task, and role interdependencies among culturally diverse team members (Bailey et al., 2010; Nordbäck & Espinosa, 2019). Despite the acknowledged salience of cultural adaptation in GVTs (Asatiani et al., 2021; Su, 2015), explicit theoretical deliberation on the subject is rather limited (Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017). Prior information systems (IS) research on GVTs has identified different team adaptation mechanisms (Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2007; Sarker & Sahay, 2003)—of which two social ordering mechanisms are particularly noteworthy for our research context: (1) the preemptive mechanism via organizational design, and (2) the reactive mechanism via informal negotiated practices between team members (Faraj & Xiao, 2006).

The preemptive mechanism is similar to the organizational design perspective, which relies on formal structuring comprising clarity in role definition, as is commonly observed in sports teams (Hambrick et al., 1998). Generally, in face-to-face contexts, the preemptive mechanism is the dominant social ordering mechanism e.g., technology structures defining the formal ordering that shapes the interactions across different team members (Brown et al., 2016; Faraj & Xiao, 2006; Majchrzak et al., 2000). However, the context of GVTs is different from regular organizational face-to-face teams in at least two ways: (1) the nature of tasks executed, and (2) the plurality of team members’ cultural backgrounds. These differences motivate us to move beyond the preemptive mechanism to understand the role of the informal reactive social ordering mechanism in the virtual team context.

GVTs are temporary organizational units that are generally used to solve unstructured, nonroutine problems quickly to deal with emergent uncertainties (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). For solving such creative problems, diversity in team members’ perspectives is likely helpful. However, cultural differences among GVT members also imply differences in values, cognitive schemas, demeanor, and language (Hambrick et al., 1998). In such dynamic and diverse settings, formal preemptive structuring mechanisms with clearly defined roles may not be sufficient for efficient team adaptation (Faraj & Xiao, 2006). We posit that reactive informal negotiated team practices will be especially salient for GVT coordination (Vlaar et al., 2008). Consequently, in our study, we integrate these two team adaptation perspectives to develop a holistic understanding of cultural adaptation in GVTs.

Recent cultural adaptation research has identified cultural intelligence (CQ) as an adaptation capability for performing effectively in a multicultural setting by recognizing and adapting to cultural differences (Van Dyne et al., 2010; Yari et al., 2020). It comprises four distinct CQ dimensions: CQ motivation, CQ cognition, CQ metacognition, and CQ behavior (Bogilović et al., 2017; Livermore & Soon, 2015; Van Dyne et al., 2012). CQ motivation signifies the interest and confidence to function effectively in culturally diverse settings, CQ cognition refers to knowledge about differences and similarities across cultures, CQ metacognition is a sensemaking and strategizing capability for dealing with culturally diverse experiences, and CQ behavior is the ability to display adapted verbal and nonverbal responses in crosscultural settings (Ang & Van Dyne, 2008). However, CQ research has thus far primarily focused on preemptive explicit face-to-face non-GVT contexts (Groves & Feyerherm, 2011; Malik et al., 2014), which may not fully explain the cultural adaptation mechanisms in virtual teams. Given the need for cultural adaptation in GVTs to facilitate creativity performance, we aim to contextualize and understand the emergent GVT adaptation phenomenon through the CQ lens (Fisher et al. 2021). Specifically, we examine the following two research questions:

RQ1: What are the mechanisms through which the CQ capabilities embedded in a GVT influence its creativity performance?

RQ2: What are the boundary conditions that can limit this influence?

Leveraging team adaptation literature, we first develop a contextualized CQ nomological network for facilitating GVT creativity performance (Frick et al., 2018). Specifically, using a decomposition approach for context-based theorizing (Hong et al., 2014), we conceptualize the role of four CQ dimensions—CQ motivation, CQ cognition, CQ metacognition, and CQ behavior in describing the team adaptation mechanisms. In addition to conceptualizing the role of the preemptive social ordering mechanism, given the nonroutine, complex, and interdependent nature of tasks entrusted to GVTs, we theorize the additional critical role of the reactive social ordering mechanism comprising deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior for facilitating team cultural adaptation. Next, we test the influence of the theorized CQ-for-GVT framework on team creativity performance using a mixed methods approach (Fleming et al., 2007; Yong et al., 2014). Specifically, the quantitative study tests the contextualized theoretical model, while the followup qualitative study corroborates the results from the quantitative study, explains the unsupported hypotheses, and delineates the boundary conditions to the theorized model (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

Together, the inferences and meta-inferences emerging from the study contribute to the IS literature on GVTs in several ways. First, moving beyond the often studied technological and social team adaptation mechanisms, we highlight the key role that cultural adaptation plays in GVT creativity performance (Asatiani et al., 2021; Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009; Hardin et al., 2007). Second, we leverage team adaptation literature (Frick et al., 2018) to develop a nomological network relating different CQ dimensions to GVT creativity performance. This contextualization of previously examined technological adaptation mechanisms enhances our understanding of GVT adaptation to cultural differences (Espinosa et al., 2015; Nordbäck & Espinosa, 2019; Windeler et al., 2015). Third, in our theorized model we infuse the dynamic GVT context required for efficient knowledge exchange and integration among virtual team members for collaborative creativity. Specifically, we incorporate deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behaviors in addition to the model’s surface-level explicitly displayed adaptative behaviors. This helps extend the applicability of the multidimensional CQ construct to multicultural creative virtual team settings (Chamakiotis & Panteli, 2017; Cui et al., 2020; Gibson & Gibbs, 2006).

## 2 Background Literature

## 2.1 GVTs, Cultural Differences, and the Need for Cultural Adaptation

Technology-mediated GVTs vary in their degree of virtuality, ranging from fully virtual to hybrid teams (Chudoba et al., 2005). Because it is possible for a GVT to include both co-located and virtual members, virtuality is conceptualized as a continuum rather than a dichotomy (Chudoba et al., 2005; Northcraft et al., 2006), which depends on the geographic distribution and cultural diversity of the team members, as well as the amount of e-communication media usage (Hoch & Kozlowski, 2014).

In addition to technologically mediated communications, prior literature has identified several other contextual characteristics that distinguish GVTs from co-located face-to-face teams. GVTs are generally temporary in nature (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009; Chamakiotis & Panteli, 2017; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2007), and the nature of the tasks they undertake is generally complex, contextual, and nonroutine, requiring close interaction between team members to facilitate the exchange of knowledge (Asatiani et al., 2021; Dubé & Robey, 2009; Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014). In addition, GVTs are largely multicultural in nature, and cultural differences among the team members may tend to impede communication, collaboration, and coordination between them (Hinds & Bailey, 2003; Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999; Watson‐Manheim et al., 2012). Hence, despite the imperative of efficient knowledge exchange and integration for executing GVTs’ complex, nonroutine tasks, the multicultural nature of GVTs, coupled with technologymediated communication, tends to hamper the collaborative process. This situation is rather paradoxical, as managers are often interested in leveraging GVTs’ cultural diversity for creative solutions but this diversity can become a bottleneck if not appropriately managed. This contradiction underscores the salience of understanding cultural adaptation mechanisms in GVTs.

Culture is defined as the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another (Hofstede, 1980). It plays a major role in determining work-related values and attitudes, as well as the behaviors and practices that prevail in a particular business context (Hofstede, 1980; Livermore, 2010). Cultural diversity within a team brings in different perspectives, which contributes to the creativity of the team’s solution (Zakaria et al., 2004). However, the heterogeneity in culturally idiosyncratic behaviors and practices may serve as a hindrance to smooth communication within GVTs. The impact of cultural heterogeneity may be further accentuated in the context of GVTs, as the tasks executed by GVTs are usually nonroutine and complex, requiring implicit understanding among team members in addition to explicit communication and adaptation. Although culture can be conceptualized at different levels of analysis, in this study, we examine the influence of differences in national cultures among team members. Prior research has found differences in national cultures to be a significant variable that influences performance in different organizational settings (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). In this study, we specifically leverage the CQ framework to explicate the mechanisms for cultural adaptation in GVTs.

## 2.2 Cultural Intelligence in Global Virtual Teams

Cultural intelligence and team performance: The concept of CQ is based on the multi-loci theory of intelligence, which proposes that intelligence is located in various mental and behavioral capabilities that facilitate goal-directed adaptative behavior (Sternberg & Detterman, 1986). CQ is defined as the adaptative capability to deal effectively with situations characterized by cultural diversity (Ang et al., 2006). CQ is a malleable and culture-neutral concept comprising four distinct dimensions that indicate different functions—motivation, cognition, metacognition, and behavior—as described in the previous section (Ang & Van Dyne, 2008; Ang et al., 2006; Erez et al., 2013).

Prior research has conceptualized CQ not only at the individual level, but also as a team-level mechanism for identifying, calibrating, and managing cultural risks (Adair et al., 2013; Livermore, 2010; Magnusson et al., 2014). Given the fact that some teams are more effective than others in culturally diverse situations (Ang & Van Dyne, 2008; Ang et al., 2007), similar to prior studies, we conceptualize CQ at the team level by aggregating the CQ of the constituting GVT members (Adair et al., 2013; Erez et al., 2013). Moreover, although prior research has shown that CQ predicts performance in multicultural face-to-face settings, there is a clear gap in our understanding of the mechanisms through which CQ influences GVT performance (see Appendix A, which summarizes the key CQ research in the ICTmediated virtual team context).

Dimensionality view of CQ: The four dimensions of CQ are conceptually distinct facets of the overall capability to function and manage effectively in culturally diverse settings (Ang et al., 2011; Van Dyne et al., 2012). Despite the many research calls, very few studies have theorized the individual role of different dimensional capabilities constituting the CQ construct (Malik et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2012; Van Dyne et al., 2012). Recent team adaptation models (such as Frick et al., 2018) provide several theoretical reasons for linking the different dimensions of CQ with outcome variables. Such investigation is consistent with the recent research calls to study group and member adaptation aspects in GVTs (Ahuja et al., 2020).

The dimensionality view of the CQ framework is also aligned with the decomposition approach to contextspecific theorization described in IS research (Hong et al., 2014). Prior studies in other fields have examined several multidimensional aggregated constructs in a deconstructed way to provide a deeper understanding of the phenomenon—for example, the constructs of job characteristics (Hulin & Blood, 1968), job stress (Edwards, 2001; Schuler, 1980), organizational commitment (Allen & Meyer, 1990; Meyer et al., 1993), and, recently, technostress (Chandra et al., 2019). The adoption of a decomposed dimensionality view also provides an opportunity to incorporate context-specific variables that can account for the dynamic role-taking and role-giving behaviors necessary for the exchange of complex nonroutine knowledge in GVTs (Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017). Consequently, we leverage contextspecific theorization to conceptualize the CQ nomological network for GVTs, comprising linkages among the different CQ dimensions and their relationship to GVT creativity performance (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Bamberger, 2008).

## 2.3 Cultural Adaptation in GVTs

While explicit cultural adaptation can be observed in modified CQ behaviors such as altering the tone or pitch of one’s voice, the exchange required for complex nonroutine tasks in GVTs may require a deeper level of understanding among team members about their emergent implicit roles as the project unfolds. This precipitates the need for spontaneous stimulus-specific cultural adaptation mechanisms that can help GVTs improvise and increase knowledge visibility through successful cultural sensemaking.

Prior literature has highlighted the salience of role structure adaptation (RSA) as a team-specific unscripted negotiated adaptative behavior in response to an evolving stimulus (such as the challenges due to cultural differences). We posit that in the case of multicultural GVTs, to facilitate efficient communication, coordination, and collaboration, RSA is imperative (Lepine, 2003). While exhibiting explicit surface-level cultural adaptation (CQ behavior) may be the dominant adaptation mechanism in face-to-face colocated teams, RSA may be required in multicultural dispersed GVTs for implicit role-taking and rolegiving behaviors to facilitate efficient knowledge exchange among team members (Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017; Su, 2015).

However, such cultural adaptation mechanisms have not been adequately theoretically integrated or empirically tested for GVTs. In a large-scale survey by RW3 CultureWizard (2018), approximately 42% of respondents reported that differences in the role expectations and general behavioral assumptions of the members of multicultural GVTs were considered major challenges for the team’s smooth functioning. This finding underscores the significance of implicit deep-level understanding and coordination among GVT members. This report highlights that in addition to adapting to surface-level cultural differences such as language and accents within GVTs (CQ behavior), team members’ abilities to adapt to the evolving role requirements (RSA) of GVTs are important.

## 3 Theory and Hypothesis Development

In this section, we explain the basic tenets of the Frick et al. (2018) team adaptation model and then leverage the model to propose a set of hypotheses linking different CQ dimensions describing the CQ nomological network for GVT creativity performance (presented in Figure 1). Our approach to theorization follows prior IS research studies that have leveraged process theories for formulating variance-based models. For example, previous studies have leveraged stress and coping models (Bala & Venkatesh, 2016; Liang & Xue, 2009), team adaptation models (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014), and information security awareness process models (Tsohou et al., 2008) to explain research phenomena by integrating both variance and process approaches (de Guinea & Webster, 2017).

## 3.1 Leveraging the Team Adaptation Model

Cultural differences within multicultural teams impact team adaptation dynamics (Feitosa et al., 2017; Su, 2015), and CQ is viewed as one of the mechanisms for facilitating team adaptation outcomes (Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017; Su, 2015). Prior research has shown that stimulus or cue-driven team capabilities within GVTs facilitate better team coordination (Faraj & Xiao, 2006; Mattarelli et al., 2017). Team adaptation thus depends on the team’s ability to continuously detect and evaluate the changing situation and accordingly modify workflow transactions among team members (Lepine et al., 2005). We build on this idea and use the team adaptation model proposed by Frick et al. (2018) to conceptualize our CQ-for-GVT framework.

Frick et al. (2018) provide a linear, cyclical team adaptation heuristic comprising four phases: recognize, reframe, respond, and reflect, as shown in Figure 1. The recognize phase signifies the recognition of a salient dissonant cue that needs team adaptation. The reframe phase marks the plan formulation and preparation for action. This is followed by adaptative action or behavior comprising the respond and reflect phases. In the specific context of cultural adaptation in GVTs, response and reflection actions are closely tied together because reflection also shapes the ongoing response. We use these phases as a sequential order of events to explain GVTs’ adaptation/maladaptation process (Frick et al., 2018; Benishek and Lazzarra, 2019). Specifically, we map the CQ dimensions as capabilities needed during different phases of the GVT’s adaptation to the encountered cultural differences. In the context of our study, the “cue stream” comprises the changed situation due to ongoing or potential multicultural interactions within the GVT that may trigger a need for adaptation (Stokes et al., 2008). In Table 1, we summarize how the four phases of Frick et al.’s model can be used to explain the team’s adaptation to the “cultural differences” cue in GVTs and the corresponding adaptative role of different CQ capabilities during each of the four phases.

## 3.2 Linking CQ Motivation and CQ Cognition to CQ Metacognition: Recognize and Reframe Phases

Cultural adaptation in GVT starts with team members identifying a culturally discrepant situation, which acts as a cue for the team members to plan their subsequent adapted interactions for knowledge coordination and integration. During the recognize phase of cultural adaptation, the two key capabilities that GVT members need are CQ motivation (drive) and CQ cognition (knowledge). These two capabilities help the GVT members identify and make sense of the encountered cultural differences (cue) to reframe and plan their imminent adapted actions. The two capabilities thus help the cultural adaptation process to move from the recognize to the reframe phase. Evidently, the key capability in the reframe phase is CQ metacognition, or the ability to strategize and plan cross-cultural interactions. However, GVT members’ ability to strategize for cross-cultural interactions will be dependent on the efficiency with which CQ motivation and CQ knowledge are leveraged in the recognize phase—team members diving into their resource repositories to understand the situation and formulating a plan for adapting to the cross-cultural situation (Frick et al., 2018).

In the recognize phase, because of their interest in cross-cultural interactions, teams sufficiently high in motivational CQ would better scan their cross-cultural environment. Their perceptions of self-efficacy, combined with their intrinsic and extrinsic interest in cross-cultural interactions will propel such teams to recognize culturally discrepant situations quickly (Su, 2015), which will be viewed as opportunities rather than threats (Abrantes et al., 2018; Chong et al., 2010; Mueller, 2012). Clearly, before the GVT members can plan and strategize their cross-cultural interactions, they need to be sufficiently motivated to assess the potential consequences and benefits of the crosscultural situation (Livermore, 2010; Xu & Chen, 2017). CQ motivation, thus, acts as a precursor to identifying a novel cultural cue requiring the team’s attention. Failure to recognize the salience of the cue would amount to maladaptation (Frick et al., 2018). Hence, GVT members’ motivation to be effective in cross-cultural interactions (CQ motivation) will be related to their strategizing and planning of their crosscultural interactions (CQ metacognition).

## H1: In the context of GVTs, CQ motivation is positively associated with CQ metacognition.

In a similar vein, cognitive CQ or cross-cultural knowledge is the most significant resource available to the GVT for making sense of the encountered cultural differences (Livermore, 2010). Cognitive CQ comprises knowledge about differences in cultural systems, norms, and values. An understanding of how cultures differ in terms of work norms, habits, and behaviors lays the foundation for planning ways to act intelligently in cross-cultural settings (Van Dyne et al., 2010). Hence, the ability of GVT members to plan their cross-cultural interactions (CQ metacognition) will also be dependent on the level of knowledge (CQ cognition) they have about different cultures.

H2: In the context of GVTs, CQ cognition is positively associated with CQ metacognition.

## 3.3 CQ Metacognition and Adaptative Behaviors: Respond and Reflect Phases

CQ metacognition is a higher-order capability to think about interactional processes, anticipate the cultural preferences of others, and adjust mental models during and after intercultural experiences (Ang et al., 2007). CQ metacognition helps in planning and precedes any adaptative behavior. However, before linking CQ metacognition to GVTs’ adaptative behavior, it is imperative to understand the different kinds of adaptation actions GVTs can undertake.

The team adaptation literature suggests that the actions that teams undertake for coordination among their members can be either explicit or implicit<sup>1</sup> (Burke et al., 2006; Klein & Pierce, 2001). Explicit coordination within a team refers to overtly adapted behaviors and actions that team members display in response to observable situational cues. Such preemptive surfacelevel behaviors can be considered communicative adaptation practices, which, in the context of cultural adaptation, are directed to counter observable cultural differences in a planned way (Stahl et al., 2010). As previously explained, these surface-level explicit adaptative behaviors are akin to CQ behavior.

Implicit coordination, on the other hand, signifies deeper-level improvised and dynamic changes that entail team members drawing on their ability to take stock of the situation in consideration of their own roles in relation to the roles of their fellow team members to execute the task at hand (Burke et al., 2006). Because implicit coordination is based on a dynamic contextual interpretation of the observed cues, the associated actions undertaken by the team members cannot be accurately scripted or preplanned. Rather, such deep-level negotiated adaptative behaviors rely on improvised role-taking and rolegiving behaviors within the team (Bechky, 2011; Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009). Such an adaptation mechanism is akin to RSA, which comprises the “reactive and nonscripted adjustments to a team’s system of member roles that contribute to team effectiveness” (Lepine, 2003, p. 28).

## 3.4 Linking CQ Metacognition with Surface-Level Explicit Adaptative Behavior (CQ Behavior) and Deep Level Negotiated Adaptative Behavior (Role Structure Adaptation)

Following the line of argument for the two previous hypotheses based on the linear team adaptation model, we posit that the metacognitive dimension of CQ is triggered during the reframe phase to develop a suitable strategy for overcoming culturally discrepant situations within the GVTs. After assessing their resources, GVT members need to plan their actions to facilitate team coordination. In the respond phase, the team executes the prepared plan reframed in the earlier phase to enact the adaptative actions directed at mitigating the emergent discrepant situation (Frick et al., 2018). However, for this to happen effectively, the team members may also need to reflect on the ongoing interactions to calibrate their actions continuously as the adaptative behavior unfolds. This is especially salient for creative tasks generally entrusted to GVTs that may require a deeper contextual understanding of the communicated information. Hence, in the context of cultural adaptation in GVTs, the respond and reflect phases are closely tied together.

Van Dyne et al. (2010) describe CQ metacognition as the lynchpin between CQ cognition and CQ behavior that signifies how individuals use their cross-cultural knowledge to enact appropriate CQ behavior in crosscultural settings. For example, prior to interacting with team members from high-context cultures, a culturally intelligent GVT member might plan to purposefully pay closer attention to nonverbal communication cues from such members. CQ behavior signifies team members’ explicitly adapted verbal and nonverbal behavior in response to the cross-cultural situation they face (Livermore, 2010). This is the action dimension of the CQ construct, without which CQ cannot be displayed (Ang et al., 2007). In our research context, the CQ behavior dimension concerns the behavioral flexibility that helps GVT members to preempt and adapt their overt communicative behaviors in the face of cross-cultural differences for better coordination (see Livermore, 2010). CQ behavior involves a broad repertoire of verbal and nonverbal communicative behaviors, such as using culturally appropriate words, tones, gestures, and facial expressions (Gudykunst et al., 1988). However, GVT members’ behavioral flexibility to display their explicit adaptability to cross-cultural situations (CQ behavior) clearly depends on their ability to strategize and plan their cross-cultural encounters (CQ metacognition) in the reframe phase (Van Dyne et al., 2010).

H3a: In the context of GVTs, CQ metacognition is positively associated with surface-level explicit behavior adaptation (CQ behavior).

As described earlier, GVTs are multicultural temporary organizational units generally entrusted to solve unstructured, nonroutine complex problems in situations requiring a quick response to deal with emergent uncertainties (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999). To facilitate the execution of such tasks, efficient realtime knowledge coordination among GVT members is needed (Driskell et al., 2018; Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017; Espinosa et al., 2015). However, cultural differences among GVT members can pose a significant bottleneck for such deep-level implicit, “on-the-fly” coordination (Hinds & Bailey, 2003; Kankanhalli et al., 2006; Watson‐Manheim et al., 2012). Role systems and sensemaking structures, which are essential for coordination, cannot be preplanned for such enactments. Moreover, the GVT context offers fewer opportunities for face-to-face interactions, which are considered to be superior mechanisms for transmitting and integrating the implicit knowledge necessary for collaborative creativity (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014; Maznevski & Chudoba, 2000; Stahl et al., 2010).

We posit that in the case of GVTs where real-time, dynamic contextual knowledge coordination is required, alternative compensatory mechanisms that do not rely on procedural ordering will be enacted. Such compensatory mechanisms, based on team members’ ability to plan and reshape its shared mental models and cognition, will enable team members to implicitly try and understand and adapt to the roles and working styles of other team members (Kock, 2001; 2005), which is described in the team adaptation literature as RSA (Lepine, 2003). Prior research has also shown that adaptive teams implement cognitive workarounds to remove the performance barriers that stem from different sources (Tesluk & Mathieu, 1999). Based on the dynamic interpretation of the behaviors and expectations of fellow team members through deep mutual monitoring, GVT members may exercise compensatory backup behaviors to fill in the explicit coordination gaps (Burke et al., 2006; Maynard et al., 2015). Such alteration of roles and structures can involve implementing specific changes in response to novel cultural cues (Christian et al., 2017; Christian et al., 2014). Such stimulus-specific RSA behaviors represent unique adjustments “as dictated by the type of disruption or trigger” (Maynard et al., 2015: p. 8), which are pertinent to the GVT context.

Clearly, in the context of GVTs, explicit behavioral adaptation in the form of CQ behavior may not be adequate for the required implicit coordination (described above). We posit that adaptation mechanisms that assume constant renegotiation of role structures and role enactments are suitable means to coordinate cultural differences within GVT plans (Schmidtke & Cummings, 2017). For example, if a culturally intelligent GVT member from a collectivist culture recognizes that other team members from collectivist cultures are hesitant to share unpleasant yet critical information because it might disrupt team harmony, the culturally intelligent GVT member can take the lead in sharing the unpleasant information by implicitly adapting in a way that ensures smooth communication—for example, without attributing the cause of the unpleasantness to any particular person or group. Or the team member can use the appropriate technology to communicate e.g., emails for indirect cultures or a video/phone call for direct cultures. Similarly, the information can be communicated to the specific person individually, or in front of the full team, depending on the cultural characteristics of the GVT member receiving the unpleasant information. Sharing critical yet unpleasant information in a culturally adapted way will prevent disruption of team harmony, but at the same time, the team performance will not be compromised because of the missing critical information.

However, such ongoing adaptations comprising the respond and reflect phases are the result of a GVT member’s sensitivity and strategizing capability determined by the GVT’s metacognitive CQ that is triggered during the reframe phase (Eisenberg and Mattarelli, 2017). Thus, GVT members’ ability to strategize and plan in a dynamic manner in a crosscultural context in the reframe phase will help them improvise and better align their own roles within the team in relation to the roles of other GVT members. This will enable situation-specific role adaptation to be enacted through iterative renegotiation until the required adaptation is achieved. Similar to the case of the previously discussed surface-level explicit behavior adaptation, the respond and reflect phases are closely tied to each other in the case of deep-level negotiated behavior adaptation as well. However, because of the implicit nature of informational inputs for such an adaptation, reflection on the ongoing interactions may be relatively more salient.

H3b: In the context of GVTs, CQ metacognition is positively associated with deep-level negotiated behavior adaptation (role structure adaptation).

## 3.5 Linking Surface-Level Explicit and Deep-Level Negotiated Adaptative Behaviors to GVT Creativity Performance

Although team members from different parts of the globe are expected to have diverse perspectives, it is challenging to integrate these myriad ideas for creative (novel and useful) outcomes (see Yong et al., 2014). In our study, we conceptualize surface-level adaptative behavior (CQ behavior) as the planned explicit behavioral coordination mechanism. While most of the communication in GVTs occurs via virtual media, technological advances have given these media a richer ability to display visual cues.

We posit that an explicit display of culturally adapted verbal and nonverbal behaviors will help in team coordination and knowledge integration. Prior literature has shown that CQ behavior affects performance outcomes (for a review see Ng et al., 2012). The team adaptation model recognizes explicit team coordination as an important mechanism leading to team performance outcomes (Burke, 2006; Christian et al., 2017). In dynamic cross-cultural environments, adaptation to changes in the team environment is considered critical for innovation performance (Rao & Pearce, 2016). The explicit display of culturally adapted scripted behaviors, both verbal and nonverbal, can help team members share knowledge and information, thus making cross-cultural team interactions smoother (Anawati & Craig, 2006). Clearly, better integration of different perspectives in a cross-cultural team because of a greater ability to enact scripted CQ behavior in the respond and reflect phases of team adaptation will result in greater team creativity.

H4a: In the context of GVTs, surface-level explicit adaptative behavior (CQ behavior) is positively associated with the team’s creativity performance.

Creative tasks require the integration of multiple perspectives. In the case of GVTs, which inherently comprise culturally diverse team members, such integration is a challenge for delivering creative outputs. Moreover, prior research has shown that multicultural teams have mixed performance outcomes. For example, Gajendran and Joshi (2012), Kankanhalli et al. (2006), and Kirkman et al. (2004) suggest positive team outcomes, while Richard et al. (2004) and Vangen and Winchester (2014) suggest that a multiplicity of cultural perspectives may have a negative influence on performance. Clearly, the integration of disparate information in multicultural teams is key but challenging.

In addition to being multicultural, GVTs are limited by the constraints arising from technological structures. These constraints exacerbate the problem of crosscultural coordination because the opportunities to develop team awareness through explicit team coordination mechanisms are rather limited (see Burke, 2006). As explained in the previous section, implicit or deep-level negotiated behaviors that are enacted dynamically by adjusting to the roles of fellow team members can be a viable team coordination and collaboration mechanism. Lepine (2003) describes such adaptations as recursive patterns of behavior enacted by the team members on a continual basis in relation to their role in the team. Such team adaptation mechanisms based on the implicit understanding of other team members’ roles in the team are expected to influence the desired creative outcomes by enabling an exchange of knowledge related to nonroutine tasks (Hutchins & Klausen, 1996; Lepine, 2003). We posit that in the context of GVTs, team members will undertake deep-level negotiated behavioral adaptation by improvising and aligning their workstyles to the emergent roles of their fellow team members. This deep-level negotiated adaptative behavior that occurs in the respond and reflect phases will facilitate smooth and effective interactions within the team, leading to effective knowledge coordination, which is expected to enhance creativity performance.

H4b: In the context of GVTs, deep-level negotiated adaptative behavior (role structure adaptation) is positively associated with the team’s creativity performance.

## 4 Method and Results

## 4.1 Research Approach and Design

Our research questions are driven by the emergent need for understanding cultural adaptation in GVTs, a specific phenomenon precipitated by advances in communication technologies. Phenomenon-based theorizing is specifically useful when investigating dynamic topics such as remote work (Fisher et al., 2021). According to Fisher et al. (2021, p. 631) “phenomenon-based theorizing starts with the identification of a new or undertheorized phenomenon that is then evaluated through existing theories, while also advancing one or more of those theories to account for the observed phenomenon.” Following this approach, we contextualize the face-to-face theory of CQ to the GVT context by leveraging the team adaptation literature.

Specifically, using a decomposition approach to context-based theorization (Hong et al., 2014), and grounding our arguments in Frick et al.’s (2018) team adaptation model, we theorized a nomological network of CQ-for-GVT. Our theorization approach allowed us to decompose CQ into its dimensions and incorporate the context-specific RSA dimension into the proposed model. Because we were theorizing a new CQ-for-GVT framework, the model needed to be assessed holistically. Accordingly, we first tested the formulated nomological net through a quantitative study designed in a natural academic setting. This was followed by a qualitative study to uncover the reasons for the unsupported hypotheses along with the boundary conditions to the theorized model. The appropriateness of our using a sequential mixed methods approach is thus justified by the objectives, questions, and the emerging context of our study (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2016).

After establishing the appropriateness of our methodological approach, we detailed the two studies (quantitative and qualitative) to first delineate inferences and then develop meta-inferences by integrating the results from the two studies. For our data analysis and reporting our results, we closely followed the guidelines suggested by Venkatesh et al. (2013). We described the quantitative and qualitative studies separately in terms of their design, analysis, and inferences. We also highlighted aspects related to the design quality and explanation quality of the quantitative and qualitative studies in terms of our design, analysis, and inferences. Together, the two studies helped us formulate a substantiative CQ theory for the GVT context (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

In the quantitative study, the respondents to our designed two-wave survey setting were members of multicultural GVTs that were tasked with completing creative projects through virtual interactions. This allowed us to empirically test the theorized CQ nomological network for GVT. However, to have confidence in a freshly theorized model, we needed robustness checks to corroborate and confirm the supported hypotheses and develop complementary insights about unsupported ones. Complementary insights gleaned from integrating the findings from the two sequential studies also helped us unearth the boundary conditions for the theorized “CQ-for-GVT framework” (Figure 1) (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

To develop meta-inferences in the form of boundary conditions, we used the outside-in approach described by Busse et al. (2017). We first identified the conditions under which the theorized framework is clearly not applicable and then went deeper into the analysis regarding the extent and direction in which the identified conditions influenced the theorized relationships to delineate an integrated CQ-for-GVT framework. A similar approach to boundary conditions has been used in prior management studies, such as Ketokivi and Jokinen (2006) and Eisenhardt and Martin (2000). This holistic approach helped substantiate the proposed CQ-for-GVT framework and also provides possible directions for future research (Venkatesh et al., 2013). Our mixed methods approach is similar to the approach followed by recent IS studies, such as Srivastava and Chandra (2018), Chandra et al., (2022) and Wunderlich et al., (2019). The strategy and process followed in our mixed methods approach is presented in Appendix B.

## 4.2 Quantitative Method: Survey Design and Design Validity

To test the research model in Figure 1, we used data from a two-wave survey of GVTs in conjunction with data from independent judges who rated the creativity performance of these GVTs. We first developed a survey instrument (with items on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree) by identifying and adapting appropriate measures from the existing literature, where psychometric properties have already been established. For the sources of the scales and the adapted items used for this study, please see Appendix C.

The survey respondents were participants with significant prior professional experience in ongoing academic coursework that was part of a joint academic program between two business schools, one in Europe and the other in India. Thus, the academic program was a natural setting that was used for fieldwork rather than artificially created GVTs. The participants from the two schools signed up for a graded virtual collaborative academic program in which the objective was to work on a joint business-related study project on a topic that had implications for both Europe and India. A total of 128 participants—64 from the European school and 64 from the Indian school—were selected to participate in this collaborative academic program based on their preferences and past academic performance. The participants were matched and grouped into 32 project teams based on their area of study interest and professional expertise, with each cross-cultural team comprising four members (two from the European school and two from the Indian school). Following the formation of the project teams, the team members interacted through virtual means (Skype, phone calls, and email) to finalize their respective project topics.

After the project topics were finalized, the European team members visited the Indian business school for one week, where, in addition to participating in other coursework, they had the opportunity to meet face-toface with their Indian team counterparts to initiate the work on their chosen study topic. After the weeklong course, the European and Indian team members worked together on their specific project for the next four months—interacting only virtually during the entire duration. Subsequently, the teams submitted their project reports to an evaluation team comprising five independent judges from diverse backgrounds who assessed the performance of each project team on the two creativity dimensions of novelty and usefulness (Yong et al., 2014). This approach to assessing creativity is similar to other studies that have used these two dimensions of creativity performance (Lee et al., 2015), in multicultural team settings (Salazar et al., 2017) and other IS domains (Medappa & Srivastava, 2020).

The research data were collected from each of the team members in two waves: the first at the beginning of the semester-long study project and the second at the end of the study project. The two data collection periods were separated by about four months (the duration of the project). The data on the CQ variables and demographics were collected during the first wave, and the data on the interactional and performance variables were collected during the second wave. The two-round data collection helped us separate the dispositional and capability-related variables (which were collected before the teams engaged in the actual task situation) from the interactional and final dependent variables, which are task contingent.

Following past research—for example, Adair et al. (2013) and Erez et al. (2013)—we aggregated our individual-level CQ variables (motivational, cognitive, metacognitive, and behavioral) at the team level. Specifically, we used Chan’s (1998) typology of compositional models, which suggests that additive aggregation (using the means of the individual-level variables) is appropriate when researchers are interested in an effect at the group level (Adair et al., 2013). Leveraging Chan’s additive compositional model approach, we posited that the levels of team members’ CQ dimensions would increase the aggregated corresponding CQ capabilities of the team and that an individual team member’s attribute could compensate for the lack of that attribute in others. That is, the teams can be characterized as having members with a certain set or combination of attributes and aggregation is necessary to account for those attributes. Our research is consistent with prior research that has used the additive model for aggregation of the attributes of individuals at the team level (Barrick et al., 1998; Taggar et al., 1999; Venkatesh & Windeler, 2012).

Although applying Chan’s model is sufficient, we also followed Bliese (2000) and computed intraclass correlations, namely, the ICC(2) coefficients, to determine the reliability of the team-level CQ dimensions. A similar approach was used by Adair et al. (2013). In particular, we estimated a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement and average measurement (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). The ICC(2) coefficient for motivational CQ was 0.79, for behavioral CQ it was 0.82, for metacognitive CQ it was 0.74, and for cognitive CQ it was 0.83. All of the ICC(2) values exceeded the recommended cut-off value of 0.70, signifying that the reliability of the teamlevel CQ means was adequate and that aggregation at the team level was therefore justified (Bliese, 2000; Klein & Kozlowski, 2000). The assessments provided by the five independent judges for each of the study project teams were aggregated and averaged to assess the final dependent variables of team creativity performance (novelty and usefulness). Such a research design with independent raters for assessing the dependent variables minimizes the possibility of common method bias (Chang, 2010).

In addition, because the dependent performance variables might be influenced by factors other than those in the hypothesized model, we incorporated suitable controls in the research model for team performance to better understand the variance explained by the research variables. Specifically, we included control variables of three different types to account for alternative explanations; these were (1) the extent of different types of interactions with the foreign team members: face-to-face, Skype, phone calls, and email; (2) project complexity; and (3) team member characteristics: average work experience and age.

Appendix D provides the demographics for the survey respondents. Among the 128 respondents, the average age was 29.98 years, with a standard deviation of 1.29 years, and the average work experience was 6.01 years, with a standard deviation of 1.37 years (see Appendix D). For the data analysis, we used partial least squares (PLS), a latent structural equation modeling technique that utilizes a component-based path modeling application (Ringle et al., 2005) and is considered appropriate for analyzing complex models with latent variables (Pavlou & Gefen, 2005). All constructs were modeled with reflective indicators. Many IS studies have employed PLS and found it to be an effective method for data analysis (e.g., Srivastava & Chandra, 2018; Sykes, 2015).

## 4.3 Measurement Model

We followed the recommended two-stage analytical procedure (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988; Hair et al., 1998). To assess the measurement model, we tested three types of validity: content validity, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. We ensured content validity by checking for consistency between the measurement items and the existing literature during the questionnaire design phase. Factor analysis showed that there is a strong correlation between each of the constructs and their corresponding items. This demonstrates convergent validity. We further tested convergent validity by examining the composite reliability (CR) and average variance extracted (AVE: the ratio of the construct variance to the total variance among indicators) for the indicators (Hair et al., 1998); both were found to be above the recommended thresholds (see Appendix D for more details). In addition, high Cronbach’s alpha values, ranging from 0.82 to 0.98, confirmed the reliability of the scales for all of the constructs.

We verified the discriminant validity of the various constructs by checking the square roots of the AVEs, as recommended by Fornell and Larcker (1981). The values of the square roots of the AVEs (shown on the diagonal in Appendix E) are all greater than the corresponding interconstruct correlations (the offdiagonal entries), exhibiting satisfactory discriminant validity. We also checked the cross-loadings of the items on other constructs; these were quite low and confirmed the indicated discriminant validity (Appendix F and Appendix G). We also found the observed statistical power for the model to be above the minimum recommended value of 0.80 (Cohen, 1988). Hence, despite the small sample size, we concluded that the model has sufficient statistical power for reliable results (Gefen et al., 2011).

## 4.5 Qualitative Method: Interview Design and Design Validity

The objectives of the qualitative study were twofold: first, to establish inferences from a rigorous analytical procedure, and second, to situate the inferences from the qualitative study within the findings from the quantitative study to derive meta-inferences. Subsequent sections delineate two kinds of metainferences: corroboration/confirmation metainferences, as well as complementarity metainferences (Venkatesh et al., 2013). However, we first needed to establish the rigor and validity of our qualitative study. For this, we performed design validity, analytical validity, and inferential validity checks (Venkatesh et al., 2013). Design validity concerns robust design and execution procedures to establish the credibility and transferability of results. We ensured design validity by maintaining rigor in selecting the interview participants in accordance with the established criteria and providing the respondents with the opportunity to communicate freely. The aim of the qualitative study was to examine the views of established GVT experts to understand the role of cultural differences in GVTs and how these differences can be managed to facilitate effective virtual team performance.

To ensure that we had an appropriate set of interview respondents, we employed a purposive sampling strategy, with the key criterion for the potential respondents being rich and substantial experience in managing GVTs. To identify potential respondents, we tapped into the alumni networks of our respective schools and our own personal networks. We also asked the identified GVT experts for referrals to expand our list of potential interviewees. Two of the authors individually screened the LinkedIn profiles of all the potential interviewees to ascertain their suitability for being classified as GVT experts. Through this rigorous process, we identified an initial list of 33 GVT experts as possible interview respondents. The shortlisted potential interviewees came from diverse work domains, and all had deep experience in managing GVTs (Patton, 2002; Trochim, 2005). Subsequently, we requested them to participate in individual semi-structured interviews so that we could obtain their views on the management of GVTs.

After conducting 10 detailed interviews, we determined that we had reached a saturation point in terms of the identified themes (as per our research objectives of confirming the hypotheses and identifying boundary conditions). Nonetheless, we interviewed seven additional respondents (for a total of 17 GVT experts; see Appendix H for details) to be sure that we really had attained saturation in terms of the findings and to obtain more details about the underlying mechanisms explaining the theorized model. All 17 GVT experts chose to be interviewed by phone. Although hypothesis testing was one of the objectives of the interviews, the respondents were asked in a general way about the strategies that GVT members employ to cope with cultural differences, as well as how the management of cultural challenges within a GVT influences the virtual team’s performance—more specifically, its creativity performance. The initial list of questions that we used for our semi-structured interviews is presented in Appendix I. We presented the questions in easily understood general terms, and we encouraged respondents to draw upon their past experiences and provide illustrative examples. In addition, we asked the interviewees several open-ended questions, with a view toward collecting additional insights, opinions, and limitations regarding the theorized research model. The interview protocol was designed to address the main concepts of CQ, the role structure adaptation, and the team’s creativity performance, i.e., the novelty and usefulness (creativity) of the team’s output in terms of process, product, or decision.

We modified questions that were posed to subsequent interviewees based on information revealed by the preceding participants regarding aspects that were relevant to the study. The respondents were also asked to identify the boundary conditions under which their enumerated strategies for working effectively in a multicultural GVT environment would no longer hold, and we asked them to describe the nature of the influence of the identified boundary conditions on team creativity performance. When the interviewees’ views were different from the theorized model or from the views of prior interview respondents, we also asked them several additional probing questions regarding their views. Such detailed data were useful not only in identifying the boundary conditions but also in assessing how these bounds influence the cultural adaptation process in GVTs.

All interviews were conducted in English, which was the usual workplace language for all respondents. The duration of the interviews ranged from 45 to 90 min. All interviews were audio recorded (with the respondents’ permission) and later transcribed for analysis. This resulted in about 350 pages of singlespaced transcribed interviews, which formed the primary data for our qualitative analysis. In addition, during the interviews, we also maintained analytical memos to record emerging themes that might need further consideration during the analysis stage.

## 4.6 Qualitative Study: Analytical Validity, Analysis, and Inferences and Meta-Inferences

Analytical validity is the theoretical validity of the credibility, plausibility, and trustworthiness of data and analysis procedures (Venkatesh et al., 2013). We maintained analytical validity by ensuring that our application of methods and our interpretation of the data were rigorous, that the data collection was of high quality, and that our data analysis and reporting were rigorous (Guba & Lincoln, 2005; Ridenour & Newman, 2008). After we collected and compiled the interview responses, two of the coauthors independently read all of the transcripts and initially coded all the data manually. Later, we also used Nvivo 11 pro to analyze the data, especially when we started to look for boundaries and their patterns of influence on the initially theorized model (Figure 1).

## 4.7 Corroboration and Confirmation: Meta-Inferences—Supported and Unsupported Hypotheses

The first objective of the qualitative study was to corroborate the model obtained from the quantitative study. To obtain such meta-inferences, we used the “bridging” approach, which is the process of strengthening the findings from a quantitative study through the results from a subsequent qualitative study (Lewis & Grimes, 1999; Venkatesh et al., 2013). Using a procedure similar to that employed in previous IS mixed method studies such as Srivastava and Chandra (2018) and Chandra et al., (2022), we tested the hypotheses using the qualitative data and situated the results within the findings of the quantitative study.

Because the respondents in both studies had professional experience, we considered them to be homogeneous to draw corroboratory conclusions. Prior studies have shown that executive student samples are similar to real-world managerial samples and can provide external validity (Loyd et al., 2005). Despite the apparent similarity in the two respondent samples in terms of professional experience, the task contexts for the two samples were different, which may also be the case when comparing one GVT to another in real life. In the context of our quantitative study, the respondents were asked questions based on their actual virtual team experience during the assigned virtual team project task as a part of their training program. The independent jury members explicitly rated the team projects on the creativity performance measures of novelty and usefulness. On the other hand, the GVT expert respondents in the qualitative study shared their insights based on their vast experience in handling myriad virtual team task contexts. Despite the differences in the actual tasks, all of the interviewed GVT experts reported that the tasks they had undertaken in GVTs were generally nonroutine in nature. Prior research has shown that the execution of nonroutine tasks involves creativity performance (Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014). The interviewed experts confirmed that although they did not have to explicitly report creativity performance indicators, most GVT tasks concerned creative team outputs related to the product, process, or decisions.

With this caveat about the difference in the nature of the creative GVT tasks, for corroboration, we based our analysis on an a priori coding scheme in which the data were coded into seven main categories representative of the hypotheses and control variables presented in the theoretical framework. Specifically, a hypothesis coding method was used, in which the most relevant and representative quotations from each respondent that offered evidence for each of the hypothesized relationships were identified (Bernard, 2006; Saldana, 2012). In particular, we used a simultaneous coding scheme so that the identified interview quotes could be classified into more than one category if required (Miles et al., 2014). The coding was done independently by two of the authors, with each relevant interview quote coded into one or more of the categories. Initial coding by the two coders was followed by discussion and consensus coding to confirm the matching of the transcribed quotes with derived interpretations about the relationships.

The final consensus coding was used to examine the hypotheses. For illustrative purposes, Appendix J presents sample quotes with initial and consensus coding. Based on this analysis, we found that the qualitative study supported H1, H2, H3a, H3b, and H4b. H4a was partially supported, as some experts did agree with the relevance of surface-level adaptative behavior for GVT cultural adaptation in certain contexts. Respondents also acknowledged the complementary influence of surface-level explicitly displayed adaptative behavior (CQ behavior) and deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior (role structure adaptation) in influencing the GVT’s creativity performance. ## 4.8 Complementarity: Meta-Inferences—Boundary Conditions and Propositions for Future Research

The second objective of the qualitative study was to complement the results from the quantitative study by using the “bracketing” technique to combine the findings from the two studies (Venkatesh et al., 2013). The metainferences, so obtained, explain the contradictions or boundaries of the findings from the quantitative study through an outside-in approach <sup>4</sup> (Busse et al., 2017). Specifically, we used qualitative data to first identify the exceptions or the boundaries to the theorized model and then unearth the nature of the mechanisms for these exceptions in relation to the leveraged team adaptation model. The understanding of the nature of these mechanisms leads us to five propositions that can be used as the point of departure for future research on cultural adaptation in GVTs.

First, we employed a structural coding method to search for themes that indicate the limits or boundaries of the theorized model (Macqueen & Guest, 2008). Such a question, based on an outside-in approach, is particularly suitable for gathering a list of explicit limits to the theorized cultural adaptation model (Busse et al., 2017; Saldana, 2012). Next, to identify the range of the identified limits—that is, the nature and mechanisms of the boundary condition, we used pattern coding to further delve into the delineated themes to identify repeated behaviors, actions, norms, routines, and relationships that might have local meanings (Miles et al., 2014). Such an approach outlines the explicit boundaries of the CQ-for-GVT model and also describes how these limits influence the model (i.e., which part of the theorized model is influenced by the identified boundaries). Through our analysis, we identified five boundary conditions of our theorized model. Discussion of each of the boundary conditions led to five broad propositions that may be fertile subjects for examination by future research. The results from this analysis are summarized in the form of a diagram in Figure 2, which extends the initially theorized model (Figure 1). The details regarding the validation of our meta-inferences are presented in Appendix K.

## 5 Delineating and Understanding Boundary Conditions

## 5.1 Boundary Condition #1: Team Member Fatigue

Cultural differences are often associated with physical and cognitive factors, which may influence GVTs’ adaptation and performance. Repeated demands for adjustments to differences in the cultural perceptions of time-zone variations, time and authority, and technological demands, are a few of the fatigue triggers encountered by GVT members. For example, for time-zone differences—what might be considered “late” in one culture may be viewed as “acceptable” by members of another culture. Similarly, in countries with a large power distance (respect for hierarchy), instructions from a superior will be given precedence, and a late-night meeting may be considered normal. In addition, cultural diversity fatigue is also possible when team members are made aware of the potential for cultural biases but are not provided with tools to translate this awareness into action (Livermore & Soon, 2016). Furthermore, GVT members are often members of multiple GVTs and the need to juggle multiple roles can be challenging. Our interview data revealed that the need for such culturally laden adaptations in GVTs can lead to team members becoming fatigued, which not only impairs their morale and motivation (Banks et al., 2019) but also their ability to plan and perform (Griffith et al., 2003; Jarvenpaa & Välikangas, 2020; Saunders et al., 2004).

I think one of the biggest challenges was time zones, trying to communicate to people that are 12 hours behind you, that’s really challenging, especially if you are part of multiple teams [time-zone-related fatigue<sup>5</sup>], right. (Expert B)

Like few [those identified by the managers to have culturally maladapted in the past] even tell me that “Oh, I am too tired today to work.” I mean, usually that is not something that you tell your manager. But it happens, this time she told me she was tired. I didn’t tell her “Hey, you are not supposed to behave like that.” I told her, “Okay, if you want you can be on leave today and then come in early tomorrow and then catch up.” She did that. She left early [took leave from virtual presence in meetings], she came the next day early and then she did twice the work that she was supposed to do. (Expert M)

Though team member fatigue seems to be an integral part of GVTs, which may limit the applicability of the theorized CQ-for-GVT model, no prior IS research has considered it explicitly in the context of GVTs’ cultural adaptation. We believe that this is an important subject for future GVT research and managerial interventions. Based on our findings, it is reasonable to posit that a fatigued team member may have an impaired capacity to plan and adapt to cross-cultural interactions, which leads us to our first proposition.

Proposition 1: In the GVT context, team member fatigue negatively moderates the relationships of CQ motivation and CQ knowledge (recognize phase) with CQ metacognition (reframe phase) in the cultural adaptative cycle of team adaptation.

## 5.2 Boundary Condition #2: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Task Motivation

In addition to CQ motivation, which is explicitly theorized in the proposed CQ-for-GVT framework, analysis of the interview data has revealed that the intrinsic task motivation of the team member contributes significantly to how the team member views and adapts to the encountered cultural differences. If team members find the task at hand to be inherently motivating, they will make concerted efforts to plan for the cultural challenge that is obstructing the task execution. Without sufficient intrinsic task motivation, team members would not devote the required resources for reframing their culturally discrepant situation. One GVT expert narrated the following incident, where the GVT member did not make an effort to plan for the crosscultural challenges because the intrinsic task motivation was missing.

An employee [remote] was working on a tedious test that was not interesting enough for him. So, he did not express about the coordination problems in the test with other team members [due to differences in cultures]. As time went by, the productivity went lower and lower. The manager was complaining, and after that, the employee just turned in his resignation. It was only in the exit interview that he mentioned “Yes, I found it [the task allocated] was something that I was not interested in,” and so on. (Expert L)

Prior research has found intrinsic task motivation to be key for creativity performance (Amabile, 2018) because it helps in resolving complexity in interdependent tasks (Hirst, 1988). Although creativity research has also shown that extrinsic motivation and excessive goal setting might hinder creativity (Amabile & Pillemer, 2012; Hirst, 1988), some of the interviewed respondents mentioned that extrinsic motivation in the form of explicit recognition or rewards are needed for motivating team members to undertake effective cross-cultural coordination.

Motivation is the key, and I would add to that, saying that there also needs to be an incentive. The incentive could be very simply to get a good review on a particular project or get a promotion of some sort or even some kind of cash benefit based on a good product launch. There could be myriad of incentives and more often than not, this aspect is not talked about or incorporated in crosscultural collaborations. (Expert J)

Lack of task-related motivation can also lead to lack of motivation to cope with cultural problem. (Expert C)

We also found that intrinsic and extrinsic task motivation is also often related to perceived significance of the task that the team members are undertaking in GVTs.

Engineers want to create stuff; they want to make things … Initially, they will do something and see if it is working. So, in my experience, people want to work together [despite being aware of cross-cultural challenges]. They want to build something. So, despite all the tensions and cultural differences and different ways of working, people will work differently, but they want to get together and get the thing working right [product design]. (Expert L)

Based on the above evidence and arguments we see that the lack of task motivation may limit the applicability of the theorized CQ-for-GVT—implying that despite sufficient CQ in the GVT, the team members may not work towards cultural adaptation if the task is not motivating enough. Hence, GVT managers need to orchestrate a proper balance of team members’ intrinsic and extrinsic incentives to motivate them to devote sufficient resources for planning and ironing out challenges due to cultural differences in GVTs. Task motivation thus needs to be considered explicitly by future research examining cultural adaptation in GVTs. Based on our analysis, we offer the following proposition:

Proposition 2: In the GVT context, team members’ intrinsic and extrinsic task motivations are positively related to CQ metacognition (reframe phase) in the cultural adaptative cycle of team adaptation.

## 5.3 Boundary Condition #3: Team Autonomy and Discretion

Team autonomy and discretion imply that the team has a certain degree of freedom in deciding and coordinating its resources. Such autonomy, empowerment, and behavioral discretion determine how the team functions as a unit (Burke et al., 2006). From our interview data, we noted that discretion about the availability and usage of resources such as people, time, technology, and money, is crucial to cultural adaptation in GVTs. For example, discretion to send a team member overseas for better coordination or training, when needed, is essential to GVTs’ success.

When there are lot of cultural differences, then that is a critical risk to the team, to the project, to the outcome. … But this might mean for them to allocate the budget to have team members travel around or it could mean to have some kind of a cultural ambassador so that they can ensure that the team collaborates in the right way … so, one needs to have resources to effectively cope with cultural issues, resources being people, time, and money. (Expert J)

Are you able to send your people to appropriate trainings or are you able to let people buy appropriate books, are you willing to fund international traveling to enable the team to actually work together properly? Are you willing to fund expatriate assignments, because sometimes crosspollination is extremely effective ... Granted that these things are expensive, but if the organization is committed and is willing to fund this, then it can make a massive difference. (Expert F)

Many of the respondents echoed the need to have the necessary autonomy and sufficient resources to install and implement appropriate communication technologies. Similarly, discretion to select the usage of specific technologies for different contexts can be an important factor for GVT adaptation in a cross-cultural scenario. Our interview data revealed that perceptions of technology are not always culture neutral. Though it is generally believed that a richer communication medium is better for resolving conflicts and improving team coordination through observable contextual cues, this may not always be true. Technology use in GVTs needs to be adapted to the specific cultural context to elicit the right kind of coordination from the diverse team members (Kankanhalli et al., 2006; Majchrzak et al., 2005; Wakefield et al., 2008). Knowledge about cultural preferences for technology use by different cultures is salient for team adaptation goals (Thomas & Bostrom, 2010). The context of the technology use should be situated in relation to the cultural expectation from the technological resources to ensure effective communication and coordination in GVTs.

The team performance is determined by respect, trust, communication. These are the three important words that I would say. The fourth one is technology. … But different cultures have more levels of affinity with different modes of communication. For example, if I am dealing with a person from Country X<sup>6</sup> and there is a language issue, I would always prefer to write an email and he would also prefer the same. Conference call will usually not work. If I am dealing with someone in Country Y, I would not write any email. Because individuals from Country Y by their very nature do not write what they think. That is a default characteristic which the individuals from Country Y have. We always have so many more things to write but we cannot write it. Write an email to any individual from Country Y, I would rather say I am generalizing—but write an email to an average individual from Country Y expecting him to respond to you. And then you call him up and ask him the same question. The response will be different. (Expert I)

In the context of GVTs, autonomy and discretion can relate to several aspects, for example, autonomy to decide on the common rules for team functioning, the use of resources, or managing the project scope and duration to be able to better adapt to cultural differences. If the GVT has the necessary autonomy and discretion, they plan their interactions and execute their plans more effectively. This finding from our data, identifying autonomy and discretion as antecedents to the GVT’s cultural adaptation is in line with recent work in IS that examines how selfmanaging agile teams use specific practices to effectuate adaptation leading to better team performance (Kude et al., 2019). It also contributes to answering calls for understanding the contextual aspects of team adaptation processes, especially with the growth and reliance on self-managing teams in organizations (Rico et al., 2020). Clearly, the extent of team autonomy and discretion may influence the applicability of the theorized CQ-for-GVT model for GVTs’ cultural adaptation. In a scenario of greater autonomy and discretion, the team members may be encouraged to plan better and also reframe their actions by undertaking creative workarounds to complement and supplement the roles of other team members. This leads us to our third proposition:

Proposition 3: In the GVT context, the team’s autonomy and discretion are positively related to CQ metacognition (reframe phase) and positively moderate the relation between CQ metacognition (reframe phase) and role structure adaptation (respond and reflect phases) in the cultural adaptative cycle of team adaptation.

## 5.4 Boundary Condition #4: Proactive Situational Team Leadership

Proactivity is defined as “taking initiative in improving current circumstances or creating new ones ... rather than passively adapting to present conditions” (Crant 2000, p. 436). “Proactivity” in team leadership is seen to motivate individual team members which positively impacts their overall performance (Chen & Kanfer, 2006; Crossley et al., 2013). However, prior IS research has also recognized the key role of team leaders to sense “situation-specific cues,” which are particularly salient for GVTs because team leaders need to preempt cultural conflicts on an ongoing basis to manage them effectively (Wakefield et al., 2008). Although proactive situational leadership is a commonly recognized virtue for all team leaders, its role is particularly salient in GVTs because of the emergent nature of the encountered cultural challenges. Because leaders have an overall picture of team members’ cultural capabilities, they can organize how team resources are leveraged for adapting to situational cultural challenges.

By default, because expectations [cultural] are usually up or down, understanding is usually up or down, role responsibility and the perception of yours and others are usually up or down. It is important to have a leader who takes that overall responsibility of looking at all the current dynamics. (Expert E)

The team leader also needs to serve as an advocate of cultural diversity within the team and explain how cultural diversity benefits team performance. Thus, a team leader simultaneously needs to be a goal driver, a goal generator, and a motivator. To this end, the team leader must be extra sensitive about the GVT’s functioning, take appropriate action in the case of a breakdown, and even proactively prevent potential issues from arising. The team leader should elicit regular feedback and should have the ability to preempt problematic areas. Our interviews revealed that incentivizing team members and reinforcing goals can be used as strategies by the team leaders to resolve cultural issues together.

I see how individuals from Country P and Country Q<sup>7</sup> operate, but if we all can have a common understanding and a goal that we are here for treating kids who have several diseases then we can overcome lot of this stuff related to cultural differences. I am responsible for how the team performs … and I am also the advocate of my culturally diverse employees within my team. (Expert D)

Team leader needs to keep everyone happy, because happy and motivated team members achieve business objectives. (Expert I)

A proactive and sensitive team leader will instill a sense of psychological safety through close and frequent interactions with team members. This will in turn enable team emotional authenticity (Connelly & Turel, 2016) and allow for better understanding of cultural differences within the team (Burke et al., 2006). The team leader can also exercise soft power and authority to enforce rules and behavior that can lead to better team coordination and collaboration. In contrast, a passive leader may give in to the constraints imposed by the severity of the cultural differences and may not be able to leverage the cultural diversity within the GVT (Tippmann et al., 2021). Hence, in the context of a GVT, we observe that a proactive situational leader has the ability not only to reinforce the use of the cultural resources that team members possess, but also to enable effective implementation of cross-cultural interactions in subsequent phase of the cultural adaptative cycle leading to better team outcomes. Hence, we propose:

Proposition 4: In the GVT context, proactive situational team leadership positively moderates the relationship between (a) CQ motivation (recognize phase) and CQ metacognition (reframe phase), (b) CQ metacognition (reframe phase) and role structure adaptation (respond and reflect phases), and (c) role structure adaptation (respond and reflect phases) and team creativity performance in the cultural adaptative cycle of team adaptation.

## 5.5 Boundary Condition #5: Engagement-Focused Diversity and Inclusiveness Climate

Recent literature views diversity and inclusiveness as a subset of organizational culture influencing cooperative behavior in teams and consequently their performance (Hajro, 2015; Mesmer-Magnus et al., 2018). Prior research has established that only engagement-focused diversity climates that use diversity to inform and enhance work processes benefit from diversity in teams (Hajro, 2015). Rather than just a policy mandate or an awareness about diversity, engaging proactively in leveraging diversity supports adaptative team processes, leading to greater knowledge exchange within multicultural teams (Hajro et al., 2017).

Our interview data revealed that an organizational culture that recognizes diversity and encourages employees’ engaged inclusion assists team members to not only cope but leverage cultural differences. A diversity-supportive and inclusive climate within an organization provides GVT members with an overarching framework to perform cooperatively and inclusively in a multicultural environment. This can limit the application of the theorized CQ-for-GVT model, which relies on the improvised team-level CQ dimensions. Prior research has shown that when distal contextual factors have a stronger influence, motivated behaviors are more homogenous than would be predicted based on motivational capabilities such as CQ motivation (Meyer et al., 2009). For example, organizations that proactively encourage cross-cultural interactions among their employees through short- or long-term overseas assignments and those that cherish cross-cultural talents for diversity foster better creativity performance in cross-cultural GVTs. Employees in such organizations are not “taken by surprise” when they encounter different cultures in GVTs. This kind of top-down influence that supports diversity and an inclusive climate fosters integration and learning from diverse resources. This is a strong situational context that can overshadow a team’s CQ capabilities in the cultural adaptation cycle. Highlighting this overriding influence of an organizational culture of engagement-focused diversity and inclusion on cultural adaptation, interviewees remarked:

We also send employees to other places so that they learn how other people work. And they are more flexible in adopting or understanding the way the other people work, so I think when you start having multicultural teams and when you start moving people around the world and let them learn about other cultures, they become more flexible, more understanding and more embracing. (Expert D)

The organizational culture is driven by a set of leadership principles … every single person in the company has to abide by the corporate leadership principles and keep them in their minds when working in crosscultural teams … they know exactly what to do when working globally and this is what they need to do on a day-to-day basis. (Expert N)

In the case of GVTs, we uncovered that strong organizational support for engagement-focused diversity and inclusiveness is a desirable contextual situational strength that reassures GVT members by providing them with a broader and legitimate framework for coping and leveraging cultural differences (see Meyer et al., 2009). This implies that the presence of a strong diversity-supportive and inclusive climate will subsume and/or replace teamlevel cultural adaptation processes. Hence, we propose:

Proposition 5: In the GVT context, an organizational climate of engagement-focused diversity and inclusiveness will have a positive influence on the team’s creativity performance and will substitute for all team-level cultural adaptation processes. This substitution might result in engagementfocused diversity and inclusiveness that negatively moderates all the relationships in the cultural adaptative cycle of team adaptation.

## 6 Limitations and Future Research Directions

In the current study, we theorize for global teams that primarily use a virtual medium for communication. Though in our quantitative study, we controlled for the extent of face-to-face interaction, it is plausible that teams that interact “exclusively” through the virtual media have different team dynamics. Similarly, while we controlled for “technology usage” to some extent, it would be interesting to test the results in light of emerging technologies such as telepresence, the metaverse, or other high-end real-time collaboration tools that promise to provide a richer medium of communication.

Further, we did not consider the maturity of the virtual teams in our research model, which can also be a factor that influences team adaptation and creativity performance. It could be interesting to see whether the results of this study can be extended to teams that are “completely” virtual or those that are considered to be a durable global virtual team. But such future investigation would demonstrate if a change in context requires rethinking the proposed CQ-for-GVT framework. It may be worthwhile to extend the quantitative study to test the proposed model using other measures for team performance, including wellbeing-related measures, to account for other humanistic implications.

In addition, future research could also leverage alternative theoretical lenses to conceptualize and test the mediating and moderating roles of “CQ behavior” that could possibly help explain its nonsignificant impact on performance outcomes as observed in the current quantitative study. Other intuitive relationships in the model, such as the relationships of CQ cognition with CQ behavior and role structure adaptation, could be further explored by future research. Moreover, using learning theories as a base, particularly the concept of experiential learning (Ng et al., 2009), future research could attempt to understand the developmental aspects of CQ, as well as its impact on learning goals. Control and coordination theory and stress and coping theory could be used as alternative theoretical lenses to study cultural adaptation in GVTs (Gassmann & von Zedtwitz 2003; Shirish, 2019). Looking at the influence of CQ training on future cultural adaptation or maladaptation could be a great applied-research question to tackle. As an avenue for future research, it would also be interesting to test the proposed model for different temporal stages of GVT functioning. It is plausible that due to the passage of time, the team will have diminished perceptions of cultural challenges, thereby requiring fewer behavioral efforts to adapt to cultural cues. This could also be due to intermediary variables like team cohesion and trust, or it may well be that there is an enhancement of teamlevel CQ dimensions due to the experience of working with each other.

The current study reiterates the assumption that cultural challenges exist in GVTs and are indeed seen as surmountable barriers in the presence of appropriate CQ capabilities. The study identifies five boundary conditions that can limit the applicability of the theorized model. It would be interesting to conduct empirical research incorporating the role of the five identified boundary conditions. Specifically, the five delineated propositions could serve as a point of departure for future GVT research.

## 7 Implications

## 7.1 Implications for Research

First, despite several initiatives by big technology giants such as Yahoo and IBM to restrict remote work (Johnson, 2020), an increasing number of organizations are switching to an online mode of collaboration (Nordbäck & Espinosa, 2019; Taras, 2020). Especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, switching to virtual work had become a necessity for most organizations. This switch has also precipitated the many challenges of virtual collaboration (Harrist, 2020). The multicultural nature of global teams, coupled with the complex and nonroutine nature of the collaborative tasks that GVTs generally undertake, will further increase collaborative challenges (Asatiani et al., 2021; Chamakiotis et al., 2020; Malhotra & Majchrzak, 2014). Although cultural diversity in global teams is one of the key contributors to creativity (Gilson et al., 2015; Hinds et al., 2011; Mockaitis et al., 2018), this diversity could also be an impediment to efficient coordination and knowledge exchange.

CQ has been recognized as a plausible modality for facilitating cultural adaptations, which is key for collaborative creativity. However, CQ has primarily been examined in the face-to-face context (Adair et al., 2013; Bogilović et al., 2017; Yari et al., 2020). In this research, we build on the contextual nuances of GVTs to theorize how the process of social ordering occurs in dynamic, temporary, nonroutine, and culturally diverse settings to influence team creativity performance (Chamakiotis et al., 2020; Tippmann et al., 2021). To contextualize CQ for the GVT context, we ground our study in the team adaptation model (Frick et al., 2018), which offers a multilevel, multiphasic, and linear framework for team adaptation and very closely parallels different CQ dimensions. The theorized “CQ-for-GVT framework,” comprising a nomological net of CQ dimensions, offers an actionable theoretical framework for understanding and facilitating GVT creativity performance. Prior research has either examined CQ dimensions in an aggregated fashion or has studied the influence of individual CQ dimensions. Through our research, we answer the calls for developing the dimensionality view of the CQ construct (Malik et al., 2014; Ng et al., 2012; Van Dyne et al., 2012) by leveraging the team adaptation model (Frick et al. 2018) to theorize the nomological network linking the different dimensions of CQ with creativity performance. This, we believe, is a significant contribution to the CQ, cultural adaptation, and team adaptation literature. Future research could also theorize the role of CQ in effecting cultural adaptation by leveraging alternate theoretical lenses.

Second, by motivating our work through phenomenonbased theorization (Fisher et al., 2021) and leveraging Hong et al.’s (2014) decomposition approach to context-specific theorization, we decompose the action dimension of the original CQ framework to introduce the notions of surface-level explicitly displayed adaptative behavior and deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior for cultural adaptation in GVTs. Because prior research on CQ has primarily focused on the face-to-face context, the action dimension has comprised only surface-level explicit adaptative behavior, represented by the CQ behavior dimension. However, in the context of GVTs, which are generally entrusted with nonroutine complex tasks, there is a need for deeper interaction in which team members can dynamically undertake role taking and role giving through deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior. In our CQ-for-GVT framework, we conceptualize this deep-level negotiated component of the action dimension as role structure adaptation (RSA) (Lepine, 2003). Through our research, we establish the greater significance of deeplevel implicitly negotiated adaptative behavior (RSA) in the GVT context, compared to surface-level explicitly displayed adaptative behavior (the CQ behavior dimension). This contextualized extension of the CQ framework for the GVT context not only aligns well with the dimensionality view of the CQ construct but also answers the call for context-specific theorization in IS and management research (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Bamberger, 2008). We assume that the main reason for not including deep-level negotiated behavior in the original CQ framework was that CQ was originally conceptualized for face-to-face interactions and not virtual interactions. The contextualized extension of the CQ framework for the GVT context, which can account for dynamic negotiated role-taking and role-giving behaviors necessary for the exchange of complex nonroutine knowledge among GVT members (Eisenberg & Mattarelli, 2017), contributes to both the GVT and the team adaptation literature.

Third, our study contributes to research on IS and culture by examining cultural enactments that incorporate both a static and a dynamic perspective on cultural adaptation. The cultural adaptation in GVTs is closely intertwined with the situating technological structures that enable virtual communication (Sarker & Sahay, 2003). We expand the realm of research on GVT adaptation by shifting the focus from planned procedural ordering through technological adaptation to emergent social ordering via cultural adaptation (Asatiani et al., 2021; Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009). Our study provides evidence that supports the concept of role negotiation as an important social ordering mechanism for dynamically changing temporary settings such as GVTs (Bechky, 2006). The deep-level implicitly negotiated behavior measured as the “role structure adaptation” of teams can also be viewed as in situ intercultural competence that may be specific to the GVT context. This competence can be either identified in individuals at the team selection stage or fostered in GVT members through proper training. Through our work, we also respond to recent calls to identify additional intercultural competences that can influence job performance outcomes in organizations (Leung et al., 2014). We also provide evidence for the salience of role negotiation in bridging the social distance between team members (Bechky, 2006; Levina & Vaast, 2005).

In addition, our study contributes to the sparse literature that empirically examines creativity performance as an important GVT outcome (Cui et al., 2020; Dubé & Robey, 2009; Taras, 2020). Specifically, we show the relevance of CQ motivation and CQ cognition as the primary inputs for successful cultural adaptation and conceptualize the roles of CQ metacognition, CQ behavior (surface-level explicit adaptative behaviors), and role structure adaptation (deep-level implicitly negotiated adaptative behaviors) as the secondary variables that comprise cultural adaptation in GVTs. The theorized CQ-for-GVT framework offers a nuanced understanding of the subject and can be a point of departure for future GVT adaptation research.

Fourth, through the follow-up qualitative study, we identify and describe five boundary conditions that can limit the applicability of the theorized CQ-for-GVT framework: (1) team member fatigue, (2) intrinsic and extrinsic task motivation, (3) team autonomy and discretion, (4) proactive situational team leadership, and (5) engagement focused diversity and inclusion climate. Further, through identified patterns in relation to the five boundary conditions, we offer five propositions that describe the influence of the identified boundary conditions on the different relationships within the theorized CQ-for-GVT framework. These propositions provide a nuanced extension to the proposed CQ-for-GVT framework and could be refined and empirically tested by future research. For example, further research could explore the mediating role of deep-level negotiated behavior between an engagement-focused diversity climate and knowledge exchange in GVTs (Hajro, 2015), as this could further enhance our understanding of cultural adaptation and team outcomes in GVTs.

## 7.2 Implications for Practice

First, although most prior team adaptation research directs GVT practitioners to focus on adapting to communication tools and technologies, the results of our study underscore the need to acknowledge cultural differences among team members as a significant challenge that requires proactive adaptation. The conditions for cultural adaptation arise when a dissonant cue can be attributed as arising partly or wholly from the cultural differences among team members. Such cues are influenced by the fact that communication within GVTs is mediated through technologies. Moreover, the presence of mediating technologies also distinguishes our research from prior cultural adaptation and CQ research set in the face-toface context. Our research offers practitioners an integrated view of cultural and technical issues, both of which are pertinent in the context of GVTs.

Second, our study proposes a CQ-for-GVT framework that links different CQ dimensions and role structure adaptation with team creativity. GVT managers can leverage this framework to plan their specific strategies for bridging cultural differences to facilitate better team performance. The study introduces managers to the idea of cultural differences as potential challenges for GVT members that can disrupt smooth working. It also explains the modalities through which these cultural differences can be better managed for effective GVT performance. In addition, the study provides guidance for GVT leaders regarding the salience of dynamically improvised “deep-level implicitly negotiated behavior” (role structure adaptation) for handling cultural differences to facilitate better team performance.

Third, CQ is a malleable construct that can help bridge cultural differences. Hence, performance-driven human resource interventions such as the provision of incentives and use of CQ measurement and training in GVT projects could be planned. Depending on the specific requirements, such interventions can be planned either prior to GVT project implementation or during the ongoing stages of GVT projects. Companies involved in training their teams for multicultural cultural competence should rely not only on training individuals to have knowledge about different “others,” but should also encourage individuals examination of their own values, beliefs, and biases in order to create awareness about their own social cultural identities (Chao et al., 2011). Using CQ training programs caters to both of these objectives and can be one of the effective ways to improve the cultural competence of technology-mediated GVTs (Leung et al., 2014). Similar to prior studies, our qualitative research also reveals the salience of individualism/collectivism and power distance perceptions among GVT members as major cultural bottlenecks in GVT adaptation. These can be proactively addressed by devising contextualized training programs for GVTs that incorporate such culture-specific adaptation variables (Lacerenza et al., 2018). Given that multicultural GVTs are generally tasked with complex nonroutine tasks, it is essential to incorporate a mechanism that can facilitate coordination and collaboration in a dynamic negotiated manner, with roles in the team being implicitly and continuously negotiated for efficient performance.

Fourth, by using a qualitative method in conjunction with a quantitative method, the present study corroborates the quantitative inferences through triangulation and identifies contradictions between the results obtained from the two methods. These incongruities provide complementary insights and are helpful for identifying the boundary conditions limiting the applicability of the results from the quantitative study. To successfully use CQ capabilities in the GVT context, managers should not only look at the contextualized CQ nomological network but also carefully assess and address the five boundary conditions. Among these conditions, proactive situational team leadership and an engagementfocused diversity and inclusion climate likely need special attention because their influence on GVT cultural adaptation appears to be highly significant. Because most decisions (even in ICT-mediated virtual teams) are top-down, much depends on the leader’s and the organization’s vision regarding the “culture” for the virtual team. This underscores the salient role of the organization and the GVT leader in handling cultural differences. In addition, the motivation of team members regarding the task and the team’s autonomy in managing itself can be important factors for the success of ICT-mediated GVTs. The propositions inspired by each of the boundary conditions offer additional insights for GVT managers on how to orchestrate better GVT coordination and collaboration. These conditions need to be appropriately considered along with the theorized CQ nomological network to facilitate effective GVT performance.

## 8 Conclusion

GVTs are generally tasked with nonroutine, complex, and creative tasks. The inherent cultural diversity within such teams is ideal for facilitating creative outcomes. However, the multiplicity of cultural perspectives poses significant coordination and communication challenges that may prevent effective knowledge exchange and integration among GVT members. This can be an impediment to their creativity performance. We posit that cultural adaptation within GVTs is a plausible solution to overcome the challenges related to cultural differences amongst GVT members. Leveraging CQ theory (Ang et al., 2006) and adopting a decomposition approach to context-specific theorization (Hong et al., 2014) in conjunction with Frick et al.’s (2018) team adaptation model, we build a nomological network describing the CQ-for-GVT framework. Specifically, we theorize the role of different CQ dimensions along with an additional contextual variable—role structure adaptation (RSA)—to understand and inform the cultural adaptation process in GVTs.

We tested a theoretically proposed CQ-for-GVT nomological network through a sequential mixed methods approach comprising a quantitative study (two-wave survey) followed by a qualitative study (expert interviews) (Venkatesh et al., 2013). The results allow us to understand how the different dimensions of CQ individually act as proximal factors that are linearly (yet cyclically) leveraged throughout the different team adaptation phases (recognize, reframe, respond, and reflect) leading to a positive impact on the creativity performance of GVTs. However, we note that the surface-level explicit CQ behavioral dimension is not sufficient to facilitate creativity outcomes in GVTs; the implicitly negotiated RSA within the GVT members is key for enabling creativity in GVTs. In addition, our study identifies five boundary conditions that may limit the applicability of the proposed CQ-for-GVT framework along with five propositions that can be instrumental in encouraging future research in this important stream of research. Our study is but one modest step in that direction.

## About the Authors

Anuragini Shirish is an associate professor at Institut Mines-Télécom Business School (IMT-BS), France. She is an elected member from her institution for the governance of the LITEM (Laboratoire Innovation Technologies Économie et Management), a joint research laboratory under the University of Paris-Saclay, France. Her research focuses on studying the humanistic and instrumental impacts of several sociotechnical phenomena in the broad areas of digital work, digital innovation, and digital society. Her research has been published in international refereed journals including the Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Communications of the Association of the Information Systems and International Journal of Information and Management, among others She has also presented her work in several premier IS and management conferences including the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), the Academy of Management (AOM), Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems (PACIS), and the Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS), among others. She currently serves as an associate editor for the European Journal of Information Systems. She has been honored with several awards including the “Outstanding Educator Award” by the Association for Information Systems (AIS) women’s network.

Shirish C. Srivastava is a professor and the GS1 France Chair at HEC Paris. Prior to joining HEC, he lectured at the National University of Singapore. His research interests include technology-enabled innovation, artificial intelligence, open source, social media strategy, e-government, and services sourcing. His research has been published in several top-tier journals including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, and MIS Quarterly Executive, among others. He has also won multiple awards, including awards for best papers at different forums such as the Academy of Management, International Conference on Information Systems, Association for Computing Machinery, and Society for Information Management. He is a four-time winner of the Prix Académique de la Recherche en Management, 2013, 2015, 2016, and 2022 in Paris, France. He currently serves as the senior editor at the Journal of the Association for Information Systems and European Journal of Information Systems. He is also on the editorial board of Journal of Management Information Systems and an associate editor at the Information Systems Research. In addition to research, his rich experience includes coaching senior executives on issues related to managing technology, innovation, entrepreneurship, and cross-border business relationships.
