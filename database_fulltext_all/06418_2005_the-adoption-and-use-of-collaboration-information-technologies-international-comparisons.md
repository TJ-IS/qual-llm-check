# The adoption and use of collaboration information technologies: international comparisons

## Abstract

Information technology (IT) applications to support group decision processes have been of considerable interest over the years. With the emergence of virtual team arrangements and the advent of emerging information and networking technologies, an increasing amount of attention is now being aimed at understanding collaboration among group members, as they make decisions to accomplish tasks. Effective and efficient collaboration is critical from a decision quality and decision timeliness standpoint. Commonly known as collaborative information technologies (CITs), many technology solutions have the capability to enhance collaboration and facilitate group decisions in task accomplishment by enabling better communication, sharing of information, ideas, expertise, and evaluating alternatives, irrespective of time and distance barriers. Many studies have investigated such individual CIT solutions in different regional settings. However, despite the fact that no single medium can support collaboration in different types of tasks, there is a scarcity of research investigating the adoption and use of multiple CIT options across regions. This paper builds upon innovation diffusion theory and tests a research model to validate five antecedents of collective adoption and use of seven CITs in the US, Australia, and Hong Kong. Sub-sample analyses of data collected from 344 organizations in these three regions suggest that not only do adoption and use patterns of some CIT solutions vary across regions but so do the antecedents that explain their proliferation. Implications of our findings are discussed for practitioners and researchers. Journal of Information Technology (2005) 20, 130–140. doi:10.1057/palgrave.jit.2000037 Published online 5 April 2005

Keywords: collaboration; collaborative information technologies; CITs; information technology adoption; information technology use; group work

## Introduction

majority of the work in modern organizations is accomplished by groups. Unlike individual decisionmaking, group decisions require coordination between several members who may be engaged in accomplishing tasks. As a result, collaboration among work group members becomes critical when decisions have to be made by several stakeholders in completing tasks. With the emergence of virtual team arrangements, collaboration can be even more challenging, especially when such efforts require asynchronous and/or synchronous mode(s) for communications to exchange data, information, and expertise among team members who are further separated by time and distance barriers. Fortunately, many information and networking technology solutions have the capability to significantly support collaborative efforts spanning all types of tasks, modes, and time/distance scenarios.

Industry practice suggests that many modern organizations are deploying information technology (IT) solutions to support collaboration in virtual arrangements (Debra and Snyder, 1999). Researchers too have conducted many large-scale empirical studies exploring Collaborative information technologies (CITs). However, for the most part, the focus of these investigations has been on a single CIT solution, despite the fact that the business press has emphasized using a variety of tools to collaborate (DeKoven, 2000). Some recent case studies also support the notion that ‘a mix of asynchronous and synchronous tools is vital to team success’ in collaboration (DeSanctis et al., 2001). Still, collective adoption and use of multiple CIT solutions remains largely unexplored at the macrolevel.

This paper attempts to fill that void by focusing on the patterns of organizational adoption and use of CITs in organizations in the US, Australia, and Hong Kong. The CITs investigated in this study include: stand-alone e-mail systems, audio teleconferencing, videoconferencing, dataconferencing, web-based tools, proprietary groupware, and electronic meetings systems (EMS). Although some of these can be considered emerging as they have been recently developed, others have been around for some time and have been the focus of investigations by many researchers (e.g., e-mail (Kettinger and Grover, 1997), web groupware (Dennis et al., 1998), proprietary groupware (Slyke et al., 2002), and EMS (Straub and Beauclair, 1988; Lewis et al., 2000).

Given that researchers have long recognized that alternative communication channels for group collaboration are important and required (Turoff et al., 1993), research is needed to explore adoption and use patterns across multiple CITs to inform practice as well as research and development efforts. The motivation to undertake such research also stems from the fact that understanding CIT support for task collaboration can significantly influence the quality and timeliness of group decision processes, especially in emerging virtual arrangements in modern organizations. This study uses innovation theory to specifically address the following research questions:

\- What is the pattern of adoption and use of CITs in the US, Australia, and Hong Kong?

\- What are some of the key antecedents of the adoption and use of CITs in the three regions?

\- Do the antecedents of CIT adoption and use vary across regions?

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section provides a brief background and the theoretical premise for our study. We then present our research model and describe the empirical study methodology. This is followed by analyses of data. In the last section, we discuss our results, address the limitations of our study, and present the implications for practice and future research.

## Study background and theoretical foundations

A review of the literature reveals several CITs that have been deployed. Among these, seven CITs have perhaps received the most widespread attention. These include: email (one-to-one and one-to-many electronic communication) (Kettinger and Grover, 1997; Lee, 1992; Markus, 1994; Ngwenyama and Lee, 1997), teleconferencing (two-way audio communication) (Carlson and Davis, 1998; Graetz et al., 1998), videoconferencing (two-way audio and video) (Dustdar and Hofstede, 1999; Goodman and Darr, 1998;

Webster, 1998), dataconferencing (whiteboards, application sharing, and data presentations) (Majchrzak et al., 2000; Malhotra et al., 2001), web-based collaborative tools (intranets, listservs, newsgroups, chat, message boards) (Majchrzak et al., 2000; Piccoli et al. 2001), proprietary groupware tools (with or without Web browser interface) (Press, 1992; Goodman and Darr, 1998; Slyke et al., 2002; also see Kersten (1999) for a review of case studies using Lotus Notes), and electronic meeting systems (electronic tools for structuring meetings in a face-to-face, usually networked environment) (Straub and Beauclair, 1988; George et al., 1992; Dennis et al., 2001).

A number of competing theories have been applied to study the adoption of CIT in organizations. These include: Information Richness Theory (Daft and Lengel, 1986), Theory of Communicative Action (Habermas, 1984), Social Interaction Theory (Carlson and Davis, 1998), Structuration Theory (Giddens, 1990), and Innovation Diffusion Theory (Rogers, 1983a, b). Among these, innovation diffusion theory has perhaps been the most widely used theoretical basis to explore adoption of IT innovations (Fichman, 1992).

In general, innovation can be defined as the adoption of an internally generated or purchased device, system, policy, program, process, product, or service that is new to the adopting organization (Daft, 1982). Innovation is often viewed as a process of initiation, adoption, and implementation (Rogers, 1983a, b). Initiation is a stage that focuses on the awareness for an upcoming change either due to external or internal pressures. This leads to the adoption stage, which usually involves commitment of resources to the innovation under consideration. Finally, implementation includes deployment activities. The culminating activity in this last stage is the use of innovation so that the benefits from the innovation can be realized. The focus of our study here is on the adoption (acquisition) and use (deployment) of CITs. Therefore, adoption is viewed as a stage when an organization has access to the CIT under investigation, that is, the decision has been made and executed to invest the required resources and the CIT is available to organizational end-users. Use is viewed as the frequency or level of utilization of the CIT under investigation.

## Research model

As previously stated, innovation can be generally viewed as a process that includes: initiation, adoption, and implementation (Rogers, 1983a, b). Many antecedents of IT innovations have been proposed by researchers, and many of these have been investigated. In their synthesis of the innovation literature, Kwon and Zmud (1987) identify five variable categories. These include: environmental factors, organizational structural factors, innovation-related attributes, task-related factors, and individual factors. In addition, technical attributes of the organization may also influence innovative behaviour (Dewar and Dutton, 1986). In this study, we focus on organizational structural factors and IT structural attributes to investigate adoption and use of CITs.

Organization size has perhaps been the most widely investigated antecedent of innovative behaviour. Although it has been convincingly argued that resource-rich organizations are most likely to afford the cost of innovations (Dewar and Dutton, 1986), the results from many IT innovation studies have been somewhat inconclusive. Researchers have found both a positive relation between organization size and technology adoption (Kimberley and Evanisko, 1981; Straub and Beauclair, 1988; Yoa et al., 2003), and no significant relationships between organization size and IT innovation behaviour (Grover and Goslar, 1993; Rai and Bajwa, 1997; Lai, 2001). In the context of CITs, recent empirical evidence suggests that size (organization and IT function) may not be a significant predictor of adoption of some inexpensive CITs like e-mail, teleconferencing, and Web-based tools (Bajwa and Lewis, 2003).

Similarly, researchers have also found that chances of IT success are greater in larger firms than their smaller counterparts (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978). However, Raymond (1985) found no relationship between firm size and IT use. It is likely that larger organizations may use CITs to a greater extent than smaller organizations since they are usually more geographically dispersed, deal with more complex tasks, and require greater coordination to conduct business. The above arguments support the hypotheses that:

H1a: Organization size will be positively related to adoption of CITs.

H1b: Organization size will be positively related to use of CITs.

H2a: IT function size will be positively related to adoption of CITs.

H2b: IT function size will be positively related to use of CITs.

The degree of centralization (or concentration of decisionmaking activity) is also likely to influence IT adoption behaviour. Synthesizing the past literature on innovation behaviour, Grover et al. (1999) argue that organic organizations characterized by decentralized structures and less formalization are likely to be more innovative than mechanistic organizations that are highly centralized and use formalized controls. Given these properties of organic and mechanistic organizations, the arguments support the hypotheses that:

H3a: Centralization of decision-making will be negatively related to adoption of CITs.

H3b: Centralization of decision-making will be negatively related to use of CITs.

Once again, a synthesis of the dichotomous arguments of organic vs mechanistic organizations also suggests that organic structures, although decentralized, need to be integrated cross-functionally and therefore use more extensive communication to coordinate activities than their mechanistic counterparts that are largely hierarchies characterized by bureaucracy with little integration between business functions (Grover et al., 1999). These arguments support the hypotheses that:

H4a: The degree of integration is positively related to adoption of CITs.

H4b: The degree of integration is positively related to use of CITs.

Finally, IT infrastructure or the extent of networking in the organization may influence innovation behaviour, especially in the context of CITs. Past research indicates that not only is superior IT infrastructure critical from organizational performance standpoint (Bharadwaj, 2000), it is absolutely essential to ‘eliminate barriers to communication that naturally occur between different parts of the organization’ for effective collaboration in knowledge management (Gold et al., 2001). Without a sound IT infrastructure, collaboration efforts between different entities can easily bog down, resulting in inefficient and ineffective communication and sharing of information and expertise. In summary, IT infrastructure that facilitates communication can greatly enhance collaboration (Grover et al., 1999). From these arguments, it follows that:

H5a: IT Infrastructure connectivity is positively related to adoption of CITs.

H5b: IT infrastructure connectivity is positively related to use of CITs.

## Empirical study methodology

A research project was initiated to explore the adoption and implementation of several information technologies to support task-oriented collaboration in US organizations. Based upon a review of the past literature, seven different CIT clusters that have the capability to support collaboration among work groups were identified. Examples of specific products for each of the seven CITs were also identified to guide responses during data collection. These included:

\- E-mail, for example, Pegasus mail, Microsoft Outlook, Hotmail, etc.

\- Teleconferencing (two-way audio), for example, NetMeeting, CU-SeeMe, etc.

\- Videoconferencing (two-way audio and video), for example, NetMeeting, CU-SeeMe, etc.

\- Dataconferencing (whiteboards, application sharing, data presentations), for example, NetMeeting, Evoke, WebEx, etc.

\- Web-based Collaborative Tools (Intranets, Listservs, Newsgroups, chat, message boards), for example, E-groups, Yahoo Groups, Open Topics, etc.

\- Proprietary Groupware Tools (with or without web browser interface), for example, Lotus Notes, IBM

Workgroup, ICL TeamWARE Office, Novell Groupwise, The Groove, etc.

\- Electronic Meeting Systems, for example, GroupSystems, MeetingWorks, TeamFocus, VisionQuest, Facilitate.com, etc.

## Measures

A formal process for instrument development was undertaken along the guidelines proposed by Sethi and King (1991). To ensure valid and reliable measures, a three-phase instrument development process was initiated. In the first phase, an extensive review of the literature was conducted. Wherever possible, previously validated item-measures were adopted. In the second phase, the formulated itemmeasures were then reviewed by a team of four researchers who have been active in conducting research on workgroup collaboration for over two decades. In these reviews, discussions were initiated to examine the clarity of the item-measures and whether the item-measures appropriately measured the study variables. The complete instrument was a result of several discussion sessions between these researchers. In the final phase, the instrument was then examined by two expert practitioners (an executive director of the Society for Information Management (SIM) and a past Chief Information Officer (CIO) of a Fortune 500 organization). Both the participants were asked to comment on the clarity of the item-measures and appropriateness of the CITs to support task-oriented collaboration. Based upon their feedback, we made minor modifications. These modifications primarily consisted of adding products names that were more popular among the practitioner community. The following measures were deployed to operationalize the variables of our research model.

Adoption for each CIT cluster was measured by requiring respondents to indicate whether the specific CIT cluster was ‘accessible and available’ to end-users in their organization. A five-point scale semantically anchored at the extremes and mid-way (1 ¼ no one in the organization, 3 ¼ some persons in the organization, and 5 ¼ everyone in the organization) was used for each of the seven CIT clusters. Although this measure includes adoption status and level of adoption, a binary measure of adoption (scale score of 1 as ‘non-adopter’ and scores between 2 and 5 as ‘adopters’) was used for analyses involving adoption status in this paper. This approach has been popular in measuring adoption status in IT innovation research (Rai and Bajwa, 1997). Adoption was computed by simply counting the number of CITs adopted.

Use of CIT was measured by a five-point scale semantically anchored at the extremes and mid-way (1 ¼ never, 3 ¼ occasionally, 5 ¼ always). This was deployed for each of the seven CITs. The use of CITs was computed by aggregating scores over all the CITs. This has been a common approach to gauge IT implementation (Grover and Goslar, 1993).

Size-related variables were measured using multiple approaches. For organization size, six categories of number of employees (less than 100, 100–499, 500–999, 1000–4999, 5000–10,000, and more than 10,000) were identified and, from a resource-based perspective, respondents were also requested to provide approximate total revenues of their entire organization. Similarly, for IT size, six categories of total number of IT employees (less than 10, 10–49, 50–99, 100–499, 500–1000, and more than 1000) were identified. In addition, we also requested information on approximate annual IT budget for the entire organization. Such measures have been deployed commonly in IT innovation studies to measure size-related variables. For our analyses to test size-related hypotheses based upon the number of employees, the six organizational and IT size categories were collapsed into three logical groupings (less than 500, 500–4999, and 5000 or more for organization size and less than 50, 50–499, and 500 or more for IT size). This approach was used to ensure that there would be sufficient number of cases within each size-related category.

Centralization of decision-making in the organization was measured by using the six-item measure validated by Grover et al. (1999). These items focused on centralization or decentralization of major decisions involving capital budgets, new product/service introduction, entry into major new markets, pricing of major product lines, methods of personnel selection, and work methods to be used. A five-point scale semantically anchored at the extremes and mid-way (1 ¼ very decentralized decisions, 3 ¼ mixed, 5 ¼ very centralized decisions) was used for each item.

Degree of integration was also measured by using a fiveitem measure validated by Grover et al. (1999). These items focused on joint project development, application sharing, exchange of ideas, information sharing, and project initiations through joint interaction between departments. A five-point Likert type scale (1 ¼ strongly disagree, 2 ¼ disagree, 3 ¼ neutral, 4 ¼ agree, 5 ¼ strongly agree) was used for each item.

Infrastructure connectivity was measured by a single item that asked respondents to indicate the extent to which a typical individual computer (including PCs) in their organizations was networked to other computers. A fivepoint scale semantically anchored at the extremes and midway was used (1 ¼ no other computer in the organization, 3 ¼ half the computers, 5 ¼ all the computers in the organization). This item was also adopted from Grover et al. (1999).

## Data collection

Data were first collected in the US using an electronic mailing to the members of SIM, a premier society for IT managers and executives with approximate membership of 1500 organizations. An e-mail message along with an electronic link to a server was sent to all the members of SIM. The message explained the purpose of the study and asked for their cooperation in responding to the survey. Specific instructions were given to consider the identified CITs only in the context of their support in task-oriented collaboration, as opposed to their generic use for communication. We also specifically requested each member of SIM to forward the message containing the hyperlink to the appropriate person if they were not knowledgeable about IT support for task-oriented collaboration in their respective organization. A follow-up e-mail message was sent after 7 working days.

The study was then extended to Australian organizations. A mailing list of the largest 1000 organizations (measured by Gross Revenue) in Australia was used to collect data in the follow-up study. This list, the ‘BRW Top 1000’, is developed by Australia’s leading business magazine, the Business Review Weekly (BRW). A target sample of 500 randomly selected organizations from the Top 1000 was used to collect data using the same questionnaire that was administered in the US study. The survey was addressed to the most senior IT person in the organization. A brief cover letter and a reply-paid envelope were included in the package. Two mailings were undertaken over a period of 2– 3 months.

Finally, the study was extended to Hong Kong. For the variables included in this paper, no modifications to itemmeasures were made. The subjects of the study were the MIS Directors of the largest 420 companies in Hong Kong. The names of these companies, along with their MIS Directors, were identified from Dun and Bradstreet Foreign Enterprises in Hong Kong (2002) and Dun and Bradstreet key Enterprise in Hong Kong (2002). Data from two rounds of mailing over a period of 4–5 months are included in the analyses in this paper.

## Analyses and results

The construct validity and reliability of the two variables operationalized using multi-item measures (centralization of decision-making and degree of integration) was tested using factor analysis and Cronbach’s alpha, respectively. Although not reported here in detail, all the items loaded on two factors as expected and the reliability coefficients were above 0.80, thereby ensuring valid and reliable measures.

Overall, 344 usable responses were received from the three regions. Table 1 shows the respondent position profile of the samples in all the three regions. About a third of our respondents belonged to the top tier. The majority (more than 62%) belonged to the middle tier, while less than 4% of the respondents belonged to lower management level. Despite the fact that our survey was mailed to the most senior IT executive (at least in organizations in Australia and Hong Kong), we received several responses from non-IT executives and managers in all the three regions, indicating that our survey may have been possibly forwarded to the person most knowledgeable about the study focus.

Table 2 shows the organization size (number of employees) of the responding organizations while the descriptive statistics for the independent variables in our research model are reported in Table 3. A one-way ANOVA was used to detect any differences in mean values of the independent variables between the three regions. They show, not surprisingly, that the US organizations are much larger than the Australian and Hong Kong organizations in both overall size (in terms of number of employees or revenue) and in IT function size. Also, Australian organizations were much more centralized (perhaps because the majority of Australian business is conducted in Sydney and Melbourne where most of these organizations are headquartered). However, the three groups of organizations were similar in degree of integration and infrastructure connectivity.

## Discussion and conclusions

The objectives of this paper were three-fold. First, our intention was to document the pattern of adoption and use of CITs to support task-oriented work in the US, Australia, and Hong Kong. These regions were chosen as the first three in our effort to extend globally the scope of this and future studies.

Seven popular CITs were identified based upon the review of the literature. Our results indicate that e-mail and audio teleconferencing are perhaps the only two CITs that have been widely adopted, followed by videoconferencing, proprietary groupware, dataconferencing, web-based tools, and finally EMS, which have the lowest adoption across all the three regions. As far as CIT utilization levels are concerned, our results indicate that there are significant differences in mean utilization of four of the seven CITs between the three regions. Overall, we found that except for e-mail, none of the other CITs had high utilization levels across all the three regions. Audio teleconferencing and proprietary groupware were the next most popularly used CITs while EMS was the least frequently used CIT.

Our second objective was to investigate some of the key antecedents of CIT adoption and use. Based upon innovation diffusion literature, five antecedents (organization size, IT size, centralization of decision-making, degree of integration, and infrastructure connectivity) of CIT adoption and use were identified. Sub-sample analyses were conducted to test the proposed hypotheses using one-way ANOVA and multiple regression analyses. Table 8 summarizes the results of our analyses in all the three regions. We had proposed 10 hypotheses to test our research model in each of the three regions, adding to a total of 30 testable hypotheses. Overall 13 of the 30 hypotheses were supported by our analyses. While the patterns of results are fairly consistent for US and Australian organizations, surprisingly, none of the proposed hypotheses produced significant results for organizations in Hong Kong. As such, we will first discuss the proposed relationships between the study variables in the context of US and Australian organizations and then focus on findings in Hong Kong.

We had proposed that organization size would be positively associated with adoption and use of CITs. We tested this relationship based upon number of employees and based upon total revenues. Both ANOVA and regression analyses results supported the notion that larger and richer organizations adopt more CITs and use them more frequently than their smaller and less richer counterparts. However, this was true only in the context of US organizations. While our results indicate that larger organizations in Australia are likely to adopt more CITs, the size of the organization (as measured by the number of employees) was not found to influence CIT use. Furthermore, the resource-based perspective (i.e. richer organizations are likely to adopt more CITs and use these CITs more frequently) was not supported by our findings in Australian organizations. C

We had also proposed that organizations with larger IT functions (based on number of total IT employees) also adopt more CITs for collaboration and use them more frequently than organizations that have smaller IT functions. Once again, the results were significant only for US organizations. While larger IT functions are likely to adopt more CITs in Australian organizations, the size of the IT function was not found to influence CIT utilization in Australian organizations. It was noted that the size of the IT function was much smaller in Australia. This was because the Australian sample had a much higher proportion of foreign-owned organizations that, in turn, had much smaller numbers of local Australian IT staff. These organizations may take many IT directives from the parent company so the ‘effective’ number of IT staff may be larger than reported measure and so be disguising the IT function size effect. Follow-up case studies may provide a clearer picture of this foreign ownership effect. This may also hold the key to understanding the lack of organization size effect on CIT use discussed above. Again, the reported size of the foreign-owned Australian organizations was much smaller than the Australian-owned organizations (only one-third of the foreign-owned Australian organizations had over 1000 employees vs two-thirds for the Australian-owned organizations), but their ‘effective size’ (as part of a global organization) may be larger and this hidden effect may be confounding the findings for the resource-based perspective.

We did not find centralization of decision-making to have any significant influence on CIT utilization in organizations in the US and Australia.

We also found that the degree of integration between different departments was positively associated with adoption and use of CITs in US and Australian organizations. Since cross-functional integration is critical for collaboration in today’s process-oriented organizational structures, it is not surprising that the degree of integration has a positive influence on the adoption and use of CITs.

Finally, infrastructure connectivity was also positively associated only with the use of CITs in the US and in Australian organizations. These findings suggest that networking of computers may not influence adoption decisions in an organization but it is clearly a prerequisite for use of most of these CITs, although this certainly may not be true for teleconferencing and traditional videoconferencing technologies.

While we found substantial support for our research model in the US and Australia, none of the antecedents were found to be significant predictors of CIT adoption and use in the context of Hong Kong. This could probably be explained in three ways. First, Hong Kong is a city, while the other two investigations were conducted at country level. This difference in study level could probably lead to different findings, contributed from geographical, cultural, economic, and infrastructural variations. Second, Hong Kong is a densely populated city with excellent IT infrastructure, including the network and communication environment. Broadband communications are readily available, at a low cost, not only in commercial districts but also throughout the city. Consequently, adoption and use of CIT, particularly e-mail systems, become a way of business life, disregarding the variables investigated in this study. Third, people in Hong Kong still prefer using telephone for communications, including at work and at home, as there is not much concern about costs associated with long distance calls. Of course, location proximity of trading partners also reduces needs for sophisticated CIT adoption and use. When the business practice in Hong Kong relies more on telephone and face-to-face meeting, the impact of organizational characteristics would become less influential on CIT adoption and use.

There are also some limitations of this study. First, a single informant in each organization was used to collect data. However, given that the majority of our respondents were senior-level corporate and IT executives (IT directors were classified as middle tier), they are likely to have the appropriate knowledge required for the organization-level responses sought for this study. Moreover, we had requested that the questionnaire be forwarded to the person most knowledgeable about IT-based support for task-oriented collaboration. We know for certain that in several cases, our survey was forwarded to another executive or manager than the one who received it initially, thereby further supporting appropriateness of our sample. Second, in this paper, adoption was measured in binary terms. Although this has been an acceptable practice in IT innovation studies, it does not reflect adoption as a continuous process of proliferation of CITs among organizational members. Finally, we must also recognize the limitations due to nonprobability sampling in collecting data in the US. We also realize that cultural effects have not been analysed here. However, one of our intentions was to test if, in fact, the predictors of CIT adoption and use differ across regions. Despite the limitations, this study fills an important gap in the literature and to our knowledge is one of the first exploring CIT adoption and use across different regions.

There are several implications for practice and research. For practitioners, especially those who are engaged in collaborative efforts in the three regions included here, or who are in the process of initiating such efforts, the study provides benchmarks for adoption and use preferences of CITs by regional organizations. For researchers, this study provides several avenues to explore. For example, it sheds light on which CITs need to be the focus of future investigations. Clearly, efforts are needed to understand barriers to adoption and use of Web-based tools and EMS. Second, we did detect significant differences in use patterns of four of the seven CITs between the three regions. Further research is needed to explain these differences.

Previous research on CITs has sometimes been criticized for its focus on laboratory experimental studies of face-toface CIT-supported environments studying artificial problems with non-stakeholding participants in the USA. More research is needed on the adoption and use of CITs by real stakeholders using them to solve real problems in a variety of organizational contexts in different countries. The research reported here addresses that and is already being extended with surveys and case studies in Europe, Scandinavia, and South America.
