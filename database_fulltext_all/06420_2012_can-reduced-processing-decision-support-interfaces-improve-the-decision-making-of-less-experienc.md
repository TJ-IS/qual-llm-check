# Can reduced processing decision support interfaces improve the decision-making of less-experienced incident commanders?

## a b s t r a c t

Computer-based decision support systems have been proposed as a tool to improve the decision-making of less-experienced personnel by reducing the information processing demands necessary for decisionmaking. This study investigated the utility of three decision support system interfaces that differed in their capacity for reduced processing. The participants comprised experienced and less-experienced Fireground Incident Commanders who used the decision support system interfaces to identify the most appropriate entry point to extract a victim from a simulated burning building. The results revealed that reduced processing interfaces enabled less-experienced participants to acquire information using a process equivalent to their more experienced counterparts. However, this process did not result in improvements in the accuracy of the decision-making process. Indeed, the accuracy of experienced participants' decisions was consistently greater than the less-experienced participants, irrespective of the decision support system interface. It was concluded that the success of reduced processing decision support systems amongst less-experienced operators is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly dependent upon their understanding of the relative value of key features associated with the decision-making process.

## 1. Introduction

In high reliability domains, decision-making represents a critical cognitive skill, errors from which can impact signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly the safety and security of industrial systems. However, accurate decision-making is also a cognitive skill that takes many years to acquire [2, 10]. Therefore, the impending loss of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of skilled workers, forecast for many industrialised countries, represents a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant threat to the management of a number of industrial systems in the future. This is particularly the case where there is a dependence on accurate, ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, and timely decision-making under uncertain conditions.

In the context of changes to the industrial workforce, <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting is a domain that is especially vulnerable. The situations that are confronted by <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters are cognitively complex, with the Incident Commander (IC) required to rapidly assess the situation, develop an appropriate response, and execute the plan of action by means of command, control, and communication, all within a limited timeframe [17]. However, inexperienced <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters in the contemporary workplace generally have less exposure to the range and frequency of <sup>fi</sup>res that confronted their experienced counterparts, providing fewer opportunities to develop skill in making rapid, yet accurate, decisions [5, 22]. This is due, in large part, to improvements in building codes and practices, and to improvements in the education of the population.

Effective decision-making is often associated with the application of normative principles which emphasise exhaustive, compensatory processing [21]. Due to the exceedingly high level of accuracy that is potentially afforded by the use of normative strategies, it might be expected that the success of expert decision-makers is due to their superior capacity to engage such strategies. However, in high-reliability situations, such as those faced by <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters, decisions are often made with considerably less cognitive processing than those prescribed by normative strategies [14, 15].

The decisions made in high reliability environments are often in response to a dynamic, unfolding situation requiring decisions to be made in real-time in response to environmental events [4]. Consequently, the timing of decisions is not controlled by the decision-maker, but rather, is dictated by the situation, often requiring decisions to be made under signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time-pressure [3]. The complex and time-constrained nature of the dynamic situations involved in high reliability environments makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for operators to engage normative decision strategies, as often vast quantities of information need to be considered within a limited timeframe. Thus, experienced personnel are argued to develop decision strategies which reduce the processing effort required to make decisions [9].

Gigerenzer and Goldstein [11], amongst others, have demonstrated that accurate decisions are possible on the basis of reduced processing strategies. Moreover, Klein [14, 15] demonstrates that expert <sup>fi</sup>reground commanders adopt a recognition-driven approach to decision-making, particularly in time-constrained situations. Nevertheless, where operators lack experience in formulating decisions, they have little choice but to engage a relatively detailed process of information acquisition and analysis. For example, Wiggins, Stevens, Howard, Henley, and O'Hare [34] demonstrated that while imposing time-constraints on decision-making reduced the amount of information acquired by experts, intermediate operators continued to acquire the same amount of information that they had acquired in the absence of a timeconstraint. This suggests that one of the hallmarks of expert decisionmaking in time-constrained environments is the capability to identify relevant from irrelevant features [23], and when the situation dictates, focus information acquisition on only that information with the greatest predictive validity [15].

The additional information acquired by less-experienced operators is presumably less predictive and therefore, possibly super<sup>fl</sup>uous to the decision process. However, it also increases the demands on information processing resources, possibly to a point of cognitive overload [6, 31]. By comparison, experts have a relatively greater capacity to regulate their cognitive activity, engaging intuitive, reduced processing strategies and thereby ensuring a relatively lower level of cognitive load [16].

Although the differences between experienced and inexperienced operators' decision-making are reasonably well established empirically, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty lies in situations where, through the attrition of an experienced workforce, inexperienced operators are required to undertake roles that were normally occupied by more experienced operators. In these situations, on-the-job training and experience are often emphasised as the basis for skill acquisition. However, there remains a requirement to protect the integrity of the operating system during the period within which an operator is transitioning towards expertise. Access to appropriately designed decision support systems provides a potential compromise between safeguarding the integrity of a system and enabling inexperienced operators to retain a level of active involvement suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to facilitate the acquisition of skills.

## 1.1. Reduced processing decision support

Decision support systems (DSSs) are devices that are designed to reduce the cognitive demands associated with decision-making [26]. They can be designed to undertake one or more of a range of cognitive processes associated with decision-making including the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and acquisition of information, option comparison, the retrieval of relevant information from memory, and/or the communication of the outcomes of a decision [20]. In effect, the purpose of the DSS is to minimise the cognitive demands associated with a task, thereby maximising the residual cognitive resources necessary for the decision-maker to achieve an optimal outcome. However, it remains unclear whether such reduced processing strategies can improve decision outcomes and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, whether different reduced processing DSS interfaces enhance or impede the utility of the decision support process.

Previous research concerning the role of interface design in the context of DSSs suggests that both the presentation of information and the types of system functions available within the DSS have the capacity to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the decision strategy engaged by the user [7, 8, 28, 29]. However, there is little information available concerning the nature of the relationship between decision outcomes and speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of information presentation and system function.

In an attempt to provide a framework for DSS interface design, Wiggins and Bollwerk [32] adopted a theoretical position in which they argued that DSSs should incorporate, as much as possible, interfaces that match decision heuristics that occur spontaneously in human decisionmaking. They designed three interfaces that corresponded respectively to the frequency, majority of con<sup>fi</sup>rming dimensions, and elimination by aspects heuristics [1, 30]. Morrison, Wiggins, and Porter [18] extended this notion and developed a fourth interface based on the satis<sup>fi</sup>cing heuristic [25]. In the case of the satis<sup>fi</sup>cing heuristic, the information avail able for acquisition by decision-makers was restricted, consistent with a reduced processing decision support interface.

The DSS interfaces developed by Wiggins and Bollwerk [32] and Morrison et al. [18] broadly represented a progression from more compensatory decision strategies consistent with analytical decisionmaking (frequency and majority of con<sup>fi</sup>rming dimensions), through to more non-compensatory decision strategies consistent with intuitive decision-making (elimination by aspects and satis<sup>fi</sup>cing).

Morrison et al. [18] examined the utility of the four heuristicbased interfaces in the context of forensic investigation and noted that the accuracy of decisions remained relatively consistent, even for the satis<sup>fi</sup>cing interface, where the amount of information available was constrained. This evidence, in particular, suggests that the use of DSS interfaces that reduce the information available to decision-makers may not necessarily lead to a reduction in decision accuracy associated with the availability of less information, and may potentially be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in terms of reducing the level of cognitive load to which less-experienced personnel are subjected during decision-making.

## 1.2. The present study

The present study was conducted in the context of <sup>fi</sup>reground incident command and was designed to test whether the use of heuristicbased DSS interfaces that restricted the information available during decision-making would result in differences in the quality of decision outcomes. Three DSS interfaces were designed based on those developed by Wiggins and Bollwerk [32] and Morrison et al. [18] to encourage the use of distinct decision strategies, each of which differed in the level of cognitive processing associated with formulating a decision [12]. The interfaces encouraged the use of decision strategies that ranged from the most cognitively demanding Analytical DSS interface (frequency)[1], a moderately demanding Quasi-analytical interface (elimination-by-aspects) [30], and the least demanding Intuitive DSS interface (satis<sup>fi</sup>cing) [25]. It was hypothesised that when using the Analytical DSS interface, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences would exist between experienced and lessexperienced ICs in information acquisition, decision latency, and decision accuracy, and that these differences would be ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed by a time-constraint. Since the reduced processing interfaces were designed to encourage less-experienced ICs to engage less demanding decision strategies, the differences between the experienced and the lessexperienced ICs were not predicted when using the Intuitive or the Quasi-analytical interfaces.

## 2. Method

## 2.1. Participants

Ninety-one <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters participated in this study, recruited from internal emails sent within <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting organisations and an advertisement placed in relevant <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting journals. Of the total 91 par ticipants, 14 were excluded due to missing data. Therefore, data from 77 participants were retained for analysis.

The participants were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as either experienced or lessexperienced ICs based on the number of years of experience that they had accumulated as a Station Of<sup>fi</sup>cer. With at least 2 years experience at the rank of Station Of<sup>fi</sup>cer or higher, 26 participants were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as experienced ICs. They had a mean age of 46.37 years (SD=8.28) and had been involved in a reported mean 39.56 (SD=61.24) incidents as the IC. The remaining 51 participants were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as less-experienced ICs and had a mean age of 32.25 (SD=7.25) years and had been involved in a reported mean 2.31 (SD=6.04) incidents as the IC.

## 2.2. Design

The study comprised a $2 \times 2 \times ( 3 )$ mixed measures experimental design incorporating the two levels of incident command experience (experienced ICs vs. less-experienced ICs); two levels of time constraint (no time constraint vs. 3 min); and three levels of DSS interface (Analytical vs. Quasi-analytical vs. Intuitive) as independent variables. The dependent variables comprised the amount of information accessed; the pattern of information acquisition; the number of re-acquisitions of previously accessed information (feature recursions); the time taken to formulate a decision; and decision accuracy.

## 2.3. Stimuli

## 2.3.1. Fire-fighting scenarios

The participants were presented with three, written scenario descriptions derived through consultation with Subject Matter Experts (SMEs) all of whom had obtained the rank of Inspector with a mean of 24.4 (SD=5.32) total years experience as a <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghter, and a mean of 7.5 (SD=4.51) years of command experience. Each of the three scenarios tasked participants with identifying the most appropriate point of entry to a burning building to rescue a victim trapped internally. Each scenario description consisted of a three sentence paragraph which contained information regarding the time of day, the type of building, and con<sup>fi</sup>rmation that a person was trapped inside. The presentation order of scenarios and the assignment to DSS interfaces were both counterbalanced.

To determine the appropriateness of participants' point of entry decisions, SMEs were presented with written descriptions of each of the three scenarios, as well as corresponding tables that presented feature values for each of the seven decision options. The SMEs were asked to review the information and rank each of the decision options from one, the most appropriate option, to seven, the least appropriate. The mean ranking for each decision option was then calculated and each of these rankings was scored from one to seven, with a score of one assigned to the lowest ranked decision, and a score of seven assigned to the highest ranked decision. The reliability of each of the SMEs responses was computed using Kendall's coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of concordance which revealed a moderately high level of inter-rater agreement, Kendall's W=.70.

## 2.3.2. DSS interfaces

Three DSS interfaces were developed based on those used in previous research [18, 32]. Each interface was designed to encourage the use of a distinct decision strategy that differed in the level of cognitive processing required to make a decision. The interfaces used in the current study consisted of what have been referred to as Analytical, Quasi-analytical, and Intuitive DSS interfaces.

The Analytical interface depicted a <sup>fl</sup>oor-plan featuring the location of seven points of entry to a building. Participants could select a point of entry which then displayed a list of eight features in a drop-down menu that could be used to investigate the appropriateness of the point of entry. In the case of the Analytical interface, only one feature could be accessed at a time and only the features pertaining to a selected decision option could be displayed. Therefore, the Analytical interface encouraged alternative-wise information acquisition which is associated with the frequency decision strategy in which options are ranked on the basis of the frequency of ‘good’ and ‘poor’ features [1]. Essentially, this requires information to be held in working memory to make comparisons across the seven decision options. An example of the Analytical DSS interface is provided in Fig. 1.

In contrast to the Analytical interface, the Quasi-analytical and Intuitive interfaces restricted the cognitive processing demands on the decisionmaking process by limiting to three, the decision options that were available to evaluate. The Intuitive and Quasi-analytical interfaces also reduced the requirement to retain information in working memory by integrating the information presented across the three options [6]. Consequently, the Quasi-analytical and Intuitive interfaces could be appropriately considered reduced processing interfaces.

The Quasi-analytical DSS interface required participants to <sup>fi</sup>rst rank-order each feature from the most appropriate to the least appropriate, given the context of the decision. Each participant's feature ranking determined the order in which features were presented in the interface, with the highest-ranked feature available at the top of the drop-down menu and the lowest-ranked feature at the bottom. Clicking a feature would simultaneously present the feature values for all three points of entry, thereby encouraging the use of an attribute-wise search strategy consistent with the elimination by aspects decision strategy [30]. An example of the Quasi-analytical DSS interface is provided in Fig. 2.

## 2.3.3. Features

The eight features that were used to populate the DSS interfaces were derived from cognitive interviews conducted with <sup>fi</sup>ve

SMEs. Using an adaptation to the O'Hare, Wiggins, Williams, and Wong [19] interview protocol, participants were asked a series of cognitive probes in which they responded with reference to a retrospective incident that they recalled from their own experience. The features were then extracted by searching for evidence to suggest that participants had made a decision on the basis of the recognition of particular features. The features that were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed were then validated through the administration of a spontaneous response questionnaire to a larger sample of <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting personnel. Eight features were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and validated from the cognitive interviews, including structural damage; the distance from the victim; the level of heat; the distance from the seat of the <sup>fi</sup>re; obstructions to the entry; smoke; entry construction; and the fuel load.

## 2.4. Time-constrain

To ensure that the time-constraint used in the study represented the constraints imposed in the operational environment, <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghter fatality reports were examined from the United States National Institute of Occupational Health and Safety. A total of 11 reports were considered, representing the available incident reports between 2000 and 2007 which involved entry to a structural <sup>fi</sup>re and where the death of a <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghter was not attributed to the selection of an inappropriate point of entry. In each case, the time taken to formulate a decision as to the appropriate point of entry was established as the duration between the initial IC arriving on the scene and the point at which <sup>fi</sup>re <sup>fi</sup>ghters entered the building. Across the 11 cases, the mean duration was 3 min and $2 7 \thinspace s \thinspace ( S D { = } 1$ min and 22 s) which was rounded to 3 min for the purposes of the study. This procedure ensured that the time-limit would be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently demanding, while maintaining a degree of face validity.

## 2.5. Procedure

Participants <sup>fi</sup>rst completed a demographic questionnaire seeking information pertaining to their level of <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting experience. The experimental condition to which participants were subsequently assigned determined the order in which the scenarios would be presented, the DSS conditions that would correspond to each scenario, and whether or not the participant would be faced with a time-constraint. Participants were then invited to complete the study, during which they were asked to use the information presented in the scenario to make a decision regarding the most appropriate point of entry.

## 4. Discussion

The primary aim of the present study was to investigate the impact of reduced processing DSS interfaces on experienced and lessexperienced incident commanders' decisions, in a series of simulated scenarios, to enter a burning building and extract victims. Participants compared various building entry points using three different DSS interfaces, each of which differed in the amount of information that was available to process and the time available in which to acquire information.

As hypothesised, signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences were evident in the process of information acquisition engaged by experienced and less-experienced ICs in response to the Analytical DSS interface. Less-experienced ICs accessed a greater frequency of feature value displays than experienced ICs, made more feature recursions, and adopted a more sequential pattern of information acquisition. These results are consistent with the notion that, in time-constrained situations, experienced personnel adopt a recognitionbased approach to decision-making in which information is processed selectively as the situation dictates [14, 15, 23, 34].

By focusing on information that is perceived as most critical to the decision process, experienced ICs have, arguably, fewer demands on working memory, with a lower likelihood that information will be forgotten, thereby resulting in fewer feature recursions. The extraneous information processed in working memory by the less-experienced ICs when using the Analytical DSS interface possibly led to more information being forgotten, requiring information that had been previously acquired, but not appropriately encoded, to be re-accessed, as evident by the greater frequency of feature recursions amongst less-experienced ICs.

When using the two reduced processing interfaces, the differences in the process of information acquisition between experienced and less-experienced ICs were less evident. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between experienced and less-experienced ICs in the frequency of feature value displays accessed, the number of sequentially accessed features, and the number of feature recursions. There were also no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences evident in the decision latency across the three DSS interfaces. In combination, these results suggest that, at least at a structural level, the reduced processing DSS interfaces required less-experienced participants to acquire information in a form consistent with their more experienced counterparts.

Despite the structural limitations on participants' acquisition of information, there was no evidence to suggest that the decision accuracy of less-experienced ICs improved in response to the reduced processing DSS interfaces. Indeed, the decision accuracy scores of less-experienced ICs were relatively consistent across each of the DSS interfaces. Consistent with Wiggins and Bollwerk [32] and Harris and Wiggins [13], a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant main effect was evident for participants' level of experience, indicating that the experienced ICs consistently made more accurate decisions than the less-experienced ICs.

Although it appears that the Intuitive and the Quasi-analytical DSS interfaces reduced the cognitive load for the less-experienced ICs, this did not necessarily result in an improvement in decision accuracy. A descriptive analysis of the features accessed by participants in response to the Intuitive interface revealed an apparent qualitative difference between the features selected. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, as a group, experienced ICs were more consistent in the features that they selected. More than 75% of experienced ICs selected the structural damage feature, with the majority also selecting the distance from the victim and heat when information acquisition was restricted to a limited number of displays.

While a majority of less-experienced ICs also selected structural damage and distance from the victim as features to consider in response to the Intuitive interface, they were considerably less likely to select heat as a feature to consider. This suggests that although the Intuitive interface enabled less-experienced ICs to acquire information in a manner consistent with experienced ICs, the features that were acquired by less-experienced ICs may not have been the features that were most relevant to the decision problem. This is the most likely explanation for the consistent differences in decision accuracy that were evident amongst experienced and less-experienced ICs.

The fact that both the process of information acquisition and decision accuracy remained consistent across the different time-constraints suggests that, despite the imposition of a time-constraint, experienced ICs remained capable of acquiring information suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to ensure a level of decision accuracy beyond that achieved by less-experienced ICs. This result is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, as it provides a degree of construct validity for the application of a reduced processing DSS interface in a time-constrained environment.

## 4.1. Implications

Previous research concerning the use of DSS interfaces has indicated that operators tend to have a preference for strategies that enable a level of personal control over the sequence in which information is displayed [13, 18, 32]. However, this research has also revealed relatively consistent differences between experienced and inexperienced operators, particularly in terms of the accuracy of decision outcomes. The present research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to compare the utility of full, partial, and reduced processing DSS interfaces amongst experienced and inexperienced operators.

The present study was not intended to examine the suitability of the particular interfaces presented as supporting decision-making in applied settings. Rather, it was a test of a theoretical perspective that examined the underlying bases of different DSS in facilitating the decisions made by relatively inexperienced personnel. The results suggest that, in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context examined, it may be possible to encourage less-experienced personnel to engage the reduced processing decision strategies that are applied by experienced personnel. The pattern of information acquisition evident in response to the reduced processing interfaces potentially reduces the demands on information processing, thereby reducing the cognitive load imposed by the task.

For inexperienced operators, the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty lies not so much in the process of information acquisition but in the type of information acquired and its interpretation in the context of the decision problem. The consistent differences in decision accuracy between the experienced and less-experienced ICs suggest that greater emphasis needs to be placed on the type of information acquired by less-experienced ICs. Certainly, the reduced processing DSS interfaces could provide a structure for information acquisition consistent with more experienced operators. For instance, an interface that indicates the importance of each feature for making a decision and encourages information to be processed accordingly, perhaps through the rank ordering of features, could potentially improve the decision accuracy of the less-experienced ICs.

Although the reduced processing interfaces in the present study facilitated information processing amongst less-experienced ICs, the development of a full-scale DSS for real-world <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting will present a challenging task. The range of situations faced by <sup>fi</sup>reground ICs is such that it will be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to support the full range of situations that are likely to be confronted. However, it may be possible to design a DSS to support a series of standard situations, such as determining the optimal point of entry to a burning building.

Despite the potential dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of the deployment of a reduced processing DSS within <sup>fi</sup>re-<sup>fi</sup>ghting, there are many other operational environments where the utility of reduced processing DSSs could be applied, including medicine, rail, and aviation. In combination with training concerning the predictive validity of particular features, it may be possible to improve and maintain the performance of lessexperienced operators, thereby safeguarding the integrity of these critical operating environments into the future.
