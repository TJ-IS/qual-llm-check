# Understanding of website usability: Specifying and measuring constructs and their relationships

Younghwa Lee <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Kenneth A. Kozar <sup>b</sup>

Keywords: Website usability E-business success Online customer behavior

## a b s t r a c t

Developing a usable website is pivotal for e-business success. Researchers have devoted effort to develop metrics, guidelines and theories of website usability, yet there still is a lack of consensus on the multifaceted dimensions of website usability and lack of investigation of the nomological networks among website usability constructs. This study <sup>fi</sup>rst investigated the common dimensions of website usability by integrating the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies and a focus group study with website usability experts. Instruments to measure the con: structs were developed and empirically validated. Then nomological networks between website usability constructs and between those constructs and online purchase intention and purchase were examined. Three <sup>fi</sup>eld studies including two questionnaire surveys and a causal mapping analysis were conducted. The research identi<sup>fi</sup>ed ten website usability constructs with strong psychometric properties. A number of nomological networks between usability constructs were discovered, contributing to identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of sources of variances of purchase intention and purchase behavior. Findings of this study are expected to provide useful insights for practitioners to develop more usable websites and for researchers to better assess the effect of website usability on online customer behavior.

## 1. Introduction

Within the e-commerce environment, the impact of a web site design on customer purchases cannot be fully understood without an evaluation of the usability of the web site

– DeLone and McLean [19, p. 25]

Developing usable websites is pivotal for e-business success since online consumers touch, feel, search, and experience products (or services) mainly through websites [18,68]. A usable website has been known to provide a positive attitude toward online stores, increases stickiness and revisit rates, and eventually stimulates online purchase [13,32,50,70]. To make comparisons with competitors' websites, benchmark performance, and plan improvements for designing a more usable website, HCI researchers have devoted effort to de<sup>fi</sup>ne website usability constructs, develop instruments to measure the multifaceted website usability constructs, assess their in<sup>fl</sup>uences on online customers' attitude or behaviors including satisfaction, loyalty, purchase or revisit intention, and actual purchases [23,30,46]. This has led to a number of metrics, guidelines, and even theories of website usability.

However, even with this knowledge and contrary to our expectation, current websites still contain a number of usability problems [9,14,67,68]. Dif<sup>fi</sup>cult-to-understand content, inconsistent formats, dif-<sup>fi</sup>culty in navigation, disorientation, lack of interaction and reliability, inef<sup>fi</sup>cient search capabilities, and ill-de<sup>fi</sup>ned help functions are usability problems frequently identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with commercial websites. Regarding the poor usability, Becker and Mottay [9] mentioned that “online business failures are increasing as consumers are turning away from unusable sites. The ‘build it and they will come’ attitude has led to the demise of several e-commerce sites which are too slow, too buggy, or too complex for ease of use” (p. 54).

There are numerous reasons for poor website usability, but this study focuses on the lack of consensus on the multifaceted dimensions of website usability and lack of investigation of the nomological networks among website usability constructs. Precise articulation of all dimensions of website usability facilitating particular design assumptions enables the design process to become more tractable for developers by focusing their attention and restricting their options, thereby improving design outcomes. In addition, designing standardized instruments to measure those constructs can facilitate direct comparisons across time, culture and geographical regions [30]. However, there is a lack of consensus on the constructs of website usability and how to measure them. Thus, previous studies were conducted based on different sets of constructs measuring website usability. Using different instruments to measure the same usability construct is another problem with previous studies, making direct comparisons of research <sup>fi</sup>ndings dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Researchers have lamented on the absence of general measures and instruments of web usability and called for research regarding this matter [46,68].

HCI researchers and practitioners have speculated about potential causal relationships among multi-dimensional usability constructs [e.g., 50,52] and found high correlations between some of those constructs [e.g., 46]. Previous usability studies assumed independent relationships among usability constructs and thus only examined their direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on online customers' perceptions and behaviors. Scienti<sup>fi</sup>c investigation is needed to reveal the relationships among those constructs to precisely assess overall in<sup>fl</sup>uence on website usability, but few attempts have been made to examine the relationships.

Given this background, this study has two primary goals: (1) to identify common dimensions of website usability by integrating the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of previous studies and through a focus group study with website usability experts. The instruments to measure the constructs are developed and empirically validated. (2) to investigate the nomological networks among website usability constructs by use of a causal mapping approach and structural modeling analysis. Through empirically testing and validating the networks, the effect of each website usability construct on online customers' purchase intention (and purchases) can be more precisely captured.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Usability and website usability

Usability has been an important theme extensively studied in the human–computer interaction (HCI) <sup>fi</sup>eld [64]. Researchers in this <sup>fi</sup>eld have emphasized the successful interaction between a human and a computer as a key factor in designing and implementing a variety of computing systems. Using their de<sup>fi</sup>nition, usability refers to “the extent to which a product can be used by speci<sup>fi</sup>ed users to achieve speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goals with effectiveness, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and satisfaction in a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed context of use”[33].

As e-business sites explosively have grown and the World Wide Web has become a dominant interface, usability researchers have applied the basic usability principles to the web environment and developed webspeci<sup>fi</sup>c usability metrics, guidelines, tools, and techniques [38,46,70]. Web usability refers to the extent to which web sites can be used by speci<sup>fi</sup>ed users to achieve speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goals to visit with effectiveness, ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, and satisfaction in a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed context of website use [2, p.170].

Effectiveness represents the accuracy and completeness with which online users achieve goals (e.g., purchase, information gathering) while visiting a website [33]. It is directly related to the right functionality so that users can do what they need or want to do while visiting a website. For example, when a website has excellent navigation features, vivid and interactive images, and content with relevant depth and scope, people can achieve their goals with fewer errors. Efficiency represents the resources expended in relation to achieving goals while visiting a website. The users perceive ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency when they can achieve goals with a quick visit without putting forth much cognitive effort. For example, they perceive more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency at a user-centered website which contains simple and consistent design features across web pages, making the pages easy to read and learn. Finally, satisfaction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the comfort and acceptability of a website to its users. When a website provides a variety of options to support the users (e.g., FAQ, real-time chatting with customer support personnel) and reliable, secure, and privacy-guaranteed services, satisfaction can increase. Website usability is considered a multidimensional construct that encompasses effectiveness, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and satisfaction due to website design.

## 2.2. Theoretical models of website usability

To identify the website usability factors and investigate their in<sup>fl</sup>uences on online customer perceptions or behaviors, IS researchers have developed theoretical models of website usability. First, based on the analogy of websites as buildings, Kim et al. [38] adopted Vitruvius' architectural quality model [58] to measure the architectural quality of a website. Kim et al. identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>rmness (e.g., security, privacy), convenience (e.g., easy to navigate, search, order processing), and delight (e.g. visual esthetics) as usability factors and hypothesized and empirically validated the relationships between these three usability factors and online customer satisfaction and customer loyalty toward the site. Palmer [55] proposed a model to explore the relationships between website success and <sup>fi</sup>ve website usability factors including download delay, navigability, site content, interactivity, and responsiveness. He found that all <sup>fi</sup>ve website usability factors signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect website success measured by customer satisfaction, likelihood of return, and frequency of site use. Based on the Microsoft Usability Guideline (MUG), Venkatesh and Agarwal [70] proposed a usability model that examines the associations between <sup>fi</sup>ve usability constructs including content, ease of use, promotion, made-for-the-medium, and emotion and web site use and purchases. They found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects of usability constructs on website use and purchases, even after controlling previous purchase and experience with similar sites. Finally, by adopting the landscape preference model from environmental psychology, Lee and Kozar [39] proposed a website usability model that explores the link between four usability factors including coherence, legibility, variety, and mystery and online customers' attitude and purchase intention. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationships between usability factors and two endogenous variables were found although slightly different nomological networks were found depending upon site characteristics, gender and age.

In summary, previous theoretical models of website usability provided a rich understanding of the effects of website usability constructs on perceptions and behaviors of online consumers. However, there is room for further investigation because each theory uses different sets of website usability constructs, measures similar constructs using different measurement items, and does not examine the nomological networks between website usability constructs.

## 2.3. Identifying website usability constructs

To assess the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of website usability on online consumer perceptions or behaviors, considerable effort has been expended to identify and measure website usability factors. First. researchers in the human–computer interaction <sup>fi</sup>eld proposed multiple usability factors for an objective assessment of website design quality. They have in general taken an engineering approach in an attempt to identify a set of principles and common practices that ensure usability of website design. For example, Gehrke and Turban [27] speci<sup>fi</sup>ed page loading, navigation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, download time, successful search rate, error rates, task completion time, and frequency of cursor movement as usability measurement factors. Schubert and Selz [63] suggested hypermedia presentation, time (availability and contact possibilities), ubiquity (availability and system performance), expert systems (e.g., personalization) and interactivity (e.g., consumer pro<sup>fi</sup>le) as factors for a good web design. Second, website design experts [e.g., 50,66] also proposed several website usability factors. For example, Spool et al. [66] speci<sup>fi</sup>ed ease of use, readability, content quality, fun, productivity, completeness, and relevance. Nielsen [50] suggested navigation, response time, credibility and content. In the corporate world, companies like IBM and Microsoft as well as competitions such as the Webby awards speci<sup>fi</sup>ed website usability design guidelines. For example, Microsoft suggested a usability guideline (MUG) with <sup>fi</sup>ve usability factors including content, ease-of-use, promotion, made-for-the-medium, and emotion. Finally, IS researchers [e.g., 2,46,68] developed perceptually-oriented website usability factors. They insisted that website usability is not intrinsically objective in nature, but rather is closely related with online consumers' subjective perception of a website through interaction with the site [2]. For instance, Loiacono et al. [46] have proposed perceptual measures

---: indirect effect, →: direct effect including tailored information, online completeness, relative advantage, information/<sup>fi</sup>t-to-task, ease of understanding, intuitive operation, trust, response time, visual appeal, innovativeness, emotional appeal, and consistent image.

Collectively, past studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a variety of website usability factors. However, each study proposed different sets of website usability factors specifying different terminology and scope. Possible reasons for such differences are that (1) they de<sup>fi</sup>ne website usability differently (some people consider website usability equally with overall website quality, while others treat it as website design quality); (2) they constrain the scope of website usability based on their theoretical boundaries [38,39] or guidelines (e.g., Microsoft Usability Guidelines [70]), and (3) researchers from different disciplines de<sup>fi</sup>ne website usability differently depending upon the goals to achieve (e.g., increase consumer retention, satisfaction, loyalty, positive attitude, purchase intention, trust, or e-business success). This variety resulted in no consensus on how to measure website usability [3,68]. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate the past efforts and propose common website usability factors.

The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of such usability factors is directly related to the development of their measurement instruments. Initial efforts in instrument development were made by HCI researchers, but they faced the problems of inconsistency and incompleteness [28]. Common problems include instruments that: (1) are developed and measured in nonscienti<sup>fi</sup>c ways (e.g., single item measures), (2) mainly measure the objective nature of website usability (e.g., error rates), (3) are intuition and experience-based, and (4) are not validated by empirical studies [66]. First, instruments were developed in nonscienti<sup>fi</sup>c ways. For example, Spool et al. [66] developed multiple constructs that cover the diverse nature of website usability, but most of the constructs were measured based on a single item. In addition, many of the current metrics have a ‘yes’ or ‘no’ format, making it dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult to capture the sensitivity of a user's response. Second, instruments from the HCI <sup>fi</sup>eld focus on objective measures. Researchers [e.g., 41] measured usability using task completion time, the number of user errors, memory recognition and recall, memory demands, the number of overused graphics and inadequate links. These factors are easy to measure but may not assess the subjective nature of web usability. Third, even though many guidelines, metrics, and instruments have been developed, they are developed based on common sense, existing practices, designer's intuition, and are company speci<sup>fi</sup>c (e.g., IBM Website Usability Assessment, Microsoft Usability Guidelines [70]). Recently several empirical studies were conducted to develop theoretical models of website usability by adopting theories from other disciplinary areas (e.g., environmental psychology [65], architecture [38]). They developed multi-item web usability constructs and validated their instruments following scienti<sup>fi</sup>c instrument development methods. However, these efforts leave room for improvement in that they did not investigate full sets of website usability constructs because theories they adopted have their own theoretical boundaries restricting the inclusion of other usability factors. In summary, while previous studies enrich our understanding of what website usability is and how to measure it, the lack of consistency in the usability constructs and their instruments renders room for improvement. Therefore, further research is needed to identify common dimensions of website usability constructs and to develop and validate their instruments.

## 2.4. The relationship between website usability constructs

Measuring the effect of website usability on online customer perceptions (or behavior) is crucial for designing more usable sites [20]. To accurately measure the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a certain construct, it generally is recommended to examine both its direct and indirect path to dependent variables. Website designers and usability engineers [50] frequently have speculated on the potential causal relationship between website usability constructs (indirect effect in Fig. 1) and some empirical studies reported high correlations among usability constructs, but no scienti<sup>fi</sup>c studies to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally investigate the relationships have been conducted.

For example, Norman [52] pointed out the positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of simplicity on ease of navigation and negative effect on interactivity, while Nielsen [50] indicated a positive effect of consistency on navigability and a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of simplicity on interactivity. Researchers have reported high correlations among usability variables including between informational <sup>fi</sup>t-to-task and tailored information (r=0.75), intuitive operations and ease of understanding (r=0.67); between learnability and information quality (r=0.72) [44], and between legibility (i.e., navigability) and coherence (similar to consistency) (r=0.57) [39]. There also are some intuitive relationships between website usability constructs. For example, users perceive better readability but poor interactivity and telepresence when they visit a website without having any redundant design components (simplicity). When the same (or similar) website design components are placed in consistent locations across web pages and consistent format (consistency), users can easily learn how to use major features of the website (learnability), quickly read the information they found (readability), and navigate easily (navigability).

In summary, while the potential relationships between website usability constructs has been speculated, no scienti<sup>fi</sup>c investigation to validate the relationships has been made. In this study, by adopting a cognitive mapping approach that helps systematically identify the nomological networks, we examine the uncharted indirect effects of website usability.

## 2.5. Relationships between website usability constructs and online purchases

Researchers have investigated the relationships between website usability constructs and diverse outcome variables including (1) attitude toward a website, (2) intention (to purchase, use, visit, further navigate), (3) action (actual purchase, revisit, use, recommend to friends), and (4) others (satisfaction, preference, loyalty) (see Fig. 2). Although previous studies render us a rich understanding of the impact of website usability on online customer perceptions and behaviors, they were criticized because they mainly utilized indirect performance measures of e-businesses. That is, they have used user attitude, use or revisit intention, and frequency (or duration) of use rather than direct purchase (or purchase intention) measures. Meanwhile, e-business managers have invested a huge amount of money/effort on improving the design quality of their websites expecting a highly positive correlation between website design quality and customers' purchase from their online stores. By selecting purchase intention and purchase as endogenous variables, attempts are made to examine the direct impact of website usability on online purchase.

## 3. Research methods and results

To identify common dimensions of website usability and develop and validate their measurement instruments (Study 1) and to investigate nomological networks among website usability constructs (Studies 2 and 3), three <sup>fi</sup>eld studies were conducted sequentially.

## 3.1. Study 1. Investigate common dimensions of website usability and develop and validate instrument items

## 3.1.1. Identify common dimensions of website usability

To identify common dimensions of website usability and their instrument items, we conducted an extensive literature review of twenty-seven academic journal and conference papers, web documents, and industry guidelines on website usability, e-quality, and e-business success and selected the thirty-seven candidate constructs of website usability. However, since there were a number of constructs that are conceptually overlapped but have different names, three researchers independently reviewed the same literature materials by examining the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and instrument items of candidate constructs and categorized or grouped them based on their similarity and dissimilarity. As a result, they commonly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed seven usability constructs including consistency, supportability, learnability, simplicity, interactivity, telepresence, and readability. Although they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed eight additional constructs (i.e., navigability, security, privacy, reliability, <sup>fl</sup>exibility, content scope, content timeliness, and content relevance), some disagreement on grouping those variables with respect to their name, scope and depth was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. To address the disagreement, seven new usability experts were invited for a focus group study to reconcile the differences and reach agreement resulting in three additional constructs.<sup>1</sup> Security, privacy and reliability were integrated into credibility, while flexibility and navigability were combined into navigability. Finally, content scope, timeliness, and relevance were integrated into content relevance. Appendix A shows how candidate usability constructs are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into ten usability constructs and Appendix B summarizes the de<sup>fi</sup>nition and related studies of the selected constructs.

## 3.1.2. Develop and validate instrument items of website usability constructs

Validity and reliability tests are critical in instrument development. Content Validity examines whether the operationalization of a construct adequately represents the domain of coverage of the construct [16]. If instrument items are derived from a comprehensive analysis of relevant literature and are evaluated by expert judgment, content validity can be ensured. Initial website usability instruments were developed based on literature reviews and converting frequently mentioned descriptions of the constructs into items. Since each item by itself is an imperfect indicator of its underlying construct, two or more items were developed to measure a construct. The use of multi-item measures improves the accuracy and reliability of the measurement and reduces measurement error [16]. The initial instruments were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by using suggestions regarding wording, format, and order from twelve experts including IS and marketing faculty members, website usability experts, and doctoral students.

A total of <sup>fi</sup>fty-three undergraduate students from an e-business class in a large Western university with at least one online purchase experience were assigned to pretest the instruments. The students were recruited via an opportunity to receive class points and win a sweepstake. Without informing participants of the goal of the study, they were requested to navigate Amazon.com and complete a questionnaire.<sup>2</sup> To examine validity and reliability, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted. After removing two items that were not grouped with other items corresponding to their constructs (see item with \* mark in Table 1), all constructs had an eigenvalue of more than 1(cumulative% of Eigenvalues was 84.29%), indicating that they satis<sup>fi</sup>ed unidimensionality and convergent validity. All constructs also showed high discriminant validity, representing that items of constructs posited not to be related to each other were actually found not to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to each other. Further they showed high reliability with the lowest Cronbach's α at 0.83. Table 1 shows the instrument items employed in the main survey. Each item took the form of a declarative statement suggesting whether needs were being met, or not, with respondents agreeing or disagreeing on a 7-point Likert-type scale.

## 3.1.3. Study 1: main survey

A main study was conducted with the target population of online purchasers who had at least one online purchase experience. A total of 776 subjects were recruited and participated in the study. The participants were compensated with an opportunity to win a sweepstake. Both online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine surveys were developed and participants were allowed to use either based on their preference. After eliminating 87 incomplete responses, a total of 689 complete surveys were gathered. Detailed demographic information is shown in Table 2. Amazon.com was selected as the target website since it is a top-ranked website in Alaxa global 500 rankings with respect to average consumer visits. Other information systems researchers also used the website for their studies, allowing direct and indirect comparison for the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 3.1.4. Study 1: results

A structural equation modeling technique (LISREL) was used to perform a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) to validate psychometric properties of the instruments. The con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis allows for tests to be conducted ensuring ‘goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t’ of the model, and to ensure unidimensionality, convergent validity, discriminant validity and reliability of the instruments. The sample size of this study (n=689) is large enough to meet the sample size requirement of Bentler and Chou [11] to run LISREL.

A goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t of the measurement model was <sup>fi</sup>rst investigated. It was found that several of these statistics for the initial measurement model were below the recommended value (e.g., GFI: 0.854). Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>tness of the model was improved by removing indicators that did not provide minimum factor loading scores (0.7) and conducting a series of model modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. After removing three indicators that show less than 0.7 factor loading scores (see the lists in Table 1 marked with \*\* in front of items.) and adding the relationships between indicators recommended by the modi<sup>fi</sup>cation indexes of LISREL, the best measurement model with excellent goodness of <sup>fi</sup>t was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as shown in Table 3.

Psychometric properties of instruments were tested by examining unidimensionality, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability. Unidimensionality refers to the extent to which indicators are strongly associated with each other and represent a single concept. This is a necessary condition for reliability analysis and constructs validation [4]. As shown in Table 4, unidimensionality was con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by ensuring that t-values of all indicator factor loadings are greater than 2.58, meaning their statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance at p b0.01.

Convergent validity was evaluated using three criteria suggested by Fornell and Larcker [26]. They are: (1) all indicator factor loadings (λ) should be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.05 and exceed 0.7, (2) composite reliabilities should exceed 0.7, and (3) average variance extracted (AVE) by each construct should exceed the variance due to measurement error for that construct. As shown in Table 4, all factor loadings in the CFA model exceeded 0.7 and were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $\mathsf { p } { = } 0 . 0 0 1$ . Composite reliabilities (CFR) ranged between 0.84 and 0.91. AVE ranged between 0.56 and 0.72, greater than the variance due to measurement error. Therefore, all three conditions for convergent validity were met.

Discriminant validity refers to the extent to which a construct and its indicators differ from other constructs and their indicators. Discriminant Validity was assessed by constraining the estimated correlation parameters (ϕ ) between constructs to 1.0 and then performing a chi-square difference test on the values obtained for the constrained and unconstrained models [4]. The chi-square differences between these models were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at pb0.05, indicating a clear discriminant validity among these constructs. The inter-construct correlation matrix shown in Table 5 also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that each construct clearly measures a different concept.

Hence, discriminant validity of the constructs is con<sup>fi</sup>rmed. Finally, reliability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which an individual subject answers the same question in the same way each time, which is assessed using Cronbach's α. All constructs showed over 0.80 Cronbach's alpha, indicating high reliability of items used for each construct.

## 3.2. Study 2. Identifying nomological network of website usability

## 3.2.1. Research design

The goal of Study 2 was to identify the nomological networks between website usability constructs. A total of 82 students who took a website usability class at a large Western university participated in the study. A cognitive mapping technique by Ford and Hegarty [25] was adopted to examine the networks. Cognitive mapping is a method to examine patterns or structures of a respondent's assertion of causality among a set of variables and represent them in a graphical manner. The method has been shown to be relatively stable and provide a basis for prediction. Thus it has been used in a diverse IS context [e.g., 49].

Ford and Hegarty's [25] approach is a quantitative cognitive mapping method that elicits causal linkages among predetermined concepts from respondents. This method <sup>fi</sup>ts the research context since respondents were familiar with the constructs of website usability and the method allows calculating causal linkages between constructs in a numerical format using an aggregated adjacency matrix. The adjacency matrix is a matrix of effects among the constructs where the ij entry indicates the existence and direction of a causality relation from construct i to construct j. An aggregated adjacency matrix is calculated by combining the adjacency matrix of all respondents.

The aggregated adjacency matrix was developed through multistaged procedures suggested by Ford and Hegarty. First, participants were asked to identify relationships among website usability factors and between website usability constructs and online purchase intention after reading the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of each website usability construct. Second, participants were presented with an 11×11 matrix in which the columns and rows were named with the ten website usability constructs and purchase intention. For each pair of constructs, they were asked to specify if there was a causal relation (scored as a 1) and if so, whether the row construct or the column construct was causal by inserting an A (row construct) or B (column construct) in the cell. At the same time, they were asked to add the direction of the relation using+(positive relation) or − (negative relation) signs. When the row and column construct has no relation, 0 was inserted into the cell. Finally, each adjacency matrix is summed to develop the aggregated adjacency matrix. The value in the cell of the aggregated adjacency matrix represents the arithmetic mean.

## 3.2.2. Study 2 results

Appendix C shows the aggregated adjacency matrix. As the cell means approach −1 or +1, this indicates that the vast majority of respondents see the same causal relation. Percentages exhibiting nonzero relations serve two purposes: (1) to identify generalized outdegrees of construct i, which serves as an indicator of the extent to which a construct is seen as the direct cause of other constructs, and (2) identify generalized indegrees of construct j, which serves as an indicator of the extent to which a construct is seen as directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other constructs. The higher the outdegree, the greater the direct causality attributed to the construct, while the higher the indegree, the more the construct is directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other constructs. When generalizing as shown in Appendix C, simplicity (4.97) is the construct with the most direct causality, followed by interactivity (4.82), and consistency (4.63). In addition, purchase intention (6.97) was the most directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced construct, followed by telepresence (5.11), and learnability (5.00). The causal map shown in Fig. 3 was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on the aggregated adjacency matrix.

Following the suggestion of Ford and Hegarty's [25], the highly positive relations (arithmetic mean is equal to or greater than 0.7) and highly negative relations (arithmetic mean is less than −0.7) were included to draw a causal map. The causal map showed several interesting relations. First, the direct in<sup>fl</sup>uences of eight website usability constructs on purchase intention were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The eight constructs were content relevance, credibility, interactivity, learnability, navigability, readability, supportability, and telepresence. Second, many indirect relationships between website usability constructs were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Telepresence was found to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by interactivity, navigability, and simplicity. Navigability and readability were in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by simplicity and consistency. Learnability was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by consistency. The negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of simplicity on interactivity and telepresence was an interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding, indicating that investment in increasing simplicity negatively affect the interactivity and telepresence of a website. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of negative in<sup>fl</sup>uences provide a useful insight to website designers in that they have to make careful resource allocation decisions during website design.

## 4. Discussion and implications

The design of a usable website is pivotal to e-business success. This study identi<sup>fi</sup>ed website usability constructs and developed and validated their measurement instruments. This study also investigated the nomological networks of website usability. A number of direct and indirect relationships between website usability constructs and purchase intention and purchase action were found through a causal mapping analysis and a questionnaire-based <sup>fi</sup>eld study. While there is a challenge in generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the current research, this study successfully demonstrated that the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed constructs have strong psychometric properties and the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nomological networks explain a large amount of variance of purchase intention and purchase action.

As shown in Table 8, this study <sup>fi</sup>rst con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the presence of nomological networks between website usability factors and online customer purchase intention. We found that telepresence, navigability, interactivity, learnability, readability, content relevance and credibility had a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on purchase intention. Content relevance was the strongest factor affecting purchase intention, indicating that providing up-to-date product and price information that has relevant depth and scope and customer review or feedback is crucial for inducing online purchases. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent of those of previous studies [e.g., 20] noting that information quality is crucial particularly for customers who visit websites with utilitarian goals (e.g. purchase products/services). Similar to previous studies [34,38], credibility and telepresence were found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchase intention. Amazon.com has built its reputation as one of the safest online stores by implementing a variety of security systems and enforcing privacy protection policies. Thus customers can perceive safety while purchasing products from the site. Amazon also has provided customers sense of presence in its online store by implementing features including a shopping cart, customizable homepage, and 360 °rotating images of products. Further, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of learnability, readability and navigability indicate that customers' online purchases are also affected by ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency experienced from websites (e.g. easy to search, move around, read, understand and remember). The value of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient website design has often been emphasized by e-business proponents who argue convenience and time-savings as two most critical success factors of ebusiness (e.g., [35,36]).

Finally, interactivity was found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchase intention. One major contributor of its signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence is the abundance of social networking features embedded in e-business sites. As social networking features including Facebook, Youtube, and Twitter become pervasive, e-business sites have rushed to integrate these social networking features into their websites, enabling enhanced interactive communications between consumers or between consumers and companies. Adding social networking features also augments site credibility by providing online customers alternative trustful sources of information rendered by other social networkers. People can build more trust toward a website when information provided by e-business is reinforced by other social networkers. Given these positive impacts of social networking features on interactivity and credibility, researchers [7] and practitioners [51,57] supported their addition into e-business sites.

This study also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the presence of nomological networks between website usability constructs. Especially, simplicity and consistency were found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on interactivity, telepresence, navigability, learnability and readability. This implies that although their direct effects on individuals' purchase intention were not observed, simplicity and consistency indeed crucially affected online purchases via other usability factors by positively and negatively interacting with them. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with those of HCI researchers who recognized simplicity and consistency as two golden rules for usable interface design [50,64]. The positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of simplicity on interactivity was a surprising <sup>fi</sup>nding, which reverses the <sup>fi</sup>nding of the causal map analysis. One possible explanation is that Amazon.com could achieve interactivity and simplicity at the same time through having minimum redundant features, simple but interactive search, and small amounts of content on a page. The personalized check out process and customized recommendation lists also may increase online customers' positive perceptions of both simplicity and interactivity. However, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence of simplicity on telepresence indicated that simplicity prevents customers from being immersed in online shopping.

This study provides several important <sup>fi</sup>ndings for both researchers and practitioners. From a theoretical perspective, this study <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to provide a comprehensive investigation of the multifaceted dimensions of website usability. Reliable and scienti<sup>fi</sup>- cally validated instruments of website usability enable developing consensus among researchers on how to assess website usability and coherently examine the structure and dimensionality of website usability. The instruments can be utilized in future usability studies and accumulated <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be directly compared. Agreeing with the importance of website usability instrument development, Green and Pearson [29] stated “it has only been recently that information systems literature addressed the need to examine website usability in understanding success in e-commerce. The development of instrument items to measure website usability allows e-business managers to make comparisons, benchmark performance, and plan improvements. Usability is increasingly important as organizations compete in the always changing, hypercompetitive online markets. Therefore, having valid and reliable instruments to measure website usability is important for both business and research.” (p. 35). Second, this study proposes and validates nomological networks between website usability constructs, absent from previous theoretical models of website usability. By examining the interactions among usability constructs, we can capture the effect of each construct precisely. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nomological networks can thus be used to enhance the existing theoretical models or develop alternative theoretical models of website usability to provide a better understanding of the website usability phenomenon. Finally, while more validation needs to be done, the proposed model can be considered as an alternative theoretical model of website usability. Through a series of empirical tests, the model was validated to explain a large amount of variances on customer online purchase intention and purchases, implying that the model can be utilized for future studies to successfully measure the effect of website usability constructs on online purchases.

This study also has several practical implications. The proposed model assists practitioners to identify website usability factors where they need to put extra resources for increasing online sales. For instance, the measurement items we developed can be used by e-business managers to establish benchmarks by evaluating the usability level of their own websites and compare them with their competitors. In addition, the newly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed nomological networks permit website designers to better understand complex interactions between web usability constructs and gage the true amount of contribution of each construct on online purchase. In particular, the networks provide useful insights to website designers in that usability constructs do not work independently but interact with each other to attract customers to buy products/services online. By closely observing dynamic interactions between usability constructs, website designers can successfully allocate their limited resources to the more impactful website usability factors. The scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of website usability constructs on online purchases also assists website designers to persuade e-business managers to invest more resources to improve the design quality of their websites. Finally, the model can be used as a standard industry design guideline which helps develop usable websites across e-commerce domains.

This study has several limitations which need to be revisited in fu ture studies. First, the single target website used in this study cannot represent all e-business domains. Previous studies in information systems and the marketing area [e.g., 9,12] have found that individual consumer's belief, attitude, and behavior were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by different products, industries, and technologies. Future replication studies with broader e-business domains are recommended. Second, the proposed constructs are primarily measuring cognitive aspects of website usability, not affective aspects. Several researchers have alluded to the ability of a website to engender emotional responses among users [53,69]. For example, van der Heijden [69] asserted that website developers should put more effort into including esthetically appealing visual layouts. Therefore, it is recommended that future studies identify constructs of affective usability factors (e.g., playfulness, <sup>fl</sup>ow, or visual esthetics) and develop respective instruments. Finally, to gather a large amount of data, this study used both an online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine questionnaire, which might in<sup>fl</sup>uence user response. Although it was found that there were no relevant differences in the psychometric properties of the different questionnaires e.g., [59], careful attention to control sampling methods and assess measurement validity should be pursued in future studies.

## 5. Conclusions

A critical understanding of perceptions and purchase behavior of consumers cannot be done without a suitable appreciation of website usability. Arguing that the usability of a website is a fundamental component of the entire user experience, this study develops website usability constructs and measurement instruments and investigates their nomological networks useful to both researchers and practitioners. Findings of this study are expected to be used for successfully measuring the design quality of websites and be used as building blocks for developing a strong theoretical model of website usability.

## Appendix D. Online purchasing scenario

Let's assume that you are a customer who wants to buy a new [cellular phone] at [Amazon.com]. Please follow the scenario and complete the whole purchasing process.

1. Go to [Amazon.com] and take a look at the homepage for three minutes. You can read the content, and navigate the website using menus or hyperlinks. Please make sure you are familiar with the structure, features, and design of [Amazon.com] website.

2. Find the web pages of [cellular phones] by either using a search engine or clicking the hyperlinks.

3. Take 5 min to select your target [cellular phone]. You may compare features, images, service plans, service providers, and rebate information of the candidate [cellular phones].

4. Go to the target [cellular phone] webpage, click customer images, read the product description, special promotion, and customer reviews in detail.

5. Click Help to see customer support information.

6. Proceed to Checkout. Enter all information that you need to complete the checkout process. It would be <sup>fi</sup>ne to provide address, credit card info, shipping method until you encounter the ‘Place Order’ screen.

7. Read ‘Privacy Notice’, ‘Return Policy’ and ‘Conditions of Use’.

8. Spend two or three minutes to check whether features (e.g., content, structure, color, buttons, hyperlinks, etc.) of this website are easy-to-learn, consistent, concise, and readable.
