# An afordance lens for wearable information systems

Raquel Benbunan-Fich

Baruch College, City University of New York, New York, NY, USA

## ABSTRACT

The proliferation of wearable technologies calls for the development of conceptual lenses to understand the drivers of their success. This research employs an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens rooted in the individual perspective of Activity Theory to examine personal information systems built around wearable sensors. Since wearable devices have size constraints, designers have produced a variety of simple technology products lacking screens and buttons, but supplemented with customised apps. Due to their novelty, the impact of these minimalist wearables on the quality of the user experience is not yet well understood. To investigate this issue, this study proposes a theoretically driven framework of a<sup>f</sup>ordances and applies it to qualitatively analyse a sample of online user reviews from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker wristband. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that minimalist wearables produce a more complex user experience if a<sup>f</sup>ordances are not properly balanced and combined. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c usability challenges stem from a<sup>f</sup>ordance integration failures and inconsistencies. These results have theoretical and practical implications. Overall, an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens o<sup>f</sup>ers a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed view of user experience that is valuable for researchers and practitioners seeking to understand what makes modern wearable information systems transparent to use at the physical and digital level.

## 1. Introduction

According to industry estimates, wearables are a global phenomenon with a large following. Projection <sup>fi</sup>gures indicate that the worldwide wearables market will reach a total of 240 million units in 2021 (IDC, 2017). While wearables encompass any device that is worn on the body in the form of clothing, shoes, eyewear or jewellery, our focus herein is on devices worn on the wrist. The two most popular wrist-worn products are smart-watches and <sup>fi</sup>tness tracking wristbands. Unlike smart-watches that can run di<sup>f</sup>erent apps to extend their functionality, <sup>fi</sup>tness trackers are single-function devices with limited capabilities.

Wearable technology is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as miniature bodyborne computational and sensory devices, designed to be attached to the body, as opposed to temporarily handled when needed to perform an action, as is the case for other technology tools, such as smart phones or laptops (Mann, 2005). From the perspective of the individual user, wearables are more than just an additional device to carry around and add to the personal technology portfolio. Wearable technology creates a new type of Individual Information System, which is generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Baskerville (2011, p. 1) as “an activity system in which individual persons, according to idiosyncratic needs and preferences perform processes and activities using information, technology, and other resources to produce informational products and/or services for themselves or others”.

Despite their limitations, single-function wearables are the most popular category and account for almost 83% of the market. Fitness wristbands – the focus of this study – dominate the market in terms of unit sales (Gagliordi, 2016). The leading company in this market segment is Fitbit with di<sup>f</sup>erent wristband models o<sup>f</sup>ering various features and price points (Gagliordi, 2016; Schüll, 2016).

In the last few years, many users have joined the “<sup>fi</sup>tness tracker craze” (Rettner, 2013), due to the popularity of the quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed-self movement, whereby people seek to self-monitor their own physical activity to improve quality of life (Gilmore, 2015; Schüll, 2016; Swan, 2013). This “tracker mania” is also fuelled by technology advances, such as the availability and a<sup>f</sup>ordability of low-cost sensors with internet connectivity embedded in wearable items (Prasopoulou, 2017). As is often the case with other technology innovations where adoption occurs at a faster pace, research lags in trying to understand actual practices. Since the adoption of this kind of individual information system is voluntary, and user training is not possible, it is not clear how nontechnical users are able to easily operate and seamlessly integrate wearables, particularly minimalist wearables, into their normal activities. Thus, the research question that motivates this study is how mainstream users incorporate wearables into their everyday life and whether they derive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they expect.

From a functional perspective, wrist-worn <sup>fi</sup>tness sensors collect physical activity metrics by estimating steps based on arm movements, and wirelessly transmit data to the Internet where they are aggregated, and displayed by specially designed software programmes. As a result of this division between physical (wristband) and digital (software and data) artefacts, the transition between its components should be seamless (Hindman, 2015). In fact, wearable devices deliver the promised functionality when all their components (sensor, data, and software) work well together as a cohesive information system that can be seamlessly inserted into the personal technology portfolio (Fernando, 2015).

From a lifestyle perspective, wearable devices are successful when they are easily integrated into everyday activities (Vella, 2013), and achieve the ultimate goal for which quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of physical movement is required, such as lose weight, or improve <sup>fi</sup>tness (Patel, Asch, & Volpp, 2015). We propose that a necessary condition for the user to achieve the e<sup>f</sup>ects of self-quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation is her ability to interact with all components of the wearable information system. Furthermore, we argue that minimalist wearables, where the physical device lacks screen and buttons, are discreet to wear but present usability challenges, as users must <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to operate them without any clues. Even if e<sup>f</sup>ortless interaction is achieved, the data collected by the device must be meaningful for the user to achieve their lifestyle improvement goals (Fernando, 2015; Vella, 2013).

To examine how regular users integrate minimalist <sup>fi</sup>tness trackers into their lives, this study adopts an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens based on the mediated-action perspective of Activity Theory (AT). It proposes an adaptation of the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework originally developed by Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) and applies to a sample of online user reviews collected during the initial 15-month period after launch of a popular minimalist <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker (Fitbit Flex). In addition to o<sup>f</sup>ering insights on how everyday users embrace minimalist wearables, this research also expands the conceptual and empirical boundaries of AT in Information Systems, with the concept of a<sup>f</sup>ordances and the use of online reviews (ie, user-generated content) as data. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The next section presents the theoretical background and the adaptation of the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework. The following section explains the research methods, sample, and data analysis. After describing the results and the statistical analyses, the paper closes with discussion, implications for theory and practice, and concluding thoughts.

## 2. Theoretical background

The concept of a<sup>f</sup>ordance has received considerable attention in various academic <sup>fi</sup>elds. The origin of the term can be traced to Gibson (1986), who used the verb aford (v.) to refer to what the environment o<sup>f</sup>ers to an organism – to what it provides or furnishes, for good or ill – and the noun afordance to capture the complementarity of a subject (a human being or an animal) and its environment. Gibson notes that “[t]he verb to a<sup>f</sup>ord is found in the dictionary but the noun a<sup>f</sup>ordance is not” (p. 127). Gibson’s original de<sup>fi</sup>nition and examples associate a<sup>f</sup>ordances with physical properties of the environment but recognise that a<sup>f</sup>ordances are not just abstract physical properties; they are unique to the subject because subjects anticipate the a<sup>f</sup>ordances in their environment and act accordingly. As Leonardi (2011) explains, in Gibson’s formulation, “people do not interact with an object prior to or without perceiving what the object is good for” (p. 153).

Norman (1988) introduced the term a<sup>f</sup>ordance to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) to designate elements that provide clues on the operation of things. In Norman’s interpretation, a<sup>f</sup>ordances are considered properties of an artefact, whose presence in the design of man-made objects suggest functionality and use via action possibilities. A<sup>f</sup>ordances serve as means to understand the usability of artefacts and their user interface. Norman’s interpretation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances diverged from the original intent of Gibson’s concept by overlooking the organism–environment complementarity and placing the emphasis on designed-in a<sup>f</sup>ordances as opposed to a range of action possibilities.

In follow up work, Norman (1999) highlighted the importance of distinguishing between perceived and real a<sup>f</sup>ordances in artefacts. A real a<sup>f</sup>ordance is about physical characteristics of a device or interface that allow its operation, whereas a perceived a<sup>f</sup>ordance is about the features in the appearance of an artefact that suggest its proper operation. Other authors have proposed additional quali<sup>fi</sup>ers to further separate the di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of a<sup>f</sup>ordances in the design of artefacts. Building on Norman’s distinction, Hartson (2003) introduces a four-way classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances: cognitive (Norman’s perceived a<sup>f</sup>ordance); physical (Norman’s real a<sup>f</sup>ordance); and two additional classi<sup>fi</sup>cations, sensory and functional. A sensory a<sup>f</sup>ordance enables the user to sense (eg, see, hear) something and thus supports physical and cognitive a<sup>f</sup>ordances in design. A functional a<sup>f</sup>ordance helps the user in doing something. By relating the user to the purpose of the system, this a<sup>f</sup>ordance expands the concept beyond user interface design to the larger context of overall system purpose. Despite the distinctions among these four types, Hartson (2003) advances the notion that a<sup>f</sup>ordances form a nexus because they work together.

Alternative interpretations have reconciled the dichotomy between real and perceived a<sup>f</sup>ordances by focusing on the relational notion of a<sup>f</sup>ordances (Hutchby, 2001; Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008; Vyas, Chisalita, & Dix, 2017). This perspective recognises that a<sup>f</sup>ordances are realised in the interaction of organisms with objects in their environment. In this conceptualisation, a<sup>f</sup>ordances are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the possibilities for goal-directed action provided by an object in relation to a goal-oriented actor (Markus & Silver, 2008). The relational interpretation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances has been theoretically grounded in AT by Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) and in Adaptive Structuration Theory (Markus & Silver, 2008; Vyas et al., 2017). Because of their underpinnings, each of these theories is more appropriate for a particular context. While the view of a<sup>f</sup>ordances in Adaptive Structuration Theory is mostly useful for the study of technology within organisational contexts, the mediated-action perspective of AT is particularly well suited for the study of artefacts voluntarily adopted by individuals for use outside organisations.

## 2.1. Activity theory

AT is a meta-theory that emphasises motivation and purposefulness of human endeavours, and recognises the role of technological tools as mediators of human activities (Kuutti, 1991; Nardi, 1996). In a broad sense, the concept of activity refers to the interaction of a subject with the world. This interaction is described as a process that relates the subject or person (P) and the object (O), depicted as $\mathrm { { } ^ { \mathfrak { s } } P \Leftrightarrow O ^ { \mathfrak { s } } }$ Activities and their subjects mutually determine one another. Objects represent the forces that motivate and direct human activity. Human activity is accomplished by sets of actions using tools (T), which can be physical or psychological, such as mental models or language (Allen, Brown, Karanasios, & Norman, 2013; Karanasios & Allen, 2014; Kuuti 1991; Nardi, 1996). An activity is mediated by the physical and psychological tools used, and at the same time, the tool evolves to capture the historical knowledge of how society works (Nardi, 1996). According to Hasan and Kazlauskas (2014), tools can be primary (physical), secondary (language, ideas, models, etc.) or tertiary (communities, context, or environments).

In general, AT o<sup>f</sup>ers an overarching framework to understand the consciousness of human activity in activity systems and their intended purposes. Some scholars point out that AT is a high-level theory with a set of well-articulated concepts for descriptive purposes instead of speci<sup>fi</sup>c prescriptions (Nardi, 1996). In terms of Gregor’s (2006) classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of IS theories, AT provides a lens for analysis and explanation of the world. In Information Systems, AT has been used to conceptualise IS use (Barki, Titah, & Bo<sup>f</sup>o, 2007), model knowledge acquisition through Enterprise

Information Portals (Ryu, Jin, Chaudhury, & Rao, 2005), understand changes in mobile technologymediated work (Karanasios & Allen 2014, Kietzmann, 2008), and develop data models for emergency management (Chen, Sharman, Rao, & Upadhyaya, 2013). According to Karanasios (2018), AT in IS research can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two distinct streams, one devoted to understanding IS intervention and use (eg, Barki et al., 2007; Ryu et al., 2005) and the other more connected with the <sup>fi</sup>elds of design and development, and the technical side of IS (eg, Chen et al., 2013; Karanasios and Allen 2013, Kietzmann, 2008). This study belongs in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stream (use of IT) but unlike much of prior work in this area, our focus is on an individual activity system.

Initially, AT was conceptualised at an individual level. This theory traces its origins to the work of Vygotsky (1978) and Leont’ev (1978), and the meaning of mediation in cultural historical psychology. Later, Engeström (1987) extended the basic Person-Technology-Object triangle to incorporate rules, community, and division of labour, and provide a more complex framework to understand collective activities (Allen et al., 2013). The individual activity variant of AT is also known as the mediated-action perspective (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2012). In an individual activity system, a subject (or person) is motivated to act upon an object using cultural-historical tools or technologies (Allen et al., 2013; Karanasios & Allen, 2014).

Since the individual conceptualisation of AT predates the proliferation of Information Technology as we know it today, the original proponents of this theory could not visualise digital technology, nor its importance in society. As Ruckriem (2009) suggests, for AT to continue to be a useful theory in the digital age, it needs to expand its methodological and empirical boundaries to accommodate an everevolving landscape of technology tools. Consistent with this view, contemporary scholars, such as V. Kaptelinin and B. Nardi have embraced the individual perspective of AT as the foundation to understand how human actors engage with technology in their activities.

In this area, Kaptelinin and Nardi’s (2012) conceptualisation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances based on the mediated action perspective o<sup>f</sup>ers a speci<sup>fi</sup>c lens to understand the dynamics of technology-mediation in human activities. Drawing on the three basic elements of an individual activity system (person, tools, and object), they conceptualise a<sup>f</sup>ordances as a relational property of the interaction between the Person (P), the mediational means given by the Technology (T), and the Object (O) on which the technology is expected to have an e<sup>f</sup>ect. The Person is an individual in the activity system and the Object is the entity on which an e<sup>f</sup>ect is expected to occur. Objects can be physical things, or intangible concepts that objectively exist in the world. The object can be understood as the motives of the activity or as the a<sup>f</sup>ected entity. To settle this potential ambiguity, Kaptelinin (2005) suggests de<sup>fi</sup>ning the object of activity as “the sensemaker” or “ultimate reason” that gives meaning to all the other possible motives associated with an activity.

Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) propose that in the P-T-O relation, a<sup>f</sup>ordances are enacted in the direct manipulation of the technology by the person (P⇔T), and in the indirect e<sup>f</sup>ect that the technology is expected to have on an object (T⇔O). The former are called handling a<sup>f</sup>ordances, and the latter efecter a<sup>f</sup>ordances. These two types of a<sup>f</sup>ordances are considered instrumental because they complete the cycle of action of the technology as an instrument. For complex technology artefacts, Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) use the example of mobile phones and identify the existence of auxiliary or supplemental a<sup>f</sup>ordances, which allow for the maintenance or upkeep of the technology instrument (eg, charging and cleaning the device), enable its aggregation with other artefacts (eg, internet connectivity), and support individuals when learning how to use the technology (eg, didactic information on how to operate the artefacts to achieve a goal). ## 2.2. Activity theory and a<sup>f</sup>ordances in wearable devices

Wearable technology is the foundation to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a new type of what Baskerville (2011) calls individual information system because it is primarily intended for personal use. Unlike other individual systems, wearables are designed to function in an unobtrusive way by operating in the background, passively so they can be seamlessly integrated into everyday activities (Gilmore, 2015; Prasopoulou, 2017). Wearable devices act as a prosthetic by forming a true extension of the user’s mind and body (Mann, 2005). This prostheticlike quality is the best illustration of tools as “functional organs” originally proposed by Kaptelinin (1996, p. 55) to highlight how humans use technology tools to extend their capabilities and “perform a new function, or perform existing functions more e<sup>fi</sup>- ciently” (Kaptelinin, 1996, p. 56).

The use of passive <sup>fi</sup>tness trackers is not associated with any speci<sup>fi</sup>c human activity. Instead, the tracker is a mediator of normal everyday activities. In this type of wearable technology mediation, the object upon which an individual seeks to exert an e<sup>f</sup>ect is his/her own body instead of a separate entity in the environment. Wearables provide supplemental insights about oneself via digital selftracking (Gilmore, 2015; Schüll, 2016; Swan, 2013). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in the case of a wearable <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker, its use is expected to have an impact on the wearer’s own body (or mind) with respect to <sup>fi</sup>tness goals and an active lifestyle. In the original P-T-O model, when the object is the person (self), T circles back to P to produce an e<sup>f</sup>ect, and transforms the e<sup>f</sup>ecter a<sup>f</sup>ordance into a self-efecter. Self-e<sup>f</sup>ecter a<sup>f</sup>ordances are related to motivational a<sup>f</sup>ordances. These a<sup>f</sup>ordances determine whether technology artefacts can support one’s motivational needs, in areas, such as psychological, social, cognitive, and emotional (Karahanna, Xu, Xu, & Zhang, in press; Zhang, 2008). While the motivation and the object are related, we follow Kaptelinin’s (2005) distinction and de<sup>fi</sup>ne the object (O) as the mind/body of the wearer (self), and the motivation as the desire to improve one’s lifestyle. This distinction clearly separates the a<sup>f</sup>ected entity (self) from the motives.

The two main properties of wearable technology with respect to form (body-worn) and function (collecting personal health or <sup>fi</sup>tness metrics) dictate two modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the original a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework. First, to capture the property of being tethered to the body, we propose to separate the handling a<sup>f</sup>ordance in the original framework into two separate aspects: wearing and interacting with the technology. Because the mediator is an extension of the self, wearing matters. Second, we propose a revision to the concept of object to properly account for the particulars of human activities when health or <sup>fi</sup>tness wearables are involved. Since these wearables are passive, they do not interfere with normal routine activities carried out by wearers, nor do they require a physical activity to improve <sup>fi</sup>tness, as it would be the case for a running app. Thus, we argue that the general motivation is to improve one’s health or lifestyle by becoming more active in the course of everyday activities. Although contemporary research has shown a wide range of motivational forces for using <sup>fi</sup>tness wearables (see eg, Etkin, 2016; James, Wallace, & Deane, in press), they are mostly related to the goal of improving the user’s physical wellbeing. Because the ultimate reason is to a<sup>f</sup>ect oneself, e<sup>f</sup>ecter is re<sup>fl</sup>exive, and the object is the wearer’s own body (or mind).

## 2.3. Research on wearable devices

An emerging body of research is beginning to evaluate the success of wearable <sup>fi</sup>tness trackers. The extant literature in this area can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into conceptual, technical, and empirical studies. The latter category includes studies focused on gathering quantitative or qualitative data about device usability (Altenho<sup>f</sup> et al. 2015; Mackinlay, 2013); accuracy of monitoring data (Lee, Kim, & Welk, 2014; Patel et al., 2015); and motivational e<sup>f</sup>ects resulting from data awareness and quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation (Etkin, 2016; James et al., in press), or from individual (Prasopoulou, 2017) or social rewards when activity data is shared with friends or with others in online communities (Fritz, Huang, Murphy, & Zimmermann, 2014). By evaluating actual users in real life contexts, these studies underscore the importance of collecting experiential data to understand what makes wearables successful. This is consistent with Yoo’s (2010) recommendations to study “humans qua users” employing information technologies in everyday settings. Accordingly, this study collects experiential data to understand the challenges that ordinary users encounter with a minimalist <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker.

## 3. Research methods

The method adopted in this study consists of analysing the content of online reviews to gather experiential data from real users of a wearable product. A number of studies have demonstrated the informational value of online user reviews (eg, Ghose & Ipeirotis, 2011, Mcauley & Yang, 2016; Mudambi & Schu<sup>f</sup>, 2010), but to the best of our knowledge, no other study has leveraged online reviews from an activity theoretic perspective. While contemporary work by Gleasure and Morgan (2018) has demonstrated how the analysis of user-generated content can be used within an activity theoretic framework, their study uses data from a crowdsourcing platform and a grounded theory approach. In contrast, our study employs a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of user generated content – online reviews – and examines its textual content with an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens and the perspective of AT.

## 3.1. Product selection

The product selected for this study is a <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker wristband with a simple design from Fitbit, the top brand in the market (Pressman, 2017). This particular model (called Flex) was launched for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in April 2013, and it is at the low end of the price spectrum. The wristband shows only <sup>fi</sup>ve minuscule light-emitting diodes (or LEDs), and lacks visible buttons or controls. The absence of obvious design a<sup>f</sup>ordances makes Fitbit Flex an intriguing product to examine (see product description in Appendix A). The only visible clues of its operation in the wristband are <sup>fi</sup>ve LED indicators that light up progressively to show percentage of goal completed during the day. The lights remain o<sup>f</sup> unless the user taps twice on the device to check their progress. When the step goal for the day has been achieved the device vibrates and shows all the lights on. The device can also track sleep. This mode is activated when the user performs a series of rapid taps on the wristband. The device vibrates and <sup>fl</sup>ashes the lights in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c sequence to indicate that the mode has been successfully changed from active to sleep tracking (Johnston, 2013). In terms of Hartson’s typology (2003), the two key device a<sup>f</sup>ordances are sensory (visual and vibration).

## 3.2. Sample

To investigate how users interact with the selected wearable, a sample of online reviews was extracted from the Amazon review dataset compiled by Mcauley and Yang (2016). A custom-developed programme selected reviews of Fitbit Flex with 300 words or more during the <sup>fi</sup>rst 15 months after product launch (April 2013 until July 2014). The word length <sup>fi</sup>lter (300 words or more) was established to ensure that the reviews contained enough textual information for qualitative analysis (ie, at least half a page of text). The review date <sup>fi</sup>lter was implemented to secure user experiences ranging from short to long term use. A total of 373 reviews met our selection criteria based on date and word length. The reviews in the sample have an average length of 494 words (± 235 words), and an average rating of 3.5 stars (out of <sup>fi</sup>ve). Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics of the sample and by rating categories.

## 3.3. Content analysis

The text of the reviews was content-analysed using QSR NVivo 11 Pro. Content analysis consists of making inferences from written text, using quantitative and qualitative techniques (Krippendor<sup>f</sup>, 2004). Quantitative content analysis entails counting manifest textual elements, such as words, whereas qualitative content analysis involves identifying unique themes to illustrate the meanings or facets of the phenomenon under investigation. Our content analysis approach leveraged quantitative and qualitative techniques and consisted of two levels: macro analysis via open coding and micro analysis via directed coding.

In the macro analysis phase, the text of the reviews was examined to identify emerging themes (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Using open coding, we catalogued how people express their expectations, concerns, and needs regarding this wearable. Leveraging the repetition technique (Ryan & Bernard, 2003), themes that appeared recurrently in the reviews were annotated. Theme identi<sup>fi</sup>cation was based on the semantic information embedded in the text, despite wording di<sup>f</sup>erences. For example, users refer to the closure mechanism of the band with di<sup>f</sup>erent words (clasp, prongs, closure, etc.) but they were all referring to the same theme (ie, wearing the band).

In the micro analysis phase, two research assistants coded excerpts of text related to the a<sup>f</sup>ordances described in the framework (directed coding). To ensure reliability, they worked independently to code 10% of the sample (37 reviews) simultaneously, according to the formal de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and examples included in a codebook (see Appendix B). The comparison of this parallel coding provided the basis to determine inter-coding reliability, as recommended by Lombard, Snyder-Duch, and Bracken (2004). Reliability coe<sup>fi</sup>cients calculated with Cohen’s kappa were 0.80, 0.78, 0.72, 0.89, 0.72, and 0.84 for wearing, interacting, self-e<sup>f</sup>ecter, maintenance, aggregation, and learning, respectively. Di<sup>f</sup>erences in categorisation were discussed openly to identify the reasons for divergent interpretations and a <sup>fi</sup>nal consensus classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was adjudicated. The rest of the sample was divided between the two assistants to <sup>fi</sup>nish the coding. Each coded excerpt (or stripe) was further tagged with a sign (−, +, or 0) to indicate its valence (negative, positive, or neutral/ descriptive, respectively). Appendix C shows a quantitative summary of the directed coding phase.

## 4. Findings

This section presents the general themes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from a holistic reading of the reviews (macro level), followed by the results of the analysis of a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures (micro level).

## 4.1. Thematic analysis

Through the open coding process, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two self-indicator themes and <sup>fi</sup>ve wearable experience themes (see Table 2). The self-indicator themes correspond to the descriptive characteristics of the user/reviewer and the lifestyle or context surrounding device use. Users disclosed personal demographics, such as gender, age range, and details of their occupation, such as whether they were o<sup>fi</sup>ce workers, stay-at-home moms, or college students. In addition, they included details of their physical activities and the extent to which they engaged in daily exercise, or a more sedentary lifestyle (eg, “desk-jockey” or “mouse-potato”). This self-disclosure of personal characteristics contextualises the experiential account, which is more directly related to how the device is inserted into the user’s personal life.

The <sup>fi</sup>ve wearable experience themes describe different aspects of device use in the user’s personal context. The most recurrent themes were related to monitoring accuracy, wearability of the band, and achievement of physical activity goals. These themes are related to how instrumental a<sup>f</sup>ordances enable users to digitally track their physical activities. The other two themes are more technical and refer to the battery power of the sensor and device connectivity issues (synchronisation with phone app). Because these two themes refer to the way the technology operates, they correspond to the supplemental category of a<sup>f</sup>ordances.

## 4.2. A<sup>f</sup>ordance coding

The results of the direct coding phase (summarised in Appendix C) show evidence of the applicability of the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework. While only a quarter of coded excerpts were tagged with a negative sign, we analyse them to identify cases of a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures in the instrumental or supplemental category. After the within afordance analysis, we highlight instances where the codes involve more than one category and use this cross-a<sup>f</sup>ordance analysis as the basis to detect additional failures.

## 4.2.1. Instrumental afordance failures

An instrumental a<sup>f</sup>ordance failure constrains the user’s ability to attain the goals that motivated the use of the technology. Cases of a<sup>f</sup>ordance failure in user-interaction or interacting a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures are also a source of constraint. Users interact with the band via taps and the band responds with vibrations when there is a change of mode between active and sleep tracking, or when the daily goal has been achieved. Goal achievement is also indicated with all lights <sup>fl</sup>ashing. Overall, the device itself is non-informative due to the lack of a numeric display showing steps:

“[T]here is no display like every other Fitbit has. It only has little dots that light up at 20 percent of your goal. so, you can’t see how many steps, calories, stairs, or the time. and it doesn’t even track flights of stairs! [sic]”

Aside from limitations associated with the light display, users are unsure on how to communicate with the device by tapping to check progress, or about the numbers of taps required to change tracking modes:

“To control the functions of the Flex you have to tap the wrist band or the unit itself and maybe I’m not doing it right but it always seems to take a random number of taps to get what I want”.

To add to the confusion, some arm movements are interpreted as taps and the tracker is put in sleep mode accidentally:

“Also, when hitting bumps or going fast on the bike, the band will vibrate a lot to notify me it has changed into the sleep setting. I then have to tap it to get it back to recording my steps. . .”

These shortcomings constrain the user’s ability to properly monitor the current step count. Another constraint stems from the fact that sleep mode activation (intentional or accidental) is manual. Consequently, the user must remember to change modes for proper tracking:

“Sleep mode is a pain. I forget to enable it probably 25% of the time. Wish it would automatically detect non-movement and enable sleep mode like the jawbone”.

Taken together, the “language of the flashing lights” and the Morse-like requirements of the unique tapping code at best decrease the quality of the user experience, and at worst prevent the user from taking advantage of the wearable.

“I gave this 3 stars because while it works well the display on the Flex is pretty minimal and the control for the unit is done by tapping it which is a pretty nebulous process most of the time”.

In addition to interaction-related failures, there are also instances of a<sup>f</sup>ordance failure in wearing. These are mostly related to band aesthetics and material durability:

“I have had my Fitbit for a month and a half and the seam where the plastic of the band meets the translucent viewing area has started to separate”.

Aside from band tear, the bracelet closure mechanism is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to master, particularly when the band is new. However, over time the slots for the clasps soften and the wristband accidentally opens, which results in lost devices:

“First, the band is futzy [sic] and virtually impossible to put on. Some of the worst industrial design I’ve seen in years. And when it is on it doesn’t stay on”.

Another wearability issue relates to moisture buildup in the band due to its exposure to water or sweat:

“The wristband and Flex are water resistant, but the band traps water and sweat inside the cavity that holds the Flex. Washing hands, taking a shower, and sweating will result in a plethora of bacteria building up inside the band”.

Together, understanding how to communicate with the device (interacting) and being able to carry it on all the time and over time (wearing) constrain the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of the technology as a mediator.

In the self-e<sup>f</sup>ecter category, a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures refer to both data feedback and the motivational e<sup>f</sup>ects of wearing the band. The most salient failure with respect to data collected by the device is low accuracy:

“The only reason I didn’t give this 5 stars is because of the wiggle room with accuracy. I don’t like how moving my wrist a lot adds to the step count. That’s misleading!”

Despite issues regarding accuracy, the motivational e<sup>f</sup>ects of the device directly on the user, or indirectly via the social features (online community and/or competition), are highlighted in the reviews:

“As far as accuracy, who can really be sure? What it is doing, however, is forcing me to get out and get moving. I’ve upped my walks, and I take little breaks during work to walk around a bit. It’s making me conscious of how much I am moving, and, accurate or not, that can’t be a bad thing”.

For other users, the motivational power comes primarily from the social features, via competition with friends or support from online user communities. As one reviewer put it:

“The disclaimer: wearing this is not going to make you fit. It’s a device that should help your motivation. YOU make yourself fit”.

## 4.2.2. Supplemental afordance failures

Supplemental a<sup>f</sup>ordances are necessary for the reliable operation of the device, and ful<sup>fi</sup>l its mediator role. Thus, failures in any of the three categories would prevent the device from helping users reach their goals. In maintenance, the challenges are mostly related to the charging process via a proprietary cable and the duration of the battery after each charge (ie, battery life):

“It takes a really long time to charge, as well–and even then it only lasts a few days”.

When there is moisture or dirt around the sensor, the charging process is unsuccessful:

“The contacts on the actual device have also gotten pretty dirty and have had to be cleaned out in order to charge correctly. This is due to the fact that the device slides into the wrist band and sweat and dirt and moisture build up inside. This to me is one of the worst design aspects of the device”.

The minimalist display is not helpful for checking battery status.

“No battery icon; have to check PC or phone to see remaining”.

In aggregation, a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures also prevent the device from achieving complete functionality and thus compromise its mediator role. Failures in this category refer to synchronisation glitches with compatible devices, or incompatibility with some smartphone models lacking Bluetooth4:

“I have a smart phone but it’s not one that Fitbits sync with so I couldn’t even look at my phone for the number”.

For Android phones there are some additional reliability issues with syncing:

“Android Phone sync not dependable (don’t worry, you never lose you data [sic], seems like I have to sync it several times before it works)”.

The other aspect of aggregation is the ability to see the data through the Fitbit software, delivered via a web dashboard or a mobile app. Since the features of both software versions are di<sup>f</sup>erent, some users prefer one over the other:

“I find that I have to use the Fitbit web portal to log most of my activities more easily than the bland app”.

In learning, a<sup>f</sup>ordance failures re<sup>fl</sup>ect the challenges encountered when users try to use the device for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time. The absence of printed instructions on the package, and the requirement to go to the Fitbit website, imposed an additional step on a new user eager to learn:

“I received a Fitbit Flex as a gift, knowing little about it. It does not come with a user manual. I went to the web site where I wasted my time finding out how unnecessarily complicated it is to use Fitbit and how little it actually does”.

Inadequate instructions prevent users from setting up the device according to their personal characteristics to improve tracking.

“The setup was a bit challenging and should of ofered [sic] more instructions setting it up. One of the reviews, I’ve read you needed to figure out and input your stride length”.

Furthermore, since instructions are only available online, the learning process is fragmented, and users experience knowledge gaps in mastering the device:

“I keep having to refer to the on-line guide to remember how to put the thing into sleep mode, how to wake it up, and what it means when various lights are flashing”.

Other gaps are related to the functionality of the device:

“I owned this device for two weeks before I realized there was an alarm on it. I’m not sure what other things I’m missing, because there is no simple manual”.

The continuous nature of the learning process generates frustration:

“You just learn as you go, which personally drives me crazy”.

## 4.2.3. Cross-afordance analysis

Some coded excerpts are related to two di<sup>f</sup>erent a<sup>f</sup>ordances. A closer analysis of the codes shows that a<sup>f</sup>ordances are intertwined and recognising their interplay is key to ensure a positive user experience. The cross-a<sup>f</sup>ordance analysis revealed three types of links between a<sup>f</sup>ordances: transfer, omission, and con<sup>fl</sup>icts. In minimalist devices, the absence of instrumental a<sup>f</sup>ordances needs to be compensated with supplemental a<sup>f</sup>ordances. In this study, the simple design of the physical product required additional digital a<sup>f</sup>ordances to provide complete functionality. These required a<sup>f</sup>ordance transfers increased user e<sup>f</sup>ort, as illustrated in these three instances.

First, the lack of numeric display means that the user must check the software to get speci<sup>fi</sup>c activity counts:

“. . .you cannot look at the Flex and know how many steps you have taken – you have to go to your dashboard on your computer or your phone to know the actual number”.

Consulting the app or the web-based dashboard to get precise feedback is an additional step for users that need constant access to their activity data or do not understand the light display.

Second, the lack of a charge icon in the band, prompts users to check battery status through software. If the device is not synchronised periodically, the battery may completely deplete without notice:

“If you don’t sync it, the device doesn’t tell you when it is low on juice and will just die quietly”.

Third, the absence of printed instructions in the Fitbit box, forces the user to learn from the help features in the dashboard or app but this process is not e<sup>fi</sup>cient. In these three instances, the minimalist features of the device or its package (no instructions) were compensated by corresponding a<sup>f</sup>ordances in the supplemental category, but they increase user’s e<sup>f</sup>ort and thus reduce usability.

The omission of compensatory a<sup>f</sup>ordances results in user frustration. For example, since there is no battery level indicator on the device, the failure to receive an alert or noti<sup>fi</sup>cation prevents users from recharging the sensor when needed:

“My biggest complaints are that the battery life isn’t easily monitored. When my battery was low, I never received an email or notification”.

Another case of omission results from the lack of comprehensive device description or training instructions.

“First and foremost, you open the packaging upon arrival and there is practically no information about the thing. Just the web address for the Fitbit, the website walks you through installation and all that, but I have still not been able to find a concise list or chart or SOMETHING about what all these damn lights mean. You just learn as you go, which personally drives me crazy”.

In both cases, there was supposed to be a transfer to aggregation a<sup>f</sup>ordances (from maintenance or from learning) but the compensating a<sup>f</sup>ordance (online instructions or email noti<sup>fi</sup>cation) was missing.

In other instances, compensatory a<sup>f</sup>ordances produce con<sup>fl</sup>icting cues, which confuse users. The content analysis of the reviews shows two speci<sup>fi</sup>c cases of a<sup>f</sup>ordance con<sup>fl</sup>ict. The <sup>fi</sup>rst case is related to battery status, as illustrated in this excerpt:

“On one hand, I’ve got 5 blinking lights indicating a full charge, on the other my iPhone is telling me the battery is empty and I’m getting emails from Fitbit telling me my battery is empty and it needs to be charged. This is infuriating”.

However, the most notable case of con<sup>fl</sup>ict is found in the inconsistencies between physical movements and recorded data.

“I guess this device use some kind of accelerometer to measure your ARM SWING vs. actual steps. I took a quick walk around the living room and the steps counted were ± 2, then I was sitting on the chair swinging my arm, it still counting my steps”.

Since the device is essentially a pedometer worn on the wrist, and the steps are estimated based on arm movements, additional hand motions (or lack thereof) in<sup>fl</sup>uence the count. Users experience a mismatch between their own sense of movement and the actual data collected by the software. These con<sup>fl</sup>icts between a<sup>f</sup>ordances confuse users and thereby reduce usability.

Earlier, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed another case of con<sup>fl</sup>ict which had the potential to produce positive results. Within the self-efecter category, we found evidence of the countervailing forces of low data precision vis-à- vis the motivational power of wearing a <sup>fi</sup>tness band. For users of the selected wristband, motivational a<sup>f</sup>ordances stem from direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of data awareness on the individual wearer (feedback and goal setting), or from indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects as users cooperate or compete with other users of the same wristband to achieve their <sup>fi</sup>tness goals (social a<sup>f</sup>ordances). This is a case of a within-a<sup>f</sup>ordance con<sup>fl</sup>ict.

## 5. Discussion

The foundation provided by the individual variant or mediated-action perspective of AT and the conceptualisation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances o<sup>f</sup>ers a theoretical lens to understand the mediator role of wearable technology. While the overall principles of AT with respect to subject-object interaction and tool mediation provide general descriptive explanations, the typology of a<sup>f</sup>ordances initially proposed by Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) and extended in this study, <sup>fi</sup>lls the gap by o<sup>f</sup>ering a <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained structure to analyse usability. The application of this framework to the study of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c wearable <sup>fi</sup>tness band leveraged data from a sample of online reviews. The content analysis of the reviews using the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework illustrated how real users in real-life contexts incorporate this wearable device in their natural contexts.

The results of the two coding phases provide complementary insights to answer our research question. The emergent themes show key experiential aspects of how this wearable device enables users to digitally track their physical activities through accuracy, wearability, and goal achievement. The remaining themes show the technical aspects of the technology in terms of charging the physical device and aggregating its digital side. While these themes show how this minimalist wearable accomplishes its mediator role, the directed coding based on the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework provides evidence of two sources of failures. The <sup>fi</sup>rst source comes from failures within a<sup>f</sup>ordances with respect to the physical device or the accompanying software. The second source stems from the failed integration across a<sup>f</sup>ordances, between instrumental and supplemental, or between speci<sup>fi</sup>c a<sup>f</sup>ordances in each category.

The cross-a<sup>f</sup>ordance analysis is a key consideration for minimalist devices. Our results show that when a<sup>f</sup>ordances are missing in one area, they must be compensated with additional ones in another area. In our analysis, there are clear instances of transfer from the instrumental to the supplemental category. For example, the lack of numeric display and battery indicator in the device implies that the user must check the accompanying software (app or dashboard) to obtain speci<sup>fi</sup>c information. By increasing user e<sup>f</sup>ort, quality of use is reduced. Occasionally, the integration between a<sup>f</sup>ordances yields contradictory signals. The most notable examples are battery status misinformation, and data inaccuracies because it is a wrist-worn pedometer.

In general, con<sup>fl</sup>icts between a<sup>f</sup>ordances negatively a<sup>f</sup>ect the quality of the user experience. However, when these inconsistencies occur within-a<sup>f</sup>ordances, they may have the power to compensate shortcomings. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the analysis of a<sup>f</sup>ordances within the selfefecter category shows the countervailing forces of low data precision against the motivational power of wearing a <sup>fi</sup>tness band. For users of this wristband, motivational a<sup>f</sup>ordances stem from the direct e<sup>f</sup>ects of data awareness (feedback and goal setting), or from indirect e<sup>f</sup>ects as they cooperate or compete with other users of the same wristband to achieve their <sup>fi</sup>tness goals (social a<sup>f</sup>ordances). This self-e<sup>f</sup>ecter paradox whereby data collection and display increases <sup>fi</sup>tness motivation, despite its questionable accuracy for some users, is one of the most intriguing <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study and is worthy of future research.

## 5.1. Limitations

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings herein must be interpreted with caution in light of the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework, the selected device, and the characteristics of the sample. The theoretical basis of the categorisation of a<sup>f</sup>ordances, originally proposed by Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012) and extended for this study, are predicated on the individual activity systems view of AT. Other applications of AT include additional elements to the basic Person-Technology-Object triangle, such as rules, community and division of labour that are not considered in this study. Incorporating these additional elements to the notion of a<sup>f</sup>ordances could provide a path to extend the theoretical underpinnings of this study.

Another source of limitation stems from the product selection. This study is focused on a particular type of wearable technology in terms of placement on the body (wrist-worn). As such, some of our results may not be generalisable to sensors worn in clothing, shoes, or jewellery. In addition, from various possible types of wrist-worn devices, we selected a <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker with two distinctive characteristics: passive tracking and minimalist design. Fitbit Flex was chosen because it was one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst and most popular wrist-worn trackers with minimal interaction cues (no display, no buttons), designed to function unobtrusively. According to our conceptualisation based on AT, the use of passive <sup>fi</sup>tness trackers is not associated with any speci<sup>fi</sup>c human activity. Instead, the tracker is a mediator of normal everyday activities. In this type of passive mediation, the object is the human body, and the purpose is improving one’s lifestyle by being more active, but there is no association with any speci<sup>fi</sup>c activity. Future research should explore how this passive mediation is embedded in the context of other types of simultaneous human activities.

The third source of limitation arises from the type of data used in this study. Instead of direct data collection, we relied on a secondary data source available through online reviews. These are voluntary and anonymous user reports where, unlike interviews, researcher interaction and follow up is not possible. It could be argued that there is a mismatch between AT and the use of online reviews to understand user activities. However, the richness of the data and our selection <sup>fi</sup>lters based on date and length of the reviews alleviate this concern to a certain extent. On the one hand, the timeframe of the reviews could also be viewed as a source of limitations. Although we selected a long period (15-month after launch), it may not coincide with the length of time users have owned the device, as users post their opinions at di<sup>f</sup>erent points in time. On the other, we only selected reviews with 300 words or more to focus the analysis on detailed accounts. While we excluded many shorter reviews, we were able to extract a considerable sample of rich qualitative data.

Despite restrictions to the generalisation of results stemming from these limitations, this study shows how an a<sup>f</sup>ordance lens provides important insights regarding the use of wearables in real-life contexts.

## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

The mediated action perspective of AT served as the foundation for this study. Since this theory does not provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms for analysing the mediating role of technology in human activities, we included the framework of a<sup>f</sup>ordances proposed by Kaptelinin and Nardi (2012), also based on this theoretical perspective. We conceptualised the main properties of the focal technology – wearable – in terms of form (wrist-worn) and function (single purpose activity tracking). Given these characteristics, the a<sup>f</sup>ordance framework originally proposed for complex but portable technologies (such as smartphones) was adapted to include “wearing” within the handling a<sup>f</sup>ordance, and to adjust the concept of “object” to re<sup>fl</sup>ect that the purpose of the activity is to a<sup>f</sup>ect the wearer’s own body/mind with respect to <sup>fi</sup>tness goals.

The speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the technology mediator (wearable technology) and the object (self) expand the boundaries of AT to accommodate new technologies and rede<sup>fi</sup>ne the objects of such activities. In so doing, we have demonstrated how despite the original limitations of AT with respect to the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of tools and their mediational capabilities, the conceptual underpinnings of this theory are applicable to a wide range of modern technologies. Accordingly, one of the theoretical contributions of this study is to incorporate the concept of a<sup>f</sup>ordances to analyse the mediator role of a new type of technology, and use it as a lens to understand actual use contexts. Our theoretically driven a<sup>f</sup>ordance-based analysis attributes the causes of usability challenges to failures within-a<sup>f</sup>ordances and failures between-a<sup>f</sup>ordances when their integration is inadequate or contradictory. In these cases, the technology cannot ful<sup>fi</sup>l its mediator role.

The second theoretical contribution of this study is related to the framework of a<sup>f</sup>ordances and consists of recognising the joint potential of a<sup>f</sup>ordances to compensate for design constraints or de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies due to minimalist interfaces. Notwithstanding their initial classi<sup>fi</sup>cation into instrumental and supplemental, a<sup>f</sup>ordances operate together. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when physical a<sup>f</sup>ordances are missing, digital a<sup>f</sup>ordances must be provided to <sup>fi</sup>ll the void. Appropriate substitutions of instrumental for supplemental a<sup>f</sup>ordances improve the mediator role of the minimalist wearable technology examined in this study. Conversely, missing or con<sup>fl</sup>icting a<sup>f</sup>ordances threaten the potential of the technology to mediate human activities and undermine the possibility of achieving the desired e<sup>f</sup>ects.

The third theoretical contribution of this study is to elaborate on the concept of complex multi-level tools. Previous work by Hasan and Kazlauskas (2014) has recognised the existence of primary, secondary, and tertiary tools. Since wearable technology includes a device worn on the body and associated digital components (web dashboard or app, and personal data in the cloud), it incorporates elements previously classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as primary (physical device), secondary (data and software extensions), and tertiary (context and community). The primary level of the tool is the physical wrist-worn device, and the secondary level is given by the digital components associated with the device, such as algorithms and data accessible through a web-based dashboard or mobile app. The third level is the larger context that opens when individuals access virtual communities that are created online around these devices. The self-e<sup>f</sup>ecter paradox identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study is the result of a con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the accuracy of the data (secondary level of the tool) and the motivational power of the community or social features (tertiary level of the tool). Personal di<sup>f</sup>erences in how users evaluate these counterbalancing forces will ultimately determine the acceptance of the tool as a mediator of the activity.

## 5.3. Practical implications

For designers and developers of wearable technology products, our results have important practical lessons. Minimalist devices with inconspicuous interfaces need more holistic evaluation tools that encompass the entire range of user experiences in natural settings. Comprehensive evaluation plans must consider not only isolated instances of use, but also continued use where wearers experience potential deterioration of the technology (eg, tear-ability of the band, degradation of battery capacity). Additionally, user studies should focus on inclusive experiences where users attempt to reconcile the physical characteristics of the device with its digital extensions (eg, dashboard, data, or charts) to understand the informational feedback delivered by the wearable, as an integrated individual information system.

The fragmentation of wearables into their physical and digital components presents not only a dual design challenge but also an integration imperative. To help with the integration challenge, the a<sup>f</sup>ordance perspective used in this study provides three speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines. First, when a<sup>f</sup>ordances are missing from the instrumental side, they must be compensated on the supplemental side without requiring additional user e<sup>f</sup>ort; for example, an easy to check battery indicator in the web-dashboard and in the app would provide much needed transparency to the battery level question. Second, when a<sup>f</sup>ordances compensate each other, they must be free of inconsistencies; for example, if a user sets up an email reminder to recharge the battery, the message should be consistent with the current battery status or clarify why this might not be the case. Third, check for potential omissions and protect the user when she/he forgets to set up complementary a<sup>f</sup>ordances; for example, alert the user to de<sup>fi</sup>ne customised noti<sup>fi</sup>cations or alerts to supplement missing physical a<sup>f</sup>ordances.

These general guidelines are valuable to improve the design of new generations of wrist- worn <sup>fi</sup>tness devices. In addition, given the similarity of contemporary technology products in the market at a particular point in time, the guidelines are also potentially applicable to the design of wearables in general, besides the speci<sup>fi</sup>c product investigated in this study.

## 6. Conclusion

This study conceptualises wearables as individual information systems with physical and digital components. A speci<sup>fi</sup>c minimalist wrist-worn <sup>fi</sup>tness tracker, devoid of visible interaction a<sup>f</sup>ordances, was examined to investigate how users incorporate new devices into their lives and whether they derive the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they expect. The results indicate that simplicity in design may result in complexity of use, if implicit a<sup>f</sup>ordances in the physical device are not balanced with supplemental a<sup>f</sup>ordances on the digital side. Beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>c results regarding the relation between usability and a<sup>f</sup>ordance integration, this study expands the conceptual, methodological, and empirical boundaries of AT in Information Systems. Conceptually, the connection between a<sup>f</sup>ordances and the individual perspective of AT provides a novel analytical foundation to understand modern technology tools, such as wearables, in human activities. Methodologically, the qualitative analysis of online reviews shows how an innovative data source with self-reported accounts from real users in actual contexts can be leveraged from the perspective of AT. Empirically, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings are valuable for designers of wearable technologies in their quest to improve the adoption and acceptance of their products, and for IS researchers seeking to understand the drivers of success of wearable information systems and to the reviewers and editors of this journal for their valuable feedback to improve this paper.

## Appendix A. Product Description

Functionally, Fitbit Flex (FF) is a digital wrist-worn pedometer with wireless connectivity. The sensor, encased in a wristband, tracks movement by turning acceleration into digital measurements. Data collected about frequency, duration, intensity, and patterns of movement is used to estimate steps, calories burned, and sleep quality. The physical device is a rubber wristband that closes by inserting a set of prongs into their corresponding openings. See Figure A1.

The device wirelessly sends the data collected to a compatible smartphone (via Bluetooth 4.0), or to a computer when the device is nearby. For the wireless transmission to occur with the computer, a small custom dongle must be plugged into one of the USB ports. The sensor contains an internal battery that requires periodic charges with a specially designed charging cable that can be plugged into a USB port. To charge it, the sensor must be removed from the rubber band and inserted into an opening in the charging cable. The battery level is only visible through the app because the sensor lacks a battery light indicator. However, there is an option to setup receipt of email noti<sup>fi</sup>cations when the battery is running low. Figure A2 shows the custom charging cable.
