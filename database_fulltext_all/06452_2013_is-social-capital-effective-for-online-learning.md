## A B S T R A C T

The popularity of online social networking has heightened academic interest in social capital. However, few studies have investigated the role of social capital in online learning. Using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses of data from online MIS classes, we find that online learning facilitates social capital formation mostly in terms of the dimensions of community, trust, collective action and cooperation, communication, and sociability and inclusion, depending on the media-based human interaction forms of online learning employed. Structural equation modeling confirms a causal effect of social capital on student satisfaction. Social capital is also found to positively affect learning outcomes as measured by students’ group project scores but not class scores. The study’s contribution to the literature and practice are discussed.

Published by Elsevier B.V.

## 1. Introduction

‘‘Online learning,’’ a term used to denote all forms of electronically supported learning and teaching, is becoming increasingly important in higher education. Popular online learning systems, such as WebCT (now part of the blackboard learning system), blackboard, noodle, etc., support asynchronous computer-mediated virtual learning, a type of learning that offers little human interaction. In response to the increasing demand for synchronous virtual learning tools, many instructors have adopted online chat, web conferencing, and popular online social media as supplements to online learning. However, how to make online learning as richly interactive as offline learning and to achieve the desired quality of collaboration and collective intelligence remain unclear [43,56].

With the development of web conferencing and online social networking tools, virtual communities and media-based human interactions are a growing trend [31,56]. Such tools have been found to expand interactivity in the virtual sphere by enabling real-time conversations about content, problems and actions in the virtual world [20], thus enhancing the development of ‘‘virtual’ social capital [34].

Social capital as an intangible asset produced by relationships and by social networking among people [29]. Thus, social capital can in principle be generated virtually, through media-based communities and social interactions. Although a link between virtual social capital and positive educational outcomes [43,74] is increasingly recognized, little effort has been made to measure the effects of social capital on online learning [96]. To the best of our knowledge, no research has been undertaken to determine whether the value of social capital varies across different learning mechanisms and modes of interaction. The answer to this question, however, is crucial to deepening our understanding of the social capital that results from various media-based human interactions and to facilitate a full assessment of its impact on online learning.

Proserpio and Gioia [87] observe that we are teaching a virtual generation (V-Gen) of students. Technological and social changes in the larger environment have major implications for teaching and learning pedagogies. This paper reconsiders some key principles of learning in light of the learning styles of the current generation of students who are facile with virtual technologies and prefer mediabased interactions. We believe that by studying forms of mediabased human interaction, more engaging forms of online learning that enable increased social capital formation and thus improved student online learning performance and satisfaction will be developed. In this paper, we explore online learning effectiveness from a social capital perspective. Our study seeks to fill the research gap by addressing the following research questions: (1) Are components of social capital the same across different online learning interaction contexts? (2) How does social capital affect students’ satisfaction with online learning? (3) How does social capital impact student online learning outcomes? (4) What and how do social networking mechanisms facilitate the emergence of social capital in each context?

To address these research questions, we conducted a study to validate measures of social capital in online learning. With the increasing adoption of social media and forms of interaction in online learning, such a research effort should be valuable in explaining how social capital fosters online learning effectiveness in different interaction contexts and in what ways media-based interaction forms should be used to develop social capital.

We first describe our research models and hypothesized relationships, as these derive from the literature on social capital and interactivity in online learning. We then discuss the validation of our model, including the methodology used and the relevant empirical results. We conclude the paper with a discussion of our findings, an analysis of the study’s contributions to the literature, the study’s limitations and suggestions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Social capital

Social capital is a prominent topic of study in the social sciences. A commonly accepted definition of social capital is that of Coleman [29,30]: an intangible asset produced by social relationships as a result of social networking. Coleman believes that social capital relies on some aspect of a social structure and the facilitation of human interaction within that structure. Putnam, another prominent contributor to the development of social capital as a concept, describes social capital as the collective value of all social networks and the inclinations that arise among people within these networks to do things for one another [90]. Putnam believes that social capital can be measured by the amount of trust and ‘‘reciprocity’’ in a community or between individuals. Various scholars over several decades have contributed to the study of social capital but have not agreed on a consistent definition. Nevertheless, researchers working in both conceptual traditions agree that the creation of social capital requires interaction between at least two individuals and usually large groups of individuals [44]. To summarize, social capital can be broadly understood as an intangible resource accumulated through relationships built from interactions among people.

However, scholars measure social capital in various ways. Nahapiet and Ghoshal [79] propose that social capital in the management field be classified into three dimensions: (1) a structural dimension (such as network ties), (2) a cognitive dimension (such as shared knowledge), and (3) a relational dimension, which includes trust, norms, obligations, and identification. While Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s work is theoretical, some well-known empirical follow-up studies include Tsai and Ghoshal [110], Wasko and Faraj [112], and Chiu et al. [27]. Although these studies follow the three-dimensional model of Nahpiet and Ghoshal [79], the survey instruments used in these studies differ greatly and generate highly inconsistent results. In all of them, however, trust, reciprocity and commitment are often acknowledged as the components of a relational social capital concept.

A report from the World Bank suggests measuring social capital to enhance our knowledge of the social aspects of economic development. The report specifies six broad categories of social capital: groups and networks, trust and solidarity, collective action and cooperation, information and communication, social cohesion and inclusion, and empowerment and political action [44]. These six social capital dimensions are commonly seen as offering relatively complete coverage of the concept of social capital, with emphasis on relational capital, and have been substantially supported in the literature.

Participation in groups and networks is often regarded, in the consumer behavior literature, as equivalent to commitment to membership in a community or group and is frequently seen, in many online communities, as a major indicator of social capital [117]. As some theorists note, interactions among Web forum users develop users’ commitment to the virtual community, where such commitment is a form of social capital [69,76].

In the trust and solidarity dimension, trust is widely recognized as a key dimension of social capital. In the trust literature, trust is defined as a willingness to rely on another party [73], and social networks are key ingredients in building trust and respect among actors [65]. In social network theory, trust is viewed as a structurally embedded asset or as a property generated by interactions. Trust helps shape interaction patterns [78] within online social networks [41]. Empirical studies strongly support fostering the development of trust to enable online communities to thrive [26,69].

The information and communication dimension refers to information and communication flow – the sharing of needed information and the smoothing of communication among members of a team or community [30]. Information and communication flow among team members is believed to increase a team’s ability to integrate knowledge [91,104]. Studies have shown that people join online communities to share information and opinions with others who have common interests [11,48]. Interactions among network members are found to improve communications and enhance information exchange and thus intensify social capital [113].

The collective action and cooperation dimension encompasses the actions of groups working toward common goals and exchanging efforts for mutual benefit. Parks and Floyd [85] believe that cooperative and collaborative exchange forge a special type of ‘‘social presence’’ in terms of psychological closeness, understanding, and communication with others that does not require physical presence. Supported by Internet technologies, virtual organizations emerged a decade ago as temporary business alliances, and virtual teams as bridge mechanisms for virtual project management mushroomed. This social aspect of virtual organizing and virtual teaming was found to be essential for coordination [46,120], creating corporate value [104] and promoting innovation and productivity [103].

The dimension of social cohesion and inclusion refers to a sense of belonging and identity in social networks [24]. Social inclusion pertains to cohesion, diversity, and shared goals within a community [60], an aspect of social capital generally believed to help develop a more engaged individual. Workplace social inclusion is found to enhance an individual’s job performance [89]. Social inclusion as a motivation of behavior has been regarded as helpful in solving such crucial issues as poverty, unemployment and social exclusion [83]. By building social networks, corporations can achieve ‘‘greater coherence of action’’ [116]. Several studies have found that the Internet affects social bonding [86,115]. Webbased social media, through a combination of convenience and anonymity, allow the voices of the socially excluded to be heard and promote inclusionary community-building practices [100].

Empowerment and political action, an individual psychological sense that one has power [64,101] to influence a decision-making process or a course of action [13], is regarded in the literature as another dimension of social capital. Interactions and participation, especially when facilitated by Internet technology, have been found to enable ordinary individuals to voice themselves in regard to local issues and issues of public interest, making themselves known to other members of society [92]. Popular web blogs and micro blogs, online consumers’ active engagement in electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM), and popular online brand communities [61,105] are good examples of empowerment, some of which ultimately lead to the formation of political alliances.

Grootaert et al. [44] believe that one way to measure social capital is in terms of the non-monetary dimensions of welfare, for example, education. They designate each dimension of social capital as a range that allows researchers and practitioners in given fields to move towards greater clarity of application.

## 2.2. Social capital in online learning

Until recently, the central concern of online learning has been how to leverage technology-mediated learning environments to stimulate improved knowledge acquisition [14]. Online learning was at one time heavily influenced by cognitive learning theory. Cognitivism regards knowledge as independently existent and explains learning as knowledge transfer through information processing by the human brain [2]. Influenced by this cognitivist perspective and constrained by the state of learning technology, online learning tended to rely on a solo learner model – independent and individualized learning through professorprogrammed instruction.

Derivative of cognitive learning theory, cognitive constructivist learning theory views learning as knowledge construction involving problem-solving skills [4–6], while social constructivists emphasize the effects of social interactions on knowledge construction [39,51]. Social constructivists believe that students most easily learn difficult concepts with the help of people with experience and peers [39,119]. According to these scholars, the online learning environment should facilitate collaborative learning with multiple representations of reality and stimulate thoughtful reflection based on personal experience [51]. Such a collaborative online learning model has gradually become prevalence in online learning, with learners creating their own knowledge through interactions with peers and instructors [14,74].

Existing literature in management and MIS has documented that social capital is a key factor in the success of online communities, which are often formed by anonymous users [69,82]. Because of the existence of social capital, members feel committed to their online community and voluntarily contribute time and knowledge to it. Studies also show that members of such communities often form generalized bonds of reciprocity and trust within the community [69,117]. Compared with anonymous users of online communities, students in online classes form denser networks. When a network is composed of many strong and direct ties, collective action is easily achieved [62], and the network will have more social capital [112]. Drawing on theories of social capital and individual motivation, Chang and Chuang [23] recently confirmed that reputation social interaction and trust have positive effects on the quality of shared knowledge in virtual communities.

Given the rapid development of social media, educational practitioners have begun to adopt social media to online learning interactions. Educational researchers maintain that social capital emerges through the social processes of both teaching and learning as heuristic activities that open up avenues of exploration of complex issues [72]. However, serious research on the role of social capital in online learning is a recent phenomenon. Researchers in psychology recently compared the efficacy of offline and online education seminars in promoting the development of social capital. The results show that students of both groups increased their academic knowledge and social capital but that students involved in media-based collaborative learning made greater use of group work skills [64]. Educational experiments also indicate that interactive social assets such as social bookmarking, repositories, networking, and other Web 2.0 technologies have a strong potential to support teaching and learning in higher education across levels and programs [28]. Some prominent initial efforts were made to examine dimensions of virtual social capital in online learning [67,96]. Schoenacher [96] studied social capital in education, confining the definitions of the six dimensions of social capital identified in the World Bank report [44] to those that have a direct bearing on education (Table 1) to facilitate the construction of full-blown measures of social capital in a virtual collaborative learning environment.

To summarize, research on social capital in online learning remains in its early stages. However, media-based human interactions in online learning are receiving increasing attention among researchers. The perspectives employed and the findings of these researchers are highly varied.

## 2.3. Media-based human interactions in online learning

Collaborative online learning emerges from media-based human interactions. Different forms of interaction are widely recognized in the online learning literature as critical determinants of the quality of web-based courses [19,77]. Interactions by individuals involved in web-based courses are categorized into learner–instructor interactions and learner–learner interactions. The latter are further divided into group-based learner–learner interactions and interactions among classmates [35,56]. Studies have repeatedly examined media-based human interactions and the effects of online learning on business education programs [6,106,121].

Studies comparing different interaction approaches yield inconsistent results.

One study finds that younger generations prefer more interaction with student peers and less interaction with instructors [102]. Benbunan-Fich and Arbaugh’s study [14] of learner–instructor and learner group collaborations reveals that students perceive higher learning as mostly teacher-centered when knowledge is delivered by instructors rather than constructed by students. Arbaugh and Rau [10], in a later study, show that learner–learner interactions positively predict learning but negatively predict delivery medium satisfaction. On the other hand, Eom et al. [36] find that high levels of learner–instructor and learner–learner interactions predict high levels of student satisfaction with online courses. A more recent study by Eom et al. [36] examines the antecedents of human interactions in online learning, for example, course structure, self-motivation, and learning styles. Group-based learner interactions are mostly found to increase a sense of online community [94], social support and task support [14], peer learning through exposure to more ideas and perspectives [84], and higher order learning through cooperation [52].

Interactions in online learning through different modes of communication can be categorized as asynchronous or synchronous, which are found to complement each other [49]. Some researchers associate synchronous communication with naturalness, less cognitive effort, less ambiguity, and increased user motivation [58,59]. Others argue that asynchronicity turns inactive learners into active learners and allows for easy tracking of other student contributions, making it more suitable for reflection and discussion at a deeper and more informed level [15,40,49,90]. Some empirical studies find that students are generally satisfied with asynchronous discussion board interactions but dissatisfied with synchronous chat room communications [56,123]. Most studies find that students are more satisfied with an interactionrich experience than with an impersonal online class. A study conducted in an online MIS course found better student performance when asynchronous teaching-oriented communications were blended with media-based real-time learner–instructor interactions [114]. McFarland and Hamilton [71] find that the quality and speed of virtual communications and the use of synchronous meetings are leading factors in increasing student satisfaction with online learning. More recent studies appear to show increased social, collaborative, and constructive learning in virtual environments in which both synchronous and asynchronous communications are utilized [38,49,98]. Researchers are seeking ways to retain as much of the rich, interactive nature of the face-to-face class as possible through media-based human interactions in online learning [43].

The literature indicates that collaborative approaches result in better perceptions of learning. At the same time, few studies are able to explain clearly why and in what ways media-based human interactions affect online learning. This discrepancy in the literature underlines the need for a consistent measure of social capital in online learning. This study attempts to fill this gap. We argue that social capital should be able to explain the quality of educational interactions [111].

## 3. Hypothesis development

Research on media-based modes of communication indicates that virtual collaborative learning is based on both asynchronous and synchronous media-based human interactions [49,51]. Our argument is that media-based human interactions generate virtual social capital, which mediates the effect of collaborative online learning. Understanding social capital as powered by media-based human interactions enables us to measure and manage online learning interactions to improve knowledge construction in collaborative online learning. Satisfaction has proven an effective predictor of learning outcomes [68]. In the learning literature, it is regarded as equivalent to students’ perceptions pertaining to their learning experience and perceived value of the education received [12]. Studies have repeatedly found that media-based human interactions are directly related to online course satisfaction [53]. Assuming that social capital stems from various media-based human interactions in online learning, we postulate the following:

$$
\mathbf {H _ {1 a}} \quad (:).
$$

Social capital gained through group-based learner–learner interactions using selected media is positively related to online group learning satisfaction.

$$
\mathbf {H _ {1 b}} \quad (:).
$$

Social capital gained through class-based learner–learner interactions using selected media is positively related to online class learning satisfaction.

$$
\mathbf {H _ {1 c}} (:).
$$

Social capital gained through learner–instructor interactions using selected media is positively related to overall online-learning satisfaction.

## 3.1. Social capital and online learning performance

Online learning success should be reflected both in perceived student satisfaction and in actual performance outcomes [56]. The literature shows that a social learning perspective has enabled an increase in academic knowledge and understanding of media-based collaborative learning [53,74]. Hay et al. [47] argue that higher level interactions between students and instructors and engagement with other students in an online format that allows both synchronous and asynchronous virtual communications should lead to improvements in learning performance in courses that require in-depth analysis of complex concepts, feedback and/or detailed case analyses. McFarland and Hamilton [71] observe that an instructor’s encouragement of collaborative learning or other forms of teamwork can alleviate feelings of isolation in online courses. A willingness to reach out to other classmates and to the instructor when students have questions or require help is highly likely to improve learning effectiveness. A recent study of virtual group membership found that early socialization experiences exert longterm effects on newcomers’ satisfaction and performance [21]. Such group-based membership emerged through newcomers describing initial participation in the group, their similarity to the group’s focal social category, and information requests, in which they asked for help. Though not focused on social capital in online learning, these studies depict online learning situations and modes of interaction that forge social capital very similarly to the way they are depicted in our study. Their positive findings motivate us to measure the relationship between social capital gained and the learning effects associated with particular modes of interaction. As learning outcomes are mostly measured by relevant performance scores, we propose the following hypotheses:

$$
\mathbf {H _ {2 a}} (:).
$$

Social capital gained through group-based learner–learner interactions using selected media is positively related to student group project scores.

$$
\mathbf {H _ {2 b}}
$$

Social capital gained through class-based learner–learner interactions using selected media is positively related to student class scores.

$$
\mathbf {H _ {2 c}}
$$

Social capital gained through learner–instructor interactions using selected media is positively related to students’ online learning performance, as shown by their group project scores and class scores.

## 3.2. Perceived satisfaction and online learning performance

Satisfaction is described by psychologists as the mental state of being contented or happy. In education, satisfaction is defined as students’ joyful feelings or positive attitudes toward learningassociated activities [109]. Most studies, however, find that such positive emotions are not prevalent among students in online learning situations [71]. Nevertheless, student satisfaction and academic performance are two inextricably related educational experiences. Students’ online learning satisfaction is believed to have an effect on their academic performance [71] and has been repeatedly studied as an important predictor of learning achievement, with positive results [10,35,50]. The explanation is that if students have a positive and satisfying online learning experience, they are more motivated and their academic success will likely be enhanced. Recent findings from business online courses also suggest that satisfaction with the use of effective learning tools and a technology fit also affect perceived learning outcomes [37,99]. In accordance with the existing literature, we assume that learners who are satisfied with their online learning experiences, performance and media use should be internally motivated to perform better in online learning. Thus, we postulate the following:

Students’ satisfaction with their online group experience, performance and media use predicts students’ online-learning performance, as shown by their group project scores.

H<sub>3b</sub> (:).

Students’ satisfaction with their online class experience, performance and media use predicts students’ online-learning performance, as shown by their online class scores.

$$
\mathbf {H} _ {\mathbf {3 c}}
$$

Students’ satisfaction with their overall online learning experience, performance, and media use predicts students’ online learning performance, as shown by their group project and class scores.

Fig. 1 displays our conceptual model with the above hypothesized relationships: social capital as an effect of media-based human interactions influences students’ perceived satisfaction and learning performance in online courses; this effect on student learning performance is also mediated through perceived satisfaction.

## 4. Methodology

This section describes the research design, instruments, sample, treatment, and data collection used to achieve our research objectives.

## 4.1. Research design and measures

We survey online students to measure social capital via three media-based modes of interaction and investigate the effects of social capital on satisfaction and learning outcomes. This study is empirical and exploratory in nature. We adopt two methods of data collection: (1) a survey questionnaire, used to determine perceptions of social capital, media use and online learning satisfaction, and (2) actual online student learning outcomes, as indicated by group project scores and class scores.

The items pertaining to the social capital dimensions in the survey were developed from findings in the literature, the dimensions identified in Schoenacher’s [96] study and the characteristics of modes of online interaction in our research context. Attributes of each dimension, rather than dimensional ranges, are used in our measures. An explanation of each concept is given in each survey item. Specific items are listed in Appendix A. The instrument has three parts: use of online social media and tools, perceived social capital in three forms of media-based human interactions, and learner satisfaction (satisfaction with the group project experience and performance outcome, with the class experience and performance outcome, and with the communication media used). A pilot study was conducted among twentyseven MBA students in an MIS class in the spring of 2010. Social capital dimensions were discussed among the students. As a result of the pilot study, certain changes were made to the survey structure, format, and item wording to improve clarity, accuracy and ease of use in an online context. Major research procedures were also tried out on the data.

To measure online student learning outcomes, the respondents group project paper score, project work score and project presentation score were selected as objective indicators of group performance. The respondents’ assignment score, case study score and discussion score were used as measures of class performance.

To determine the effects of media on the generation of social capital, the amount of use of each selected form of interaction was logged and tracked.

## 4.2. Participants

The target population was MBA students taking MIS classes online, using blackboard as the major learning environment, at a regional university in the United States. We believe that the target population serves our research purposes well. With recent improvements in learning technology and social media, online learning has evolved to emphasize social constructive learning. The social constructivist perspective is commonly implemented at the MBA level, as MBA learning emphasizes teamwork and problem solving to develop student application and leadership abilities. One hundred and seventy-four online students from six MIS classes participated in our survey. The participants were mostly non-traditional students, enrolled in the online course as a first-year core course in their MBA program. Each class was taught by the same instructor and involved with the same teaching materials, the same assignments (including the group project), the same communication tools and media, and the same tests. Consent was obtained from all participants to gain access to their data.

## 4.3. Treatment

This study is not merely interested in the match between a given task and technology medium but more importantly in the fit between a form of interaction and student use of combined media for the purpose. The students in the blackboard online MIS course were randomly assigned to groups at the beginning of each semester to work on group projects (four to five in each group). The media used in the study included blackboard discussion board (DB), blackboard email, blackboard chat, adobe connect (AC) pro meeting, and other social media of the student’s choice. Media access levels and the modes of communication used by the students in each form of interaction are described in Table 2.

While three online interaction forms are distinct, the same media tools can be used in different ways, depending on access needs and the desired mode of communication. For example, the Introduction Forum and Free Discussion Forum on the DB were available to all classmates for self-introduction and free public communication on topics that might be interesting to the entire class; group project work area was a privately reserved category on the same DB for each group, and only the group members and the instructor could post and read messages there. In addition to this private group work area on the DB, each student project group was also provided with a virtual group meeting room in adobe connect. Optional virtual meetings with the instructor, using the same web conferencing program, were conducted roughly once a week for Q/ A sessions, test reviews, and hands-on skill demonstrations, as needed. Adobe connect, a popular web conferencing program, was selected for its functionality to facilitate synchronous instruction and meetings over the Internet on numerous platforms. Participants attended a virtual meeting by using their web browsers to visit the specified meeting room URL. Students were also allowed to use any popular social media of their choice for interactions with group members.

## 4.4. Data collection

Perceptions regarding social capital and satisfaction were elicited from the participants after the mid-term exam in each semester from Fall 2010 to Summer 2011, using an online survey (see Appendix A). Participation in our study was voluntary, with students’ consent given at the beginning of each semester. As a reward for participation, bonus points, not exceeding 1%, were added to a student’s total class score.

The online learning performance scores, namely, the three group project scores and three class scores of each participant, were obtained from the blackboard grade book at the end of each included semester. We compared the mean students’ perceptions and their performance scores across the three semesters, using ANOVA, finding no systematic differences across the timeframe. Quantitative media use information came mostly from blackboard online tracks and AC meeting logs. Although we did not have a complete record of student interactions online, it was possible to decompose most online interactions into the predetermined components. Qualitative feedback from the participants constitutes another source of data.

## 4.5. Data analysis plan

The Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS), version 20.0, and AMOS for windows 18 were used for the data analyses. The alpha was established a priori at the .05 level as suggested in the literature. We began by using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis to confirm the dimensions of social capital for each form of interaction. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) procedures were followed to test the fit of the three proposed research models and examine the models’ hypothesized relationships. Regression tests were conducted to discover whether student media usage with respect to given forms of interaction were associated with social capital formation.

## 5. Results

Of the 174 participants, 98 were male (55.2%), and 76 were female (42.5%). We performed a normality check to examine the suitability of the empirical data for the predetermined statistical analysis procedures by examining univariate skewness values and univariate kurtosis values. None of the values exceeded the threshold, identified by Kline [57], for extreme violation of the normality assumption. The present data set is within the tolerable range for non-severe violation.

To examine the internal consistency of the scale variables, a reliability test was performed on the satisfaction items. Cronbach’s alpha value for the five satisfaction variables is .898. Reliability was then checked for the social capital items for each of the three forms of interaction, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.919 (GL), 0.890 (CL) to 0.810 (IL), well above the threshold of 0.7 given by Nunnally [80].

## 5.1. Exploratory and confirmatory analyses

To identify dimensions of social capital in the empirical data, we performed exploratory factor analysis, using principle component with varimax rotation for each interaction form. According to Hair et al. [45], only components with eigenvalues above 1 and items with factor loadings above 0.4 are considered representative. For GL interaction, eleven variables were tested. Variables on conflict and political action were dropped for low loading values. Nine variables remained as social capital indicators. For CL interactions, the same nine variables were retained. For IL interactions, six variables were tested and five were retained.

Based on a review of the literature on social capital dimensions, a second-order confirmatory factor analysis model was developed to examine social capital formation in each of the three interaction modes. In the GL model, two variables of the previous nine were dropped because of heavy cross loading. The measurement model fit indexes were clearly acceptable (X<sup>2</sup>/df = 2.09, GFI = 0.958, AGFI = 0.901, NFI = 0.984, IFI = 0.991, TLI = 0.971, CFI = 0.983). The variables remaining represent five of the six predetermined dimensions: community, trust, cooperation/collective action, inclusion/sociability, and communication. The same procedures were repeated for the CL model, with the CFA test revealing a good model fit (X<sup>2</sup>/df = 1.803, GFI = 0.966, AGFI = 0.921, NFI = 0.965, IFI = 0.984, TLI = 0.972, CFI = 0.984). The seven variables remaining in this model represent the same five dimensions identified above.

The model for IL interaction also fits the empirical data well, with the indicators representing community, trust, sociability, and communication dimensions (X<sup>2</sup>/df = 0.622, GFI = 0.994, AGFI = 0.978, NFI = 0.991, IFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.000, CFI = 1.000).

As measures of learning performance in the GL model, group project paper scores, project work scores, and group presentation scores were used as the observed variables. For the CL model, the learning performance construct is composed of the assignment score, the case study score, and the discussion score. We used these scores in the CL model because respondents (who were classmates) were allowed to help each other with hands-on assignments and communicate with each other openly in case study and discussion board sessions. For the IL model, overall learning performance comprises both scores in the GL model and the CL model. Thus, a second-order construct was developed. All scores were converted into logs for data analysis.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was then performed to examine the convergent and discriminant validity of the model constructs for each interaction form. The measurement CFA procedure results indicate acceptable convergent and discriminant validity for each of the three models. The specific results are displayed in Tables 3 and 4.

## 5.3. Robustness check

To examine the robustness of the results, we conducted further analyses by including two control variables – gender (g) and prior knowledge (pk). According to Witkin’s theory [118], male students tend to be more field independent than female students. Severiens and Dam’s [97] meta-analysis also confirms that male students prefer abstract conceptualization modes of learning. According to the literature, this gender difference often significantly affects the academic performances of business students in quantitative courses, including MIS, owing to differences in students’ learning styles [18,55,122].

In addition to gender differences, educational psychologists believe that prior student knowledge may affect the learning of subject content [70,107] and problem analysis [95]. Thus, prior knowledge of both computer operations and content may affect students’ understanding of new and related material in a computer-supported learning environment [42].

Therefore, we conducted SEM path analyses with gender and prior knowledge (a degree in or a job directly related to computer science or information systems) as control variables. The tests show minimal effects of gender $\left( p _ { \mathrm { g } } = 0 . 2 1 1 \right)$ and prior knowledge $( p _ { \mathrm { p k } } = 0 . 2 9 9 )$ on group learning performance. Similar results were obtained for the classmates’ interaction model $( p _ { \mathrm { g } } = 0 . 7 2 1$ $p _ { \mathrm { p k } } = 0 . 9 1 1 \AA$ and the instructor–learner model $( p _ { \mathrm { g } } = 0 . 6 8 4$ $p _ { \mathrm { p k } } = 0 . 3 9 4 )$ . The magnitudes of structural paths linking learning performance in the three models are unchanged. Thus, the control variables, gender and prior knowledge, do not appear to have significant effects on online learning performance.

Out of concern that student demographic data may interfere with the main effects under study, we performed multiple group invariance tests, with respondents’ gender used as the grouping variable. The male group has 98 data points, while the female group has 76. Table 6 displays the group comparison test results. According to Byrne [22], while both chi square difference scores and CFI difference scores should be reported, a CFI difference score of less than 0.01 should be used as a more reliable threshold for estimating non-invariance between groups. The group comparison test reveals a significant CFI difference score across gender groups at the structural level for learner–learner class interaction. When each structural path was set free one by one in further comparisons, satisfaction of male respondents was found to negatively affect their class learning performance outcomes $( r = - 0 . 4 9 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , an effect not observed for females (r = 0.03, p = 0.930).

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Findings

The empirical data clearly indicate the existence of social capital in different online learning interaction modes. Moreover, the results show that the media-based modes of human interaction also affect the various dimensions of social capital. In group-based learner–learner interactions, the dimensions of community, trust, cooperation/collective action, inclusion/sociability, and communication were most prominent. Comparing our results with those of Schoenacher [96], our study appears to support all of Schoenacher’s predetermined dimensions except empowerment and political action in group-based learner–learner interactions, while two dimensions, cooperation/collective action and inclusion/ sociability, are fully supported. These confirmed aspects of social capital are consistent with the characteristics of the group project setting of our study – mandatory and involving a large amount of teamwork among group members. The literature, especially the collaborative learning literature, supports the use of task-focused mandatory interactions within virtual teams as a means of facilitating social activity and collective action needed for cooperation and collaborative knowledge creation [16,21,25,33].

Class-wide learner–learner interactions with access to both public and private media appear to have generated the same dimensions of social capital. Again, the dimension of cooperation and collective action and that of social cohesion and inclusion, in Schoenacher’s study, are fully supported. Other dimensions, such as groups and networks, trust and solidarity, and information and communication, by contrast, are only partially confirmed. One possible explanation is that most class-wide learner–learner interactions in our study are undertaken through the free use of asynchronous or synchronous media and are thus voluntary and social in nature. Such free social interactions encourage voluntary exchanges of information, advice and ideas, based on need and common interests. When we compare the magnitude and dimensions of social capital generated through each mode of interaction, denser communities (groups) are found to generate larger amounts of social capital in our study than sparser groups, as claimed by Wasko and Faraj [112].

Instructor–learner interactions through both public and private access to selected asynchronous and synchronous media in our study reveal that community membership, development of trust, sociability, and smoothing of communication are especially outstanding elements of social capital. Students, through such interactions, satisfy their need to become acquainted with instructors, obtain answers to questions, access test reviews and receive skill demonstrations that are normally dependent on the most authoritative person, the instructor. The literature also supports the view that in web-based classes, active learner–instructor interactions are more learning-oriented and are stronger predictors of learning outcomes than learner–learner interactions [9,10].

None of the modes of interaction used in our study foster the development of all six dimensions of social capital identified by Schoenacher [96]. And in most situations, specific attributes of social capital rather than dimensional ranges are generated. While the findings may require confirmation by further studies, the evidence seems clear that the dimensions of social capital may vary, depending on the form of interpersonal interaction and the actors involved. This finding supports Best and Krueger’s [17] finding, from a sociological perspective, of a positive relationship between online interaction levels (scope of community) and social capital. This finding could be explained by the communication constructionist theory, which states that the value of the communication process cannot be separated from the social context in which it arises [75].

Conflict as the opposite of social cohesion and inclusion is not confirmed in our study. In Schoenacher’s study [96], conflict is used to verify construct validity. The relevant literature defines social cohesion and inclusion as the bonding of individuals into more inclusive groupings through a sense of common belonging [88]. Exclusion of conflict should not affect the emergence of social cohesion and inclusion as a dimension of social capital.

Contrary to the findings of Schoenacher [96], the dimension of empowerment and political action is not evident in our results. According to the definition of Grootaert et al. [44], empowerment is the extent to which individuals have control over decisions and processes directly affecting courses of action. An online class, without decision-making projects or assigned authority levels among students (no group leader was assigned by the instructor in our study), might mostly empower students at the level of knowledge and skill enhancement. As such, empowerment most likely could not reach levels observed in some online political discussion forums, online committee work, or online brand communities. Schoenacher [96] defines political action as students working with others on issues relating to people in power. After examining peer evaluations from the project groups and student feedback on class teaching, we found no strong evidence of conflicts between students or between students and the instructor. It is thus not surprising that participants gave low scores on the relevant items. Our review of the literature on social capital also provided little information on the incidence of political activities in learning.

However, the hypothesized causal effect of social capital on student satisfaction is strongly supported by our study. Social capital is found to enhance group work experience and group performance satisfaction, online class experience and class performance satisfaction, and social media satisfaction. This positive result strongly supports findings repeatedly reported in the online learning literature [53,74]. In addition, our findings support the view, common in the literature that frequent interactions with classmates and with instructors enhance MBA online learning [7].

The strong link observed in our study between social capital and perceived satisfaction may reflect two trends: much improved communication technologies, with increased capacities to serve media-based human interaction needs; and students increasingly familiar with virtual technologies and accustomed to online human interactions [88]. In the social sciences, behavior is regarded as a function of both the individual and the environment [63]. More specifically, the theory of planned behavior [1] posits that behavior is conditioned on an individual’s attitudes, social norms, and perceived control over situation variables. When students’ perceived control over communication tools increases, their interaction needs can be better served, and their satisfaction is likely to increase. Our argument is that when the complexity and quality of communication technologies are suited to the needs of given forms of interaction, virtual social capital emerges and increases, thereby enhancing learning in an online learning environment.

In our study, certain aspects of social capital gained in groupbased learner–learner interactions were positively related to students’ group project scores; social capital gained in class-based learner–learner interactions were, on the whole, less strongly related to student class performance. This finding, to a certain extent, supports the social capital literature by showing that it is easier for denser communities (i.e., project groups) to achieve stronger social capital effects than loosely structured communities (i.e., an entire online class, in our case). Furthermore, the effect of social capital, in our study, was strongest on collective action and sociability, a finding that provides solid support for the argument that virtual social capital exerts positive effects on learning outcomes in virtual collaborative learning [10,35,74]. Virtual collaborative learning occurred most often and most directly in group project work. Although participants may also have collaborated to some degree in other class activities such as discussion sessions using blackboard, the total class scores also reflect their performances on other class assignments and activities that did not require intense collaboration. It is thus understandable that the effect of social capital on total class scores was more distant.

Contrary to some previous studies [10,35], perceptions of satisfaction were not found to significantly affect online student learning outcomes. In other words, satisfaction levels did not mediate the effect of social capital on learning performance scores, as had been expected. One explanation for this result is that the literature we surveyed used students’ perceived learning outcomes rather than actual performance scores. The literature has repeatedly warned of the difference between perceived learning outcomes and observed measures of learning performance [3,93]. Another possible explanation is found in the expectation– confirmation theory of Oliver [81]. According to this theory, satisfaction is equivalent to confirmation of expectations. Thus, satisfaction may be positively related to continuance intention in e-learning [66] and to continued use of learning media, if students’ expectations of online learning are met. However, student learning expectations can differ greatly. Under-expecting students may feel satisfied even with substandard scores, while over-expecting students may not be satisfied, even when scores received correctly reflect their capabilities [32]. A recent study of the relationship between student satisfaction and academic performance in higher education found the relationship to be significant but rather weak and negative [108]. Such a negative relationship was also found among male participants in our study. Our explanation is that numerous factors affect students’ learning achievements, including students’ learning capabilities, learning styles, teaching or grading styles of instructors, the difficulty of the online course materials, and the virtual learning environment. A satisfied student may have learned a great deal relative to his or her knowledge level at the start of a course, even though his or her class score is not very high. In addition, combined effects – for example, of gender and learning style [122] – may occur, making it difficult to identify the true relationship between learning satisfaction and learning achievement in online learning. Due to the limited scope of this study, we cannot control for all possible factors. Thus, future research, with a more comprehensive research design, is needed to explore the full effects of social capital and satisfaction on online learning.

Our findings with regard to the five selected communication tools reveal that participants most favored the asynchronous communication tools, which provide flexibility and freedom to leave and read messages as desired without significant conflict with personal schedules. This observation supports the findings in some previous studies involving non-traditional students [8,56]. Our participants were also mostly adults with full-time jobs. The preference for flexibility and better control over the pace of learning is most likely associated with having multiple life goals and busy schedules typical of non-traditional students.

Our study results appear to support the view that synergies only arise from the integration of more than one component, tool and use of facilities [54]. In our study, asynchronous and synchronous tools were used together to nurture the formation of social capital. Most student groups chose both synchronous and asynchronous tools. Blackboard email for asynchronous interactions and blackboard chat for synchronous communications were found to be most effective tools in fostering trust and collective action among students. Both private email responses and public AC meetings helped to enhance a sense of community and communication flow between the instructor and learners. Even the same asynchronous learning tool can be used for different purposes. Various free public discussion sessions on blackboard also contributed to the development of sociability among classmates. Participant feedback reveals that access to a wide variety of tools and options is seen as offering greater flexibility for working professionals, facilitating online communications and encouraging a better flow of information and ideas. Our study, in examining the effects of combining various components and agents to create a student-desired learning experience, appears to be relevant to current trends in online learning [54].

At the same time, participant feedback reveals that, although adobe connect group meetings provide a very effective synchronous online learning experience for some students, the learning curve involved in that application is challenging for many others. Occasional connectivity problems had some negative effects. Hesitation about using real-time communications in the virtual sphere also stimulated some resistance to utilizing the medium. Two groups selected skype and gotomeeting.com for synchronous communication. Another group used conference calls instead. Past studies provide strong evidence of inefficiency and dysfunction in student-to-student interactive modalities, caused by cumbersome technologies at the MBA level [8,56]. As observed in information systems research, further technical advance in networking technology and a more seamless online learning environment that facilitates both asynchronous and synchronous interactions seem necessary to offer a smoother online learning experience.

## 6.2. Contributions

This study contributes to the literature on the role of social capital in online learning through media-based forms of human interaction. Previous studies of media-based human interactions have been unable to agree on whether and how media use and online interactions affect learning outcomes, for lack of standardized measures of such effects. With the increasing adoption of social media in online learning and growing attention to online forms of interaction, a need has emerged to explain and compare research findings. An examination of social capital is the missing link. In particular, an examination of social capital formed through media-based human interaction can explain in what ways online learning is influenced by online interactions and why. This study is an effort to investigate measures of social capital in online learning; it furthers Schoenacher’s study [96] by developing a survey instrument that integrates three media-based learning modes of interaction. In seeking information about social capital, Schoenacher merely categorized student discussion board postings, using frequency and percentage measures to quantify his data. By contrast, we use exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses to statistically validate the social capital components of class-based learner–learner, group-based learner–learner, and instructor–learner based interactions, with attention to both asynchronous and synchronous communication tools used in online learning. This approach enables us to reveal the complexity and dynamics of social capital in online learning. To the best of our knowledge, such an approach remains rare in both social capital studies and the online learning literature and thus may be useful for theory construction in both these fields.

This study, using structural equation modeling procedures, finds strong evidence of a positive effect of social capital on satisfaction with group and class experience and performance, a finding that demonstrates the potential value of fostering social capital formation in online learning interactions. This study also examines the relationship between social capital formed via various modes of interaction and online student performance outcomes. The positive finding regarding the impact of social capital formed through group-based learner–learner interactions on group project scores supports the view that virtual social capital exerts a positive effect on online collaborative learning. Such a finding has rarely been seen in the online learning literature.

Though limited by our quantitative data, the findings regarding how media use affects social capital formed through various forms of interaction, especially the findings on media usage in groupbased interactions provide a glimpse of how treatment, social capital and learning outcomes are associated with each other. Researchers who have studied the relationships between types of participant interaction in online learning environments and their outcomes strongly believe that group collaborations should provide learners greater opportunity for increased social presence and a greater sense of online community, which is associated with positive online learning outcomes [9]. However, such predictions have not been supported by study results. Our study, by contrast, offers new clues that deepen our understanding of how communication media and media-based interaction forms may foster social capital formation and, in turn, improve satisfaction levels and learning outcomes.

In addition, our study should have practical implications for learner-centered online course design and management. Instructors of online courses may find our results helpful in designing student projects. One implication, in particular, is clear: instructors should select designs that leverage the role of social capital in enhancing learner satisfaction and possibly achieving improved project outcomes as well.

To achieve learner satisfaction with online learning, the formation of social capital is crucial. Because of the complexity and dynamics of social capital, adequate attention should be given to forms of virtual interaction in online learning to forge desired social capital components. For this purpose, the advantages and disadvantages of both synchronous and asynchronous media and of both public and private uses of media should be considered to achieve an effective balance among these elements.

Our study results appear to imply that, while investigating the effect of implementing a particular medium or technology tool in online learning is important, it is of greater practical importance to understand how different learning tools can be combined for certain purposes. As observed above, synergies can only arise from effective integration of different components, tools and different uses of facilities [54].

Instructors teaching online classes should be aware that human interactions in the virtual sphere are usually more difficult than in the everyday physical world [55]. Various communication tools and media-based forms of interaction must be studied and managed effectively to achieve coordination among them. To maximize virtual social capital and minimize the online learning curves of students, brief hands-on training programs are required before adoption of social media in the classroom can take place; efforts must be made to standardize uses of communication tools across different online classes to help reduce learning curves and ensure adequate skill development, as students may take more than one online class in a given semester. Small project groups equipped with familiar social media should be effective in reducing possible technophobia and social anxiety among members of virtual work groups. Web conferencing programs would be more attractive and convenient for online students if the web links are accessible from within the main online learning system. Meanwhile, we must have realistic expectations of social capital in online learning, as learning outcomes depend on many other factors as well. ## 6.3. Limitations

As with any study of this complexity, the reader should be aware of its limitations when interpreting its results. The sample used was from a real MIS course and was small in size. The study did not measure the model fit itself but rather inferred it by comparing the perceptions from using the varying tools. The findings must therefore be confirmed by further studies. In studying the possible effects of media use on social capital, only a few media uses were involved; hence, we cannot assess the effects of all social capital components identified in this study. This limits the explanatory power of the true nature of media use influences on social capital formation, In terms of the social media under study, although both blackboard and adobe connect provide some useful interaction tools and functions one is a popular online learning system, while the other is a web conferencing program. Thus, our findings may differ from those of studies using popular social networking media. In addition, only one subject area and one instructor were involved in this study, which, as noted above, used a small and convenient sample. The findings most likely were influenced in some degree by the characteristics of the subject area and how media-based interactions were managed by the instructor, potentially limiting the study’s generalizability to other samples or contexts.

## 7. Conclusions

Our study is one of the initial efforts to validate measures of social capital in online learning. The findings reveal a strong positive effect of social capital on student satisfaction toward online learning and on virtual group learning outcomes. To further our understanding of the role of social capital in online learning, the study should be replicated using popular social media in different contexts, more sophisticated research designs, and different MIS classes. The components of social capital and the hypotheses not supported in our study deserve special attention in future studies to reveal the true nature of social capital and its role in online learning.
