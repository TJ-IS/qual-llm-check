RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Is traditional, open-source, or on-demand first choice? Developing an AHP-based framework for the comparison of different software models in office suites selection

Alexander Benlian

Institute for Information Systems and New Media, University of Munich, Munich School of Business, Ludwigstr. 28, Munich 80539, Germany

Correspondence: A. Benlian, Institute for Information Systems and New Media, University of Munich, Ludwigstr. 28, Munich 80539, Germany. Tel: þ 49 89 2180 6396; Fax: þ 49 89 2180 13541

## Abstract

Until recently, organizations planning to acquire application software (AS) have had no choice but to adopt proprietary, on-premises software. With the advent of open-source and on-demand solutions, new models for developing and distributing software have entered the stage providing IS managers with more options in AS selection. On the basis of an Analytic Hierarchy Process model, we propose a framework including software package and implementation attributes on different hierarchy levels to examine how IS managers evaluate the relative fulfilment of key selection criteria by traditional, open-source, and on-demand office suites. By testing the framework with a random sample of 254 IS managers of 166 smaller and 88 mid-sized/larger firms, we validate its validity and usefulness in evaluating different software delivery models. Our empirical results show that open-source office suites were consistently perceived to be superior in meeting ease of customization (i.e., extensibility and adaptability) and cost criteria (i.e., acquisition and maintenance cost), whereas traditional software models were superior in fulfilling functionality, ease of use, and support requirements. On-demand office suites excelled in the fulfilment of time-to-value and data recoverability. We discuss further results of the comparison of the three software models, and derive practical and research implications for office suite selection. Keywords: office suite selection; evaluation criteria; software models; open-source; on-demand; Analytic Hierarchy Process

## Introduction

Until a few years ago, IS (purchasing) managers in companies have had no choice but to evaluate and select proprietary and on-premises application software (AS). With no real alternatives available in AS markets, traditional providers such as Microsoft, SAP, or Oracle could maintain high operational margins over time with this traditional method for developing and distributing AS. However, with the emergence of successful open-source and on-demand solutions in different vertical software markets in the last couple of years, novel models for developing and distributing software have evolved providing IS managers with new options in AS selection. In the productivity suites market, for example, open-source office suites such as OpenOffice.org or KOffice and ondemand (i.e., Software-as-a-Service (SaaS)-based) solutions such as Google

Apps (Docs & Spreadhseets), Zoho or ThinkFree Apps are already affecting the revenues of incumbents such as Microsoft (MS Office). For the first quarter in 2009, Microsoft posted the first decline in revenues in its 23-year history for its Business Division, of which MS Office represents the bulk (Wingfiled, 2009). According to analysts, the drop in revenues is not only attributable to a general pullback in consumer spending in the wake of the financial crisis. It is also due to a barrage of new threats such as open-source and SaaS, which constantly make inroads into the office suite market and may change the competitive landscape in ways that erode Microsoft’s historical advantages. According to Gartner, worldwide software revenues for SaaS delivery for the aggregate AS market will grow from 2008 to 2013 by around 20% overall and by more than 250% in the office suite segment (Mertz et al., 2009). An analysis by the Wharton School has also revealed the fallacy of the notion that 95% of users were using MS Office (Knowledge@Wharton, 2007). Instead, this often-quoted number simply states that 95% of the revenue collected for the sale of office suites goes to Microsoft. However, if one measures market share as the number of computers with the software installed as a percentage of all computers, then Microsoft has only around 80% market share, while other office suites, in particular opensource solutions, would appropriate a market share of up to 20%.

Despite the growing diffusion of alternative AS models, prospective buyers in companies are still puzzled about how to evaluate these new alternatives relative to traditional options and what option is superior in the fulfilment of key selection criteria. However, while there is an abundance of practitioner-oriented and research literature on the selection of proprietary, on-premises AS, research and organizations still lack a comprehensive framework to evaluate and compare new options in software delivery. In this study, it is our intention to add to the body of AS evaluation and selection research by proposing a framework that should help organizations examine how traditional, open-source, and on-demand AS compare in the fulfilment of key evaluation criteria based on the example of office suites. More specifically, we address the following two research questions:

RQ1: What comprehensive framework can be used to compare different software models (i.e., on-premise, closed-source vs on-premise, open-source vs on-demand, closed-source) based on the fulfilment of key evaluation criteria in office suite selection?

RQ2: What type of software model is comparatively superior in the fulfilment of key evaluation criteria in office suite selection from the perspective of IS managers?

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we discuss the relevant literature on AS selection in general and on office suite selection in particular. Then, we describe the characteristics of the most important alternative software models emerging in the field of office suites. This is followed by a discussion of our research methods, including the development of an AHP-based framework, data collection procedures, and sample descriptives. We continue with a presentation of the results of our empirical analyses. Finally, we discuss the major research and practical implications of our findings and conclude by presenting the study’s limitations and future research opportunities.

## The evaluation of office suites

## Literature review

Prior research on the selection of AS has emphasized the distinction between custom development environments, in which software is either developed in-house or under contract and intended for internal use, and package development environments, in which software is developed as a product for external sale. While a custom environment allows companies to specify their needs prior to development, with the software then being developed to meet those needs, a package environment is fundamentally different (Keil & Tiwana, 2006). Companies must select from an array of packages that have already been developed with the aim of finding one that best meets their organizational needs. The acquisition of AS packages, such as ERP, CRM, or office suites, thus often involves making trade-offs between a variety of attributes such as cost, functionality, or ease of customization. Many practitioner-oriented and academic studies in IS research have investigated the relative importance of these evaluation attributes for different application types. Jadhav & Sonar (2009) provide a comprehensive literature review on the evaluation and selection of software packages. They find that the majority of empirical studies investigate software packages based on criteria that are related to the software package itself (e.g., functionality, reliability, ease of use, cost) and to the implementation (e.g., ease of customization, ease of implementation, support) of the software package (e.g., Colombo & Francalanci, 2004; Keil & Tiwana, 2006).

Research on the evaluation and selection of office systems is embedded into the stream of office automation literature that goes back to the late 1970s and early 1980s (e.g., Konsynski & Bracker, 1982; Pava, 1984; Hirschheim, 1985). In their seminal study, Beck & Lin (1981) proposed a hierarchical system selection methodology for automated office systems and found in a case study that software capabilities (i.e., functionality and reliability) were the most important factors, while ease of imple mentation, compatibility issues, and vendor support were less important for the selection of office systems (Beck & Lin, 1981). After these findings were largely confirmed by empirical follow-up studies (e.g., Block & Kalyoncu, 1983; Seidmann & Arbel, 1983; Sassone, 1987), more recent studies have embraced elaborate research methodologies (such as fuzzy-based approaches or Analytic Hierarchy

Process (AHP) models) to yield more specific answers regarding the evaluation and selection of office systems. Anderson & Chen (1997), for example, found that ease of use has become much more important over time due to new hardware and software capabilities, while functionality has remained the dominant factor in office suite selection (Anderson & Chen, 1997). Noble (1995) focused his research more on implementation strategies for office systems and claimed that ease of implementation and customization are indeed crucial evaluation factors prior to purchasing and implementing office systems, as otherwise these systems would not be accepted by endusers (Noble, 1995).

## Key evaluation criteria

Consistent with Keil & Tiwana (2006) and in line with literature on office suite selection (e.g., Anderson & Chen, 1997), meta-analyses on AS evaluation (e.g., Jadhav & Sonar, 2009), and software quality models (e.g., Botella et al., 2002), seven factors of two categories emerged as the most used and most salient factors that have been assessed so far in packaged software selection.

(1) Software package attributes: cost, functionality, reliability, and ease of use.

(2) Implementation attributes: ease of customization, ease of implementation, and support.

These seven factors not only provide a practical and manageable list of evaluation criteria, but also allow researchers to analyze a parsimonious model on office suite selection, which is an important ingredient for good theory-development (Whetten, 1989).

Cost is a common factor influencing the purchaser to choose an application system (Davis & Williams, 1994). It is simply the expenditure associated with an application system and includes product, license, subscription, training, maintenance, and conversion costs. Technically, these costs can be grouped into three major categories, namely, acquisition, maintenance, and conversion costs (Borenstein & Betencourt, 2005). Acquisition costs are the non-recurring costs involved in providing the AS. They usually include product costs (i.e.. the basic cost of the office suite) and licensing costs (i.e., the cost of the office suite in terms of the number of users). Maintenance expenditures are the recurring costs involved in operating the office suite, which include maintenance/support costs as well as software subscription costs (i.e., a subscription sells periodic (monthly, yearly, or seasonal) use or access to the software or/and the right to upgrade the product to a major software release). Finally, conversion costs represent the amount paid to introduce the new system into a company’s application landscape and user environment. This amount may be considered the sum total of infrastructure software and/or configuration and equipment expenses, plus training costs (Borenstein & Betencourt. 2005).

Functionality refers to those features that the AS performs and, generally, to how well the software can meet the user’s needs and requirements. Thus, functionality is usually one of the most important selection criteria considered when selecting software packages (e.g., Lai et al., 1999). When investigating the functionality of a system, module completion and function fitness play the most important roles (Wei et al., 2005). The typical core components of office suites are word processing, spreadsheet analysis, and presentation programs, which are often but not always complemented by communication and groupware functionalities.

Brown & Stephenson (1981) suggest that one of the advantages of buying packaged software is that it represents a superior product relative to what might be developed in-house. Thus, what drives many packaged software purchases is the desire to have a higher quality, more robust, and more reliable piece of software. Wei et al. (2005) list reliability as one of the most important objectives that every software package should satisfy. Reliability refers to the capability of a software product to maintain its level of performance under stated conditions for a stated period of time (Losavio et al., 2003). The main constituent parts of system reliability are stability, security, and recoverability (Butler & Gray, 2006). While stability refers to the fault/failure tolerance and the robustness of the application system to run consistently without crashing, recoverability refers to the ability to restore a system deployment to the point at which a failure occurred (Fayad & Altman, 2001). The ability to recover quickly from a system failure or disaster depends not only on having current backups of the data, but also on having a predefined plan for recovering that data on new hardware. Bishop (2003) states that security is also a closely related concept to reliability because access in a system should be granted only to the allowed actors, and if the actor is not the one authenticated by the system, certain states guaranteeing system integrity should be assumed by default.

Chau (1995) stresses the importance of ease of use/userfriendliness as one of four technical factors that should be considered in selecting packaged software. Johannsen (1980) puts ‘usability’ third in a list of 11 objectives that every software package should satisfy. Montazemi et al. (1996) stress that the software package should be easy and straightforward to use, since the product should not be too complex or sophisticated for the average user, as the efficiency of end-users directly affects the efficiency of the organization. Technically, (perceived) ease of use is defined as the degree to which a prospective user expects the target system to be free of effort (Davis et al., 1989). The most important facets of ease of use to screen in software selection are the design, usability, and documentation of the software ( Jadhav & Sonar, 2009). While system design refers to the attractiveness and consistency of a user interface, usability refers to the capability of a software to be understood, learned, and used by addressing principles such as easy-to-use navigation, minimal response time, and high interactivity (Shneiderman, 1998; Palmer, 2002). The documentation of an application system refers to detailed information, in either written or computerized form, about a computer system, including its capabilities and functionalities, design and interfaces, data flow, and programming logic ( Jadhav & Sonar, 2009). Especially for software evaluation purposes, easy-to-use system documentation should meet the needs of both end-users and system administrators.

Bryce & Bryce (1987) suggest that software buyers usually consider ‘what changes to the system are required in the future’. Borenstein & Betencourt (2005) thus note that flexibility and agility are important considerations in selecting packaged software. By this, they mean whether the package can be easily adapted and customized to new, different, or changing requirements (Borenstein & Betencourt, 2005; Sarker et al., 2009). Existing MIS literature refers to three main forms of customization to consider in software evaluation: upgradeability, extensibility, and adaptability (Laudon & Laudon, 2002). Upgradeability refers to the ability of a system to extend its functionality over time through the integration of new system modules or entire releases (Wei et al., 2005). Extensibility (which is used synonymously with configurability) is defined as the capability of a current release of a system to be extended or adjusted by additional modules called add-ons (or bolt-ons) provided in the system or by third-party vendors during run-time. Finally, adaptability can be defined as the ability of an application system to be changed to fit individual requirements, often by using parameterization of given functional modules or even by modifying the software’s source code (Sawyer, 2000).

Pivnicny & Carmody (1989) list ease of implementation as one of nine criteria for evaluating packaged software. This criterion is usually highly ranked because of the extensive changes in policies and procedures required to implement new applications. Romanow et al. (1998) note that the time to implement and set up a software package (i.e., the so-called time-to-value) often surface as a key factor in a company’s packaged software selection process. In addition, the application components of the software package should dovetail easily with other applications of a company’s application architecture and provide seamless data flow among those applications and thus a high level of interoperability. In case a thirdparty application is needed, office suite components should be able to exchange data with the application, since data import/export and standardized file formats are crucial for business processes. Finally, ease of implementation does not only cover technical aspects but also relates to how easy a system is accepted by users and thus fits into the culture of a company (e.g., Ke & Wei, 2008). Studies show that the ease of implementation is enhanced if the absorptive capacity of an organization is high, which means that it recognizes the value of a new application system and is ready to assimilate it (Harrington & Guimaraes, 2005).

The quality of support provided and its characteristics are of major importance in the selection of software (e.g., Byun & Suh, 1996). It is particularly critical for the successful installation and maintenance of the software. Brown & Stephenson (1981) list ‘supplier support’ as the first of six general factors that should be considered in evaluating packaged software. Keller (1994) even emphasizes the importance of vendor stability over functionality, stating that ‘it is not so important that a company can meet a functional specification as it is that it has a viable plan to be in business in 5 years’. Important indicators of reliable and resourceful support include a hotline service, the quality and flexibility of training, and the presence of an active technical support program (Keller, 1994).

As shown above, a large body of research in software package evaluation and selection has evolved over the last three decades. However, the existing findings all refer to traditional methods of developing and distributing AS that are based on closed-source and on-premises software models. With the advent of viable alternative software models, namely open-source and on-demand, however, new options for IS managers have come up in the selection of AS in general and of office suites in particular that increase the degrees of freedom of IS managers in companies.

## Evaluation characteristics of emerging software models

## The open-source software (OSS) model

Open-source can be seen as a movement where communities of software developers, bug testers, patch submitters, and end-users collectively develop software in public bazaar-like spaces (Raymond, 2001). These spaces are distributed, delegated, and loosely coupled communities kept together by strong common values. Open-source development efforts are referred to as ‘projects’ and developers (who may be volunteers or paid employees) generally support continued development. OSS refers to software that is distributed under an Open Source Initiative license, which guarantees the buyer the right to read, redistribute, modify, and use the software freely (Ljungberg, 2000). By contrast, proprietary software usually belongs to a specific individual or company and is often protected by a patent or copyright law, while its source code is kept closed (Hemphill, 2006). Although Titterton (2003) states that 70% of business users are motivated primarily by cost savings to adopt open-source AS, OSS is not necessarily free of charge. An open-source community or software distributor may charge for services related to distributing, implementing, or otherwise supporting the software, or there may be charges for proprietary extensions or customizations built on top of OSS.

In terms of functionality, open-source AS are said to provide functionality that proprietary packaged AS often does not have (Bessen, 2005). Firms with specialized requirements can benefit from access to the software’s source code and thus may modify and augment the functionality of the application system according to their specific needs. On the other hand, OSS has traditionally built on infrastructural applications where the functional requirements are typically not derived from the needs of a company’s end-users but rather from system administrators (Fitzgerald, 2006). The reliability of OSS is often discussed in light of software code quality. OS advocates emphasize the superior quality of the open-source model due to the possibility of massive code-level peer reviews and developers’ more intrinsic motivation and higher sense of ownership within the OS community (Capra et al., 2008). Empirical studies have found, however, that testability, simplicity, readability, and self-descriptiveness as indicators of OSS structural code quality provided results lower than the quality implied by industrial standards (e.g., Stamelos et al., 2002). Opinions are also divided over the criterion ease of use. Since OSS developers are known to emphasize function over form, most OSS projects are driven by the functional requirements of developers who tend to look at usability from their own perspectives. In contrast, researchers arguing in favor of OSS claim that it has progressed from these early, amateurish stages. An empirical analysis by Olbrich & Koch (2006) comparing the usability of proprietary and open-source office systems has shown, for example, that there were no significant differences between both systems and also no major costs when switching from proprietary to open-source office systems according to the perspective of users.

On the basis of case studies in the U.K. public sector, Waring & Maddocks (2005) find that public organizations benefited from open-source AS’ high ease of customization. Using OSS enables organizations to provide tailored applications instead of the limited choices that proprietary software packages may offer. Having full access to the AS source code can facilitate this unavoidable customization and should make upgrades relatively easy and affordable (Serrano & Sarriegi, 2006). On the other hand, practitioners report that AS’ ease of customization is often hampered by the lack of in-house expertise, which is necessary to write fresh code to make an open-source program work in the way a company wants it to (Bartholomew, 2008). Proponents of open-source AS argue that OSS, in contrast to proprietary software, is based on open standard interfaces that allow software to be more easily implemented and integrated into an existing application landscape, which is especially important for application types that need to interact with many other applications in the company. Goode (2005), on the other hand, observes that OS-adopting firms see the adoption of and migration towards OSS as a significant undertaking, with a long and steep learning curve. Openstandard interfaces often do not support interoperability with legacy systems, leading to incompatibilities that must be solved by the user company. Another potential rub for organizations looking to deploy open-source applications lies in the support that is provided around the AS. Commonly described as a very ‘lean’ connection, relationships with open-source communities are built on trust rather than service (Bartholomew, 2008). Many organizations therefore view open-source applications as risky and carrying little or no assurance that the software will be supported in the present and future. Since community support is often available only via mailing lists associated with a project, response ranges from rapid to non-existent (Karels, 2003). However, open-source advocates claim that similar to proprietary AS, there is a spectrum of support models available for open-source AS with considerable momentum behind them: a company can perform support itself, join a consortium, and get consultancy or vendor support (Rahtz & Metcalfe, 2004).

## The on-demand software model

On-demand software application delivery models have existed since the late 1990s and have come in many forms and varieties such as Application Service Providing (ASP) or Business Service Providing (BSP). The common denominator of all these concepts is that this kind of demand-driven application sourcing model provides network-based (typically Internet-based) access for companies to AS functionality in the form of software services (Kern et al., 2002a). While discussions on ASPbased outsourcing have become rather silent in recent IS research and management literature, SaaS has gained attention from IS executives and is said to become a fundamental pillar of revenues for IT vendors in the next couple of years (Pettey, 2006). Although often discussed as an old wine in new skins concept, SaaS is promised to breach the last barriers of adoption due to an improved infrastructural and architectural set-up (Hall, 2008; Xin & Levina, 2008). SaaS is said to be superior to traditional on-premises software models in the sense that it helps companies to focus on core competencies, to reduce vendor lock-in and to increase the flexibility of choosing between different types of (state-of-the-art) technologies (Benlian et al., 2009).

More specifically, IT experts claim that software engineered for SaaS will have dramatically lower cost structures than traditional software models. In the SaaS business model. ownership and thus development and maintenance of software applications (and the whole software stack) remain in the vendor’s hands because the software is not installed on to a user’s computer as in the on-premises model. In this way, software vendors have full control of their software applications and the underlying infrastructure. By running a shared, multi-tenant infrastructure and providing a single instance of a highly standardized software service to multiple users, the SaaS business model can be based on an extremely scalable and cost-efficient platform. These improved economics in the provision of SaaS-based applications can be passed on to customers who benefit from a lower total cost of ownership. In particular, this lower total cost of ownership is said to be due to lower costs for basic and application infrastructure deployment, testing and ongoing business requirements change management as well as due to cost savings through higher server availability based on less unscheduled downtime and the avoidance of unused capacities (e.g., storage space, server utilization, licenses) (Dubey & Wagle, 2007). Despite having a limited set of functionalities and features compared to their on-premises counterparts, the simplicity of purpose and ease of use would allow SaaS to compete effectively with the traditional software model (Chou, 2008). Many users, in particular the upcoming younger generation, are accustomed to use browser technologies and user interfaces as a way to navigate through digital information environments and use personal and business-related applications. Proponents of the SaaS model indicate that on-demand applications borrow their design from web friendly consumer applications such as Google and Amazon.com and thus are more accessible and easier to learn than bulky on-premises applications (Menken, 2008). The most significant advantage of on-demand solutions is seen in the fact that they can be brought up and run more easily and quickly than their on-premises counterparts. SaaS-based solutions do not require tedious software installations on users’ computers and thus offer faster time-to-value. According to SaaS proponents, customers can effectively implement and use SaaS in a substantial way within days and weeks of purchase compared to the months or years usually required for traditional on-premise software (Chou, 2008). This is because SaaS vendors are responsible for making the pieces of the entire application stack work together and that they already know configuration and usage patterns. Technical support services are thus also considered more effective, as software vendors are much more familiar with the technical details of the installation than in a traditional on-premises situation where customers have to deal with installation issues on a daily basis (Xin & Levina, 2008). As most SaaS providers offer the same portfolio of end-user support services, hotline services or user trainings are largely comparable to traditional software models.

Countering these opportunities are the acknowledged risks of reliability (e.g., how can IT departments ensure that the business can access its applications and recover data in case of an emergency?) and security (e.g., how can IT departments guarantee data privacy is in line with company regulations?) when sourcing AS via a SaaS interface (Dubey & Wagle, 2007). When using SaaS, some if not all of the data of a SaaS client will be stored at the SaaS provider’s data center. SaaS clients give their direct control of their data and thus valuable assets ( Jurison, 1995) to an external provider without knowing how this provider will protect the data against breaches and losses and what backup and disaster recovery procedures the provider will have in place. Although service-level agreements can be used to write down exactly what data security controls should be maintained, uncertainty still remains on the side of SaaS customers (Benlian et al.,

2009). Also, the nature of Internet-based technologies and environmental uncertainties are still unpredictable, so that potential provider outages may cause feelings of anxiety and discomfort that the business will come to a halt or that poor application response time will hamper productivity (Kern et al., 2002b). Open questions also remain regarding the ease of customization of SaaS applications. In the multi-tenant architecture of the SaaS model, only a single instance of the common code and data definitions for a given application exists on the vendor’s server, and no customization of this code is permitted (Menken, 2008). Customer-specific configuration can, however, be made at the meta-data layer on top of the common code using interfaces provided by the SaaS vendor. The SaaS vendor is responsible for maintaining the common code base that delivers the standard application services to all customers, while each customer is left to his own devices to enhance and maintain his/her custom-developed code. The SaaS model still has to show whether pre-existing standard modules are good enough to fulfil the customization requirements of its customers or whether specialized system integrators have to come into play that, however, would eliminate much of the cost and flexibility advantages of SaaS.

The abovementioned characteristics of open-source and on-demand software models suggest that novel software models in comparison to traditional ones may better match some of the evaluation criteria in AS selection. However, although there is an abundance of research on the selection of proprietary, on-premises AS, IS research still lacks a systematic framework that helps evaluate and compare different software models in the selection of AS. In addition, although practitioneroriented literature highlights the industrial significance of competitive analyses comparing the effectiveness of different software models (e.g., Dekkers, 2002), academic research is still sparse in this area. In this study, we attempt to address these research gaps by developing and validating a comprehensive framework to examine how traditional, open-source, and on-demand AS compare in the fulfilment of key evaluation criteria based on the example of office suites.

## Research methods

## The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)

The AHP was developed by Saaty to solve complex multicriteria decision problems (Saaty, 1980). It is a flexible and powerful technique for handling both qualitative and quantitative multi-criteria problems. The AHP technique aims at integrating different measures into a single overall score for ranking decision alternatives. In addition, it enables decision-makers to assess the relative importance of the different attributes on a particular outcome (Bagchi & Rao, 1992) and thus, direct more focused attention to the alternatives that matter the most to an outcome. In our study too, to provide more guidance to decision-makers in charge of evaluating and selecting AS, it is important to have a comprehensive framework that helps determine which of the software model alternatives are superior in fulfilling specific evaluation criteria. Deriving knowledge of relative importance based on a systematic model can assist decisionmakers by providing guidance as to where to invest time and resources, and thereby maximize the probability of fostering successful AS selection. Thus, we believe that the use of AHP as the conceptual basis for the development of a framework is an interesting and productive way forward.

AHP has been applied to a wide range of decision contexts, such as vendor selection (Tam & Tummala, 2001), IS project selection (Muralidhar et al., 1990), distributed systems development (Sarker et al., 2009), and decision support systems (DSS) effectiveness (Gorgionne, 1999). Although there has also been a large number of research studies using AHP for software selection in different application domains such as antivirus and content filtering software, executive IS, simulation software, expert systems, multimedia authoring systems, logistics IS, or collaboration software ( Jadhav & Sonar, 2009), to date, however, no research has included software delivery models as key alternatives underlying the AS selection. The novelty of the framework presented in this paper is thus not only to provide an updated AHP model in the context of office suite selection based on an established set of evaluation criteria (e.g., Beck & Lin, 1981; Anderson & Chen, 1997). The framework also extends existing frameworks by considering different software delivery models as decision alternatives that are available to IS managers and thus provides a more comprehensive and realistic representation of both the problem and solution space in AS selection.

## An AHP-based framework on the selection of software models for office suites

To develop an AHP-based framework, Saaty suggests the following four steps to generate priorities and corresponding local and global weights of evaluation attributes that allow to make a selection decision in a structured and consistent way (Saaty, 2008): (1) Define the problem and determine the kind of knowledge sought. (2) Structure the decision hierarchy from the top with the goal of the decision, then the objectives from a broad perspective, through the intermediate levels (criteria on which subsequent elements depend) to the lowest level (which usually is a set of the alternatives). (3) Construct a set of pairwise comparison matrices. Each element in an upper level is used to compare the elements in the level immediately below it. (4) Use the priorities obtained from the comparisons to weigh the priorities in the level immediately below. Do this for every element. Then for each element in the level below, add its weighed values and obtain its overall or global priority. Continue this process of weighing and adding until the final priorities of the alternatives in the bottommost level are obtained.

Steps (1) and (2) were addressed in our study based on the literature review provided above including previous office suites evaluation studies (e.g., Anderson & Chen, 1997), meta-analyses on AS evaluation (e.g., Jadhav & Sonar, 2009), software quality models (see ISO/IEC 9126- 1 or its successor ISO/IEC 25000 (ISO, 2001, 2005)), and previous literature on new software model options. The problem definition and the kind of knowledge sought coincided with our second research question to identify the type of software model that is comparatively superior in the fulfilment of key evaluation criteria in office suite selection from the perspective of IS managers. The goal of the decision was thus to select a suitable software model for an office suite based on comparative assessments of well-established evaluation criteria. We grounded the decision alternatives on a conceptualization of software models (see Figure 1) that was based on two key dimensions capturing the most radical shifts in the development and delivery of AS in the last two decades (e.g., Cusumano, 2004, 2008).

Consistent with prior research (e.g., Cusumano, 2008), the first dimension refers to the location of software installation and deployment. While software that is installed at users’ premises (i.e., clients or servers) is deployed and delivered on-premise, software application can alternatively be delivered on-line via a network connection (e.g., the Internet) with the software installed at the provider’s or a third party’s premises (i.e., offpremise or on-demand software models). The second dimension that is well informed by the OSS literature (e.g., Aksulu & Wade, 2010) captures the openness of the software code being developed. As opposed to commercial proprietary or closed-source software models, OSS models allow the users to have access to the source code of the software, the freedom to use the software, improve it, augment its functionality, and redistribute the software or its derivative to other users (Nelson et al., 2006; Sen, 2007). In this framework, each dimension suggests two distinct aspects of software models rather than being a continuum-based variable. On the basis of this conceptualization of software models, we chose to use the three most common software models (i.e., traditional, open-source, and on-demand software models) as key alternatives for the development of the AHP-based framework.

Besides the three software model alternatives, two main categories of evaluation criteria (i.e., package and implementation attributes) and seven first level sub-criteria were used as main structural elements for the decision hierarchy of the AHP-based framework (see Figure 2). On the basis of previous office suite selection studies and recent AS evaluation meta-analyses (see section ‘Key evaluation criteria’), each first level sub-criterion was then sub-divided into three second level sub-criteria that covered the most salient evaluation factors reported in previous research studies. In addition, the face validation process of the resulting decision hierarchy was carried out together with IS practitioners and researchers having an expertise in AS evaluation (see also section ‘Empirical study’).

To address steps (3) and (4), the attributes (or subcriteria) of office suites on each level were compared in terms of their importance within a given category by a randomly selected sample of respondents (see section ‘Sample descriptives’ for an overview on the respondent profile). Several methods for making the comparisons exist. The most common one, suggested by Saaty (2005), requires from the respondents to provide a rate, w , regarding the importance of an attribute, X, in comparison to the importance of another attribute of the same category, Y (see Table 1).

On the basis of this pairwise comparison method using Saaty’s (1980) standard scale (i.e., we used this scale with an odd number of ratings to make sure there is a reasonable distinction among the measurement points and to determine a middle point), the respondents of our study first had to compare the relative importance of all second level sub-criteria within their corresponding first level sub-criterion (e.g., relative importance of security vs stability regarding reliability). Second, they had to compare all first level sub-criteria within their respective Level 2 criterion (e.g., reliability vs cost regarding package attributes). In a third step, our respondents had to assess the relative importance of product vs implementation attributes (as a whole) for achieving the goal of choosing a suitable office suite. Finally, the respondents had to assess the three alternative software models on a pairwise basis and indicate which one they preferred in terms of the fulfilment of each of the second level sub-criteria.

After having derived the rates $w _ { X Y }$ for all levels and alternatives, the rate of the importance of attribute Y over X, the reciprocal comparison, is deduced from the previous (and is given by $1 / w _ { X Y }$ . By assigning numerical ratings to each comparison, a matrix of weights can be derived reflecting the importance of each attribute with respect to each other. By using this procedure, there are no symmetric inconsistencies (i.e., the importance of Y over X will always be consistent with the importance of X over Y ). However, the transitive property may not be hold $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the degree of importance of X over Y does not have to be consistent with the importance of X over $Z$ and Z over Y ). Therefore, the possibility of potential inconsistencies has to be analyzed by using a normalized consistency ratio (CR). In this context, the threshold value of 0.1 is an accepted upper limit for CR (Zahedi, 1986). To determine local weights for the alternative sub-criteria (e.g., functionality) with respect to the upper criteria (e.g., package attributes), normalized matrices are calculated based on the values of the pairwise comparisons (Saaty, 1980). Local weights are then derived by calculating the average value in each row of each normalized matrix (Lai et $a l . ,$ 1999). Global weights are calculated by multiplying the weight of the upper criteria by the local weights (Saaty, 1980). Finally, once all the relative weights have been calculated, a composite weight, $c _ { d } ,$ for each decision alternative $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ for each software model in our study) is determined. This is accomplished by aggregating the weights over the hierarchy for each decision choice. To do this, the weights along the path from the top of the hierarchy down to a decision choice are multiplied and then added across all the different pathways to that decision choice (Saaty, 2008).

## Empirical study

Data collection In order to test and validate our AHPbased framework, we conducted an online survey during June and July 2009 using the Expert Choice’s Decision Portal (ECDP) among IS decision-makers in companies. All German companies in manufacturing, wholesale and retail trade, TIME (i.e., telecommunication, information, media and entertainment) industries, financial services, construction and real estate, public and healthcare, logistics and electricity/gas/water supply were chosen as the population of this study for two reasons: (1) the concentration of office system use is relatively high in these industries; (2) company size restrictions are not necessary because office suites are implemented and used in companies of all sizes in the selected industries. The survey underwent both a pretest and pilot phase. Content and face validity of our survey instrument was ensured by asking 10 IS practitioners and three academic experts in a workshop setting (Borenstein, 1998) to go through the hierarchical model presented in the survey. According to Falkner & Benhajla (1990), they were asked to provide feedback on the comprehension, clarity, completeness, non-redundancy, and representativity of the criteria and sub-criteria used in the AHP model. After two rounds of minor revisions, the survey was pre-tested in a pilot phase with another 10 IS managers. Only minor changes and adjustments had to be made to the survey instrument.

For the online survey, a random sample of IS managers in 1500 German companies was drawn from Hoppenstedt’s firm database based on the population characteristics mentioned above and invited by mail and e-mail to participate. To foster participation and reduce self-reporting bias, each participant was given the opportunity to receive a report regarding how the evaluation results of his/her organization compares to those of firms with similar size and in similar industries. In our cover letters, we explicitly addressed those IS managers that had the qualification and mandate in an organization to purchase ${ \mathrm { A } } S ,$ as they were the most appropriate respondents for our study. Consistent with previous AHP studies that usually address one expert/manager to collect a single set of pairwise comparisons (Saaty, 1994), the current study utilized a ‘key informants methodology for data collection. In organizational survey research, targeted respondents assume the role of a key informant and provide information on a particular unit of analysis by reporting on group or organizational properties. However, if a respondent lacks appropriate knowledge, results can be confounding and may lead to erroneous conclusions. Therefore, within the context of this study, it was important to identify respondents within organizations who were intimately involved with and most knowledgeable about office systems evaluation and selection practices within their organization. With this in mind, we introduced our survey with clear definitions of the different types of office alternatives and evaluation criteria used in the survey and instructed the respondents on the use of the ECDP platform that allowed users to determine the pairwise weighting of the criteria and alternatives in a very user-friendly environment. In addition, we pointed out to respondents to fill out the survey from the perspective of their organization.

As for the alternatives, we chose to use very prominent representatives for each type of software model, since we assumed that participants had a sufficient level of familiarity with these types of office suites: MS Office for on-premise, closed-source (with a focus on MS Word, Excel, and PowerPoint); OpenOffice.org for on-premise, open-source (with a focus on OpenOffice Writer, Calc, and Impress); and Zoho for on-demand, closed-source (with a focus on Zoho Writer, Sheet, and Show). We asked participants to focus on three core and common functionalities of office suites, namely ‘word processing’, ‘spreadsheet analysis’, and ‘presentation’. By choosing these three functional modules, we aimed to reduce the complexity for participants and ensure comparability among the abovementioned software packages (e.g., OpenOffice.org does not include a communication-collaboration module). The participants were also provided with the possibility to play with the different application systems online by providing links to the respective systems on ECDP.

After the introduction, participants had to compare the (sub-)criteria on each level on a pairwise basis with respect to the upper criteria or goal. Overall, the participants had to conduct 94 pairwise comparisons including one for level 2 criteria, nine for first level sub-criteria, 21 for second level sub-criteria, and 63 for level 3 alternatives (see Figure 1). While making the comparisons, participants had the opportunity to comment on each comparison by entering qualitative statements into text boxes. At the end of the survey, participants had the opportunity to review the consistency and correct their judgments to verify the model. In the case that a respondent’s comparisons were too inconsistent (i.e., CR410%), ECDP automatically requested a respondent to re-score his or her ratings. Finally, participants had the chance to provide overall qualitative comments as well as socio-demographic information. Overall, the mean of the consistency scores of the input judgments at all levels was well below the 10% cut-off value (CR ¼ 4.3%) suggesting a high level of consistency in the respondents’ answers. Likewise, the means of the input judgments’ consistency scores for both sub-samples (i.e., small and mid-sized/ large firms) we assessed in this study were also well below the 10% cut-off value $( \mathrm { C R _ { \mathrm { { s m a l l } } } } = 4 . 5 \% ; ~ \mathrm { C R _ { \mathrm { { b i g } } } } = 4 . 1 \% )$ validating the framework’s validity (Saaty, 1980).

Sample descriptives We received 254 completed sets of responses with an average completion time of 27.15 min, resulting in a response rate of 17%. Non-response bias was assessed (1) by verifying that respondents’ demographics (see Table 2) were similar to those of previous studies and (2) by ensuring that early and late respondents were not significantly different (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). Early respondents were those who responded within the first two weeks (slightly over 55 percent). All t-tests between the means of the early and late respondents showed no significant differences, and the demographics of our sample were similar to the demographics reported by other software evaluation studies (Keil & Tiwana, 2006).

More than 75% of the respondents stated to be IS managers and among other things in charge of procuring AS and around 14% indicated that they were Chief Information Officers (CIO) or Chief Technology Officers (CTO). On average, the respondents had 12.8 years of experience in the management of information systems and had previously been involved in making AS selection decisions for around 28 software packages on average. They also indicated on a 5-point Likert scale that they had personally used the different types of office suites investigated in our study to a great extent and thus were highly confident in being able to judge the relative advantages of the different office suite types. Altogether, the respondents were highly qualified to answer the survey questions. Table 2 shows the sample characteristics of our empirical study.

Our sample included firms with the following industry split: manufacturing (22.0%), wholesale and retail trade (20.9%), financial intermediation (18.9%), TIME industries (14.2%), construction and real estate (11.0%), logistics (7.1%), public and healthcare sector (3.9%), and electricity/gas/water supply (2.0%). 92.9% of the firms in our sample used MS Office, 65.0% OpenOffice.org and almost half used Zoho or Google Docs & Spreadsheet. This split was considerably different when looking at smaller (o50 employees; N ¼ 166) and midsized to larger firms (4 ¼ 50 employees; N ¼ 88). Smaller firms more frequently employed open-source (74.0%) and on-demand based (55.1%) alternatives to MS Office (90.1%) compared to mid-sized and larger firms (Open-Office.org: 48.0%; Zoho/Google Docs & Spreadsheets: 32.0%; MS Office: 98.0%).

## Results

Relative importance of evaluation criteria in office suite selection

According to the perspective of IS managers of smaller companies, package attributes (0.604) were valued more highly than implementation attributes (0.396) in the selection of office suites (see Figure 3 at the top). More specifically, functionality was the most critical factor with a local weight of 0.267 for package attributes followed by ease of use (0.258), cost (0.255), and reliability (0.221). Support was the most critical factor for implementation attributes with a local weight of 0.399 followed by ease of implementation (0.319) and ease of customization (0.282).

For a global weights-based ranking of evaluation criteria, the AHP framework for small firms showed that functionality was the most valued sub-criteria with a global weight of 0.161 followed by support (0.158), ease of use (0.156), and cost (0.154). Interestingly, reliability (0.133) was ranked fifth only followed by ease of implementation (0.126) and ease of customization (0.112) as the two least critical sub-criteria. On the second sub-criteria level, the fulfilment of word processing functionality (0.067), the provision of hotline services (0.064), and usability/design (0.053) were the most important factors in office suite selection as perceived by IS managers of smaller firms. Interestingly, extensibility (0.033) and adaptability (0.037) were the factors ranked lowest.

For IS managers of mid-sized/larger companies and in contrast to their counterparts in smaller firms, package and implementation attributes were more or less on an equal footing with local weights of 0.532 and 0.468, respectively (see Figure 3 at the bottom). In line with the ranking of IS managers in smaller firms, however, functionality (0.314) was also ranked highest followed by ease of use (0.271), cost (0.231), and reliability (0.184). Likewise, support (0.361) was rated as the most important sub-criteria of implementation attributes followed by ease of implementation (0.351) and ease of customization (0.288). For a global weights-based ranking of evaluation criteria, however, we found a different picture for midsized/larger compared to smaller firms. Support was ranked highest among all evaluation criteria with a global weight of 0.169. Functionality was ranked second (0.167) followed by ease of implementation (0.164), ease of use (0.144), ease of customization (0.135), cost (0.123), and reliability (0.098). Explaining the relatively strong position of ease of implementation in the ranking by IS managers of mid-sized/larger firms, interoperability was by far the most important attribute on the second subcriteria level with a global weight of 0.080. Usability (0.064), the provision of hotline services (0.063), and spreadsheet functionality (0.058) followed interoperability in the ranking. Finally, an unexpected result was that security (0.025), cultural fit, stability, and documentation (all 0.034) were ranked lowest.

## Comparison of software models in fulfilling evaluation criteria

The results in Table 3 show that the assessments of evaluation criteria for traditional, open-source, and ondemand office suites differ considerably between smaller and medium-sized/larger firms. While open-source $( c _ { d } = 0 . 3 5 4 )$ and on-demand $( c _ { d } = 0 . 3 4 6 )$ solutions have an edge over traditional office suites $( c _ { d } = 0 . 3 0 0 )$ in smaller firms, traditional office suites $( c _ { d } = 0 . 4 0 2 )$ are in turn clearly the dominant option in medium-sized/larger organizations compared to open-source $( c _ { d } = 0 . 3 1 6 )$ and on-demand $( c _ { d } = 0 . 2 8 2 )$ office suites. Although the distribution of the top positions in the rankings of the first level sub-criteria does not differ between the two sub-samples, the absolute values of the evaluation criteria diverge significantly from one another leading to a differentiated picture in lower levels of the AHP model. According to the results of our two sub-samples, package and implementation attributes are overall best met with open-source and on-demand solutions in smaller firms, whereas traditional office suites are superior in fulfilling package and implementation attributes as perceived by IS managers of medium-sized/larger firms.

Digging deeper into the AHP-based frameworks of smaller and medium-sized/larger firms, we found as most striking differences in fulfilling package attributes that perceptions on stability, security, and conversion costs differed significantly between the two sub-samples. While open-source and on-demand office suites were perceived to perform better in providing stability and security and to entail less conversion costs (i.e., less compatibility issues) in smaller companies, it was exactly the other way round for bigger companies. An IS manager of a smaller firm provided an insightful statement that may explain this difference to some extent: As a smaller organization, our requirements regarding data security and network stability are less complex than in bigger organizations. As such, we do not need the full-blown technical features of proprietary office solutions that are oftentimes even overengineered. Given the usually less complex functional and technical requirements in smaller companies, this comment may also provide some indications why traditional office suites’ dominance in fulfilling the criteria ‘functionality’ and ‘ease of use’ is less pronounced in smaller than in bigger firms.

With regard to implementation attributes, we found that smaller firms perceived traditional office suites to be relatively more difficult to customize (particularly regarding upgradeability and adaptability) and to implement (i.e., all three sub-criteria were weighed significantly lower in smaller than in mid-sized/larger companies) compared to open-source and on-demand solutions. It is also noteworthy that, in line with our expectations, the cultural fit of traditional office suites was weighted significantly lower in smaller companies than in bigger companies suggesting that smaller companies are less open to proprietary software models, while being more willing to accept alternative ones. Several qualitative comments we collected from respondents of smaller companies provided interesting insights pointing into this direction. One of the IS managers for example indicated that our employees are very keen on trying out new things. Another put forth that our organization is less trustful in commercial software because we cannot grasp what’s going on behind the scenes.

Analyzing the strengths and weaknesses of each alternative across the two sub-samples, we found that the traditional software model comparatively excelled in the fulfilment of the evaluation criteria ‘functionality, ‘ease of use’, and ‘support’. All of the second level subcriteria were consistently better fulfilled by traditional than by open-source or on-demand software models. On the downside, the traditional office suite option was considerably lagging behind on-demand and open-source alternatives with respect to ‘cost’ (i.e., acquisition and maintenance cost), ‘reliability’ (i.e., in particular data recoverability), and ‘ease of implementation’. A qualitative comment of one IS manager fittingly summarized the results: MS Office can look back on a long tradition of providing office functionality and constantly honing the usability of its programs. However, when it comes to cost, system interoperability and data recoverability, it is outperformed by alternative solutions.

Across both sub-samples analyzed, the OSS model was the superior alternative in meeting cost criteria (i.e., acquisition and maintenance cost), ‘ease of customization’ (i.e., extensibility and adaptability), and ‘ease of implementation’ (i.e., interoperability and cultural fit), while major pain points were ‘time-to-value’ and particularly all support attributes. As expected, low acquisition costs and the ease of adapting the source code to individual requirements were the strongest contributors to the overall score of the open-source alternative. In this respect, another respondent put an insightful comment forth: The big advantage of opensource [office suites, the authors] for our organisation is that our in-house developers can easily adjust the basic functionalities to our specific company requirements and put these customizations up for discussion in the OS community for quality checks. It is also interesting to note that across the two sub-samples, the open-source alternative also scored highest with regard to the attributes ‘interoperability and ‘cultural fit’ indicating that open-source solutions can be integrated rather smoothly into the technical and cultural landscapes of smaller, but also mid-sized/ larger companies than traditional or on-demand Office solutions.

Finally, the on-demand software model’s major weaknesses in the fulfilment of evaluation criteria across the two sub-samples were the factors ‘functionality’ and ‘ease of use’, while its outstanding strengths were ‘data recoverability’ and in particular a fast ‘timeto-value’. In this context, an interesting comment of an IS manager of a smaller firm regarding the on-demand provision of office suites was that [y] on-demand solutions such as Zoho or Google Apps prevent us from having the hassle to install and update the software on a regular basis. It helps us to avoid the regular effort to integrate new releases and, in addition, have access to the latest software releases.

## Discussion

## Major findings and contributions

This study is the first to develop a comprehensive framework to examine how new (i.e., open-source and on-demand) software delivery models of AS compare with traditional ones in the fulfilment of evaluation criteria in AS selection. By integrating different types of software models in an AHP-based evaluation framework and by validating it based on an empirical sample of 254 IS managers, the study extends previous models providing a more comprehensive and realistic representation of both the problem and solution space in AS selection. It also updates and extends the existing body of knowledge in office suite selection by investigating the relative superiority of the different software models in fulfilling key evaluation criteria. On the basis of the development and application of our framework, we can derive several implications for practitioners and researchers.

First, by developing and validating a comprehensive framework to examine how alternative software delivery models compare in the fulfilment of evaluation criteria in AS selection, the study’s results can be used as a blueprint or guideline to which IS managers can compare their own thinking. More specifically, the proposed framework could serve as a prototype that can be adopted by organizations to assess different software delivery models in AS selection as a basis for decision-making. At the same time, organizations may use the empirical results of this study as benchmarks in own scoring methods or choice models (such as linear weighted attribute or linear assignment models) to specify organizational requirements in AS selection (Anderson, 1990). Our assessment framework is particularly useful for assigning greater priority or attention to those criteria found to be substantially contributing toward the goal of choosing the most suitable office suite. Given that most IS decision-makers are constrained by budget, time, and other resource issues, by using this framework, decisionmakers can direct more focused attention to the criteria and sub-criteria that are most relevant, as opposed to directing resources toward an arbitrary set of selection criteria or wastefully toward all criteria.

Second. and on the basis of the empirical validation of our framework, we found in our study that open-source and on-demand office suites outperformed traditional ones in smaller firms, while it was the other way round for medium-sized and larger firms. These results provide valuable insights into how differently IS managers of small and mid-sized/larger companies perceive and value the relative performance of each software model in fulfilling the investigated evaluation criteria. IS managers of smaller firms perceived on an aggregate level (i.e., including all sub-criteria) that package and implementation attributes are best met with open-source and ondemand office solutions. Conversely, the traditional ondemand software model is clearly superior in fulfilling package and implementation attributes (i.e., including all sub-criteria) as perceived by IS managers of mediumsized/larger firms. On the basis of the qualitative statements we collected, we could gather some indications on the reasons for the divergences between the two sub-samples. Smaller firms seem to have less complex functional and technical requirements and appear to need more ‘quick-and-dirty’ solutions compared to midsized/larger firms in terms of the fulfilment of evaluation criteria such as security, stability, ease of use, and scope of functionality. Since traditional office suites are perceived to be ‘over-engineered’ and to provide rather a monolithic set of full-blown technical and functional features, smaller firms may rate the fulfilment of evaluation criteria by open-source and on-demand solutions relatively stronger. In addition, smaller firms tend to prefer open-source and on-demand solutions to traditional ones also on a cultural basis adding an emotional dimension to the predominantly rational evaluation criteria included in our framework. Conversely, bigger companies that usually require a more comprehensive spectrum of technical and functional features weigh traditional office suites’ fulfilment of the majority of evaluation criteria higher than smaller firms do. They thus tend to perceive traditional office suites as being more professional and mature, which seems to be relatively more important to them than cost or implementation (including cultural) criteria. Taken together, these results underscore the relevance and necessity of a comprehensive framework that captures a diverse set of evaluation criteria at different hierarchy levels.

Third, we can also derive recommendations for office suite providers regarding what factors they should emphasize to address potential weaknesses in their software offerings. If traditional software models and their providers want to maintain their dominant role in the office suite market in the long run, they can and should address not only pricing schemes but also specific subcriteria of reliability (e.g., data recoverability), ease of customization (e.g., adaptability), and implementation of their software (e.g., interoperability). As the traditional software model was – especially in smaller firms – ranked lowest in these areas, covering these aspects in product development (e.g., increase stability, security and data recoverability of the system), pricing (e.g., introduce subscription-based pricing models for specific customer groups), partnering (e.g., increase influence on thirdparty implementation and integration partners), and portfolio initiatives (e.g., enter the on-demand or opensource market for light-weight versions) seem to be promising avenues for the future.

With respect to open-source office suites, we can derive from the results of this study that two crucial factors should be addressed to increase the attractiveness of the open-source model. First, open-source office suites still seem to fare relatively poorly in providing professional software support that encompasses hotline services (i.e., instant support from the OS community), technical support (i.e., support in terms of implementation and customization), and training of end-users. In order to address these support issues, open-source project administrators should think over different options to provide support that is more professional than it has been in the past. One option could be to outsource support to professional IT services firms. Another is to establish a core group of employees to provide support services as a complement to the broader group of volunteers; this has already been done in the successful Mozilla project (Mendonca & Sutton, 2008). Second, open-source office suites also still seem to lag behind its traditional counterpart in the coverage of the factor ‘functionality ’. In this regard, open-source project administrators should also think about how to extend the feature set of their office suites to further close the functionality gap with traditional products.

Furthermore, we can learn from the study’s results that on-demand products are strong in fulfilling implementation attributes (i.e., in particular time-to-value), as they obviously spare user companies (i.e., in particular smaller firms) from big installation intricacies and hassles. However, if on-demand office solutions want to be a serious contender to traditional and open-source products, they particularly have to improve on the fulfilment of package attributes. However, on-demand office solutions’ biggest weaknesses were – from the perspective of IS managers – not seen in the areas of security and network availability, as often mentioned by SaaS sceptics. Instead, on-demand office suites seem to offer just lightweight functionality and a lower level of usability that cannot compete with on-premise installations in an organizational setting. If they want to gain a stronger foothold in business markets, a high priority should be to enhance functionality and ease of use. In addition, the lack of customization possibilities may be another vulnerable Achilles heel for on-demand software models, especially from the perspective of larger firms that oftentimes need proprietary extensions to the standardized set of functionality provided. Overall, IS managers of SaaS companies should think about how they can reconcile their interests for providing a platform that builds on economies of scale with customized solutions that in turn increase a provider’s total cost base.

Finally, we could partly validate previous results in office suites selection research (e.g., Beck & Lin, 1981; Anderson & Chen, 1997) indicating that functionality and ease of use are among the most crucial selection criteria. In addition, we could confirm that implementation attributes such as ease of implementation and in particular ease of customization are of less concern in the eyes of IS managers when evaluating office systems. This may be due to the fact that they usually do not require much sophisticated implementation and adaptation. However, we could also show that specific evaluation factors that have been attached a lower level of importance in the past have become much more important in office suite selection and vice versa. More specifically, ‘support’ has been a less important evaluation factor in previous studies, while IS managers have put much more emphasis on ‘reliability’ than could be found in this study. One possible explanation could be that by considering relevant new options for the provision of office suites such as open-source, IS managers have shifted their attention and preferences towards evaluation factors that are more important for the evaluation of other than traditional software models. For example, since open-source office suites that, according to our results, are deemed a highly relevant option, are performing worse than traditional and on-demand software models in providing good software support, IS managers may want to put relatively more emphasis on this aspect when scrutinizing different alternatives.

## Limitations, future directions, and conclusion

Our research has some limitations that provide opportunities for further research. First, we compared traditional, open-source, and on-demand software models with regard to their fulfilment of evaluation criteria in office suite selection. Although office suites are a widely used type of AS (i.e., more specifically a type of productivity software), they are highly standardized, which means that they do not require high levels of customization. Given the validation of the AHP-based framework comprising different software models as key decision alternatives, future studies should investigate how these different software models compare in the fulfilment of evaluation criteria for application types that usually require higher levels of adaptation such as ERP or CRM systems. Second, since we used a cross-sectional design for the investigation of our research questions, the results of the study are limited regarding their validity over time. Although our findings on the relative superiority of different software models in fulfilling specific evaluation criteria and the AHP-based assessment framework should have an enduring value to IS managers, future studies should examine how the relative and global weights that IS managers ascribe to the evaluation criteria and alternatives will evolve over time. Third, for the sake of clarity, we restricted our analysis to pure forms of software models opposing each other at the extremes of different continuums (i.e., closed-source vs open-source; on-premise vs on-demand). Future research has to examine how hybrid forms of software models (e.g., open-source core functionalities with proprietary modules on top or open-source/on-demand options) may ‘blur’ the clear lines that we have drawn with our results. Fourth, we focused our sub-group analysis on smalle and mid-sized/larger firms to reveal that the percep tions on the fulfilment of evaluation criteria by different software models vary considerably across firm size. The analysis of other relevant sub-samples including different industries or degrees of usage intensity would certainly further deepen our understanding of the relative performance of different software models. Finally, we used a key informant method for data collection. This method, while having its advantages, also suffers from the limitations that the data reflects the opinion of one person. Although our data represent the perceptions of IS managers who were highly qualified to participate in our survey, we recommend that future studies consider research designs that allow data collection from multiple respondents within an organization who are important for AS selection such as end-users or system administrators.

To conclude, new emerging software models have evolved as serious contenders to the traditional methods of developing and delivering software and promise to challenge the dominant position of the traditional players in different AS markets. More recent dynamics in the office suite market have shown that traditional software companies try to assume and benefit from the open-source and on-demand software models.
