# Closing the loop: Empirical evidence for a positive feedback model of IT business value creation

## a b s t r a c t

Researchers have established that information technology (IT) can improve firms’ productivity. Whether improved productivity leads to additional investment in IT, however, remains largely uninvestigated. In this paper, we consider whether the relationship between productivity and subsequent IT investment might be positive, negative, or ad hoc, and hypothesize that this relationship is positive. We analyze seven years of panel data from 1236 healthcare firms and present empirical evidence supporting our hypothesis. When our finding is combined with extant research, it becomes reasonable to propose that unidirectional causality does not fully describe the process of IT business value creation. Instead, we argue that existing static models of IT business value with unidirectional causality can be recast as dynamic models that explicitly incorporate multiple time periods and a positive feedback relationship to more accurately capture the complexity of this process. The creation of IT business value can thus be understood as a positive feedback model where IT investment in a given time period builds the stock of IT inputs, where those IT inputs then impact productivity, and where productivity leads to IT investment in a future time period, beginning the cycle anew.

## 1. Introduction

What is the relationship between IT investment and an organization’s productivity? The importance of this question is illustrated by the fact that approximately \$3.52 trillion was spent worldwide on IT in 2015, with continued growth expected through 2019 (Gartner, 2016). Given these levels of investment and expenditure, IT leaders need to understand the relationship between IT investment and productivity, and be able to articulate to senior management the ways in which these variables impact the firm over time. The relationship between IT and productivity has been an important research agenda for over two decades (Bender, 1986; Cron and Sobol, 1983). Numerous review articles (Brynjolffson and Yang, 1996; Dehning and Richardson, 2002; Kohli and Devaraj, 2003; Kohli and Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj, 2015), and special issues of academic journals indicate that this topic continues to draw considerable interest (Clemons et al., 2004; Fichman et al., 2011; Mahmood et al., 2004; Mahmood and Mann, 2000).

In spite of the work that has been done, questions about the process of IT business value creation still remain (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Chang and Gurbaxani, 2012; Devaraj and Kohli, 2000; Salge et al., 2015; Tambe and Hitt, 2012, 2014). One of the most important of these questions is the question of causality. By now it is well established that investments in IT can lead to improvements in productivity (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996; Kohli and Devaraj, 2003; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj, 2015). But what is the influence of improved productivity on subsequent IT investment? It may be the case that IT investment drives productivity and that productive firms are then able to invest more in IT. To investigate this issue of causality, we consider rationales for three possible scenarios: that productivity positively influences subsequent investment in IT, that productivity negatively influences investment in IT, or that investment in IT is ad hoc rather than related to prior productivity levels. Drawing upon economic theory, we hypothesize the positive causal relationship, namely that increases in productivity in a given time period lead to increases in IT investment in future time periods.

By coupling our arguments and our empirical findings with extant research into IT business value, we are able to make two contributions to IS literature. First, we help bring clarity to the equivocal results provided by existing examinations of causality in IT business value. As we investigate a large, longitudinal dataset, we perform statistical tests explicitly designed to determine causality, and find support for positive feedback from productivity in one time period to IT investment in the next. Second, we integrate our results with prior research findings as we propose and describe a comprehensive, dynamic model of IT business value creation.

The paper proceeds as follows. We begin by reviewing IT business value literature and note that the influence of productivity on subsequent IT investment is largely unresearched. In the Hypothesis Development section, we consider the question of causality and argue for a positive relationship, one where productivity gains in a given time period influence a firm to increase investment in IT in future time periods. The Method section describes our seven-year panel of data from 1236 firms in the healthcare industry, our variables, and the measurement of those variables. There, we also describe the econometric techniques that we use to test our hypothesis. In the Results section, we note strong support for our hypothesis. Our hypothesis is supported by analysis of our entire dataset as well as when different subsets of organizations are examined. In the Discussion section, we highlight that our empirical results can be combined with foregoing research to re-cast existing static models of IT business value as dynamic models that explicitly incorporate multiple time periods and a feedback relationship. The limitations of our study and potential directions for future research appear in the Discussion section as well.

## 2. Background and theory

## 2.1. The influence of IT on firm performance and productivity

The investigation of how IT impacts firm performance and productivity has been and continues to be a topic of interest to researchers. Early studies in the 1980s and 1990s identified the ‘‘productivity paradox,” a phenomenon where investments in IT failed to produce expected benefits in terms of increased productivity or performance (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Strassman, 1997; Weill, 1992). The remediation of measurement and modeling issues during the late 1990s, however, allowed researchers to start observing the positive impact of IT on productivity and performance. A series of papers found clear evidence of the beneficial effects of IT for organizations and laid the ‘‘productivity paradox” to rest (Brynjolffson and Yang, 1996; Bryniolfsson and Hitt, 1996: Dewan and Min, 1997: Mahmood et al, 1999) Several reviews of this phase of research sum: marize the key findings and methodological advances that enabled researchers to identify the productivity and performance gains attributable to IT (Brynjolffson and Yang, 1996; Mahmood et al., 1999; Sircar et al., 1998).

This research stream has continued to evolve. Some researchers have begun to study the impact of specific types of information systems on performance and productivity (Aral et al., 2006; Hendricks et al., 2007; Hitt et al., 2002; Lee and Choi, 2003). Other researchers have proposed a number of models and frameworks to investigate the impact of IT on performance and productivity (Kumar, 2004; Santhanam and Hartono, 2003; Thatcher and Oliver, 2001; Thatcher and Pingry, 2004). Still others have focused on factors that may catalyze IT investment, such as business process re-engineering (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000), corporate diversification (Chari et al., 2008), organizational transformation (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000; Milgrom and Roberts, 1990), and organizational learning (Tippins and Sohi, 2003). Researchers have also emphasized several aspects of contract design to capture the business value of IT investments (Wu et al., 2013). Each of these studies has shown similar results: the impact of IT on performance and productivity may be affected by contextual factors beyond merely the information system or technology that is being purchased, implemented, or utilized

More recent work has questioned whether rapid technological progress has altered the nature of IT as a production factor (Chwelos et al., 2010) and whether returns to IT are diminishing because of common standards for IT infrastructure (Bardhan et al., 2013: Bhatt and Grover, 2005: DosSantos et al., 2012). Researchers have also examined the impact of IT investments on intangible outputs such as innovation and knowledge creation (Kleis et al., 2012). Still others have examined the presence and effects of IT spillovers (Chang and Gurbaxani, 2012; Tambe and Hitt, 2012), as well as their associated measurement issues (Tambe and Hitt, 2014). Within this research, the question of causality has been raised (Tambe and Hitt, 2014), with particular attention paid to whether IT investments lead to improved productivity, or if the reverse is true. It is to this specific issue of causality that we now turn.

## 2.2. The effect of productivity on subsequent IT investment

Early work in the study of IT business value (Cron and Sobol, 1983; Gold, 1964; Kriebel and Raviv, 1980; Lucas, 1975a,b; Roach, 1989) led to a preliminary exploration of the question of reinvestment. An early model proposes a circular relationship in the form of a positive feedback loop where IT investment improves firm performance, which in turn enables additional IT investment in future time periods (Weill, 1992). Empirical evidence, however, only partially supports a positive association between performance in a given year and IT investment in the subsequent year. Three types of IT investments are examined in the testing of this early model, with ‘‘transactional IT” significantly associated with the previous year’s performance, but neither ‘‘strategic IT” nor ‘‘informational IT” displaying any relationship (Weill, 1992). Elsewhere, a study of knowledge management (KM) systems presents a conceptual model that describes how KM systems produce an organizational performance payoff that, in turn, stimulates positive feedback to initial enablers, processes, and intermediate outcomes (Lee and Choi, 2003). This feedback relationship is not, however, part of that study’s research model and not empirically tested.

An additional study examining the direction of causality in IT business value creation has been conducted in the context of enterprise software systems. These researchers find that ERP usage improves organizational performance and productivity, which enables the subsequent adoption and use of SCM and CRM systems, which further improve performance and productivity (Aral et al., 2006). The study found that ERP purchase events are not correlated with improved performance, but that go-live events are. This indicates that ERP usage, rather than simply purchase, contributes to improved performance. Further, the study found that both purchase and go-live events for CRM and SCM systems are positively correlated with performance and productivity. This finding implies that firms that experience improved performance from ERP systems make follow-on investments in these additional systems, creating a ‘‘virtuous cycle” of IT investment in enterprise systems. While this third study comes closest to empirically addressing the issue of causality, it is limited in that it only examines enterprise systems. It is difficult to generalize from this study as ERP, SCM, and CRM systems are often viewed as components of enterprise software suites, and are planned for sequential implementation from the outset.

The investigation of causality has since become more direct, with researchers expressing a concern that the rate of return for IT investments in extant work may distorted due to reverse causality (Tambe and Hitt, 2012). One approach to this has been to develop new datasets and employ generalized method-of-moments (GMM) estimators in data analysis. This approach, nevertheless, is primarily a refinement of the traditional production function approach and seeks to eliminate the influence of reverse causality in productivity estimates rather than establish its role and incorporate it into a theoretical model.

In sum, causality has been explored, but equivocal or inconclusive results have been found. In one study, a given year’s performance was associated with only certain types of subsequent IT investment (Weill, 1992). In another study, positive feedback from productivity gains to initial conditions was proposed, but not tested (Lee and Choi, 2003). And while performance and productivity benefits arising from enterprise systems led to follow-on investments in other enterprise modules (Aral et al., 2006), this study contradicts an earlier one that fails to find support for the idea that strategic IT systems such as CRM systems improve productivity (Weill, 1992). Ultimately, foregoing studies fail to conclusively answer the questions of reinvestment and causality. Additional work is needed to clarify causality in theoretical explanations of IT business valu creation.

## 3. Hypothesis development

To address the issue of causality, we consider three possible scenarios: that productivity positively influences subsequent investment in IT, that productivity negatively influences investment in IT, and that the decision to invest in IT is ad hoc and not systematically related to prior productivity. We will argue that the rationale for productivity’s positive influence on subsequent investment is more consistent than the other two rationales, and therefore the positive rationale outweighs the others, if they occur at all.

## 3.1. Positive relationship between productivity and subsequent IT investment

We argue that IT investment is a function of productivity in the previous time period. The outline of our argument is (1) improved productivity in one time period increases a firm’s financial resources, thus enabling greater investment in subsequent periods, (2) firms decide on the level of investment in the various inputs to production based on the observed contribution of those inputs in previous time periods, and (3) firms decide on the level of investment in the various inputs to production based on anticipated (but unobserved) contributions to productivity. We elaborate on these points in the following paragraphs.

First, improved productivity in one time period is an enabler of greater investment in subsequent periods, including investment in IT. When organizational productivity improves, by definition a firm is producing more output per unit(s) of input(s). The result is either that financial resources are conserved because fewer inputs are being purchased or that the additional output can be sold to generate additional financial resources. In either case, after production ceases in that time period, the firm has increased financial resources that can be invested in factors of production for the next time period. When productivity improves as a result of the application of IT factors of production such as IT labor or IT capital, the financial resources available to potentially invest in IT in future time periods increase. No guarantee exists that increased financial resources will indeed be invested in IT, but research has nevertheless shown that firms with financial slack increase IT investments (Dewan et al., 1998; Kobelsky et al., 2008), including firms in the specific context of healthcare (Angst et al., 2010; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981). Thus, increases in financial resources increase the likelihood of investments in IT.

Second, firms must routinely decide on the level of investment in the various inputs to production, and they do so, in part, based on observed productivity in previous time periods. Indeed, every period of time presents a firm with three decisions: (1) whether to exit or continue operation, and if the firm chooses to continue, (2) what factors of production should be invested in, and (3) at what level should the factors of production be invested in (Olley and Pakes, 1996). Economic evaluation of the productive value of prior investments is rational. Observed increases in productivity that arise from investments in IT will be noted by the firm, with investments of a similar type likely to occur in the future (Stuart and Podolny, 1996). Given that IT has been shown to improve profitability (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000; Mithas et al., 2012), and also to improve a host of other non-financial outcomes (Aron et al., 2011; Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Kohli et al., 2012), there is reason to believe that IT will recommend itself during future rounds of investment decision making.

We also note that it has been observed that the costs of IT have consistently declined over time (Schaller, 1997). With costs for IT resources such as processing power, storage space, and bandwidth declining predictably over time, organizations that have observed productivity gains from such investments in the past will observe that similar improvements can be expected in the future – and at a lower cost than in the past (Brynjolffson and McAfee, 2014). The benefits of today’s IT capital will likely be available tomorrow at a lower cost, providing yet another reason for observed productivity gains from IT to be reinvested there.

Third. firms decide on the level of investment in various inputs to production. including IT. based on estimated future contributions to productivity (Olley and Pakes, 1996). There are several reasons why IT can be expected to increase productivity. Most directly. IT provides a way for firms to substitute capital for labor in the execution of their business processes (Brynjolfsson et al., 1994; Dewan and Min, 1997). Realized returns on IT capital in the form of reduced labor costs provide a clear and consistent rationale for investment. Indeed, with regard to the specific question of reinvestment of returns to IT, investments in labor-saving IT have been shown to be positively and significantly associated with the previous year’s performance (Weill, 1992). Thus, the anticipated gains of substituting IT capital for labor are one reason why firms would anticipate productivity gains from subsequent IT investment.

Organizations have also found that the productive value of a primary investment such as IT capital can be increased by making complementary investments. Firms that support their IT investments with complementary investments in areas such as IT personnel, training, and management initiatives earn superior returns, whereas firms that do not make such complementary investments receive lower returns on their IT investments (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 2000).

Additionally, spillovers from the broad application of IT within and across sectors augur for future investment. IT-related spillovers, or ‘‘benefits from the IT investments and IT-related knowledge of its trading partners” (Chang and Gurbaxani, 2012, p. 868) have been broadly observed (Tambe and Hitt, 2012, 2014). As firms become more IT-intensive, they can expect greater future spillover benefits from the IT services industry, benefits that will be sustained over time (Chang and Gurbaxani, 2012). We therefore argue that as firms become more IT-intensive, the synergistic effects of working with value chain partners who themselves possess IT skills and expertise will increase the likelihood of additional future IT investment.

Perhaps most powerfully, we observe that firms that take a real options approach to valuation of their IT assets will be systematically more likely to reinvest returns to IT. Many IT investment decisions are sequential, such as the investment in an ERP system with follow-on investments in additional modules (Aral et al., 2006). In such scenarios, an investment in IT in one time period may create a valuable option for making an additional, related investment in the subsequent time period (Fichman, 2004). Furthermore, when organizations observe that improvements in productivity result from an initial investment in IT, they have an incentive to reinvest those returns to IT and follow-on investments are likely. Firms can choose additional investments that will build upon or complement the capabilities delivered by the earlier IT investment (Aral et al., 2006; Weill, 1992). Thus, productivity gains that arise from IT investment in one time period may enable a firm to exercise its option(s) and realize additional gains in a future time period.

## 3.2. Negative relationship between productivity and subsequent IT investment

Next we consider possible reasons that IT-related improvements in productivity might negatively influence future investment in IT. We will argue that these reasons, if they occur at all, are temporary. For example, it is conceivable that highly productive firms may have developed successful ways to manage IT and thus may not sense a need for high levels of additional IT investment in the short run. A firm with higher IT-derived productivity measures than are standard in that firm’s industry could be tempted to scale back future IT investment. For example, infrastructure that is fully installed and operational can vield quantifiable improvements in productivity over a long time horizon (Weill and Broadbent. 20o0). Thus, a large infrastructural investment at a single point in time may yield enough productive benefit that subsequent investments may not seem necessary in the short term. It has been empirically shown that the productivity benefits of investments in IT capital can continue to manifest themselves for several years (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1998, 2003). Such firms could identify productivity gains from IT investment and opt to reap the return on their investment rather than immediately reinvest those gains.

While such conditions for a negative relationship between productivity and subsequent IT investment might occasionally manifest themselves in an individual firm at a point in time, such conditions do not provide a consistent, long-term motive when compared to the forces that encourage a positive relationship, as presented in the prior sub-section. Moreover, we are unaware of any empirical or conceptual work that suggests possible negative feedback to productivity. Rather, researchers have observed broad and sustained growth in productivity, in IT investment, and in IT stock over the past two decades (McAfee and Brynjolfsson, 2008).

## 3.3. Ad-hoc relationship between productivity and subsequent IT investment

There are several possible scenarios that could lead to ad-hoc investment decisions depending on the particularities of the firm. For instance, a firm’s IT maturity might impact its future IT investment (Karimi et al., 1996). Firms’ IT investments are determined by how mature, prepared, or equipped they are to assimilate and utilize a particular type of IT. Firms that are planning to increase IT investment ‘‘tend to have achieved a higher degree of IT integration, organization, and control” (Karimi et al., 1996, p. 73). Thus, IT investment decisions may be driven by the firm’s evaluation of its own maturity, and not by economic analysis of a technology and its benefits. Alternatively, IT adoption has been observed to occur in fads, where significant bandwagon effects are observed (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004). Very simply, IT investment decisions may at times be driven more by what is taking place among competitors and peer firms than by an analysis of the realized benefits of already-implemented IT, or by an organization’s ability to derive value from IT. Finally, IT investment may occasionally be influenced by government regulations or strategic initiatives that are unique to a given firm. The United States Sarbanes-Oxley legislation and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) have necessitated IT investment quite apart from previous productivity gains. Also, within a particular firm, IT may be the highest investment priority in one period, while another strategic need may take precedence in another period.

In sum, IT investment decisions may not always be based exclusively on evaluations of prior productivity, but may instead be based on an assessment of the firm’s IT maturity, or on a variety of unpredictable and idiosyncratic factors such as bandwagon phenomena, government decrees, or firm-specific strategic considerations. We observe, however, that the conditions for these ad hoc investment decisions are neither persistent nor systematic. Furthermore, the existence of such conditions does not in any way invalidate the fact that rational economic decision-making at firms indicates that the productive influence of IT investments will be evaluated when considering future investments. Thus, when compared to the negative or ad hoc relationship, we find the logic of a positive feedback loop more compelling. The forces that favor the positive relationship remain consistent, while the factors leading to the negative or ad hoc relationships are more idiosyncratic and temporary. For these reasons, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1. Productivity in a given time period is positively associated with the level of IT investment in the subsequent time period.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Data source

The context for our study is the healthcare industry. Data were collected from the HIMSS Analytics Database<sup>1</sup> for the years 1998 through 2004. We have chosen the years 1998–2004 because this time period was free from major macroeconomic shocks that could lead to idiosyncratic results. Our unit of analysis is the healthcare system. The annual number of healthcare systems included in the database ranges from 1467 to 1388, with an average value of 1434. Of these firms, 1236 (86.2%) provided information on all measured variables in two or more consecutive years and were included in our sample. With each of the 1236 firms reporting data in multiple years, our panel contains a total of 4036 firm-year observations<sup>2</sup>.

## 4.2. Measurement of variables

The primary independent variable in our study is productivity (Q). There are different measures of a firm’s performance and productivity in IS and management literature, including Tobin’s q, Return on Investment (ROI), Return on Assets (ROA), Return on Equity (ROE), Return on Sales (ROS), labor productivity, inventory turnover, profit margin, asset utilization, collection efficiency, and value added (Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996; Kohli and Devaraj, 2003; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj, 2015). Revenue-based measures of productivity (rather than profit-based measures) are more appropriate in healthcare contexts because most healthcare systems operate on a nonprofit basis (Devarai and Kohli. 2000. 2003: Menachemi et al., 2006: Menon et al., 2000). Following precedent, we will construct our proxy for productivity using net revenue.

Here, we note the importance of controlling for the effect of organizational size when selecting proxies for this and other variables (Kimberly and Evanisco. 1981). Without controlling for size the effect of this potentially confounding variable cannot be ruled out. Because size has been recognized as being potentially influential in similar studies, sizeindependent measures, such as revenue per day or revenue per admission (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000, 2003) as well as net inpatient revenue per bed per day and net patient revenue per bed per day (Menachemi et al., 2006), have been used. On the basis of these precedents, several measures of size may be justifiably chosen as a divisor for revenue, including the number of physicians, beds, or admissions (Kimberly and Evanisco, 1981; Post and Kagan, 1998; Zuckerman et al., 1994). The result of dividing revenue by some measure of size would be to create a size-controlled proxy for productivity. We have chosen to use the number of beds to control for the potential effects of organizational size. While patient admissions patient discharges, or patient days would control for size in acute-care facilities, we study healthcare systems, which also include system components such as sub-acute care facilities, ambulatory care facilities, physician offices and clinics, home health businesses, and hospice businesses. While acute-care centers are certainly important, standalone acute-care centers are becoming increasingly rare, with integrated healthcare systems offering a wide variety of acute and sub-acute care becoming the norm. We desire our results to be broadly applicable to firms across the healthcare industry, not just within a subset of acute-care institutions. Inpatient care, which patient admissions, patient discharges, and patient days measure, is thus only one part of what we are measuring. Our choice of revenue per bed thus includes not only acute-care centers, but also many of the various forms of sub-acute care that nevertheless consider beds to be one of their primary revenue centers and measures of size. Healthcare systems that generate more revenue per bed will be understood to be more productive than those that generate less revenue per bed<sup>3</sup>.

While IT investment has been infrequently examined in studies of IT business value, we include it here as our dependent variable in order to examine the direction of causality in IT business value creation. Our proxy for IT Investment (IT I) is constructed from the amount of the healthcare system’s total operating budget devoted to IT. Operating budget has been used as a dependent variable in numerous other studies of IT business value (Harris and Katz, 1991; Mahmood and Mann, 1993; Ray et al., 2005; Salge et al., 2015). Our proxy for IT I is the dollar amount of the total operating budget devoted to IT, divided by the total number of beds in the healthcare system. Thus, we will be examining whether productivity gains lead to an increase in the IT expense dedicated to each bed, a measure of IT investment intensity, following a recent precedent in research studying IT investment decisions (Salge et al., 2015)<sup>4</sup>.

Several control variables have been included as well. The type of services offered by the healthcare system may play a role in determining the way a healthcare firm chooses to reinvest its productivity gains. The type of services offered may also influence the amount of revenue the healthcare system generates, our primary independent variable. For instance, an acute care facility could generate substantially more revenue per bed than a walk-in clinic or hospice facility. For this reason, we have included the number of services offered (SVCS) as a control variable (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000; Salge et al., 2015). We have included the percentage of revenue from Medicaid (MDCD), and the percentage of revenue from Medicare (MDCR). Healthcare systems that primarily serve the elderly, indigent, uninsured, or underinsured populations that benefit from Medicare and Medicaid may earn relatively less in revenue than healthcare systems that do not serve these populations. We have also included the age of the healthcare system (AGE) as a control variable as well. These control variables: SVCS, MDCD, MDCR, and AGE, are often included as controls in studies of healthcare organizations (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000, 2003; Menachemi et al., 2006; Salge et al., 2015). Each of these control variables is size-independent, as are our dependent and independent variables listed earlier. Finally, we have included the number of facilities in each healthcare system (FCLT) as an additional control variable to check for the effect of organizational size on our results. This variable is a count of the number of locations within a given healthcare system where medical treatments and related services are delivered. Each of our variables, the definition of its proxy, and its basis in literature is summarized in Table 1. Alternate proxies that will be introduced in the upcoming Robustness Checks subsection are included in Table 1 as well.

Both random-effects (RE) and fixed-effects (FE) specifications are tested. RE models estimate explanatory variables under the assumption that any unobserved effects have a zero mean, are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables in all time periods, and thus do not influence the estimates. If this assumption does not hold, FE models, which produce unbiased and consistent estimates even when unobserved effects are present, are preferred. It is common to estimate equations with both RE and FE, and then formally test for statistically significant differences in the coefficients (Wooldridge, 2006). If the Hausman test indicates a rejection of the null hypothesis of a zero mean for unobserved effects, FE results are preferred (Hausman. 1978). We will therefore present the results of the Hausman test to check the assumptions of the RE model. and then will present the set of results, either FE or RE, that are supported by this test. Statistical tests and model estimation are performed with the STATA software package.

## 5. Results

We find support for our hypothesis that productivity causes subsequent investment in IT. As Table 4 shows, $Q _ { t } .$ is positively and significantly related to $I T - I _ { t } .$ This result provides support for the primary argument of this paper, namely that increases in productivity (Q) in a given time period lead to increases in IT investment (IT I) in future time periods.

In Table 4, the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value given by the Hausman test indicates that results from a fixed-effects (FE) model are preferred to those from a random-effects (RE) model. Again, results show significant effects of $Q _ { t } .$ on IT $I _ { t \cdot }$ Thus, our hypothesis is supported.

## 5.1. Specification checks

Our primary analysis, which is described in Eq. (1) with results shown in Table 4, indicates that $L o g ( Q ) _ { t - 1 }$ is a statistically significant predictor of IT Investment, $L o g ( I T - I ) _ { t }$ . This approach and this result are consistent with prior research on the business value of IT in healthcare contexts, where time lags of one year or less are used (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000; Salge et al., 2015). Nevertheless, to verify the specification of our model, we have tested our model with additional time lags for Productivity (Q). In multiple alternate models with up to 5 years of time lags for Productivity, empirical evidence does not exist to support the inclusion of $L o g ( Q ) _ { t - 2 } , L o g ( Q ) _ { t - 3 } , L o g ( Q ) _ { t - 4 } ,$ or Log(Q) as predictors of IT Investment, Log $( I T - I ) _ { t } .$ Thus, based on both precedent and empirical results, we believe that we have specified the appropriate lag structure in Eq. (1).

We also perform a Granger causality test to check the specification of our model and clearly identify the direction of causality. The Granger causality test is an econometric procedure for identifying causal direction (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Granger, 1969; Greene, 2003; Marvell and Moody, 1996). We perform this test because regressions with panel data can often return similar results when the independent and dependent variables are reversed (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003). Questions have been raised within the IS research community about whether productive firms invest more in IT (our assertion in H1) or whether firms that invest more in IT are more likely to see productivity gains. We therefore include a specific test for directionality in our hypothesized relationship.

When conducting Granger causality tests, we can find no specification, nor combination of time lags, where IT Investment is a statistically significant predictor of productivity. Post-estimation tests further reveal that no combination of lags of IT Investment is jointly significant in predicting productivity. This result that IT Investment does not predict Productivity need not be interpreted as a revival of the ‘‘Productivity Paradox”. Instead, we interpret this as evidence of important intermediate variables between IT Investment and Productivity – as has been noted elsewhere (Kohli and Grover, 2008). We return to this issue of intermediate variables in the Discussion section of the paper. In summary, we see the specification checks of various lag structures and the Granger test for causal direction as providing additional support for the specification we have described in the paper’s Eq. (1).

## 5.2. Robustness checks

We have also performed a series of robustness checks to examine whether our results are idiosyncratic and based only on the particular empirical specification and proxies that we have chosen, or if instead they are robust across a number of possible alternate specifications.

We begin by checking to see if an alternate proxy for Productivity yields the same results as the one used for our main analysis. In our main analysis (shown in Table 4, above) the proxy for Productivity (Q) is revenue per bed. Table 5 indicates that results are very similar when using revenue per physician (designated Q RVBYPH). We consider revenue per physician as an alternate proxy for multiple reasons. First, it provides a direct measure of labor productivity, complementing the supply-side single-factor productivity measure (revenue per bed) in our main analysis. Second, healthcare systems generate revenue by treating patients, performing medical procedures, and prescribing medicines. The presence of physicians is essential for these revenue-generating functions to occur. It is because physicians are at the center of the revenuegeneration process in a healthcare system that we use revenue per physician. Labor productivity measures similar to this one have been used in several other IT business value studies (Aral et al, 2006: Black and Lynch. 2001: Hitt et al., 2002 Mukhopadhyay et al., 1997)<sup>5</sup>.

One important insight from this robustness check is that regardless of the proxy chosen, whether revenue per bed (as in Table 4) or revenue per physician (as in Table 5), results are the same. Productivity at time t-1 is positively and significantly related to IT Investment at time t.

## 6. Discussion

We have presented robust empirical support for the idea that productivity in a given time period influences the level of IT Investment in the subsequent time period. As we discuss these results, we will also describe the implications of the finding that productivity Granger-causes IT investment; while IT investment does not Granger-cause productivity

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

As we have noted, the primary empirical finding of this paper is that productivity leads to subsequent IT investment. By presenting this empirical evidence, and by performing tests to demonstrate the direction of causality in this relationship between productivity and IT investment, we go beyond earlier research in several key ways. We go beyond research that proposes positive feedback in the creation of IT business value but does not test for it (Lee and Choi, 2003), beyond research that tests for but fails to find support for positive feedback (Weill, 1992), beyond research that examines feedback limited to enterprise systems (Aral et al., 2006), and beyond research that guards against reverse causality but fails to incorporate it into a theoretical model (Tambe and Hitt, 2012). We test for positive feedback and find support for it, building on earlier work in IT business value that emphasizes the importance of investigating time lags in data (Devaraj and Kohli, 2000) and responding to a stated need to directly examine reverse causality (Tambe and Hitt, 2012). This leads us to consider what variables might be included in a dynamic model of IT business value and how those variables might be linked.

We have failed to find support for the idea that IT investment increases productivity. Rather than reviving the productivity paradox, we argue that this result indicates the presence of variables that mediate the relationship between investment and productivity. In research that formed part of the foundation for the productivity paradox, a direct link from IT investment to organizational performance was proposed and tested, but support for this link was not found (Weill, 1992). This should not be surprising in hindsight, for investment itself does not impact productivity or performance. Instead of IT investment directly affecting productivity, IT investment affects performance and productivity through the IT inputs that an organization can obtain. In fact, researchers have explained that IT investment allows firms to acquire IT inputs; that IT inputs bring about IT impacts; and finally that IT impacts alter the firm’s productivity (Soh and Markus, 1995). IT investment may therefore be properly understood to be the vehicle that allows IT inputs such as IT capital and IT labor to be acquired. This aligns with research that highlights the need to identify important intermediate variables between IT investment and productivity (Kohli and Grover, 2008). Indeed, research in economics explains that a firm’s capital stock is a function not only of previously accumulated capital, but also, as we have stated here, of investment (Hulten, 1992; Jorgenson, 1966; Olley and Pakes, 1996; Solow, 1962).

We summarize our positive feedback model of IT business value visually as Fig. 1. Our model explains that IT investment in a given time period builds the stock of IT inputs, those IT inputs then impact productivity, and finally that increases in productivity lead to IT investment in a future time period, beginning the cycle anew. The primary focus of this paper has been the investigation of the relationship indicated by the black arrow, with support for the gray arrows extant in IS and economics literature (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996; Dewan and Min, 1997; Menon et al., 2000; Bresnahan et al., 2002; Mithas et al., 2012; Schryen, 2013)<sup>8</sup>.

We now summarize our positive feedback model of IT business value mathematically in a series of equations. The initial variables that we will use in our model allow us to explicate the conversion of IT investment to IT inputs. Separating IT investment from the use of IT inputs has been highlighted as a way to isolate the impact of specific types of technology on productivity and performance (Devaraj and Kohli, 2003). Modeling IT inputs as distinct from IT investment also clearly identifies intermediate variables in the process of IT business value creation. In addition to the theoretical advantage of identifying each variable in the model in order to clearly specify the relationship between IT and productivity, it has been stated that an ‘‘ideal” empirical solution to the question of causality would separate IT investment from the use of IT inputs (Aral et al., 2006, p. 1820). Thus, for both conceptual and empirical reasons, our model considers IT investment as distinct from IT inputs such as IT labor and IT capital. Note also that a firm’s stock of inputs at a given point in time is a function not simply of investment in the current time period, but also of previously accumulated inputs (Hulten, 1992; Jorgenson, 1966; Menon et al., 2000; Olley and Pakes, 1996; Solow, 1962).

$$
I T - K _ {t} = f (I T - I _ {t}, I T - K _ {t - 1})\tag{4}
$$

$$
I T - L _ {t} = f (I T - I _ {t}, I T - L _ {t - 1})\tag{5}
$$

Traditional production functions examine the transformation of inputs into outputs. In IT business value research, it is common to disaggregate capital into IT capital and non-IT capital, with the same done for IT labor and non-IT labor (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996; Dewan and Min, 1997; Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996; Menon et al., 2000; Schryen, 2013). Expenditures on technological inputs such as computer hardware, a type of IT capital, increase firms’ abilities to store, analyze, and manage data. As these activities become more efficient and effective, productivity improves (Brynjolffson and Yang, 1996; Mahmood et al., 1999; Sircar et al., 1998). Similarly, investments in IT labor allow firms to hire additional personnel or add to the skill sets of existing personnel. The cumulative knowledge and skills of the IT labor input then positively influence the productivity of firms by enabling them to work more effectively and efficiently (Byrd et al., 2004; Tippins and Sohi, 2003). Thus, investments in IT capital and IT labor increase organizations’ ability to compete in the marketplace (Mata et al., 1995). The entire body of post-productivity paradox literature attests to these relationships (e.g., Hitt and Brynjolfsson, 1996; Dewan and Min, 1997; Menon et al., 2000; Bresnahan et al., 2002; Devaraj and Kohli, 2003; Aral et al., 2006; Mithas et al., 2012; Schryen, 2013; Sabherwal and Jeyaraj, 2015). Mathematically,

$$
Q _ {t} = f (K _ {t}, L _ {t}, I T - K _ {t}, I T - L _ {t})\tag{6}
$$

Finally, while productivity is in some sense a final output of the production process, we have provided evidence in this paper that it is also important to understand productivity as a determinant of IT investment in the next time period. Why does this key relationship hold? Improved productivity in a given time period increases financial resources and makes IT investment in future time periods possible. Research has shown that when financial slack exists, future IT investment becomes more likely (Angst et al., 2010; Dewan et al., 1998; Kimberly and Evanisko, 1981; Kobelsky et al., 2008). Once this financial slack exists, a firm must decide on the levels at which to invest in each of the various factors of production. Firms do so based on expectations about productivity (Olley and Pakes, 1996). We argued in the Hypothesis Development section that these expectations are based on both previously observed levels of productivity as well as on the investments’ anticipated productive potential. For these reasons, the final portion of the positive feedback model of IT business value can be stated mathematically as

$$
I T - I _ {t + 1} = f (Q _ {t}).\tag{7}
$$

We see additional empirical support for this relationship in our tests for changes in IT investment over time (Table 9) There, we identified that highly-productive healthcare systems are investing more in IT than are less-productive healthcare systems. This is a trend that exists across our 7-year panel. We interpret this as evidence for positive feedback, specifically that productive firms are reinvesting their returns in IT, creating a virtuous cycle of IT investment. Researchers have noted variation in the adoption of healthcare IT (Bardhan and Thouin, 2013); we observe that one potential explanation for this variation is that productive firms have financial resources that they can reinvest in IT. In summary, Eqs. (4)–(7) provide a mathematical explanation of our positive feedback model of IT business value.

## 6.2. Methodological implications

The inclusion of IT investment as distinct from IT capital and IT labor has allowed us to test for positive feedback from productivity in one time period to IT investment in the next. It has also allowed us to examine the direction of causality, a concern that has been noted by researchers (Tambe and Hitt. 2012). Indeed, as we noted earlier, it has been stated that an ‘‘ideal” empirical solution to the question of causality would separate IT investment from the use of IT inputs (Aral et al., 2006, p. 1820). The use of IT investment in studies of IT business value thus allows researchers to clearly explain this dynamic process where productivity in one time period impacts IT investment in the next time period which then dictates the level of IT inputs to production, beginning the cycle anew. The measurement of IT investment in future studies of IT business value will allow for more detailed examinations of the process of IT business value creation.

## 6.3. Managerial implications

The primary managerial implication of this study is that executives and managers should evaluate their IT investments over the long run and with advanced approaches. The methods most commonly used for evaluating IT investment are still generally limited to techniques such as net present value (NPV), return on investment (ROI), return on equity (ROE), and discounted cash flow (DCF). Although such analyses are useful, reliance upon book values and discounting approaches introduces the possibility of distorting values (Brealey et al., 2005; Luehrman, 1997). Rather than simply conducting discounted cash flow analysis to justify IT investments, managers should consider how investments in IT may improve organizational performance, provide gains for reinvestment, and create options for future investment (Fichman, 2004). Similarly the costs associated with IT investments may also need to be calculated by taking into account the positive externalities or ‘‘spillovers” that arise from value chain partners’ and even from competitors’ IT investments (Chang and Gurbaxani, 2012; Tambe and Hitt, 2014).

The application of our advice may require businesses to implement new methods of tracking the costs and benefits of their IT investments. Nevertheless, we encourage the development of such tools and techniques to analyze the value of IT investments over longer time horizons than firms may ordinarily consider, and by taking novel approaches to valuation.

## 6.4. Limitations and future research

The large, comprehensive dataset that we have analyzed for this study is a strength of the study because it allows us to examine firms of varying sizes, and because it is drawn from a period of time free from macroeconomic shocks. Nevertheless, our dataset also presents some limitations. Our analysis would benefit from information on other potential measures of organizational performance such as ROA, ROI, and ROE, which are often seen in studies of IT business value. Similarly, other measures of performance that are unique to the healthcare industry such as patient satisfaction and mortality could provide a more complete picture of how IT creates business value. These measures do not exist in our data. Also, while our proxy for IT investment, the IT operating budget, has a strong precedent in literature (Alpar and Kim, 1990; Bender, 1986; Harris and Katz, 1989, 1991; Mahmood and Mann, 1993; Ray et al., 2005; Salge et al., 2015), additional information on the IT capital budget would be beneficial. Our examination of productivity’s influence on subsequent increases in IT capital budget (see Table 10) reveals support for our hypothesis, but is based only on four years of data. A final limitation of the dataset is that a change in database administration took place after the 2004 data were collected, resulting in changes to the number and type of variables measured. Comparisons of data collected before and during this year with data collected afterward are problematic.

Several directions for future work are possible. First, replication of these findings in additional time periods would be ben eficial. Some researchers have speculated that common standards for IT infrastructure in the 2010s (Bardhan et al., 2013), and rapid technological progress in general (Chwelos et al., 2010) may affect the nature of IT as a factor of production.

Second, a more detailed examination of IT business value creation in the healthcare industry could be undertaken. In this industry. the role of IT labor would likely be of great importance, Management of professionals, a class of emplovees that possess advanced training and specialized knowledge, requires that special considerations be made (Gouldner, 1957; Newman and Wallender. 1978: Raelin. 1991). Professionals have narrow areas of specialization and firm ideas about what activities lay inside or outside the scope of their job. In settings where professionals have dominant roles, such as healthcare systems, professional traditions may prevent behavior patterns from changing (Newman and Wallender. 1978). Because of these factors, physicians may be reluctant to embrace new technologies such as physician prescription entry, computerized patient charts, or clinical decision support. Increasing the number of personnel to help train and support healthcare system users may improve the likelihood that users will embrace new technologies.

Third, as we have implied above, the study that we have conducted here, as well as the proposed future research, could be conducted in different industries and contexts. The evidence presented here is based on analysis of the healthcare industry in the United States. Because of this, the boundary conditions of our claims should be evaluated. Future work could thus focus not only on different healthcare contexts, such as locales where government control of healthcare is more pronounced than in the United States, or where ‘‘medical tourism” is common. Future work could also focus on different industries, different levels of analysis, or different national and cultural contexts. The model that we have described should hold in the vast majority of organizational settings. Nevertheless, when firms do not or cannot accurately monitor their productivity, when macroeconomic shocks prevent the reinvestment of productivity gains, or when non-economic forces dictate levels of investment (such as government regulations), the links described in this model may be weakened. This model will be most likely to be observed in stable firms with moderate rates of productivity change in in developed market economies. As one moves farther from such settings, the usefulness of the model may decline. Extremely rapid firm growth, economic instability, labor unrest, centralized economic planning, or open conflict would likely prevent firms from behaving in the manner described herein

Fourth and finally, the influence of an organization’s strategy on IT investment could be investigated as well. Researchers have examined how IT strategy and business strategy can be aligned to improve organizational performance. These studies often investigate how alignment can mediate or moderate the relationship between IT inputs and the firm’s performance (Kohli and Grover, 2008). Strategy could also be examined as a possible variable that may determine the type and level of investment made by firms.

## 7. Conclusion – closing the loop

In this study, we have sought to complement existing research by identifying what variables should be included in a model of IT business value and by explaining how and why those variables are related. The model proposed here improves on static models with unidirectional causality and challenges those that allow for bidirectional causality. Our arguments and evidence allow us to ‘‘close the loop” conceptually and empirically by modeling a positive feedback relationship in the creation of IT business value. To summarize, we have stated that IT investment in a given time period builds the stock of IT inputs, those IT inputs then impact productivity, and that productivity then leads to IT investment in a future time period, completing the loop and beginning the cycle anew.

In the process of developing this conceptual and empirical model, we provide an answer to one of the remaining issues in IT business value research, the question of causality. The relationship between IT investment and productivity is not a unidirectional either/or relationship where either IT investment leads to productivity, or where productivity leads to IT investment. Neither is the relationship simply a bidirectional both/and relationship, where IT investment leads to productivity, and where productivity leads to IT investment. Instead, the explanation is slightly more nuanced, involving key intermediate variables that must be measured, and also multiple time periods that must be examined. Thus, we also seek to ‘‘close the loop” at a higher level by bringing a measure of resolution to IS researchers’ questions about causality. Our endeavor to address this question should benefit the community of researchers and practitioners, as well as stimulate additiona research.
