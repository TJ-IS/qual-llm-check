# Managing e-government implementation in China: A process perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 1 March 2007 Accepted 15 February 2009 Available online 5 March 2009

## A B S T R A C T

Launching a municipality-wide e-government program is an extensive, meticulously planned initiative involving ambitious goals and large-scale operational transformation. This has been particularly true in Shanghai, an urban area, occupying approximately the same area as New York or London. Here we report on our study of a social security smart card project developed and now operating in Shanghai. From the perspectives of modularity and societal learning, this case study demonstrates how localized egovernment efforts may be implemented throughout the tiers of the municipal government, to presen an e-government model that can be diffused to other parts of the country.

## 1. Introduction

E-government involves the use of IT by governmental organizations to improve their operational efficiency and effectiveness in meeting citizen needs. Municipal governments are of particular importance; they interpret policies and guidelines from the central government and their actions influence and involve people, process and environmental contingencies. Local governments face pressure from citizens and the private sector as well as higher government authorities [28]. The value of e-government initiatives, such as government websites, can be assessed with both quantitative and qualitative results [3,16]. Both leadership and IT quality can affect organizational performance at a municipal government [20].

Through an in-depth case study, we examined the process of e-government diffusion in the municipal context. Shanghai has a land area of 6340 km<sup>2</sup> and a population of 18 million people (including a floating population of around 2 million); the municipality consists of 19 districts and one county. It is a thriving metropolis on China’s east coast and has maintained a GDP growth rate of over 10% in the last 11 years. In response to the Chinese central government’s push and in order to enhance local government performance, Shanghai government embarked on a wide range of e-government initiatives, such as a website portal, e-procurement system, and a social security card system.

Shanghai’s success in the latter initiative has been cited by China’s central government as a showpiece of e-government development.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. E-government

The public sector has made enormous progress in implementing e-government since the 1990s. Grounded in observation of practice [15], the effort was been split into four stages: cataloging, transaction, vertical integration, and horizontal integration, while from a review of 1300 government initiatives, Balutis [2] divided it into: information dissemination, providing only forms, end-to-end electronic transactions, and government transformation. Similarly, Baum and Di Maio [4], in the Gartner Group model, split the process into presence, interaction, transaction and transformation. These models highlight the evolutionary nature of e-government, and suggest the need for a tool for examining the growth of egovernment. However, there has only been limited attention paid to the process of e-government adoption and diffusion so far.

With limited skills and capabilities in e-government implementation, developing economies tend to adopt the theories and experiences of their developed counterparts. Yet in terms of capabilities, there are substantial differences between these two economies, in their history and culture, technological infrastructure, and people (citizens, government employees, and technical manpower). Given the sheer size of countries such as China, the central government can hardly engage in a full range of public service delivery, as could smaller countries. Thus some researchers have studied municipal governments that perform functions around the country to develop a picture of e-government [11].

Some e-government systems automate in-house operations, serving the government employees while others automate the service provided by the government, mainly to serve the public. The first type barely differs from a system automating but the second was the focus of our study; it is dramatically different because it serves the public and has a country-wide impact with local differences, both geographic and social. Also, such projects potentially mobilize resources from all over the country.

## 2.2. Modularity, societal learning and large-scale transformation

Digitizing the public sector involves a large-scale transformation involving multiple stakeholders. Modularity, as a means of designing flexible systems and handling complexity, can segment the system into manageable components [21]. Valuable experiences and knowledge can then be generated from implementing the smaller portions. Societal learning is a mechanism that aids the fluid and seamless integration of public and private sectors and people. A coordinated and synthesized application of both modularity and societal learning can help in a large-scale transformation effort, such as e-government.

Modularity represents the degree to which the components of a system can be separated and recombined [24]. A modular organizational or technological system can be agilely reconfigured in response to environmental shifts [22]. Partitioning an egovernment system into loosely coupled sub-systems allows the design and construction of each to be undertaken concurrently in a distributed manner. Likewise, when it comes to project rollout, the government can reduce complexity of the process by reducing the affected population to a manageable size: by using demographics (age or employment status), or location (major city or town).

Societal learning concept stems from the notion of the ‘‘learning capacity of nations’’ [13]: the capability of the entire society to adapt to the fast changing socio-cultural, techno-economic, and political environments, and also developing mutual gains through new partnerships between sections of society. It provides a knowledge integration platform [7] to aid stakeholders in reducing any barriers to implementing e-government.

## 3. Research methods

We adopted the case study method, an appropriate way to examine how and why events were sequenced by directly examining them. We followed the approach of ‘‘soft positivism’’ or ‘‘scientific realism’’ [14,17]. Our analysis was based on a mixture of prior theories and unexpected findings that emerged, allowing us to objectively study the process of e-government implementation from the perspectives of modularity and societal learning, and to subjectively examine the data to determine how a municipal government leveraged modularity and societal learning to develop an e-government model that was subsequently to be replicated across China. The study collected both primary and secondary data from two government agencies, which had worked on Shanghai’s e-government project; they are now known as Shanghai Labor and Social Security Bureau and Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission.

Our fieldwork was conducted over 3 months (June–August 2005). In particular, field visits at Shanghai Labor and Social Security Bureau took about 80 h while those at Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission added to about 120 h. An additional round of field visits and interviews with key informants was conducted, for the purpose of verification and clarification in July 2006.

Retrospective and semi-structured in nature, face-to-face interviews were arranged with people from all ranks of Shanghai’s government and across multiple agencies (see Appendix A). In total, our field studies yielded 36 interviews. Eight out of the 36 interviews were with the Vice Director of Shanghai Informatization Office and former executive mayor. We interviewed the Vice Chair of Shanghai congress and the Vice Director of Shanghai Labor and Social Security Bureau. Interviews with these government officials generated details on strategic planning and decision-making during the project. They further introduced us to six key informants, three from each agency. They shared operational details during sixteen interviews. In addition, we gathered secondary data from reports of both central and municipal governments, detailed project documentation, and online media (e.g., the Shanghai government website).

In order to derive a theory, we analyzed our database of field evidence. As part of this, notes summarizing the emerging themes and issues were taken during each interview. Direct quotes were classified and compiled according to the emerging themes. A further step was the creation of a list of critical events, including the founding of the new government agency, initiation of certain parts of the system, etc. Examining how the Shanghai government managed to transform the way it provided social security services to its 18 million residents identified mechanisms and subprocesses. We verified the data by cross-checking it from multiple sources to create a chain of evidence. The entire process was highly iterative. The iterative process ended when saturation was reached, with no new data further explained the process of egovernment implementation.

## 4. Case description

In view of the central government’s emphasis on e-government, leaders of the Shanghai government decided to formulate a single municipal e-government strategy. Its direct goal was to pre-empt losses from uncoordinated e-government attempts by different agencies. The indirect goal was to strengthen Shanghai’s leading position in China’s transformation.

By then, Shanghai government had already established an online presence (i.e., www.shanghai.gov.cn). In addition, various government agencies in Shanghai had also adopted specialized stand-alone information systems to computerize their back-office processes. However, in terms of sophistication, most of these egovernment projects were in their embryonic stage. Shanghai government aimed to move its e-government applications from static information presentation to dynamic interactive services. A few government agencies mooted the idea of adopting smart card technology. In 1998 alone, eight agencies submitted individual proposals to Shanghai government about adopting smart cards while many others were about to. Shanghai government decided that a systematic and collective e-government implementation was needed, and eventually rejected all the proposals. As such, the project of Shanghai social security card was conceived to address the operational constraints faced by different government agencies, and aligned with the e-government directives from central government at the same time.

Shanghai government decided to develop an all-in-one smart card that could record key personal information (such as name, identification number, fingerprint, medical insurance account number, housing fund account number, etc.) and provide users with access to a wide variety of G2C services. As Table 1 shows, functionalities of the smart card extend beyond social security related services, though it is often known as the Social Security Card.

Shanghai government hoped that computerizing and networking the administration of social security and citizen services would provide a new impetus to the municipality’s economy and strengthen its leading position in China’s transformation. One informant noted that ‘‘Shanghai is the commercial center of China. We hope to take the lead in the country-wide endeavor of digitization. We aim to draw further attention from the central government through showcasing our significant achievement. This will put us in an advantageous position in the competition for resources against other provinces and municipalities.’’ The social security card project has now been in place for over 8 years, with new functionalities developed and implemented over time. The project may be viewed in three stages: initiation, dissemination and popularization.

## 4.1. Stage one: initiation (September 1998 to December 2000)

The initiation stage was from the start of the project till August 2001, when the social security card was activated for a certain segment of the population. Shanghai government played a vital role in the process: establishing and empowering a task force, partnering a chip maker and a research institute to set up the technological infrastructure, building up a regulatory framework, and educating user population to prepare for a smooth transition to the new mode of service delivery.

## 4.1.1. Defining the scope

On 21 September 1998, Shanghai government set a 3-year time frame for the social security card project. Settling on the schedule marked the beginning of the project. The municipal government of Shanghai laid great emphasis on the social security card project. Social security-related benefits make a substantial component of employee remuneration, and concern major aspects of people’s work and life. The project was championed by a government agency—Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission, which reported directly to the vice mayor in charge of municipal development. This arrangement empowered the commission to coordinate efforts by five other government agencies directly involved in the project (Shanghai Labour and Social Security Bureau, Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Public Security, Shanghai Municipal Bureau of Civil Affairs, Shanghai Municipal Medical Insurance Bureau, and Shanghai Municipal Public Health Bureau). During 1999 and 2000, the project had been listed as a key government project, and thereby been funded by Shanghai Science and Technology Development Foundation, besides considerable personal attention from the mayor. The Chinese central government noticed and got interested in Shanghai’s exploration in this egovernment project. China’s Ministry of Labor and Social Security, seeking to further computerize China’s social security system, decided to collaborate with Shanghai government in this area. Approved and announced in September 1999, the collaboration enabled Shanghai government to leverage the intellectual and financial resources of the higher-level government authority. An informant noted that ‘‘Engaging and sustaining government attention is crucial for a mega-project like this one. . .Government support in the forms of financial resources and institutional endorsement makes the project move forward.’’ With support from both municipal and central governments, the project team started to streamline back-office processes and explore feasible technological solutions.

## 4.1.2. Back-office processes

The social security card project was designed to cover a variety of G2C service offerings, with direct impact on at least five government agencies in the initial stage. The project champion (i.e., Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission) drove project progression through regular meetings, where representatives from different agencies discussed their operational processes and problems, and project team proposed solutions and made action plans. Rounds of brainstorming were followed by review sessions, where the mayor and vice mayor, as project sponsors, made decisions to forge consensus among various agencies which would amend their back-office processes according to the agreedupon functions of the social security card.

Agreements on a series of key issues, including the overall system design, plan of an administrative institution, design of the regulatory framework, and channels of information collection, were reached through joint conferences among government agencies. Then the project team translated these key agreements into actable directives, and thereby ensured access to the resources and support of agencies at different levels within Shanghai municipal government. The authority delegated by Shanghai municipal government to the project team enabled it to reduce resistance to back-office process re-engineering and break down barriers between government agencies, making possible an integrated e-government system across agencies.

## 4.1.3. Technological framework

The task force relied heavily on local researchers and practitioners to formulate a technological solution for the social security card system. With a number of leading manufacturers of semiconductor IC components converging on Shanghai, the municipality had been established as China’s first IC design and manufacturing base. With the strong backing of the municipal government, the task force successfully engaged two partners in Shanghai: Shanghai Hua Hong (Group) Co., Ltd, which had undertaken the 909 Project (to enhance IC technology and establish a semiconductor industry in China; the largest project in China’s electronics industry with a national budget of approximately US\$1.2 billion), and Fudan University, one of China’s major universities. In particular, the Vice Director of Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission mentioned that ‘‘in order to mobilize resources and motivate partners from the private sector, our project team introduced and experimented new business concepts and models, such as the build-operate-transfer model.’

After intensive analysis of Shanghai’s existing technological situations and project needs, a few alternative designs were devised and proposed to the municipal government. Finally, one of the solutions was selected—to build an integrated central system through seamlessly integrating major citizen service sub-systems (Fig. 1). The social security card system, through a connected central database and the Internet, would enable citizens to use smart cards as their identification, conduct enquiries, and make payment at government service counters. Service transactions involving multiple agencies could be completed at any networked agency counters. For example, when users update their personal particulars at the counter of one government agency, their new information would be captured by the central database and accessed by other agencies. The enhanced data accuracy would lead to a better-informed government, which is the basis for decision-making effectiveness and efficiency.

## 4.1.4. Institutional framework

Given the scope and complexity of the fledging social security card project, there emerged the need for an administrative institution dedicated to managing the system. In response, Shanghai Social Security Card Service Center was founded in early 1999. When the project scope was expanded to cover a citizen service information system in November 2000, the organization was renamed Shanghai Social Security and Citizen Service Information Center.

## 4.2. Stage two: dissemination (January 2001 to December 2001)

In the preceding stage, the project team had engaged multiple government agencies and partnered private-sector experts to set up the technological, organizational and procedural architectures for the social security card system. With an overall project plan in place, the project team set out to transform the social security administration through project implementation in January 2001.

Adopting a centralized approach, the project team first set out to found an exclusive administrative institution and establish the legal and regulatory framework to guide project implementation. In March 2001, Shanghai Municipal Informatization Commission, the project champion and owner, created a subordinate organization – the Administrative Office of Shanghai Social Security and Citizen Service Information System – to oversee project implementation. After 5 months of preparation since its foundation, this organization crafted two policies which laid down a set of guiding principles and practices regarding the social security card: The ‘‘Administrative Method for Shanghai Social Security Card’’ spelled out the roles and responsibilities of government and citizens in the usage of social security card. The ‘‘Administrative Method for Shanghai Social Security and Citizen Service Information System’’ detailed the roles and responsibilities of government agencies in charge of social security, civil administration, public security, housing fund and medical insurance, in addition to the emphasis on electronic security.

Endorsed and announced by Shanghai government, both policies took effect from August 2001. Throughout project implementation, the municipal government provided high-level guidance to resolve any dispute. Furthermore, based on the policies, broad-based training was conducted among government agencies, hospitals and citizens. The project team communicated with government staff mainly through internal documents and newsletters circulated in a top-down manner, which trickled down from the municipal government to its 19 county-level government divisions (18 districts and one county), which, in turn, spread information to its 220 township-level government divisions and their 5430 village-level government divisions (i.e., neighborhood and village committees). The project team spared no efforts in reaching the public through a variety of channels. One informant recalled that ‘‘we tried to introduce the new system to the public through TV, newspapers, banners at main streets and shopping centers, information booths at community centers, and an around the-clock hotline. We tried to reach different user groups, and gain their trust and buy-in for the smart card system.’’

The municipality-wide implementation of e-government represented a large-scale endeavor with high-impact potential in Shanghai. One informant remarked that ‘‘Making every step manageable is critical to the success of our project in Shanghai, one of the most populated urban areas in the world’s most populated country.’’ Considering the scope and impact of implementation, the project team opted for an incremental approach: social security cards would be first launched in nine districts from the central urban area (Fig. 2), and subsequently be expanded to peripheral areas (i.e., the other 10 districts and one county). To ensure a smooth rollout of this large-scale initiative, the project team decided not to activate the medicare function of the social security card simultaneously even within the nine central districts. In addition, the project rollout was staggered among different user groups (i.e., retirees, Shanghai employees and non-Shanghai employees) to allow the project team adequate control and a period long enough for effective trial-and-error learning.

By the end of 2001, smart cards had been issued to majority of the target population. This marked the large-scale use of the revamped social security system. One informant noted: ‘‘we has launched hundreds of major promotional activities since kickoff of the e-government project.’’

Fully aware of the importance that Shanghai government accorded the project, Shanghai Hua Hong – the technology provider – committed superior resources to project implementation. A technical support team of experienced engineers and technicians was formed. The team was organized into groups with specialized expertise, including smart card technology, equipment maintenance, and system application. When about 4000 machines were installed at 108 hospitals in Huangpu and Luwan districts for system testing, team members took turns to be stationed at the hospitals. The team worked around the clock to tackle system problems, circuit errors and encryption system issues. Shanghai Hua Hong tested the smart card design at an unprecedented scale, with its engineers devoting hours in the tedious and repetitive process of problem identification and solution searching. It also comprehensively documented all the knowledge gained over the process for future rollout of social security card and its own product development.

During the activation of medicare function of social security card, the administrative office closely monitored project progression. Strategic importance was accorded to the problems escalated or emerging from lower-level government divisions over implementation, detected or encountered by Shanghai Hua Hong, or gathered from the hotline operation. To make sure the problems were solved properly and efficiently, the administrative office held joint meetings with relevant agencies and privatesector experts to adjust government back-office processes, modify regulatory guidelines, and fine-tune the technological architecture. Both problems and solutions were carefully documented.

## 4.3. Stage three: popularization (January 2003 onwards)

Given that social security cards had been well received among users, magnetic card, which used to be the means of medicare account management, was completely abolished in Shanghai in March 2002. Social security card started to transform how people in Shanghai received their social benefits. Before March 2002, Shanghai residents as well as people from other parts of China had to produce personal identification along with many other documents for social security-related transactions, such as claiming medical expenses, paying home loans, applying for unemployment subsidy, etc. Shanghai residents became the first group of people who could use a single card to receive a variety of social security-related services. Apart from medicare, a series of new functionalities were on the way, such as employment- and unemployment-related benefits.

By June 2005, Shanghai Social Security Card Service Center had issued over 9.3 million cards to around 70% of the eligible user population. There are four types of social security cards as shown in Table 2. The central database of the social security system now hosts key personal information of 20 million citizens in Shanghai. A wide range of citizen services is provided through a network of 2895 service centers all over Shanghai.

The Administrative Office of Shanghai Social Security and Citizen Service Information System, together with a few major banks and technology providers, are studying the feasibility of linking up the citizen database to banking systems to enable online payment for G2C services. Shanghai’s success story in digitizing social security administration has spread country-wide as an egovernment model. Cultivating an e-government culture among citizens, however, is not a one-off task. A few neighboring provinces entered into collaborative agreements with Shanghai government, to capitalize on the knowledge and experiences accumulated during its own e-government implementation. The project team’s private-sector partner, Shanghai Hua Hong, gained a foothold in government smart card technologies, and further won a few new contracts (such as the Shanghai public transportation IC project). E-government implementation in Shanghai was also a process of knowledge creation: a few patents were filed, including a chip technology, POS machine, a collection of 7400 rare Chinese characters for the smart card database, etc. Additionally, success of the e-government project was of strategic importance to Shanghai. Proactive participation and significant advancement in the country’s key initiatives would warrant Shanghai’s privilege over other cities or provinces in resource allocation by the central government in the future.

## 5. Discussion and findings

Based on the case of Shanghai’s social security card project, we developed a process model (Fig. 3) of e-government implementation from the municipal perspective. The model consists of three processes: seeding the vision in the initiation stage, learning through modularization in the stage of municipal actualization, and scaling up through rapid replication in the popularization stage. Modularization, as the segmentation mechanism, and societal learning, as the integration mechanism, play key roles throughout the development of both municipal and national egovernment models.

## 5.1. Finding one: initiation—seeding the vision

The central government of China has been capitalizing on IT to enhance service quality and provision in the public sector by embracing e-government. We found that given the way the central government and municipal governments are set up in China, country-wide e-government campaigns are largely driven by top-down directives from the central government. The directives give generic e-government directions—general statements of e-government goals and principles. Municipal governments vary in their local situations and needs. A municipal government has to customize strategies and working methods to anchor e-government initiatives to local reality. Therefore it needs to formulate a municipal e-government strategy, which complies with higher-level government directives and addresses local interests. This is done through localization, by tying the municipal-level reality (i.e., grassroots initiatives, operational constraints, etc.) with national e-government agendas [9]. Priority and importance are accorded to transactions for which there are maximum potential of user benefits and efficiency savings. Aiming to boost government productivity, Shanghai’s social security card project arose from a mixture of opportunities, new vision, top-down directives from the central government, and operational constraints within the municipal government. In order to move beyond websites providing static information to dynamic integrated service interactions, the municipal government of Shanghai embarked on the implementation of egovernment, which offers a tremendous opportunity to organize services and agencies as service providers.

In executing the localized strategy, three pillars of e-government—process, technology and people [5], need to be considered, in order to formulate a viable and feasible municipal e-government design as a roadmap for subsequent actualization. The design aims to effectively harness IT to address local problems and realize opportunities in the public sector, and it calls for new partnerships both between governments (i.e., G2G partnership) and between public and private sectors [1].

One of the features of successful e-government attempts, as reported by the Organization of Economic Cooperation and

Development (OECD) in 2005, is the development of a single egovernment ‘‘brand’’ and a consistent way to deliver G2C services based on the integrated efforts of different agencies in a government. Therefore, inter-agency collaboration is fundamental to benefiting from synergized service delivery across agency boundaries. Through within-government partnership, the municipal e-government design aims to synergize the values and visions of different agencies, and engage their joint efforts and commitment.

In the case study, partnership of the municipal government with the public and private sectors played a vital role in carrying out the rudimentary strategy at the municipal level and creating benefits for both citizens and businesses. Both vertical government–government partnership and government–business partnership were critical to materializing the e-government strategy into a feasible design for execution. Here vertical government– government (G2G) partnership refers to the relationship of Shanghai municipal government with the central government, in particular, the agency in charge of China’s social security system. Through the vertical government–government partnership, the municipal government gained the advantages of expertise and financial support from the central government, rather than being restricted to local resources. Under the guidance of the highest authority in charge of the social security system, Shanghai government redesigned its back-office processes and incorporated new procedures.

Through government–business (G2B) partnership, a municipal government may engage local private-sector partners to tap into their specialized technological resources. Private-sector experts are from industry and academia. The mutual accountability between government and businesses (as technology provider) is widely recognized as critical for e-government success. Government and researchers base their partnership on a shared interest in serving today’s needs and building possibilities for tomorrow through innovation [23]. A technological solution is devised to realize e-government conceptions, through innovation based on the joint efforts of practitioners and researchers. In the case of Shanghai’s e-government project, the synergistic efforts and partnership of public and private sectors allowed the engagement of the best technological resources (i.e., research and engineering expertise) from a nation-famous university and a leading player in the country’s booming integrated circuit industry.

## 5.2. Finding two: municipal actualization—learning through modularization

Devising a sound e-government strategy and design is important, but operationalizing the strategy also demands great attention. Municipal actualization is an intermediate phase, institutionalizing localized e-government designs and learning through modular implementation.

In countries such as China, where a centralized e-government strategy is adopted, the need to conform to the municipal egovernment design is reinforced by institutionalization efforts [25]. New organizational, procedural and technological standards are solidified into regulative guidelines, which are issued and reinforced in a top-down manner in the form of government— issued policies and procedures. Verbal mandate is not sufficient for a grand project with direct impact on 18 million people, as is the case of Shanghai. In cross-agency projects, it is necessary for policies and guidelines to spell out new roles and responsibilities of agencies. These policies and guidelines must also be further reinforced through the establishment of new government agencies (i.e., the Shanghai Social Security and Citizen Service Information Center in the case study). Moreover, a government nurtures new cultural and normative beliefs among user population in order to drive technology-enabled transformation. Managerial innovation orientation [18] is fostered within government agencies. Successful rollout of e-government project also requires awareness, mindset change and new skills of the users, even when the change is initiated from the top. Through broad-based training via a variety of media and channels, Shanghai government instills new norms and beliefs regarding the e-government endeavor into the user population, in order to achieve high e-government take-up. The normative and cultural elements of the institution intertwine with the codified regulative rules to fit organizational operations to the dynamic market conditions and achieve performance goals.

Modularity emerges as a means of managing complexity, especially in a dynamic environment with limited resources. By limiting the extent of change and incrementally expanding the scale, modular implementation enables the municipal government to cope with the overwhelming complexity intrinsic in large-scale changes in a stepped manner [10]. In the case study, Shanghai government reduced complexity by narrowing down project coverage to a manageable scope through the adoption of modularity. Internal and external resources were then focused on a controllable niche, enabling the municipal government to develop insights into the specific area and capture the attention of major sponsor groups. Shanghai’s G2C e-government rollout started with retirees from the urban central districts, and incrementally extended in coverage to the entire user population in the municipality. Step by step, implementation in each user group of a certain part of the municipality represented a shortterm win that accumulated and built confidence for future success. Likewise, based on the success of medicare rollout, other social security functionalities were further incorporated. By concentrating on small wins instead of big ones, the municipal government could progress more steadily with the e-government venture, changing course if necessary, without going off track. Moreover, modular components or sub-systems reduce complexity and coordination costs associated with experimentation, leading to more cumulative learning [19]. Implementation of the smart card system in medicare as one component of social security system changed how the overall system of social security was managed; however, it hardly made other social security components (such as pension, housing fund, etc.) to adapt to constraints imposed by medicare actions. Likewise, implementation in one population segment (such as retirees) barely affected how other people received their medicare benefits. Due to the heterogeneity among components of the social security system, among population segments, and among municipal governments, knowledge and experiences gained in one e-government project may not be perfectly replicable across situations. However, enhanced transparency and reduced complexity through modular designs will maximize imitability of the system. Although high level of imitability enabled by modular design often jeopardizes sustained competitive advantage in the market, the case of Shanghai’s e-government is different, as relationships among municipal governments cannot be accounted for in purely market terms. The imitability was what the partnership of Shanghai government and the central government aimed to achieve—an e-government model that was transferable to other municipalities and provinces. Shanghai government agreed to guide a few neighbouring provinces through their e-government development by adapting the Shanghai version of e-government to local realities.

With the knowledge snowballing over the stepped rollout of the e-government system, modular implementation is of utter importance to municipal societal learning—the large-scale learning at the municipal level which involves the collaboration and interaction among government, businesses and citizens [29]. Thus, privatesector partners from both academia and industry further their technological knowledge and sharpen their engineering capabilities; the municipal government improves overall control and understanding of e-government initiatives; and new norms and beliefs are instilled into the public. In the case study, user feedback was solicited wherever possible—at the points of installation, and over the service hotline, as user acceptance plays an important role in e-government success [8]. Over the process, when emerging problems were identified, analyzed and rectified, Shanghai government had its preliminary e-government conceptions tested, modified and stabilized. The intense experimentation and learning from the modular installation provided the basis for the municipal government to extend the implementation scope and for the central government to build a strategy for country-wide propagation at a later stage. Gradually, the knowledge and experiences generated from the local implementation were consolidated and conceptualized into an e-government implementation model, as the end result of the stage of municipal actualization.

## 5.3. Finding three: popularization—rapid scaling-up through replication

The popularization stage entails replicating to other municipalities and provinces the e-government implementation model validated and developed in the preceding stage. As key facilitators in the stage of popularization, government–government and government–business partnerships play a key role in achieving extended implementation by reproducing the proven practice beyond the local government where it is first conceived and established to elsewhere in the country. The lateral government– government partnership (i.e., lateral G2G partnership), namely the municipal government’s relationship with other municipal governments, creates opportunity for the dissemination of the egovernment model to a larger scale. By this means, the knowledge generated from the pioneering venture of one municipal government will be transferred to other municipal governments seeking to embrace e-government, so that the same lessons will not have to be learned anew. Different from the partnership between municipal and central governments (i.e., vertical G2G partnership), the partnership between municipal governments is largely based on a collaborative and reciprocal basis. Likewise, the government– business partnership between local businesses and other municipal governments stimulate the transfer of indigenous technological innovation and capabilities. From involvement in the pioneering e-government attempt, local partners from the private sector would have refined the technological solutions and enhanced their executional capabilities. This partnership provides other municipal governments with instant acquisition of proven technological solutions, and in the meantime, generates profits for the local businesses and government. In the case of Shanghai’s social security card project, the Chinese central government was gratified by the model implemented at the municipal level, and decided to scale up the e-government adoption through accelerated replication by other municipal governments.

In order for lasting changes to occur, the established e-government implementation model needs to be continually renewed in response to the changing environments, locally, nationally and even globally. Extended implementation provides fertile ground for the ongoing improvement of the model, with a spirit of discovery and exploration highly valued [30]. Replicating the success story in other areas is, in turn, a process of national societal learning, which happens at the national level involving government agencies, businesses and the general populace from various parts of the country. Over the dynamic and cyclic process, the e-government model is refined gradually, with collective efforts from the government, businesses and citizens. The unwavering commitment of the government ensures improved public welfare, with sound legal and institutional frameworks continuously tailored to different stages of the project; private-sector participants enhance their profitability through the constant technological improvement and innovation [29]. The broad and participatory approach shifts the beliefs of the general populace, striving for a successful large-scale transformation which relies on public attitude about the ‘‘ways of being and ways of doing, individually and as a community’’ [12]. For example, in the case, after the trial-and-error implementation of the medicare functionality, the tested model was rapidly replicated to a broad range of other functionalities, such as employment-related services and online payment.

Societal learning, at both municipal and national levels, is essential for the actualization of e-government as a large system change, considering the difficulty in anticipating the responses and coordinating the needs from multiple stakeholder groups simultaneously [27]. It generates from the implementation attempts significant insights and knowledge, which leads to continual regulatory, technological and ideological adaptation. The Shanghai government worked entrepreneurially with business participants and the community by trial and error. The implementation at the trial sites generated considerable insights into the project implementation and also for the subsequent add-on functions through a series of learning processes.

## 6. Conclusion

Harnessing IT to boost public sector performance is an imperative no government can ignore. Researchers and practitioners have long held an interest in digitization in the public sector, especially in a developing country with a distinctive political system such as China. With uneven adoption and diffusion across the country, e-government is in a nascent stage of development in China. Through the study, we hope to generate interest and insights into Information Communication Technology (ICT) practices and research in China. We hope that the study will result in additional research in ICT implementation and diffusion at different government agencies as well as private organizations, especially in a dynamic and complex governmental and socialist business environment such as China. Shanghai’s social security card project represents a large-scale transformation effort and highlights a few lessons for both researchers and practitioners. Beyond the revamped social security administration system, the gratifying outcomes also include a change in people’s values and beliefs, enhanced efficiency in G2C interaction, and development in business product portfolios.

## 6.1. Implications for research

The process model developed in this study is of important implications for researchers. The study examines the process of how a top-down e-government initiative is implemented and diffused at the municipal government level. Previous research on China’s egovernment endeavor, including the handful of case studies, typically focuses on the progression of e-government in general or primarily explores certain issues involved in e-government development, e.g., the accessibility of government websites [26]. This study complements the existing literature by unraveling the black box of the process of e-government implementation in the complex and emerging e-government environment in China. While the theoretical model is based on the trajectory that unfolded over the G2C e-government project in Shanghai, we believe that aspects of the model are sufficiently general to be applied to a range of egovernment attempts concerning governments of different levels. Although the situations which triggered the actions in the process model are case specific, a few of them are among the most likely or feasible actions that can be taken in e-government adoption and diffusion within and beyond the municipal level. The study is based on a particular municipal government in a developing economy where top-down approach is well supported through centralized government control. Developing economies represent a great number of countries in the world. Adoption of information systems in developing countries plays a crucial role in diminishing the digital divide. The study has yielded useful implications for implementing information systems in developing economies, in particular, implementing e-government system in the public sectors of developing countries. But, the particular political system associated with the case may differ from other countries where a top-down approach does not work as well as it did in the case of China. Therefore, researchers may want to take this into consideration when they use the framework for explanation or prediction in other settings.

The framework can be used as a descriptive tool to organize and analyze the activities involved in e-government implementation at the municipal level. It adds to the knowledge of the mechanisms through which the e-government initiative is conducted and carried through. In addition, the study also contributes to the research on modular systems. Although modularity is often studied in the context of product architecture and organizational structure, we extend it to project level in the public sector. With modularity as an approach to complexity, the project is decomposed into functional components (i.e., different functionalities of the social security card). With modularity as a means of facilitating imitation, the project is decomposed into geographical compo nents (i.e., governments located at different areas).

Moreover, as evidenced by a few successful e-government initiatives (e.g., [6]), the institutionalization of an e-government system does not preclude traditional ways of delivery. An egovernment system like the one in our study has a society-wide impact rippling through multiple government functions. It is especially so in a developing country where unfavorable environmental contingencies, such as regional disparity, outdated technology and mindset, and digital divide, could prolong undesired co-existence of electronic and traditional channels. In the mean time, the traditional means can serve as a desirable buffer during the transition to electronic modes. The institutionalization of certain practices could generate and sustain desirable co-existence of both channels. For example, face-to-face interaction with users could nurture trust, solicit first-hand feedback, and provide personal attention to extraordinary cases which cannot be programmed in the computerized systems.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

By examining the sequence of actions associated with egovernment development and implementation at the municipal level, the process model developed in this study provides important implications for governments as well. Useful empirical insights have been highlighted on how a municipal government carries the e-government initiative through to success.

Shanghai took its pioneering step towards e-government by experimenting in the field of social security administration. It is our hope that the study can be of value in the development and implementation of other e-government initiatives in both developed and developing economies. Furthermore, with a comprehensive account of the G2C e-government journey experienced by a municipal government, we aim to help other governments to anticipate and prepare for what they will go through as they ascend the learning curve of e-government implementation. It is important that governments gain some awareness of how modularity emerges as an approach to complexity management over the process and how societal learning integrates the knowledge to ensure project progression. Moreover, drawing on the literature of societal learning and large-scale transformation, we also hope that the process model will provide some guidance for governments, within China or across the world, to achieve their own aspiration for change.

A society-level change like the one we presented in this study requires enormous amount of resources and commitment. To better the chances of success, e-government practitioners need to leverage the resources from multiple sources in the forms of financial funding, expertise in a professional field, peer-to-peer knowledge transfer, and the commitment from various stakeholders. This study offers insights for governments from different levels. Aiming for a national-level e-government initial, the central government may want to guide any municipal-level endeavor in a manner such that the knowledge and experience generated from such efforts would be readily transferable across other municipal and provincial governments. In this study, the central government developed an e-government model from working with the municipal government of Shanghai, and later pushed the model across the country. Along the way of developing an integrated government, integration through partnership at various levels is necessary. At the nexus of central government and lower-level governments, municipal e-government practitioners need to integrate a wide range of services offered to the local residents, and synthesize efforts from public and private sectors to deliver a solution. Partnership with local research institution and manufacturer allowed Shanghai municipal government access to locally available knowledge and manufacturing techniques in order to build a reliable, secure, and technologically sound infrastructure. At the same time, e-government practitioners may not want to neglect the importance of modularity. As a decomposing effort by definition, modularity seems contradictory to the ultimate goal of an integrated government. However, it contributes to that goal by reducing the complexity and thus making the societal level integration more manageable. How to decompose a grand societal level e-government in a meaningful way is a question worth serious consideration by both practitioners and researchers.
