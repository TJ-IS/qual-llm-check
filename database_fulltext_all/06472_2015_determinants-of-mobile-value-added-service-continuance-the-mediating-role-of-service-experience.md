# Determinants of Mobile Value-Added Services Continuance: The Mediating Role of Service Experience

## Abstract

In previous research, MVAS subscribers are regarded as technology users. By adopting the service-dominant logic perspective from the service management discipline, this study proposes and empirically tests a model integrating both technology user and service consumer perspectives, emphasizing the role of service experience in MVAS continuance. The findings show that both technology excellence and service experience are principal determinants of MVAS continuance intention. Moreover, this study proposes the second-order factor structure of service experience and demonstrates the mediating role of service experience on the relationship between technology excellence and MVAS subscribers’ continuance intentions. The theoretical and practical implications are discussed.

Keywords: Mobile value-added services, technology excellence, service experience, continuance intention

## 1. Introduction

The development of mobile telecommunication technologies coupled with the rapid growth in mobile phone subscriber base contributes to the ongoing popularization of mobile telecommunication services. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) estimated the global mobile phone subscriber base to reach 6.9 billion by the end of 2014, representing a worldwide penetration rate of 95.5% [57]. With such a high acceptance of mobile telecommunication services, mobile value-added services (MVAS) have become increasingly important for mobile network operators because the conventional voice service tariffs keep declining [46, 117]. MVAS, in contrast, contribute to the ARPU (average revenue per user) as another source of corporate revenues and profits [14]. The adoption and continuance of MVAS have therefore drawn the attentions of practitioners as well as researchers.

The emphasis of prior research on mobile services adoption and continuance was predominantly placed on notions of instrumentality. This perspective sees technology as providing useful functionalities and instrumental values to enhance users’ performance on task achievement [29]. Researchers have thus focused on instrumentality and cognitive beliefs that drive usage behavior [1].

#

Cognitive determinants of satisfaction and continuance have been investigated by employing theoretical frameworks such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) [24, 25] and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [4] to study the use of mobile services [5, 66, 106, 141]. The expectation-confirmation model [89] has also been adapted for studies in information systems and mobile service continuance contexts [11, 13, 50, 53]. These studies presume that conscious decisions based on a series of evaluations on the merits of the technology under study – such as perceptions of ease of use, usefulness, and beliefs concerning system quality and service quality [2, 5, 17, 68, 141] – determine users’ adoption and continuance intentions. From the instrumental view, MVAS are usually seen as technology products that provide value or utility to subscribers. This view reflects the conventional perspective of the goods-dominant logic (see Lusch et al. [77], Vargo and Lusch [132], Vargo and Lusch [135]), which sees technology as a product to be delivered to users. Mobile phone subscribers are therefore seen as technology users [5, 94].

With the proliferation of mobile services, an understanding of technology adoption determinants beyond those focusing on technology user aspects is necessary [86], especially when MVAS subscribers play the roles of not only technology users but also service consumers [5, 62]. Although one other stream of research emphasizes the role of non-instrumental qualities such as experience in driving technology usage [1, 29], few studies on MVAS have attempted to integrate both views to provide a holistic picture on the determinants of MVAS continuance. Such gap in research requires the incorporation of the service-dominant logic [77, 132, 135] into MVAS continuance research.

The service-dominant logic view of MVAS continuance complements rather than contradicts the goods-dominant logic view. As indicated by prior research, technology is the “appliance” that assists consumers to enjoy service experiences [96, 132, 134], meaning that technology excellence serves as a facilitator for MVAS subscribers to engage in a positive service experience. Furthermore, to understand the factors that lead to MVAS continuance, it is necessary to consider determinants beyond traditional adoption and continuance antecedents, which primarily focus on the influence of technology excellence. There is scant empirical research, however, on the conceptualization of service

#

experience and the effects of service experience on users’ continuance intentions, particularly in the MVAS context. Borrowing the service-dominant logic from the service marketing discipline, this research extends MVAS continuance research by proposing and empirically testing a model of MVAS continuance that integrates both the effects of technology excellence and service experience. Emphasizing the influence of service experience beyond technology excellence alone, the model represents a novel approach to understanding consumers’ continuance intentions toward MVAS. Besides the objective of unpacking and understanding the complex nature of service experience in the MVAS context, one other objective of the current study is to examine the mediating role of service service-dominant logic perspective into the research model, our findings should contribute to MVAS continuance research on individual consumers in a voluntary and personal context. In practice, our findings could guide mobile network operators in augmenting and managing their MVAS offerings.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, a discussion of the theoretical background of the current study is presented. In Section 3, we propose the research hypotheses. Section 4 describes the research methodology followed by the data analysis in Section 5. In Section 6, we discuss the theoretical and practical implications of our research. Section 7 concludes the paper and suggests future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical Background

## 2.1 The Growing Importance of Service Experience in MVAS

In the mobile telecommunications industry, core services usually refer to voice call services, and MVAS are non-core service options that add value to the basic service offering and may involve a premium charge. Examples of MVAS include a variety of communication, system-based, download and subscription, and Internet access services [33]. (The definition of MVAS is shown in Appendix A.)

#

With the popularization of smartphones and the rapid development of mobile telecommunication technologies, MVAS have exemplified diversified usage, as they are more than mere technological advancements but, rather, offer unique service experiences. For example, the 2G technology of mobile telecommunications supports only text [130], whereas 3G and 4G technologies support rich media such as video clips, mobile TV, and streaming media. This expanded capability has enabled diversified forms of mobile services and commerce to become one of the most profitable markets with promising growth [62].

The intense price competition and regulation in mobile termination rates have caused voice ARPU to drop significantly [45]. In the U.S. market alone, the monthly spending on voice services per subscriber has declined by more than \$12 from 2007 to 2010 [114]. Because voice service is the core service provided by all mobile network operators, the only way for the operators to compete in voice and messaging services is by lowering tariffs [114]. This circumstance arises from the commodity nature of voice and messaging services, which are widely used by all subscribers but are difficult to differentiate; thus, these services must be sold solely on price.

The industry operates this way because mobile network operators consider voice and messaging services as “goods” rather than “services.” The more subscribers make voice calls, the more the operators earn in voice revenue. The problem for operators thus lies in the differentiation from competitors. Due to the number-portability policy, however, subscribers are free to switch to another mobile network operator while keeping the old mobile phone number. Some operators even provide customers a subsidy as an incentive to cover the costs for service transfers from other operators. For the above reasons, even though voice and messaging services may be easy to use and useful, this fact does not guarantee subscribers’ continual subscription.

Mobile network operators thus endeavor to provide a rich collection of MVAS to differentiate themselves from other competitors to ensure subscribers’ continual usage. However, as mobile technologies and MVAS evolve over time, not all services contribute to profitability as expected. Some MVAS are commonplace among mobile network operators and thus become commoditized and

#

can no longer differentiate from competitive offerings [14]. For example, mobile network operators revenues associated with the once-lucrative mobile messaging services are expected to decline in the next few years [45, 46, 83, 111]. SMS is a simple service and has no stringent requirement on latency and data rates. However, declining SMS revenues indicate that merely offering usefulness in serving communication needs, ease of use in operation (i.e., text typing), and high system and service quality backed by the mobile network operators is not enough to retain subscribers. The reasons are twofold. First, the long-term price erosion effect of all-you-can-eat bundles has caused the commoditization of SMS, which not only erodes messaging revenues but also makes the service provided by different mobile network operators non-differentiated. Second, mobile apps such as iMessage, Facebook, Viber, Twitter, and WhatsApp allow users to message each other without extra costs for SMS as long as they are connected to the Internet via their data plan or WiFi connection [46, 111]. The failure of mobile network operators to provide a differentiating service experience has caused some MVAS to become commoditized. These services, such as mobile weather forecasting, mobile fortune telling, and SMS, fail to differentiate themselves from competitors’ offerings and to contribute to revenues because they are “performing a function” that is also available from other competitors rather than “providing an experience” that is unique to the interaction between the service and the subscriber.

Entering the 3G and 4G eras, a wider collection of MVAS offerings has been available that aims to serve either hedonic or utilitarian subscriber needs. Multimedia capability coupled with high bandwidth transmission in the 3G and 4G eras has radically changed the service experience in contrast to the communication-centric services in the 2G era [3, 92]. For example, mobile broadband has contributed to the rollout of new services such as streaming music and video, mobile navigation, and mobile TV. Advancements in global positioning system (GPS) technologies have also enabled a variety of location-based services (LBS). These services focus not only on “performing a function,” as SMS does in personal communication, but also on advancing a personal and intimate usage experience in information, music, video, commerce, and socialization [92, 130]. During the process of interactions with the services, users gain unique experiences that lure them into continual use.

With the rollout of new mobile technologies and affordable smart handheld devices that have evolved from talk-based devices to mobile computing platforms, MVAS are becoming deeply involved with the service consumption experience. For example, as technologies such as GPS, mobile phone pinging, and network triangulation become more accessible and act in an accurate and real-time manner, LBS have emerged as an indispensible part of mobile commerce [113]. This altered landscape could give subscribers a completely different experience from what they could gain from MVAS. For example, subscribers navigating in a certain area may have different needs. One who shops for bargains in an outlet mall is likely to have a completely different LBS need and consumption experience from that of a person who is on the way to the office.

The breadth of user needs not only indicates the opportunities as well as challenges for mobile network operators in offering MVAS but also suggests that MVAS have evolved from communication-centric services toward personal-centric services, serving both utilitarian and hedonic purposes of personal use. Being able to differentiate by offering unique service experiences is thus crucial to the success of MVAS. A unique service experience is crucial because service value is phenomenologically determined, and it is service experience that connects the customer with the service provider during the service provision process [134]. If an LBS provider knows the subscriber’s exact location and is able to target useful information at that point in time, the benefit can be mutual. The service provider gains subscriber trust and loyalty that differentiate itself from competitors, and the subscriber gains a positive experience from the LBS usage, which lures him or her into continual use. Mobile network operators could consequently gain premium profits beyond those from voice and messaging services [38].

The above discussion indicates the growing importance of service experience beyond traditional IS adoption and continuance determinants, namely, technology excellence, and also illustrates the roadmap of MVAS development in recent years. Not only goods but also services may become commoditized, particularly in competitive environments such as the mobile telecommunications industry. It thus requires unique service experiences to differentiate from competitors and retain subscribers. Mobile network operators invest heavily in infrastructure construction to provide communication services to subscribers; however, merely providing services that are perceived high in ease of use, usefulness, system quality, or service quality is not enough. The essence of the experience economy has suggested the real value of service experience that would be appreciated by subscribers. This insight calls for a service-dominant logic perspective [77, 132, 135] in investigating mobile phone subscribers’ continuance intentions regarding MVAS. Therefore, we will discuss the service-dominant logic of service management in the next section.

## 2.2 Toward the Service-Dominant Logic

In response to the global transition from a goods-based economy to a service-based economy and the accompanying opportunities for service innovation enabled by information technology advancements, prior research has suggested that focus be paid on the promising opportunities for research in the domain of digitized services innovation [107]. This refocusing calls for the integration of the service-dominant logic perspective into IS research to reflect the multidisciplinary nature of designing and delivering services.

The traditional goods-dominant logic views goods as tangible outputs embedded with value, and the value is usually measured in terms of price mechanisms and value-in-exchange. Services are seen as an add-on that enhances the value of goods [77, 78]. Such a view has led marketers and researchers to conceptualize services as a particular type of intangible products. Instead of focusing on the effective creation of value through service, marketers and researchers focus on the efficient production of products [78]. The qualities of the services become undesirable because a lack of consumer involvement often causes higher marketing costs, rapid perishability, and a failure to respond to changing consumer needs [132].

In contrast, the service-dominant logic sees services as an exchange process in which one party may also benefit itself by applying specialized competences for the benefit of another party [77]. That is, the service-dominant logic shifts the focus from value distribution to value co-creation, a collaborative process in which customers play the role of collaborative partners to produce and sustain value [77, 132, 133, 138]. In contrast to the value-in-exchange view of the goods-dominant logic, which sees value as determined by producers and embedded in products during the manufacturing phase, the value-in-use view of the service-dominant logic suggests that value is “defined by and cocreated with the consumer” (p. 6) during the consumption process, when customers exert their knowledge and skills in using the goods and services [132]. Thus, the suppliers act as value facilitators who assist in the value-generation process by providing customers with necessary resources, and the value propositions developed by the suppliers are regarded by the customers as value foundations [40].

Prior research on mobile service adoption and continuance mainly focuses on technology excellence factors including perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, system quality, and service quality [2, 17, 50, 66, 68, 141]. These determinants reflect the goods-dominant logic view and suggest that if technology developers and service providers are able to design these qualities into the “products” they offer, high adoption or continuance intentions can be expected. In other words, values of using the technology were determined at the development stage [e.g., 17, 66, 137]. However, because the use of MVAS involves interaction among subscribers, mobile network operators, and other third-party content providers, the value of such co-created experience will be defined and realized only during the consumption process. The notion of value-in-use requires the integration of the service-dominant logic view into mobile service continuance research to identify the role of service experience in MVAS continuance. Because value in the service-dominant logic view can only be co-created through the collaboration among the service provider, the customer, and other value-network partners [107], service value may present itself in the form of better service experiences. Therefore, whereas the technology excellence factors provide high explanatory power in prior research models, considering the role of service experience in the MVAS context would further our understanding of factors leading to MVAS continuance [e.g., 145]. Drawing on the service-dominant logic perspective, the current study thus aims to bridge the gap between the growth potential of MVAS and the limited understanding of what is service experience and how it affects MVAS subscribers’ continuance intentions.

## 2.3 Service Experience

Vargo and Lusch [134] have considered service value to be experiential in nature. Although there are many definitions and conceptualizations of service experience (e.g., [63, 96, 99]), it is considered the result of the interaction and the memorable process of co-creation among the service provider, the customer, and other value-creation partners that can occur at any conscious moment [96, 99]. Moreover, as Pine and Gilmore [96] stated, “the newly identified offering of experiences occurs whenever a company intentionally uses services as the stage and goods as props to engage an individual” (p. 10). Poulsson and Kale [99] further defined a commercial experience as “an engaging act of co-creation between a provider and a consumer wherein the consumer perceives value in the encounter and in the subsequent memory of that encounter” (p. 270).

Service experience has received researchers’ attention in areas such as information systems [1, 52, 65], education [69, 122], service design and management [32, 47, 90, 105], and marketing [7, 84, 99]. Whereas a generalizable model that captures the complex nature of service experience has yet to be developed [47, 90], researchers have proposed and discussed a number of feelings and sensation elements that reflect the essence of service experience. These elements include flow [20, 22, 58, 87, 104, 102, 123, 129, 139], superior functioning [104, 102, 103], intense joy [1, 58, 104, 102, 142], stimulation [81, 82, 116, 121], and engagement [96, 99, 121, 122]. Because service experience represents a general concept that is manifested by these five experiential feelings and sensations, the current study conceptualizes service experience as a superordinate second-order construct reflected by these five first-order factors.

Flow is “the holistic sensation that people feel when they act with total involvement” [20, p. 36]. When customers enter a flow state during a service encounter, they tend to become absorbed in their activity because they usually lose sense of time and self and shift into a mode of experience characterized by “a narrowing of the focus of awareness, so that irrelevant perceptions and thoughts are filtered out, by loss of self-consciousness, by a responsiveness to clear goals and unambiguous feedback, and by a sense of control over the environment” [20, p. 72]. It is interesting to note the difference between flow experience and positive emotion (such as joy and happiness) [21]. When people are in a flow state, they feel only what is relevant to the task and screen out irrelevant thoughts and perceptions. People will feel the salience of the experience only after they get out of the flow state; i.e., when the task is completed [22, 21]. Consequently, many past studies consider flow as a construct reflecting a positive consumer experience [52, 65, 87, 129, 139].

Superior functioning is analogous to peak performance, meaning full use of one’s potential to behave effectively and achieve optimal functioning [102, 103]. Superior functioning has two distinguishing characteristics, namely a sense of self in clear process and full focus [102], and is used for investigating a range of research issues pertaining to human performance. Examples of these research issues include productivity, excellence, and creativity [102]. Different from flow, which is associated with total concentration in the task and a subsequent loss of self, superior functioning involves a strong sense of self [103]. In addition, whereas flow may be highly associated with joyfulness or playfulness, superior functioning may not entail such qualities [104]. Furthermore, whereas flow may involve companionship during the task, superior functioning is usually noncompaniable [104].

Intense joy represents an optimal level of subjective experience which shows moments of highest happiness and is a synthesis of intrinsic interest and perceived enjoyment [1, 104, 102]. It is the personal and highly valued experience characterized by significance, fulfillment, and spirituality [102]. Such an experience is a heightened emotion that surpasses the usual level of psychological experiencing in intensity, meaningfulness, and richness [80, 104]. Intense joy often has a high emotional content and a lasting impression of focused awareness, complete absorption, perception of complete control, and spontaneity [7, 58, 104, 142]. In addition, although both superior functioning and intense joy represent cherished experiences of a person, the former focuses on the optimal behavioral outcomes, whereas the latter focuses on positive emotions or feelings. That is, intense joy and superior functioning can be seen as the two extremes of the spectrum spanning from feeling to performance, respectively [102]. One can also distinguish intense joy from flow experience because the former is non-motivated, whereas the latter is intrinsically motivated [102].

Stimulation is one of the important elements of experience. Earlier research indicated that stimulation captures one’s emotional response to a place [81]. Stimulation of interest or of positive feelings is an essential aspect of experience to encourage continuing motivation and subsequent involvement. Prior research has argued that interest sets the foundation for a person to be engaged with a topic or event [26]. That is, activities or events that arouse feelings of stimulation lead people into deeper and further exploration. Stimulation creates positive emotions that entice people to seize opportunities to explore, imagine, learn, and gain feedback in a way that not only satisfies their curiosity but also serves as a bridge to other tasks [121].

Facilitating people’s experience is of key importance for a service encounter to be successful. As the competition becomes more intense in today’s environment, experience is not only about entertaining consumers. Providing engaging experiences to maximize the impact of services by engaging customers in a personal and memorable way is the central concern [96, 123].

To create a successful dialog between the service provider and the consumer, experience co-creation by the engagement of both parties is necessary because valuable experiences cannot be created by either of the two parties alone [100, 101]. A successful and valuable experience makes process involves enabling co-creative interactions so that individuals can have meaningful and compelling engagement experiences” (p. 11). Moreover, actively involving customers through effective interaction with feedback can increase their engagement with an experience [99].

## 2.4 Technology Excellence

The current study defines technology excellence as the degree to which MVAS is perceived as being superior in use. As discussed earlier, the goods-dominant logic considers technology as a tangible product. Users continue using a technology if the technology is perceived to be superior or excellent in user-friendliness, performance enhancement, reliability, and responsiveness [13, 15, 23, 50, 51, 54]. Given that perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, system quality, and service quality reflect these prominent user perceptions regarding MVAS, the current study conceives technology excellence as a multidimensional construct consisting of these four subcontructs that represent subscribers’ cognitive beliefs regarding MVAS.

Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are two important user beliefs that lead to technology continuance [13, 16, 50, 56, 70]. Following the original definition of Davis [24], this study defines perceived ease of use as the degree to which a subscriber believes that using MVAS would be free of effort. In addition, perceived ease of use is defined as the degree of one’s perception that using MVAS would enhance the performance of his or her task. System quality has to do with interface design, functionality, and response time, reflecting the technical level of success of a system with respect to information production [2, 27]. The definition of service quality, derived from Parasuraman et al. [91], is how well the delivered service level of MVAS matches subscriber expectations.

Because perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, system quality, and service quality captures differing aspects of technology excellence, this study conceptualizes technology excellence as a multidimensional construct that has a formative relationship with the four subconstructs, each measured using reflective items [95].

## 3. Hypotheses Development

The role of information technology in the service-dominant logic as an appliance through which superior service experience can be delivered and enjoyed has gained increasing importance [107, 118,

132, 133, 134]. When one uses MVAS, one is essentially collaborating with MVAS providers in creating a positive MVAS user experience.

The IS literature has supported the effect of technology excellence on flow [52, 115, 146]. The rich entertainment functions provided by MVAS would result in user immersion and concentration because one of the primary reasons people engage in co-production is for intrinsic enjoyment [75, 77]. Technology excellence features such as usefulness, ease of use, system quality, and service quality allow subscribers to use MVAS on the move without being abruptly interrupted by unreliable or slow responses, leading to higher enjoyment, perceived control, and attention focus, which are three salient features of flow [65, 146].

In addition, the advancement of mobile technologies allows MVAS subscribers to perform multiple tasks simultaneously (e.g., through push notifications), communicate with others efficiently (e.g., using SMS and instant messaging apps), and integrate with other personal tasks seamlessly (e.g., using cloud services), leading to maximum productivity in personal performance. For example, subscribers could use mobile instant messaging services for online discussion with colleagues while listening to music and accessing the Internet [145]. That is, technology excellence contributes to the superior functioning experience by improving subscribers’ working and living efficiency and effectiveness.

Technology not only is concerned with instrumentality that fulfills users’ requirements on utilitarian performance but also offers non-instrumental qualities that are intrinsically pleasant [1, 29]. Technology excellence assists users in enjoying the joyful experience. For example, perceived ease of use leads to higher enjoyment through the contribution to the positive interaction experience [2]. Technology excellence also ensures a reliable and responsive service platform on which MVAS subscribers feel totally immersed to achieve highest happiness.

Prior studies have investigated the role of technology excellence and stimulation in user experience [44, 61]. MVAS with technology excellence bring about stimulation because, from the perspective of optimum stimulation level [110], these services can be considered a way that entices subscribers to take risk (e.g., mobile banking), seek variety (e.g., mobile IM service as an alternative to PC IM service), and motivate curiosity (e.g., personal innovativeness in adopting new MVAS). Technology excellence thus serves as a positive catalyst for stimulation experience in MVAS use.

One experiences higher engagement when his or her skills and the perceived challenge are high and in balance [34, 49, 87, 121]). MVAS with technology excellence could appropriately adjust the challenge experienced to fit user skills through properly designed interface, effective customer feedback, and reliable backend systems. Moreover, subscription is a sign of personal relevance and allows incremental learning of skills that build upon previous usage experiences during the subscription period. Such environment, issuing appropriate challenges and providing opportunities to enhance skills, positively contributes to the engagement of subscribers [121].

Through technology excellence, MVAS providers are able to approach and interact with subscribers to understand their needs and then develop solutions to meet the needs by offering desirable service experiences, as reflected in flow, superior functioning, intense joy, stimulation, and engagement. Hypothesis 1 is thus proposed as follows:

H1: Technology excellence is positively associated with the MVAS service experience.

One difference between the service-dominant logic and the goods-dominant logic is that the former values the interaction between the service provider and the customer and places high priority on the importance of understanding and delivering customer experience because it affects users’ continuance intentions, whereas the latter focuses on the transfer of ownership of output [78].

A unique and favorable experience not only creates positive marketing outcomes but also opens up business opportunities [10, 96, 124], because customers want to generate longer relationships and tighter bonds with the service provider to continue enjoying the service experience unavailable from other competitors. Prior research has illustrated the influence of service experience on continuance intentions. For example, cognitive and emotional responses associated with a positive experience increase consumers’ intentions to return to a website or to repurchase [65, 105]. Confirmation of service experience with user expectations positively contributes to service continuance because users are satisfied with the service [11]. Moreover, a positive service experience contributes to customer loyalty and further leads to customers’ willingness for building a continued relationship with the service provider [96, 105].

Service experience is crucial in the service provision process because the phenomenological Service providers build relational ties with customers through offering unique and unforgettable service experience so that customers are willing to become continual “beneficiaries” of the service [134].

The above discussion leads to hypothesis 2:

H2: Service experience is positively associated with MVAS continuance intention.

Past literature and theoretical models on technology adoption and continuance, such as the technology acceptance model [24, 25], the expectation-confirmation model of IS (ECM-IS) [13], and extensions of these models, have suggested that positive perceptions of technologies lead to user adoption and continuance intentions. The basic idea behind such direct causal relationship is that if users have positive evaluations on characteristics such as usefulness and ease of use of a technology, their intention to adopt or to continue using that technology will be higher (e.g., [64, 66, 67, 127]). Particularly in the mobile Internet context, Hong et al.’s [50] results supported the influence of perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness on continuance intention. Besides these two technology excellence factors, other studies such as Ahn et al. [2] and Cheong and Park [17] have attempted to extend TAM with quality perceptions for a stronger explanation of the acceptance of retailing websites and mobile Internet, respectively. System quality, reflecting user-desired qualities of response time, usability, reliability, availability, and adaptability of the technology or information system under study [27, 28, 71], determines users’ continuance intentions. Users may be reluctant to use the technology if they frequently experience delays in response, system disconnections, failure of access, or poor security [27, 71, 120]. Service quality also serve as a predictor of continuance intention in both IS and consumer behavior literature [23, 54, 143, 144]. High service quality provides values in the form of trustworthiness, responsiveness, personalization, and attentiveness [15, 28] and entices MVAS subscribers to continue using the services.

However, facing the rapid advancement of mobile technologies and the consequent challenge of commoditization, mobile network operators need to do more beyond offering useful, easy to use MVAS with high system and service quality. The key is whether these user perceptions can be turned into or foster the creation of positive service experiences. As discussed in section 2.1, the use of MVAS has extended beyond personal communication purposes and requires a high degree of interaction between mobile subscribers and service providers. The significance of such interaction lies in servicing the needs of the users in their unique usage contexts [39]. That is, mobile technologies need to be appropriately designed and utilized to capture the unique experience of value co-creation between subscribers and MVAS providers for the ultimate goal of subscriber retention. The above discussion suggests the key role of service experience in which mobile technologies serve as a vehicle to convey MVAS value and enable such interaction experience. Take LBS as an example [59]. The experience of using LBS is based on whether the services require minimal learning and configuration efforts, can effectively deliver location-based information, and is reliable and responsive [18]. Subscribers in a big shopping mall, for example, may be surprised and enjoy the experience in which location-based ads are pushed to their mobile devices to notify them of the existence of a friend in the nearby area and of the chance to win a special discount on appearance together. The existence of such impressive experience further attracts subscribers for continual use of LBS.

Based on the discussion above, hypothesis 3 is proposed as follows.

H3: Service experience mediates the relationship between technology excellence and MVAS continuance intention.

## 4. Research Methodology

## 4.1 Construct Operationalization

The research constructs in this study was measured using validated items from prior studies. Technology excellence is formed by four first-order factors: perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, system quality, and service quality. Perceived ease of use was adapted from Davis [24] and Venkatesh and Davis [136], and the scale for perceived usefulness was derived from Van der Heijden [131]. System quality and service quality were measured using items adapted from Ahn et al. [2]. Flow was adapted from Trevino and Webster [129]. The measurement items for superior functioning were adapted from Rai et al. [108], and the measurement items for intense joy were adapted from Agarwal and Karahanna [1]. Stimulation was measured using items adapted from Richins et al. [116] and Moon and Kim [82]. Measures of engagement were adapted from Shin [122]. Lastly, the measurement of continuance intention was adapted from Bhattacherjee [11] and Bhattacherjee and Premkumar [12]. All items were measured using seven-point Likert scales ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree.

This study included three control variables into the research model. These control variables were respondents’ MVAS experience, age, and gender [48]. MVAS experience referred to the number of years he or she had used MVAS.

Because the survey was to be conducted in a Chinese context, a two-way translation was performed to ensure that the respondents had no difficulties reading the items and that no loss of information was present in the translated version. A professor specializing in mobile services research English-Chinese translation then performed a back-translation into English. Discrepancies were identified and discussed, and only slight modifications were needed. The draft version of the questionnaire was sent for review by two IS professors, one industry analyst, and three MVAS users to avoid ambiguous wording or expressions. ## 4.2 Data Collection

This study conducted an online survey for the data collection to reach a wider group of MVAS subscribers of different demographics. The invitation to participate in the survey was posted for two weeks on two of the largest community portals in Taiwan: PTT (telnet://ptt.cc) and Wretch (http://wretch.cc). As indicated by Digital Life Research Group [30], young users dominate the MVAS subscriber population in Taiwan (52.2 percent were under 30 years old, with 9.1 percent under age 19 and 43.1 between ages 20-29). These two platforms thus were considered appropriate targets for data collection. In Taiwan, there were 126.4 mobile phone subscribers per 100 inhabitants as of the end of February 2013 [85]. Moreover, data ARPU were forecast to account for 22 percent of the overall ARPU in 2014 [55]. Therefore, Taiwan is a suitable context for MVAS research. The respondents who completed the questionnaire had a chance to enter a lottery and win cash coupons for a local chain store. To avoid the possibility of multiple survey participation, each respondent was required to provide his/her mobile phone number at the end of the questionnaire. The IP address of each respondent was also recorded for filtering purposes. Because this study is on MVAS continuance intention, the respondents who had no experience with MVAS were excluded from data analysis.

A total of 304 valid responses were collected. Among the valid respondents, 153 were female MVAS users (50.3 percent) and 151 were male users (49.7 percent). Most of the respondents were in their twenties, with ages 21-25 accounting for 62.2 percent and ages 26-30 for 23.0 percent. The length of experience with using MVAS ranged from less than one year (33.9 percent) to more than three years (20.4 percent). Moreover, 40.5 percent of the respondents reported spending NT\$301 to NT\$600 on their monthly bills, and 29.3 percent reported spending NT\$101 to NT\$300 on their monthly bills (US\$1 is approximately equal to NT\$30). SMS was the MVAS adopted by most of the subscribers (75.0 percent), and the other MVAS adopted, in descending order, included ring-back tone downloads (57.9 percent), ringtone downloads (46.1 percent), and wallpaper and graphics downloads (31.3 percent). Other types of MVAS ranged between 3.9 percent and 19.1 percent.

## 5. Data Analysis

The variance-based partial least squares (PLS) method was chosen for the data analysis because it is prediction-oriented and thus recommended for early stages of theory development [35, 36]. Given that there has been little prior theory and few empirical studies on MVAS continuance from the perspective of the service-dominant logic, this topic is new. Moreover, being able to accommodate formative as well as reflective constructs makes PLS suitable for our research model [19].

## 5.2 Hypotheses Testing

A bootstraping procedure using resamples of 500 was performed to test the statistical significance of the path coefficients in the research model. The first two hypotheses were supported. The significant effect of technology excellence on service experience $( \beta = 0 . 7 8 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ supported H1. The significant effect of service experience on continuance intention $( \beta = 0 . 3 5 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ offered support for H2.

To examine the mediating effect of service experience on the relationship between technology excellence and MVAS continuance intention, a two-step process was followed [109, 125]. First, a nested model analysis was performed in which the model that contains both direct and mediated paths from technology excellence to continuance intention was compared against a competing model that contains only the mediated path. The former represents the partial mediation model and the latter represents the full mediation model. The two models are nested because only one more path (from technology excellence to MVAS continuance intention) exists in the partial mediation model. The $R ^ { 2 }$ values of the two models were 0.548 and 0.481, respectively. The $R ^ { 2 }$ difference in the two models was found to be significant at the $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ level (effect size $\begin{array} { r } { f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 4 8 ; } \end{array}$ pseudo $F \mathrm { - v a l u e } = 4 4 . 1 7 3 )$ . ## 6. Discussion

The service nature of MVAS suggests that research on the factors that lead to MVAS continuance should consider service experience as one of the prominent determinants of subscribers’ continuance intentions. This observation suggests the necessity to incorporate the service-dominant logic as the theoretical base in the current study. By proposing and testing a model incorporating technology excellence and service experience as antecedents of MVAS continuance intention, the current study demonstrates the important role of service experience in determining MVAS continuance intention. We discuss theoretical and practical implications below.

#

## 6.1 Theoretical Implications

Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, system quality, and service quality have been indicated by earlier researchers as salient factors in explaining continuance intentions [11, 13, 23, 50, 54, 60, 70]. These studies posited that technology excellence reflecting these features positively contributes to users’ evaluation of the technology and motivates them to continue using the technology. In contrast to prior studies, which primarily focused on pure-purpose systems such as word processing programs, online games, and websites [25, 52, 56], the current study extends this line of research to the continuance of MVAS, which are more complex due to the service nature and serve both functional and recreational purposes.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, researchers have called for studies to investigate service experience in the MVAS continuance context [62]. The current study not only echoes the vast body of work on technology continuance but also makes one significant contribution by integrating the service-dominant logic perspective into the research model and demonstrating the interplay between technology and service experience. Although service experience is closely related to service continuance [41], few researchers have attempted to unpack its complex nature. Flow, commonly used by prior research as the proxy for experience and regarded as a salient factor affecting the extent of technology use [1, 52, 87, 123, 129], only reflects a portion of the holistic experience. In addition, some other studies have been conceptual [10, 96, 99, 100, 112]. This study makes a methodological contribution by modeling experience as a second-order reflective construct comprising flow, superior functioning, intense joy, stimulation, and engagement, and the research findings provided empirical support. Such conceptualization with sound psychometric properties echoes Woodruff’s [140] view of customer value as a hierarchically structured construct and would contribute to the literature by improving our understanding of the body of knowledge on service experience, particularly in the MVAS continuance context. Moreover, given the growing recognition of the importance of service experience and in response to the call for tools to measure service providers’ success at delivering unique experiences across multiple contexts [96], researchers are invited to test the generalizability of the second-order structure in other service contexts and the predictive strength in other models of service continuance.

Second, drawing on the service-dominant logic [77, 132, 135], this study proposes that the effect of technology excellence on MVAS continuance can be indirect, and that one needs to look beyond the cognitive characteristics of mobile technologies to the service experience that subscribers can enjoy during MVAS usage. Specifically, this study shows that service experience mediates the relationship between technology excellence and MVAS continuance intention. The result not only supports the view of earlier work on specific MVAS, such as Chin [18], but also demonstrates the mediating role of service experience in bridging the influence of technology excellence on MVAS continuance. This notion echoes the service-dominant logic in that technology excellence serves as the catalyst to deliver enjoyable and favorable service experiences [107, 132, 133, 134], which consequently lead to user continuance. Furthermore, the empirical findings contribute to our understanding of the role of technology excellence and service experience in digitized services, reflecting the transformative role of mobile technologies in designing, developing, and delivering services [107].

Third, there are two streams of research that explain individuals’ behaviors toward new technologies [1]. Much of the prior research emphasized the influence of instrumentality on technology usage behavior. For example, TAM posits that instrumentality and cognitive beliefs determine one’s usage behavior regarding a technology [24, 25]. One other stream of research focuses on the experience with the technology. For example, Skadberg and Kimmel [123] and Trevino and Webster [129] investigated the role of flow experience in the use of websites and computer-mediated technologies (e-mail and voice mail), respectively.

However, these two streams are complementary rather than competing. Contemporary technologies offer not only instrumental values but also riveting interaction and engaging experiences [1], thus transitioning MVAS subscribers from passive users of mobile technologies to active participants in the MVAS consumption experience. Subscribers who experience pleasure or joy from using MVAS and who perceive the activities involving MVAS to be emotionally as well as instrumentally favorable are more likely to enjoy using MVAS extensively and continuously. Because MVAS subscribers play the dual role of technology users as well as service consumers [5, 94], this study represents an interdisciplinary effort of integrating the service-dominant logic perspective from the service management discipline into IS research to further our understanding of the determinants of MVAS continuance intention. The high explanatory power of our research model provides support for such interdisciplinary effort, which is both timely and necessary for the study of MVAS continuance [6]. The findings shed light on MVAS continuance research by demonstrating the tight coupling between technology excellence and service experience in contributing to MVAS continuance. Researchers are invited to extend this model to other service contexts to examine whether the model could be generalized and applied to services other than MVAS.

Fourth, whereas technology excellence is found to be a stronger predictor than the mediating effect of service experience in the current study, this finding presents an inconclusive yet promising line of research on continuance intention. Because limited studies exist to conclude whether technology excellence or service experience is the stronger predictor of continuance intention, we suggest that more studies be accumulated to examine these relationships.

Lastly, because each MVAS subscriber experiences the interaction in a unique context, service value is experiential in nature [134]. Technology excellence features represent service providers efforts to offer useful, easy to use, reliable, and accessible MVAS. In contrast to the rationalistic value determination, in which value is designed and determined at the MVAS development and deployment stages and in which mobile phone subscribers use MVAS based on rational consideration (e.g., [66]), the idiosyncratic determination of service value considers the usage context that generates unique service experiences [77, 78, 134]. The findings respond to Hu et al.’s [54] call for context-based research to investigate the influences of technology and service factors on continuance intention. Drawing on the service-dominant logic perspective, the current study contributes to a richer understanding of MVAS continuance by examining the second-order structure of service experience and investigating the role of service experience as the mediator between technology excellence and MVAS continuance. Future studies are invited to apply and extend the research model to other service contexts to gain a comprehensive insight into the interplay between information technology and services.

## 6.2 Practical Implications

This study has practical implications for the development, design, and marketing of MVAS. Mobile network operators are under intense pressure to seek other sources of corporate revenues and profits in response to the declining voice service tariffs. It is thus a challenge to persuade MVAS subscribers into continual subscription and use. Because subscribers are concerned about technology excellence when assessing the experience of using MVAS, efforts have to be made to enhance the perception regarding technology excellence such that subscribers will have positive experiences in using the services. Furthermore, as has been illustrated by the current study, the interplay between technology excellence and service experience suggests several ways to improve MVAS continuance. First, the results of this study show that technology excellence is still an important driver of MVAS continuance. To ensure system quality, dense coverage of base stations and reliable deployment of backend infrastructure are important for providing responsive and available MVAS. This is particularly important on special occasions such as peak hours or the New Year’s Countdown Party. Service quality contributes to service experience because quality service that offers effective support and prompt response to customer requests can generate positive experiences that the subscribers enjoy. In addition, ease of use and usefulness are two key factors that drive subscribers’ adoption and continuance intentions [66, 76]. From the subscriber’s perspective, these are the salient factors that demonstrate the excellence of the mobile technology in use.

Second, the advance of mobile technologies enhances the experience of MVAS use. For example, a number of mobile devices support glassless-3D technology, which is capable of displaying

stereoscopic objects without the need for viewers to put on special 3D glasses. Because mobile phones are predicted to be the 3D display device with the largest shipment volume in 2018 [31], this technology may offer an opportunity for service providers to create unique and innovative experiences in MVAS use, which further entices subscribers into the continual use of MVAS.

Third, for mobile network operators, because service experience plays a vital role in face of the competitive pressure of commoditization, it is important to develop a service-dominant logic thinking and grasp the idea of what is involved in a service experience. The advancement of mobile technologies has created huge opportunities for innovative service models that offer unique experiences in MVAS use. Examples include mobile ticketing service, mobile social networking, and location-based advertising. In view of the inconsistent definitions and conceptualizations of service experience, this study has proposed a second-order structure of service experience to reflect the phenomenological nature of the interaction between customers and service providers. Such conceptualization applies not only to MVAS providers who intend to differentiate from competitors to achieve greater subscriber retention but also to other service designers across different service contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

By adopting the service-dominant logic perspective from the service management discipline, this study has developed a model incorporating technology excellence and service experience to explain MVAS subscribers’ continuance intentions. In the hypothesized model, technology excellence was conceptualized as a formative second-order construct consisting of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, system quality, and service quality. Service experience was conceptualized as a second-order construct reflecting the experiences of flow, superior functioning, intense joy, stimulation, and engagement. All of the hypothesized relationships in the proposed model were found to be significant. Moreover, drawing on the service-dominant logic perspective, this study has not only supported the conceptualization of service experience as a second-order construct but also highlighted the role of service experience as the mediator between technology excellence and MVAS subscribers’ continuance intentions.

Service experience as a prominent factor in understanding MVAS continuance indicates that properly designed MVAS that optimize the service experience will foster MVAS continuance. Moreover, because the proliferation of mobile commerce builds on the foundation of the wide adoption of MVAS such as mobile Internet [62], the current study can be considered an important step toward understanding the role of service experience in mobile commerce. Future research addressing other important theoretical constructs relating to service experience would contribute to our understanding of this topic. The current study encourages and welcomes continuous efforts on the investigation of the driving factors of MVAS experience and mobile commerce by incorporating cross-discipline perspectives. Such efforts will improve the understanding of the body of knowledge on service experience and MVAS continuance for practitioners as well as researchers.

One limitation regarding the data collection process exists in the current study and should be noted. PTT ceased to announce its user profile since February 2011. Although Section 4.2 explains the appropriateness of choosing PTT as the source of data collection, researchers should be cautious in generalizing the research findings because of the convenience sampling in online surveys. Future studies are suggested to proceed with other data collection sources and techniques. For example, sampling from mobile network operators’ subscriber databases may provide a more accurate picture of MVAS usage and continuance.

## Appendix A. Definition of Mobile Value-Added Services

According to FIND [27], Mobile value-added services (MVAS) refer to all subscriber-paid services beyond standard voice communications services and add value to the basic service offering. MVAS may be developed in-house by mobile network operators or supplied by other third-party content providers. MVAS can be categorized into the following four types:

(1) Communication services include SMS (short message service), MMS (multimedia message service), instant messaging, and videophone service. These services allow subscribers to communicate with each other through a variety of formats including text, image, and video.

(2) System-based services are provided through setup at the mobile network operator’s system end. Examples for services in this category include ring-back tones, phonebook backup, and mobile secretary.

(3) Download and subscription services include ringtone, wallpaper, and game download services and subscription to weather forecast updates and newsletters.
