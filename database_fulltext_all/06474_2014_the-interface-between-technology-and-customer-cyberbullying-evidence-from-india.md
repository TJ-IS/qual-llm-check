# The interface between technology and customer cyberbullying: Evidence from India

Premilla D'Cruz ⁎, Ernesto Noronha

Wing 14C, IIM Ahmedabad, Vastrapur, Ahmedabad 380 015, Gujarat, India

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 20 November 2013 Received in revised form 6 June 2014 Accepted 9 June 2014 Available online 27 June 2014

Keywords: Workplace cyberbullying Customer abuse Customer sovereignty Service work Call centres Offshoring India Media richness Media synchronicity Channel expansion

## a b s t r a c t

Drawing on a phenomenological inquiry of the subjective work experiences of Indian call agents employed in international-facing call centres, this paper highlights the interface between information and communication technologies and devices and employee experiences of customer cyberbullying. Providing holistic and contextualized insights into the genesis, course and outcome of customer cyberbullying, the paper shows that whereas the absence of visual cues does not impede employees' accurate interpretation of their negative experiences, it exacerbates customers' misbehaviour since the latter feel dissociated from and cannot see the impact of their actions on employees. While the technology-linked pace of work affects employee coping with customer cyberbullying, the maintenance of records and archives brings in concreteness and permanence through which retaliation is ruled out but reviewing the interaction for purposes of learning and even redressal is possible.

## 1. Introduction

Service work is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as work, below professional level, in which a key part of the labour process involves interaction with customers (Korczynski & Evans, 2013). Frontline employees who perform such work become the face of the service providing organization and walk a <sup>fi</sup>ne balance between ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective service that pleases customers and leaves the latter with a positive impression of the service provider. Employer competitive advantage in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial gains and reputation as well as customer satisfaction and retention are at stake here (Bettencourt, Brown, & Mackenzie, 2005; Grandey, Diefendorff, & Rupp, 2013). The entrenchment of customer sovereignty in the service economy as organizations pursue success (Korczynski & Evans, 2013) accords customers a fair amount of freedom to behave as they please with frontline employees (Harris & Reynolds, 2003). As a result, customer bullying (alternatively termed customer abuse in this paper), de<sup>fi</sup>ned as forms of customer behaviour that are perceived by frontline employees as aggressive, intimidating or insulting to themselves (Korczynski & Evans, 2013), is commonly reported in a wide range of service occupations (Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008).

Empirical research on customer bullying which throws light on its antecedents, manifestations and outcomes remains concentrated in face-to-face, real or physical encounters. The rise of computer-mediated, cyber or virtual service interactions, which are only expected to increase as technology undergirds and drives business, adds an interesting and important dimension to the debate as it throws up the question of how customer bullying plays out in such circumstances. The present paper addresses this gap, highlighting the interface between information and communication technologies and devices (ICTDs) and employee experiences of customer abuse. Drawing on a study of Indian agents working in international-facing call centres, the paper focuses on how technology in<sup>fl</sup>uences the genesis, course and impact of customer abuse. Following the section on customer bullying in service encounters (Section 2) and customer bullying and the mode of service (Section 3), the inquiry's method (Section 4) and <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented (Section 5) and discussed (Section 6).

## 2. Customer bullying in service encounters

Customer bullying, which is manifested mainly as person-related (D'Cruz, 2013) verbal aggression (Yagil, 2008), represents an extra-organizational source of misbehaviour operating at the interpersonal level of analysis (D'Cruz, 2013). Customer bullying is primarily fuelled by the ideology of customer sovereignty (D'Cruz, 2013; Korczynski & Bishop, 2008; Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008). Indeed, protagonist-related factors such as personality, mood, attitudes, etc., invoking the psychological approach and situational-linked in<sup>fl</sup>uences such as ambience and servicescape referring to the contingency approach (Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008) are subsumed within sociological explanations which emphasize that customer bullying is systemically present in the service economy (Korczynski & Evans, 2013). This latter view maintains that customer bullying arises when the enchanting myth of customer sovereignty propagated by service organizations breaks down. With the pressures of balancing the contradictions between production and consumption nullifying the highly publicized and supposedly revered goal of customer supremacy, disillusionment gives way to aggression as customers encounter the reality of their powerlessness (Korczynski & Evans, 2013). Negative reactions are displaced on to frontline employees who are socialized by their employers into accepting customers' sense of entitlement, thereby not only legitimizing and normalizing customer abuse but also internalizing a sense of their own culpability (Korczynski & Bishop, 2008; Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008). Operating under such imbalanced power equations, employees feel helpless and report having no choice but to please and indulge customers and to continue the service interaction with cordiality and engagement (Yagil, 2008).

Whereas most service interactions are disembedded (Korczynski & Evans, 2013), repeated encounters and/or extreme instances of customer bullying bring in the element of persistence or severity (D'Cruz, 2013) which occasion short-term and long-term emotional injury to frontline employees (Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008). Apart from anger and depression, exposure to sustained customer misbehaviour depletes the employee's resources such that a state of burnout, manifested primarily as emotional exhaustion and a sense of worthlessness and humiliation, results (Yagil, 2008). Yet, employee coping is constrained by the ideology of customer sovereignty. With employers rationalizing customer bullying such that not only intra-organizational redressal mechanisms are unavailable (D'Cruz, 2013) but also performance appraisal systems accord customers reward and coercive power (Yagil, 2008), employees wishing to continue in such employment must invoke emotion-focused strategies to address the challenges of their negative experiences (Yagil, 2008). Employees consider cognitive restructuring, affective blunting and distancing, compartmentalization, social support, environmental manipulations and customer bribing as effective coping mechanisms (Yagil, 2008). Quoting Czarniawska's point that ‘insult as such does not have to be humiliating’, Korczynski and Evans (2013) hold that “it is the inability to meet the challenge constituted by an insult that is humiliating”.

Though organizations promote customer sovereignty as means to competitive advantage, customer bullying carries indirect organizational costs. Poor morale and motivation and high absenteeism attests to employees' lowered job satisfaction and organizational commitment with spillover effects on performance (Yagil, 2008). Notwithstanding these outcomes, where employees do draw the line between legitimate and illegitimate customer behaviour, they are seen as non-conformists (Yagil, 2008). That management goes to the extent of punishing frontline employees who react against abusive customers evidences the rightfulness they accord customer bullying even at the cost of harming their own staff (Korczynski & Evans, 2013; Yagil, 2008). Nonetheless that these employees believe that some boundaries should not be crossed indicates the gap (at least at some point for a few) between management thought and staff perspectives, underscoring that customer sovereignty exists as a contested rather than hegemonic discourse in service organizations (Korczynski & Evans, 2013).

## 3. Customer bullying and the mode of service

Face-to-face interactions in the real world have been the mainstay of service encounters and our insights into customer bullying come from this mode. With physical proximity in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the degree of media richness and synchronicity (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Dennis & Valacich, 1999) in the service encounter, employees not only accurately know when they are being bullied by customers but must also circumscribe the range of alternative solutions that can adopted to deal with the situation. To elaborate, real service encounters in such cases encompass the highest level of media richness allowing for maximum cue capacity, feedback immediacy, personalized focus and naturalized language (Daft & Lengel, 1986). Customers and service providers are subjected to the entire array of non-verbal cues including visual and auditory cues, in addition to verbal cues. With interactions being played out synchronously, both parties involved can respond back to each other at once, bringing a dynamic feel to the process. The sense of physical and emotional closeness and relational focus afforded by the mode of interaction are enhanced through the use of an individualized approach. Frontline employees must capitalize on these features to ensure communication convevance and convergence (Dennis & Valacich. 1999) so that equivocality is decreased and social presence is enhanced (Lippert, 2008), with the end result of furthering customer satisfaction and organizational effectiveness. Under circumstances of customer bullying, frontline employees must manage their verbal and non-verbal responses such that customer sovereignty is not violated. That is, given the clear, dynamic and connected nature of the service interaction, it is imperative that employees do not betray any anti-customer sentiment in the face of customer bullying but maintain cordiality and respect. To this end, media synchronicity is tailored to facilitate the communication performance (Dennis & Valacich, 1999) required by the circumstances such that frontline employees deal with their reactions to customer bullying without compromising on customer supremacy. Thus, apart from taking care of issues related to transmission velocity and symbol variety indicated earlier, employees must curb parallelisms and rely on rehearsed responses (Dennis & Valacich. 1999) to maintain effective and congruent communication consistent (Lippert, 2008) with the dictates of the service economy. In other words, frontline employees must limit their reactions to customer abuse to make sure that the cues and immediacy that characterize the rea interactions cohere with the speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of customer service. In addition, where employees engage in concomitant mental reprocessing (Dennis & Valacich, 1999) of the negative experience, this intrapsychic dynamic cannot overtly affect their service behaviour. Quite clearly, then, in face-to-face physical encounters, the ideology of customer sovereignty prevails such that media richness and synchronicity must operate to accommodate its demands even in instances of customer bullying.

Contemporaneously, <sup>fi</sup>nancial imperatives are leading business organizations across the world to opt for the virtual mode of customer interactions enacted in cyberspace through the reliance on ICTDs. Since, alongside economic considerations, service agendas demand continuity in quality such that customer sovereignty is not compromised, most organizations engage call centres to this end. Their premise is that, except for the absence of visual cues, telephones retain the media richness and synchronicity of real service encounters and do not cut back on customer satisfaction (Noronha & D'Cruz. 2009). While customer interests are being thus safeguarded in spite of the organizational cost-cutting agenda, does the adoption of technology-mediated service interaction eliminate customer bullying and, if not, what does it mean for employee experiences of customer bullying?

There is substantial evidence to answer the first question. Van Jaarsveld and Poster (2013), in their recent review, provide irrefutable data from across the world to establish the presence of customer cyberabuse in the context of call centres. The second question, viz, what is the interface between ICTDs and customer bullying in call centres, remains unaddressed. This paper, reporting a phenomenological inquiry of Indian agents working in international-facing call centres, speaks to the issue of how technology in<sup>fl</sup>uences agents' discernment of, attribution about and coping with customer bullying in an offshored, virtual context. The following paragraphs of this section highlight the numerous gaps in our knowledge here, raising various points that the present research seeks to uncover to overcome the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed lacuna (See Table 1 for a summary of the main research questions of the study).

Telephonic customer service allows for feedback immediacy, personalized focus, naturalized language (Daft & Lengel, 1986), rehearsability and limited parallelisms (Dennis & Valacich, 1999), though cue capacity (Daft & Lengel, 1986) does not include visuals. To elaborate, virtual interactions via the telephone, apart from the absence of visual indicators, result in clear, dynamic and connected service. Frontline employees can communicate warmth and politeness synchronously, being aided by pre-learnt scripts, such that the quality of the service relationship is not compromised due to its non-visual nature. Yet, how does the absence of visual cues affect employees' discernment of customer cyberbullying? Once customer abuse is detected, the employer must capitalize on available but not visual cues, feedback immediacy, personalized focus, naturalized language (Daft & Lengel, 1986), rehearsability and limited parallelisms (Dennis & Valacich, 1999) such that they maintain customer supremacy. Their response must be unequivocal and their social presence high and agreeable (Lippert, 2008). In other words, employees maintain the positive relational focus of the call in spite of having been bullied. It is important to understand what this means for employees' reaction to and coping with the experience. Moreover, whether the absence of visual cues at the customer's end makes a difference to employees' attempts to deal with their dif<sup>fi</sup>cult experiences needs to be looked into.

Call centre technology involves ACD (automatic call dialling) and PD (predictive dialling) systems. The ACD system automatically processes incoming telephone calls and distributes them to agents' headsets while simultaneously generating a constant stream of up to 200 sets of statistics about the activities it coordinates including call volume, duration, wrap-up times, waiting times and abandonment rates at the call centre, the team and the individual agent levels. Not only does the ACD system set the pace of work and monitor performance, but also managers and supervisors can view these statistics, as they are generated in real time, on their desktop computers, and can track each employee's activities throughout the day. Aggregate information from the ACD can also be made available to everyone in the call centre, via displays on large electronic display boards throughout the of<sup>fi</sup>ce. Often ACD systems are connected to one or more databases using computer telephony integration (CTI) software, which allows for customer records to appear on the agent's screen at the same time that the call comes through on the headset. In addition, some ACD systems also incorporate interactive voice response (IVR) technology that may be used to obtain preliminary customer information before a call is connected to an agent. Predictive dialling (PD) technology is used in outbound call centres to telephone large pre-programmed lists of customers. Predictive dialling involves programming a database of customers into a computer which then 'telephones' them. via multiple dialling, in a pre-determined order. It is generally used in conjunction with an ACD system which, when a potential customer answers a call, automatically transfers it to an agent. If the centre also has CTI, the customer's details will simultaneously appear on the agent's screen. If a number is engaged or rings a certain number of times without answer, the computer moves on to the next number. Call records are maintained and archived and can be accessed for quality checks and performance evaluations (adapted from McPhail, 2002).

The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of call centre technology on employees' experiences of customer cyberbullying warrants investigation. Does the adoption of such technology where ACD and PD systems drive work and calls are recorded and archived play a role in how employees' perceive and respond to customer cyberbullying?

Given that employees must maintain a pre-determined pace of work, their opportunity to recover from abusive calls is restricted. Employees may ruminate on their experiences while on the next call but such reprocessing cannot colour the service interaction. Recording and archiving imply that employees' reactions can be scrutinized and hence must defer to customer sovereignty. Employees must manage cues, feedback, language, focus and parallelisms to ensure a positive experience for the customer where equivocality is low and social presence is high and it is likely that rehearsed responses are instrumental in this case. At the same time, while employees' concomitant mental reprocessing of the negative experience cannot be overtly displayed, the call recording and archiving facility allows for a replay of the misbehaviour for ascertaining accuracy of events as well as providing insights for improvement.

It is widely known that offshoring is resorted to as a cost-cutting measure resulting in cross-cultural service interactions in spatially far-<sup>fl</sup>ung countries (Noronha & D'Cruz, 2009). Are physical distance and cultural difference factors here and, if so, how do they interface with the medium of communication? On the one hand, ICTDs have brought about boundarylessness in connecting people across the world, throwing up the issue of whether the sense of proximity afforded by the telephone affects employee experiences of cyberbullying. On the other hand, exploring if boundarylessness subsumes cultural integration such that diversity is celebrated and interdependence is appreciated and the fall-outs of this for customer cyberbullying cannot be ignored. In addition, the implications of offshoring and its spatial and social dynamics for employees' interface with media richness and synchronicity in the light of call centre technology calls for attention.

## 4. Method

This paper draws on a study whose aim was to explore employees' subjective experiences of work in international-facing call centres in Bangalore and Mumbai, India (See Note 1 for an overview of the sector). Keeping in mind the objective of the study, a phenomenological approach was considered to be appropriate, and Van Manen's (1998) hermeneutic phenomenology was adopted as the research strategy. Van Manen's (1998) hermeneutic phenomenology was seen as complementing the study objectives as it allowed for the exploration of the essential meaning of participants' experiences of work as they were lived.

Following Van Manen's (1998) approach, the conversational interview was used to gather experiential narrative material. Though the interview was unstructured, it was steered by the fundamental question that prompted the research and the basic issues it sought to uncover were speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in an interview guide. The queries contained in the guide included:

Describe your typical work day and work week.

What de<sup>fi</sup>nes your work?

What de<sup>fi</sup>nes you in relation to work?

Describe the gains and challenges you face at work, their underlying dynamics and your reaction to them.

How do you see your career over time?

How do you see the offshoring–outsourcing sector in the long run?

Yet this clarity did not preclude exploring issues that emerged during the interview, since the researchers were aware that such leads could generate important insights into the phenomenon under study. Informed consent, voluntary participation and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality marked the ethical protocol of the inquiry.

With organizations being unwilling to permit us access to their employees or the operations <sup>fl</sup>oor, we resorted to snowball sampling initiated via personal contacts with managers and unionists associated with the sector. Initial participants then put us in touch with others. With multiple sources of potential participants re<sup>fl</sup>ecting divergence, we endeavoured to guard against homogeneity within the sample, in addition to ensuring that the sample mirrored known sociodemographic trends in the sector (See D'Cruz & Noronha, 2010).

All interviews, held as per the convenience of the participant, were conducted in English and were audio-recorded with the permission of the participant. No participant objected to the use of the recorder once its advantage of accuracy was spelt out to them, and its presence did not appear to hinder their responses. During the interview, observations about the participants were made and written up after the session ended. Recorded data were later transcribed verbatim by the research staff.

Fifty nine agents, 25 from Bangalore and 34 from Mumbai, employed in a range of international-facing call centres participated in the study. Thirty nine worked in inbound processes, 12 in outbound processes and 8 in both inbound and outbound processes. All participants were employed at the entry level of the organization, having been with their current employer from 1 month to 3.5 years and in the ITES–BPO sector up to 6 years. While there were 29 women and 30 men whose ages ranged from 20 to 55 years, the largest number of participants was in the 22 to 25 years age group. Forty participants were unmarried and forty were graduates. The average monthly salary of participants was approximately Rs. (Indian Rupees) 12,900, based on a range of Rs. 8,000 to Rs. 25,000. All the participants served overseas clients and customers based in English-speaking countries, namely, the USA (United States of America/US), UK (United Kingdom), Canada and Australia. None of the participants were members of any unions. Participant sociodemographic characteristics were in keeping with sectoral features, as per D'Cruz and Noronha (2010). Tables 2 to 7 provide more details about the sample.

Due to the nature of the sampling technique adopted as well as the ethical protocol of the inquiry, we deferred to participants' desire for anonymity and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality as well as degree of ease and comfort and hence we did not collect data on participants' employer organization or client(s) in idiographic terms. Accordingly, apart from ascertaining that they worked for international-facing call centres serving English-speaking overseas clients and customers in the US, UK, Australia and Canada, we solicited employer-linked information only in terms of the process (inbound or outbound) and nature of organization (Indian MNC [multinational corporation] or foreign MNC) that they worked for. Further, readers may note participants' great satisfaction with the salaries they received from jobs in this sector. Using a conversion rate of Rs. (Indian rupee) 40 = US\$ (US dollar) 1.00, participants' average monthly income is equal to about US\$ 320.00. With the monthly expense for a middle class existence being approximately US\$ 280 in cities such as Bangalore and Mumbai, this salary was viewed positively especially because similar remuneration for those with equivalent quali<sup>fi</sup>cations was not available in any other sector in the Indian economy.

Thematic analyses, including sententious and selective approaches, following Van Manen (1998) were undertaken. Through the sententious thematic analysis (Step I), each transcript was read as a whole to capture the essence of participants' experiences, uncovering the core theme. Through the selective thematic analyses (Step II), each transcript was read repeatedly and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant statements relating to various dimensions of the core theme in terms of categories, patterns, sub-themes, themes and major themes were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. While “being professional” coalesced as the core theme (Section 5.1; Noronha & D'Cruz, 2009), “negotiating limits” was a major theme that highlighted participants' experiences of customer cyberbullying (Note 2; D'Cruz, 2013).

Elaborating on Step I, in terms of sententious thematic analyses, the holistic perusal of each participant's narrative showed the professional self as the overarching dynamic that undergirded the whole range of their complex work experiences. With “being professional” emerging from the entire contents of each transcript a capturing the fundamental meaning of participants' lived experiences at work, it made up the core theme.

Elaborating on Step II, in terms of the selective thematic analyses, after carefully perusing each participant's narratives to demarcate statements contributing to various dimensions of the core theme, categories/patterns were discerned and labelled such that terms were standardized across transcripts. Where categories/patterns could be combined, their labels were accordingly altered and standardized across transcripts. Within each transcript, categories/patterns were examined for their linkages. A comparison across transcripts was completed to highlight congruence in the patterns/categories and their inter-relationships across participants. Next, across transcripts, those categories/patterns that dovetailed together in meaningful yet distinct ways were developed into sub-themes. At the following stage, transcripts were compared to identify linkages across sub-themes and those sub-themes that held together were formed into themes.

A closer examination of the above sub-themes along with another emergent sub-theme labelled “eschewal of collectivization endeavours” which included patterns/categories such as “redundancy of unions”, “organizational commitment to employee well-being”, “in<sup>fl</sup>uence of extra-organizational business environment” and “inconsistency with professional identity” showed their linkages in terms of employee coping in emotion-focused rather than problem-focused strategies. Beyond Steps I and II, the attempt to establish the interface between ICTDs and agents' experiences of customer bullying involved four stages of further analysis of the aforementioned major theme of “negotiating limits” (Step III). First, to provide a contextual understanding, the backdrop of service work in virtual offshored situations was created, integrating call centre technology with media richness and media synchronicity theory. That is. from the data, participants' work organization, job design and performance parameters were explored not just from the point of view of offshoring and the ACD and PD systems but the reliance on the telephone was analysed using media richness and media synchronicity concepts. In this way. the particularities of the technology-mediated service work context were demonstrated. Second, participants' experiences of customer cyberbullying were divided into genesis, course and outcomes. These dimensions formed part of the analysis process, being identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by us as three aspects of participants' experiences evident in the data. It may be noted that participants themselves did not always divide their experiences likewise but often described them in a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed manner. Third, the role of technology in these three dimensions of customer cyberbullying was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by examining and selecting relevant sub-themes. Accordingly, technology was used as the lens through which the analysis was conducted to ensure that this focus was not obscured by other categories but remained at the forefront. The insights into participants' technology-mediated service work context whereby the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of call centre technology and the relevance of media richness and media synchronicity in telephonic communication, both within the realm of offshoring, were studied vis-à-vis the genesis, course and outcomes of cyberbullying to pinpoint inter-relationships. Fourth, for each dimension, its contributing sub-themes were written up into a coherent text which together provided in-depth and holistic perspectives into the linkage between ICTDs and customer cyberbullying. In so doing, it became apparent that some of the sub-themes of the genesis and course dimensions existed in tandem such that they jointly threw light on these aspects. Since their separation would prove redundant to acquiring a rich and complete understanding of participants' experiences, the genesis and course dimensions have been integrated and written up such that their speci<sup>fi</sup>cities and their linkages are retained.

## 5. Findings

Following an elaboration of service work in virtual offshored settings, three dimensions of the interface between ICTDs and customer cyberbullying, namely, genesis, course and outcomes, are presented in this section. Participants' quotes are included as illustration.

## 5.1. Service work in virtual offshored contexts

International-facing call centres form an important component of the global offshoring landscape where jobs are migrated from developed to developing countries for cost savings purposes. Relying on ICTDs linked to ACD and PD systems, international-facing call centres allow for disembedded, voice-based, cyber service interactions between geographically dispersed parties. Seeking to maintain continuity in service delivery in line with the ideology of customer sovereignty alongside their <sup>fi</sup>nancial goals, international facing call centres adopt a mass-customized model (Batt & Moynihan, 2002; Frenkel, Korczynski, Shire, & Tam, 1998) as they operate inbound and outbound processes for overseas clients and customers. To this end, service level agreements (SLAs) between service providers and clients draw on call centre technology and qualitative and quantitative performance parameters such that the consequent technobureaucratic controls (Callaghan & Thompson, 2001) which incorporate a strong customer focus de<sup>fi</sup>ne the functioning of the call centre and are translated into employee job speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Hence, simultaneous with outcomes such as process-linked targets, maintenance of prescribed procedures and accuracy of documentation, average handling time (AHT) of the call, call wrap-up time, call waiting time, call abandonment rates and call opening and closing, customer interaction measures linked to sensitivity, politeness, warmth, accuracy of communication, understanding and addressing customer needs, correctness of information provided and handling abusive customers are instituted, setting the work context and employee task requirements. Call centre technology not only sets the pace of work but also allows for monitoring and evaluation of performance through call barging in real time and through records and archives, serving as the enabling foundation here. Sidejacking as well as customer feedback complete the measurement process. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting Van Jaarsveld and Poster (2013), that ICTDs in call centres are relied upon to direct work<sup>fl</sup>ow and workpace as well as in<sup>fl</sup>uence workforce management, often resulting in a high pressure environment in which ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness are jointly valued, cannot be overlooked.

Given SLA speci<sup>fi</sup>cations in the light of the vocal-linked offshored virtual service context, clients and employers recognize that effective communication with a view to customer satisfaction and retention forms the cornerstone of organizational success. Accordingly. strategies and tactics to ensure communication congruence and decrease noise (Lippert, 2008) in service interactions in spite of the cross-cultural and spatial divide are engaged. Customer interests are not compromised and client and employer goals are met in two ways. First, call centre technology allows for high levels of media richness and synchronicity (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Dennis & Valacich, 1999) despite the exclusion of face-to-face contact and visual cues. Second, the capitalization on audio cues and the availability of controls through the ACD and PD systems as well as the inclusion of national identity management/NIM (Poster, 2007) and scripts are undertaken to ensure service quality. Indeed, clients and employers bank on these mechanisms so that customers feel comfortable and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their service experience and remain loyal to the service provider. In this way, seamlessness in service delivery is sought to be maintained.

The feedback immediacy, personalized focus, cue capacity and naturalised language that telephones offer by way of media richness (Daft & Lengel, 1986) along with the use of scripts contributed to the call's low equivocality and high social presence, promoting task performance and customer service. Scripts, while encompassing all dimensions of emotional labour as well as nuances of process-linked requirements, ensure clarity between agent and customer and standardization across agents. Similarly, NIM, covering accent neutralization, adoption of pseudonyms and locational masking, is described as aesthetic labour relied upon to disguise the stigmatized self (Nath, 2011). NIM, whose adoption is aided by the auditory-linked, rather than visual-based, nature of call centre technology, attempts to bridge cultural and spatial divides by presenting agents as similar to customers as well as to facilitate employee task performance by decreasing inhibition and increasing dissociation (Flanagin, Pearce, & Bondad-Brown, 2009). In this way, both process ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and relationship building are equally emphasised.

Telephones enable communication performance in offshored call centres because media synchronicity is appropriately handled to suit the requirements of the situation (Dennis & Valacich, 1999). Apart from transmission velocity and symbol variety from audio cues, the use of scripts and rehearsals and the control of spontaneity and parallelisms facilitates task performance, emotional labour and customer satisfaction to allow for increased conveyance and effective convergence in a virtual transnational context. Call centre technology engenders reprocessability through which learning (apart from quality checks, performance evaluation, customer satisfaction and redressal) can occur.

Socioideological controls, incorporating employee identity regulation (Alvesson & Karreman, 2004) through which the notion of professionalism de<sup>fi</sup>nes employees' sense of self and ensures their compliance with client and employer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, complement the aforementioned communication effectiveness endeavour as optimal service delivery in the pursuit of competitive advantage is sought. That is, through induction training, on-going socialization, performance parameters, and other elements of organizational design, employer organizations inculcate their employees with a professional identity. As a result, the notion of professionalism embraced agents' identity, altering their self-concept and enhancing their self-esteem. Seeing themselves as professionals, agents stated that they possessed superior cognitive abilities, advanced quali<sup>fi</sup>cations and a sense of responsibility and commitment to work. In keeping with this identity, they prioritise work over personal needs and pleasure, comply with job and organizational requirements and perform optimally and rationally on assigned tasks, always behaving in a digni<sup>fi</sup>ed and restrained manner. Under such circumstances, not only do agents perceive job-related gains such as remuneration, designation, material artefacts, etc., accruing from their job as consistent with the notion of professionalism but also transactional psychological contracts of employment as means of discipline are similarly justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Indeed, agents' professional identity precludes engagement with collectivization attempts which are considered both as inconsistent with the essential features of professionalism and as redundant in instances where employers protect employee interests.

## 5.2. The genesis and course of customer cyberbullying

Notwithstanding the concerted efforts to keep customer interests at the forefront of business within offshored virtual service contexts where competitive advantage is the primary driver, customer bullying was displayed. Customers' negative behaviour towards agents was manifested through sarcastic and/or caustic comments. raised voices and use of expletives, moving on a continuum from impolite and rude to threatening and intimidating. Such behaviour was person-related, attacking the agent in terms of various aspects of his/her self. Participants observed that such claims were made even though there was no acquaintance between themselves and customers. Refusals to interact and cutting off calls abruptly also formed part of customer misbehaviour. Besides, whereas customer interactions were all one-off time-bound encounters in relation to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c customer, participants reported being subjected to numerous such abusive calls during a day or a week. In their view, there was a cumulative effect of such repetitions particularly if they occurred on a daily basis. Additionally, customer behavioural turnaround during a call was alluded to. That is, calls starting positively could end negatively, calls starting negatively could end positively and calls could move from positive to negative back to positive or from negative to positive to negative.

Various factors trigger customer bullying. Macroeconomic factors, a sense of being deceived, perception of intrusion, call duration and pace, reliance on scripts, call waiting time and personal orientation and communication incongruence and ineffectiveness are relevant here.

Despite clients' and employers' best attempts to ensure customer satisfaction maximizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts offered by the telephone as a medium and by call centre technology, widespread knowledge about and/or auditory detection of offshoring and/or identity concealment during calls precipitated customer displeasure and resulted in abuse, which usually carried racist sentiments.

Based on guesses about and/or con<sup>fi</sup>rmations of participants' sociodemographic details, customers' ire arose due to the loss of jobs and rising unemployment in the West and a sense of having been deceived by the NIM endeavour. In this manner, the biases customers harboured against developing countries which they perceived as inferior, parasitic, incompetent and untrustworthy, were re<sup>fl</sup>ected. As a reaction to their suspicion/detection of participants' antecedents, it was not uncommon for customers to cut off the call either immediately and abruptly along with a couple of rude comments or after putting participants through an intimidating monological tirade.

They (customers) know that calls are routed here, this is public knowledge. Then sometimes, they can pick up slips in our accents. Even one sound can go slightly wrong and we get it (the brunt of their ire). Or maybe (they) read about this (accent) neutralization somewhere. So they just take out their anger, “Why should I trust a terrorist country?” “You are a liar, why should I trust you?” They will scream and yell, some will cut off the call. You cannot do anything…just listen.

Participants considered customers' aggression to be misdirected towards them because they were responsible for neither global offshoring nor identity concealment. Highlighting the misattribution, participants acknowledged the role of their frontline position in <sup>fi</sup>elding customer anger. Participants pointed out that client and employer attempts to gain customer trust back<sup>fi</sup>red and deepened the latter's suspicions and prejudices, giving rise to abuse. Indeed, the technobureaucratic controls in place were not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to assure customers of foolproof and dependable service.

The disruption of customer schedules as the underlying factor for bullying behaviour was reported by agents in outbound processes. The predictive dialling system in use here contacted those on its list such that the calls were not anticipated and planned for by customers. Feeling involuntarily intruded upon, it was not uncommon for the interruption to trigger abusive behaviours in some customers. Being suddenly disturbed angered several customers. In instances where collections were involved, customers' negative reaction was exacerbated. According to agents, customers felt upset that <sup>fi</sup>nancial payments were being followed up on and displaced their distress on to agents.

So what happens (in outbound processes), sometimes when we call, the customer is busy and he will get very angry that we have disturbed him. So we have to hear from him…Then if we are making credit card payments, (he will show) more fury.

Having to manage call handling time requirements while meeting other quantitative and qualitative parameters and following the prescribed text during customer interactions left participants with little time and <sup>fl</sup>exibility. That the call proceeded in a hurried and rigid manner, regardless of all other attempts to cater to customer interests, displeased some customers who explicitly indicated their dissatisfaction in abusive ways.

Call waiting time was a source of bullying in inbound processes. Depending on call volumes, customers calling in various services could have to wait their turn in the call queue before being attended to by an agent. Being thus delayed in receiving the desired service led to aggressive behaviour being displayed to the agent who <sup>fi</sup>nally handled the call.

In these foregoing cases, customer's messages of and prompts for bullying come through clearly, being vivid, obvious and direct. Comprising verbal statements, they are accompanied by consistent emotional cues that are communicated non-verbally. Though visual cues were unavailable to participants, the clarity of customers' verbal and non-verbal audio cues were suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to facilitate participants' accurate interpretation of customer behaviour. Thus, by hearing the content, tone, volume, in<sup>fl</sup>exion, pace and quality of the customer's communication, agents knew that they were being subjected to abuse. Customers' harsh words, rude phrases and, sometimes, expletives, combined with curt, cold and abrupt in<sup>fl</sup>exions, raised voices and hurried and unceasing delivery provided participants with precise insights.

In other instances where either the bullying and/or the underlying cause were more covert, familiarity was critical in ensuring correctness in participants' explanation of customer behaviour. Familiarity with the medium, arising from work experience linked to two factors, contributed to participants' understanding. First. the long-term reliance on the telephone as the main artefact for task execution such that the overt and the subtle aspects of communication across a range of audio cues such as tone, volume, in<sup>fl</sup>exion, pace and quality, are mastered. Second, conversance with the message topic, the nature and trajectory of the interaction, the organizational and business context and the joint sensemaking of the work group (Carlson & Zmud, 1999) such that accuracy emerges. Apart from richness, experience with media capability across a range of situations, involving channel expansion (Carlson & Zmud, 1999), is relevant.

Accordingly, participants described being able to discern customer dispositions and moods. That is, through vocal indicators pervading the service interaction, participants could gauge the friendliness versus hostility and the positivity and negativity of the customer and hence could make appropriate attributions about customer misbehaviour. In their view, more sociable and optimistic customers were also more tolerant and patient than more introverted and pessimistic customers whose cut-and-dry approach laid the ground work for abuse. Not only did the latter customers prefer quick service but they were also less trusting.

See, I have experience with this job since 2 years, so I can make out the customer also. If he is sad, he will be quiet and can get angry also if he feels the call is too long or too slow. Sometimes, we can get a happy customer who will want to keep chatting. But we have the AHT (average handling time of the call) at the back of our mind. So we have to hurry them up and sometimes they feel it (to be) rude. Picking up the mood from the phone…you catch it with time.

Similarly, customers whose encoding and decoding abilities promoted clarity of communication were referred to by participants as less likely to bully in contrast to customers who did not always understand agents or successfully express themselves. Participants alluded to being able to assess this from the manner in which calls proceeded. Customers who either comprehended or spoke clearly in terms of asking, responding and querying posed little threat. Customers whose confusion was apparent in either receiving and/ or sending out information ended up misbehaving as their frustration got the better of them. Encountering dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in following agents' messages and putting forth their own views, these customers resorted to abuse to let off the steam arising from communication incongruence and ineffectiveness.

That personal orientation and communication performance as elaborated upon above also activated racist biases in some customers whose misbehaviour then acquired an additional ethnic angle, was reported

In other rare cases, customers under the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of alcohol or drugs or appearing to suffer from psychiatric disturbances were described.

In participants' view, the invisibility and partial anonymity of the interaction, aided by its one-time and perceived one-on-one occurrence, lowered customer inhibitions though the concreteness, permanence and reprocessability of the call due to the archiving process posed little deterrence.

That customers neither could see participants (and their reactions) nor knew them (though customer identity was available prior to or during the call) brought in an element of personal and social dissociation that diluted customers' regard for politeness and restraint about incivility. Participants opined that while embedded interactions may have curbed such behaviour due to the accompanying interpersonal bonding customers' knowledge that participants' colleagues and superiors and even clients could witness the call may impose limits on their misdeeds owing to the effects of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. Notwithstanding being thus uninformed, even the fact that their calls were being recorded and provided proof of their negative actions appeared to be irrelevant in setting the appropriate direction and tone for them.

My team mates and I, we discuss that if they could see us, like how hard we work, how we are like them only, then maybe they will not abuse so much. Right now, they don't know us. Maybe also, they don't care. Because they are customers and they are foreigners, so they can get away. Others (quality analysts, supervisors, clients and even team mates) can listen, call is recorded…but (customers have) no issues.

## 5.3. The outcomes of customer cyberbullying

Participants highlighted feeling upset, sad, hurt, taken aback, shocked, disturbed, angry and irritated by their experiences of customer cyberbullying. In their view, they were being unfairly and unjustly treated despite the lack of provocation. Considering customer bullying to be unethical and wrong, agents grappled with why such behaviour should be directed towards them without their having provided any cause for the same. Contingent upon their personalities and moods, the nature of the misbehaviour and/or the length of their tenure, the degree of distress varied but was never completely obliterated. Instances of both consequent somatic ailments and prolonged emotional disturbance (particularly the latter) were shared.

Agents who were more hardy and/or more sociable by nature reported being less upset compared to their softer and/or more introverted colleagues. Being able to absorb or bounce back as well as having interacted with a variety of people accounted for this difference.

Mood further contributed to the experience of distress. Whereas a pile-up of work and personal demands (including work-life balance issues) in<sup>fl</sup>uenced agents' moods, somatic issues arising from disrupted circadian rhythms, altered due to shift-based employment, also determined agents' physical and mental state. On both these counts, agents' moods affected their emotional reaction to customer bullying.

Sometimes, when I was raw in particular (during the initial part of the tenure), I used to long for a break after the bullying type call. But what to do? The call queue, the monitoring board, the targets…no time, only numbers, only tracking. That time, getting used to the night shift, long hours…but then one gets used to it and move(s) on to the next call

Unbridled roughness and aggression evoked extreme distress, with participants being aghast at the extent of customer bullying. According to those who had encountered such customers, the behaviours displayed were beyond the commonly experienced levels of abuse and hence even more odd and disturbing.

For most participants, race-linked bullying was too much to bear as citizenship constituted a critical de<sup>fi</sup>ning dimension of the self, bringing into play national identity centrality (Das, Dharwadkar, & Brandes, 2008). Participants spoke of feeling extremely slighted when their nationality and culture were doubted, ridiculed and insulted. A surge of patriotic sentiments resulted.

Yet notwithstanding their reaction to the experience, customer sovereignty, stringently espoused by clients and employers and linked to performance parameters, dictated their overt and direct response during the call, aided not just by customer feedback in some processes but more by call recording and archiving, if not by call barging and side-jacking. In addition to intra-organizational qualitative and quantitative appraisal measures referred to earlier, customer feedback formed a component of some processes though it could operate on a random basis with only a few customers' views being solicited either by email or phone calls. Apart from quality and evaluation checks of archived calls, listening in on live calls either remotely or proximately was resorted to. Both employers and clients undertook such checks, sometimes going to the extent of posing as customers to ascertain agent behaviour on calls and in response to abuse. Technobureaucratic controls, where surveillance and output indicators combined, ensured that agents complied with client and employer requirements. Failure to meet customer sovereignty and performance parameters resulted in warnings and/or dismissals.

In call centres, everything is recorded. Quality analysts, managers, even clients, can listen to the calls…(they) pose as customer also. Any call is taken for evaluation purpose. So giving back to the customers will not help at all. (In) some processes, customer feedback is taken randomly and so we have to be careful. Because then the customer can mark you down. Customer is on top always, is the guiding force.

Consequently, agents never communicated any obvious negative reaction to customers. Keeping their adverse thoughts and feelings to themselves, and in some instances, curbing their desire to retaliate, agents handled the situation with patience and tact, apologizing to and appeasing customers while attempting to steer the call back on track. Calls were either escalated to the TL (team leader) if they proceeded beyond the agent's brief or capacity or were ended if the customer wished to hang up.

Being thus circumscribed, emotion-focused strategies predominated agents' coping, being coloured by two interesting dynamics. On the one hand, in spite of their desire to do so, participants never reacted to or retaliated with customers but complied with client and employer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, seeing their own long-term economic and career interests in such an approach. On the other hand, technology interfaced with and influenced participants' handling of the situation, bevond the aforementioned issues of customer feedback and recording, archiving, side-jacking and call barging.

Despite being forewarned about customer bullying and being exposed to it through listening to others' recordings and participating in mock calls during their induction training, the actual experience of such misbehaviour caught participants' off-guard.

During their initial exposure, participants usually needed time-out from calls as well as support from colleagues and superiors. Being taken off calls for part of the shift, escalating the call to the TL and communicating non-verbally with team mates on the call <sup>fl</sup>oor were relied upon. As participants progressed with their tenure, they got accustomed to expecting and undergoing customer abuse due to repeated encounters but this dulled neither their distress nor their sense of being wronged. Participants reported no choice but to go through with the situation in keeping with organizational demands, if they wished for job-related continuity. Not only did client and employer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations privilege customer sovereignty but also the mass-customised model actioned through the ACD and PD systems of call centre technology meant having to ful<sup>fi</sup>l requisite quantitative and qualitative parameters with lag period for a separate recovery process. Engaging various strategies to address the challenge of customer bullying were described.

Whereas some participants referred to their traits such as self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and toughness and drew on inner strength, others framed such experiences to be part of their jobs and displayed resigned acceptance. Laughing off their experiences by making fun of themselves and/or of customers was spoken of. Adopting an air of condescension towards customers and their societies for being prejudiced, ill-mannered and ignorant or a stand of empathy towards customers' pre-occupations neutralized participants' distress.

We have to manage. We are professionals in a good job. So keep it that way. Don't take it personally. Anyway, the Westerners do not know much about outside their country, so just overlook. My team mate from (the) other process says that anyway, I am \_\_\_\_ (pseudonym), not \_\_\_\_ (real name), so customer is not harassing me.

The virtual nature of the service interaction within the offshored context helped participants compartmentalise their experiences by seeing it as a role to be performed or by distancing their self from the task. The perception that customers were “faraway” and “did not really know them” (especially due to the NIM strategy) showed how physical and emotional distance brought in detachment which reduced the “sting” of customer bullying.

Non-verbal support from colleagues continued while call escalations and time-out were resorted to in exceptional circumstances as participants knew that these avenues were inconsistent with performance speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and would adversely affect their evaluations and careers.

A few participants took advantage of call centre technology to engage in “reverse customer abuse” (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2013a). Manifested in two forms over live calls, reverse customer abuse represented routine resistance (Prasad & Prasad, 1998) which was not just cathartic for its initiator but also promoted peer/team bonding in the work group. Apart from providing a break and allowing for a sense of support, it facilitated collegial coping. In form 1, when agents placed the phone in mute mode and loudly cursed the abusive customer, team members would respond to this either (a) non-verbally from their work stations if they were busy on calls, (b) verbally and non-verbally from their work stations if they were between calls, and (c) verbally and non-verbally by coming to the agents' work station if they were between calls. In form 2, when agents pressed the mute button and enabled the loudspeaker such that the customer was publicly audible on the call <sup>fl</sup>oor, their team members jointly listened to, jeered at and enjoyed the tirade in real time. Team members who were between calls would come to the agent's work station to participate while those who were busy with calls would participate non-verbally from their work stations.

Sometimes one gets tired of so much work and then, on top of it, a customer starts abusing. So what some people do—they put the customer on mute and curse him back. There he is thinking that we are listening to the scolding but actually we are giving it back to him.

Though such reverse customer abuse was not formally sanctioned and could be punished as going as against organizational requirements, superiors usually turned a blind eye to it if they observed it taking place, occasionally gently reprimanding agents for their action. Rarely, TLs who shared good relations with team members brie<sup>fl</sup>y joined in, before getting agents to stop and resume work. If needed, TLs and other superiors would then proceed to help the agent to feel less upset about the call.

Reverse customer abuse, which resembled Korczynski's (2003) communities of coping and re<sup>fl</sup>ected routine resistance was described by participants as a temporary outlet resorted to occasionally by some agents as a breather to provide a break from and release job-related pressure. Coexisting with professional identity, it was not indicative of anti-work and anti-employer sentiment and did not catalyse co-employee mobilization or collectivization endeavours.

With the ACD and PD technologies leaving agents' with no special undivided time to mentally reprocess their negative experiences, they could relive them concomitant with the next call or during breaks or after the shift. While distortions linked to overload in the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance and faulty recall in all three instances could arise, such ruminative behaviour either contributed to or detracted from agents' coping. Agents whose reprocessing emphasized situational attributions and areas for improvement gained whereas agents whose reprocessing led to dispositional attributions lost out. This does not discount the fact that there were people who did not engage in reprocessing because of the coping choices they made. At the same time, that call centre technology facilitated the maintenance of records and archives promoted reprocessability. Such data could be used by agents to learn from their experiences, apart from the routine quality checks and performance evaluations undertaken by the emplover and sometimes by the client

## 6. Discussion

Customer bullying has been seen to arise in service contexts due to the espousal and entrenchment of the ideology of customer sovereignty. While most evidence supporting this argument comes from face-to-face physical interactions in the real world, that such misbehaviour occurs in virtual service encounters enacted in cyberspace as already reported by Van Jaarsveld and Poster (2013) and further evidenced in the present paper comes as no surprise. What remains to be understood here is the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of technology on customer cyberbullying. This paper addresses the gap through a focus on employee experiences of customer cyberbullying in offshored call centres, drawing on a phenomenological inquiry conducted in India. Theorizing from the study, we highlight how the use of telephonic communication in call centres affects employees' discernment of, attributions about and coping with customer bullying, linking our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to media richness, media synchronicity, channel expansion and call centre technology.

The limitations on cue capacity due to the absence of visual indicators do not impede employees' accurate interpretation of their negative experiences. Apart from the compensatory effects of vocal cues, familiarity of experience linked to channel expansion assists their endeavour. Employees are able to correctly identify customer bullying through auditory features over time. Clearly, issues of equivocality and social presence apply not just to the customer but have implications for employees. That is, it is not only customers who must enjoy clarity and connectedness but the feel of lucidity and propinquity come through to employees as well and, that too, in a negative manner.

Further, the lack of access to visual cues is believed to exacerbate customer misbehaviour due to restrictions on feedback immediacy. That is, customers are unable to observe the effects of their negative actions on employees, and since the latter must defer to the ideology of customer sovereignty, they cannot provide an indication of this through the available alternatives cues. The feedback loop is thus partial due to limited symbol variety. Yet, it is this very absence of visual cues which assists emplovees' individual and joint coping. Since customers cannot see employees, the latter can rely on non-verbal behaviour to vent their reactions on the call <sup>fl</sup>oor, seek support from colleagues and superiors and engage in ‘reverse customer abuse’ while continuing with the call.

The non-verbal communication relied upon in offshored call centres sets the stage for NIM but cannot negate the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of auditory cues through which the attempt to enact aesthetic labour is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by customers. That is, clients and employers in offshored business contexts make employees adopt pseudonyms, locational masking and accent neutralization to provide a seamless transition in customer service provision when it moves from developed to developing countries. But the combination of various vocal cues sent out by employees and hearing capacity possessed by customers highlights the sharpness of the telephonic medium despite restricted cue capacity on the one hand and sets the stage for aversive racism (Gaertner & Dovidio, 1986) due to political economy, sociocultural and trust issues on the other hand. At the same time, NIM facilitated by the absence of visual cues allows employees to conceal their true identity and adopt another persona, adding to their sense that customer bullying is not directed at them but at their pseudoself. The consequent sense of detachment helps them to cope better.

In line with earlier literature that speaks of the invisibility and anonymity of cyberbehaviour (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2013b), limited cue capacity promotes a sense of dissociation in customers which lowers their inhibitions and triggers misbehaviour. That is, though customers' identity and contact details are generally known to employees before/during the call, restrictions on the scope of observation due to the mode of communication lead customers to greater degrees of detachment and lower levels of propriety which, along with their sense of customer sovereignty, give rise to bullying behaviour.

The ICTDs engaged by call centres allow for the transcendence of time and space boundaries. Boundarylessness, while giving rise to connectedness, simultaneously maintains distance which enables employee coping. That is, the mediation of the service interaction via technology while bringing geographical and culturally diverse individuals together cannot obliterate the actual gap between them. Employees experience the customer as distant and hence less menacing. Boundarylessness implies temporal continuity which is particularly relevant in outbound processes since the PD facility results in intrusions into customers' lives and the consequent unleashing of abuse.

The ACD and PD systems of call centre technology, in setting the pace of work, affect employee experiences of customer cyberbullying in two ways. On the one hand, meeting the AHT requirements means maintaining the momentum of the call, which if interpreted as hurrying and so disregarding the customer, can lead to abuse. On the other hand, there is no in-built opportunity for employees to recover from dif<sup>fi</sup>cult service interactions as the call queue must be attended to. Reprieves from this high pressure environment come through specially requested and granted breaks. Either way, technobureaucratic controls integral to call centres often result in stringent working environments where depersonalized bullying (D'Cruz & Noronha,

2009) predominates such that the technology-mediated communication aids the emergence of but hampers coping with customer bullying. It may be pertinent to highlight that call centre technology is controlled by employers to achieve organizational effectiveness. This means that employers can programme the ACD and PD systems to set the pace of work in order to ensure <sup>fi</sup>nancial gains—a situation that is even more pronounced in the case of offshoring where competitive advantage in the acquisition and renewal of clients is the sole driver of business (Noronha & D'Cruz, 2009).

Reprocessability as a feature of call centres, owing to the recording and archiving technology which results in the concreteness and permanence of service interactions, works as a double-edged sword. Employees' opportunity for direct action and retaliation is constrained as such problem-focused coping and resistance strategies can be tracked and taken up as disciplinary matters. Clients, employers and employees get a chance to review the service interaction and to identify areas for improvement at the organizational, process and individual levels. In this way, gaps in the organizational strategy of customer service and employee training, anomalies in process implementation in terms of scripts, speed and measures as well as errors in employee learning can be tackled.

As alluded to earlier, the concreteness and permanence associated with particular types of cyber interactions (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2013b) are also true call centres, due to the automatic maintenance of records and archives that can be accessed and replayed. Yet, while the availability of such evidence is effectively built into technobureaucratic controls that determine employee behaviour, such inhibitory effects are not apparent in the case of customers. It cannot be assumed that customers are unaware of these mechanisms but the rei<sup>fi</sup>cation of customer sovereignty appears to precipitate a disdain for proof and an air of self-absolution.

Overall, theorizing from the study <sup>fi</sup>ndings in terms of media richness, media synchronicity, channel expansion and call centre technology, we emphasize that while service interactions through call centres cater to the interests of customers and employers (and clients in case of offshoring), they do not eliminate customer bullying, though they can provide alternative means to employee coping unavailable to employees in real service settings due to the face-to-face and direct nature of communication

## 7. Conclusion

Various possibilities for action emerge from the inquiry. These recommendations are proposed within the realm of telephonic service delivery through call centres keeping in mind organizations' preference to ensure customer sovereignty while cutting costs yet retaining a rich media.

Eliminating customer cyberbullying through the adoption of zero tolerance policies is an important option for organizations to consider. Given that workplace bullying is unethical behaviour that goes against universal norms of social acceptability (LaVan & Martin. 2008: Ramsay, Troth, & Branch, 2011) and that most organizations adopt codes of conduct which address internal abuse, extending this position to external stakeholders is the next logical step. Indeed, such an approach does not impinge on customer sovereignty but protects employee well-being recognizing that bullying is unreasonable behaviour. Employees continue to defer to customers as long as their behaviour is within appropriate limits but pull stops when their own dignity is violated. Such tactics have already been adopted successfully in numerous airlines and retail organizations in the West (George Abraham, Indian trade unionist, personal communication, December 2013). Depending on the particular process involved, customers can be informed about the service provider's anti-bullying stand and about call recording and archiving, alluding to the issue of proof, either at the beginning of or during the call through IVR messages or by agents themselves.

Relying on EDS (emotion detection systems) where wave frequencies in a person's voice are analysed to detect a wide range of human emotions is another viable alternative (Van Jaarsveld & Poster, 2013). In real time, employees as well as supervisors, managers and quality analysts listening to the call could track the onset of customer abuse and make requisite interventions. Employees could solicit their superior's support while supervisors, managers and quality analysts could initiate processes whereby they handle the call. In retrospect, analyses of calls could assist both employers and employees. Employees could be sensitized to in<sup>fl</sup>exion points when customer bullying seems imminent and armed with mechanisms to pre-empt its further progress. Employers could identify triggers for and symptoms of customer misbehaviour and address these through changes in call processes, modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in employee behaviour and incorporation of various forms of assistance to suit the nature and stage of the problem.

Altering the pace of work would be helpful in two ways. Increasing the AHT of a call would allow for a less hurried and more meaningful interaction that keeps customers more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed and hence less prone to bullying behaviours. Making allowances in employees' expected targets such that they have the chance to take a break after severely abusive calls would not only be a sensitive aid to their recovery but also prevent their re<sup>fl</sup>ections on and replaying of the negative experience from interfering with subsequent calls. Support should also be provided to employees during the break to assist their coping.

Capitalizing on the reprocessability afforded by call centre technology, employers and employees can study both negative and positive calls with a view to improving organizational and call processes as well as employee behaviour. Analyzing both types of service interactions would highlight the strengths of good calls and the weaknesses of dif<sup>fi</sup>cult calls as in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by employer speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, employee capability and customer characteristics. In this way, the gains of the former can be harnessed and the disadvantages of the latter can be minimized. Insights from positive calls can be transferred to the better management of negative calls.

Looking after employee interests in the foregoing manner, organizations can hope for employee reciprocity to such perceived organizational support/POS (Eisenberger, Huntington, Hutchison, & Sowa, 1986) in the form of enhanced organizational commitment (Meyer & Allen, 1991). Improvements in employee performance and retention at the individual level accumulate as consolidated positive outcomes at the organizational level, strengthening competitive advantage.

The availability of quality talent at cost effective rates, emphasis on optimal cost ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and client-centricity, creation of a supportive ecosystem and maintenance of a scalable and secure environment form central pillars of India's offshoring value proposition (NASSCOM, 2013). Though higher-end services and knowledge process outsourcing (KPO) are part of the Indian ITES–BPO industry, the main focus remains lower-end services embodying the mass-customized model (Noronha & D'Cruz, 2012). India's ITES–BPO workforce was calculated at 917,000 for international-facing jobs and 640,000 for domestic jobs (in the latter case, the <sup>fi</sup>gure includes IT and ITES– BPO jobs) in 2012–2013 (NASSCOM, 2013), the industry having become a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant avenue for employment especially for the country's youth (NASSCOM, 2013). Despite ITES–BPO employees being covered by several labour laws as promulgated in various Indian states as well as central legislations, the popular notion held in Indian society (and espoused and promoted by ITES–BPO employers, aided by government apathy) is that Indian labour legislation and related institutional measures do not apply here (D'Cruz & Noronha, 2010). On the contrary, the image of the workforce in this sector is that of white-collared professionals (Noronha & D'Cruz, 2009). As Taylor and Bain (2005) assert, India remains attractive to companies who wish to capitalize on the possibilities for <sup>fl</sup>exible labour utilization and the absence of trade unions in the Indian ITES–BPO industry facilitates this.
