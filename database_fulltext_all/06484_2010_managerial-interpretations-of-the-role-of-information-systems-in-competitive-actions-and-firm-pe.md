## 1. Introduction

Academics and practitioners have long sought to understand the relationships between investments in information systems (IS), competitive advantage, and firm performance (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998, Chi et al. 2007, Malone 1997). Assuming that competitive advantage is difficult to achieve through the acquisition of IS alone (Carr 2003, Wade and Hulland 2004), many IS researchers have examined IS competitive advantage, and firm performance using such theoretical foundations as the resource-based view of the firm (Barney 1991, Bharadwaj 2000, Sambamurthy et al. 2003, Pavlou and El Sawy 2006) and the knowledgebased view of the firm (Alavi and Leidner 2001, Grant

1996, Kearns and Sabherwal 2007) and by evaluating the fit between a firm’s information processing needs and its information processing capabilities (Gattiker and Goodhue 2004, Premkumar et al. 2005).

Recently, competitive dynamics—a fine-grained and dynamic view of strategy that focuses on competitive actions and competitor responses—has been advocated in the strategic management literature (Chen 1996, Ferrier 2001, Smith et al. 1989). This perspective follows Porter’s (1980) characterization of strategy as the undertaking of competitive actions and responses to achieve competitive advantage and improve relative firm performance. Several definitions of competitive action/response exist in the literature. Consistent with Shumpeter’s (1934) theory of “creative destruction,” which describes interfirm rivalry as a “    race to get or to keep ahead of one another” Kirzner (1973, p. 20), Smith et al. (1991) have defined competitive action as “a specific and detectable competitive move, such as a price cut or new product introduction, initiated by a firm to defend or improve its relative competitive position” (p. 61) and competitive response as “a clear-cut and discernable counteraction taken by a competing firm with regard to one or more competitors to defend or improve its position” (p. 61). Additionally, competitive dynamics research has identified three primary drivers of interfirm rivalry: awareness of the competitive environment, motivation to act or respond, and a firm’s capability to act or respond (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007).

There have been recent calls for research that examines the role of IS in the context of competitive dynamics. “Adopting a competitive dynamics perspective could enhance understanding of the impact of digital systems on competition” (Smith et al. 2001, as cited by Ferrier et al. 2007, p. 228). To date, few studies have investigated the role of IS in the context of competitive dynamics. In particular, the answer to how IS contribute to the specific competitive actions/responses that impact firm performance remains unclear.

It has been suggested that the IS-firm performance relationship is so complex that the answer may well hinge on microexaminations of practices and procedures within certain companies (Fairbank et al. 2006). Considering that organizations are socially constructed systems of shared meaning (Berger and Luckman 1966, Burrell and Morgan 1979, Weick 1979), interpretive sociology provides a foundation for such examinations. Additionally, understanding the factors that shape how top managers interpret their strategic environment is critically important, because their interpretations ultimately affect organizational actions (Dutton et al. 1983). Most empirical studies investigating IS and firm performance have relied on cross-sectional quantitative data and variance models (Orlikowski 1993) that have yielded mixed results (Fairbank et al. 2006). Furthermore, many IS-firm performance studies have primarily focused on discrete outcomes, neglecting the intentions and interpretations of key players in the process by which competitive actions or responses are conceived, enacted, and executed, as well as the organizational context in which such actions occur. This is a significant omission, given the suggestion that “    human and social biases, filters, and idiosyncratic processes at the top of the organization substantially influence competitive behaviors” (Hambrick et al. 1996, p. 660). Equivocal findings in extant research along with continued investments by firms in IS drive the need for a deeper understanding of the IS-firm performance relationship.

This study is unique in its integration of IS and competitive dynamics research and in its interpretive sociological approach (Berger and Luckmann 1966, Burrell and Morgan 1979, Weick 1979). “Conventional strategic management research has come under increasing criticism partly because this research starts from a positivist, rationalistic model of the strategic planning process” (Smircich and Stubbart 1985, p. 733). Similarly, it has been pointed out that IS research often ignores the “   contextual and process aspects as well as interpretations of key players   ” as these elements are “    often omitted in IS studies that rely on variance models and cross-sectional quantitative data” (Orlikowski 1993, p. 310). Our utilization of the interpretive sociological approach addresses some of the limitations of cross-sectional studies by exploring what managers were thinking, why they acted as they did, and what they wanted to accomplish within the organizational context.

We have conducted a field investigation using the grounded theory research method, as this approach allows a focus on the intentions and actions of key players in the context of IS, competitive dynamics, and firm performance. We asked the following research question: How do managers interpret the role of IS in the process of conceiving, enacting, and executing competitive actions to improve relative firm performance?

This research makes five unique contributions. First, our central contribution is a model that explicates the role of IS in the process by which competitive actions or responses are conceived, enacted, and executed, and the resulting impacts on firm performance. This research contributes to both IS and competitive dynamics literature domains, as it uncovers the competitive dynamics process firms follow and presents an indepth view of the embedded role of organization-wide information systems in the competitive dynamics context. Second, this is the first study in IS or competitive dynamics to use the grounded theory method to discover the process of conceiving, enacting, and executing competitive actions and the role of IS in that context. This approach demonstrates the value of using alternate research methods to flesh out complex linkages among investments in IS, competitive actions/responses, and firm performance. Third, contrary to much existing research, this study finds that IS facilitate a highly rational competitive decisionmaking process that is somewhat moderated by such factors as organizational culture, strategy, and managerial perceptions. Fourth, this research uncovers the notion that current information technology (IT) investments lay the foundation for future competitive actions. Fifth, this research unearths the abandonment of competitive action, a fundamental component of competitive activity that has not been previously addressed by competitive dynamics literature. Most important, this research is unique in its journey inside the “black box” of organizational competitive processes to learn about how and why managers engage in competitive actions or responses and the role of IS within that context.

The paper is organized as follows. We first present background for our research approach and situate this study in competitive dynamics, IS, and interpretative sociology literature. Next, we present the research methodology and an overview of our focal firm, FCI (pseudonym). After that, we present the grounded theoretical model that conceptualizes the findings in terms of four categories across the competitive dynamics process: IT-enhanced organizational information processing and competitive action, information-driven competitive action decision, execution/abandonment of competitive action, and firm performance. Last, we provide implications for research and practice, limitations and suggestions for future research, and a conclusion.

## 2. Background

In this section, we position our study in the relevant literature in IS and firm performance and competitive dynamics, with particular emphasis on the awareness-motivation-capability (AMC) framework (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007).

## 2.1. Competitive Dynamics and the AMC Framework

Competitive dynamics literature describes a view of strategy as the undertaking of competitive moves to achieve competitive advantage (Porter 1980), suggesting that firms engage in competitive actions and responses to improve relative firm performance (Ferrier 2001). Such competitive actions include the struggle for market share through price cuts and advertising campaigns (Vilcassim et al. 1999), new product development (Banbury and Mitchell 1995), new market entry (Ferrier et al. 1999), and competitive differentiation (Caves and Ghemawat 1992). Firms may engage in competitive actions that have proven successful or develop new competitive actions when past actions become ineffective (Miller 1990) or are found to be flawed (Kirzner 1997). Over time, firms become familiar with potential competitive responses of rivals and take this into account when weighing the benefits and costs of actions (Zajac and Bazerman 1991). Such competitive interaction provides opportunities for strategic deterrence and threats of retaliation to influence the motivations of a rival and erode its position in the industry (Chen and Miller 1994, Gimeno 1999).

The AMC perspective has identified three primary drivers of interfirm rivalry: awareness of the competitive environment, motivation to take action, and capability to take action (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007). Although the AMC framework has been utilized across a range of interfirm rivalry studies (e.g., Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007, Ferrier 2001), to date, neither the IS nor competitive dynamics streams of research have used this framework to investigate how IS might be used to overcome temporal, spatial, and cognitive limitations on awareness of the competitive environment, to motivate action through improved access to situational and market signals, or to recognize and augment a firm’s capabilities to generate or take action.

Although important insights have been gained through the efforts of competitive dynamics scholars, most studies have taken a largely dyadic focus, without taking into account the process firms follow as they engage in competitive activity or the interpretations of key players in the competitive context. Additionally, most studies have focused on discrete outcomes, without considering how or why competitive actions or responses were carried out. “One possible explanation is that there is little crossfertilization across research streams” (Ketchen et al. 2004, p. 780). Moreover, studies utilizing the AMC framework have not fully examined the circular issues of how awareness, motivation, or capability influences decision makers or vice versa, despite encouragement by Chen et al. (2007) to use this perspective to “    explore the nuance and complexity of the interrelationships    under various industry conditions, and extend this promising perspective to develop a predictive theory not only of competitive action, but of organizational action in general” (p. 116).

## 2.2. IS and Firm Performance

It has been suggested that the resource-based view is the paramount theory used in IS research to understand the relationship between IS, competitive advantage, and firm performance (Jarvenpaa and Leidner 1998). Other IS researchers suggest that the knowledge-based view of the firm (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001, Kearns and Sabherwal 2007) or the concept of fit (Gattiker and Goodhue 2004, Premkumar et al. 2005) can best explain the relationship between IS, competitive advantage, and firm performance. Sambamurthy et al. (2003) point out, however, that despite insights gained through these studies, “    theoretical frameworks have yet to explain how and why these investments enhance firm performance” (p. 238).

McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) offer that “[m]uch attention has been paid to the connection between productivity growth and the increase in IT investment. But hardly any has been directed to the nature of the link between IT and competitiveness” (p. 100). Neither the IS nor the competitive dynamics stream of research has addressed the role of IS in the strategic process by which top managers design the structure and composition of a firm’s competitive repertoire or the set of possible competitive actions (conceive), endorse a particular action (enact), or carry out (execute) a competitive action. This study is the first to integrate the IS and competitive dynamics streams of literature using an interpretive perspective.

## 3. Research Approach

Following Orlikowski (1993) and Sabherwal and Robey (1993), who studied processes inside real organizations, this research derives theory from real organizational data by empirically examining the collective interpretations of a firm’s managers with regard to the role of IS in a process of conceiving, enacting, and executing competitive actions, and impacts on firm performance.

## 3.1. Interpretive Sociology and Organizations as Interpretive Systems

“Conventional strategic management research has come under increasing criticism partly because this research starts from a positivist, rationalistic model of the strategic planning process” (Smircich and Stubbart 1985, p. 733). Similarly, it has been noted that few IS studies utilize the interpretations of key players in the organizational context (Orlikowski 1993, Markus and Robey 1988). According to Klein and Myers (1999), “Interpretive research can help IS researchers to understand human thought and action in social and organizational contexts   ” (p. 67). Managers’ interpretations are particularly significant, as managers control the manner by which actions occur and provide the basis for the meaning of organizational actions (Isabella 1990, Smircich and Stubbart 1985).

Isabella (1990) contends that four critical assumptions are necessary in interpretive studies. First, organizational members actively create their own reality. Second, the social interchange of shared experiences of organizational members creates a collective logic of the occurrence of events. Third, the interpretive literature has identified managerial views of phenomena as critical. As managers are leaders within their organizations, their interpretations of reality have tremendous influence on the construed reality of other organizational members. Finally, interpretive research is built on events that have already transpired, and a collective viewpoint has had time to emerge. In this study, we utilize the grounded theory method, which is an interpretive research approach.

## 3.2. Methodology

Grounded theory (Corbin and Strauss 2008) is employed in this study, an approach utilized across

IS research (e.g., Hunter and Beck 2000, Orlikowski 1993) as well as organizational studies (e.g., Isabella 1990, Rerup 2004) to investigate complex phenomena. Grounded theory allows a focus on context and process as well as interpretations of key players, elements often omitted in IS and competitive dynamics studies that rely on variance models and cross-sectional, quantitative data (Orlikowski 1993, Smircich and Stubbart 1985). The aim of grounded theory research is to derive theory from data rather than force-fitting data to a priori theory and hypotheses (Corbin and Strauss 2008), resulting in theory so intimately tied to the data that it is likely consistent with empirical observation (Eisenhardt 1989).

3.2.1. Theoretical Sampling and Site Selection. Like Chen (1996), who studied the airline industry because of its competitive nature, we identified a firm that operated within a competitive environment and regularly utilized various information systems in its operational and strategic activities. FCI is the U.S. division of a global manufacturing firm and is identified by trade and financial publications as the global leader in developing, designing, manufacturing, marketing, and distributing high-quality products. The firm pursues a worldwide growth strategy, going after new markets, developing innovative products, and substitutes for competing products, and discovering new uses for its current products. The financial and trade press regard FCI as well positioned in its industry through technological innovation, the introduction of new product lines, manufacturing expertise, domestic and international marketing, branding, and strong management.

3.2.2. Unit of Analysis. The unit of analysis in this study is the competitive action or response of FCI. As the research endeavor took place within one organization, it was expected that each competitive action would be carried out with inherent procedural similarities and organizational philosophies. Thus, difference conditions were derived by examining different types of competitive actions and the role of IS within actions FCI undertook through the interpretations of 14 managers. This triangulation across various types of competitive actions and across multiple managers provides manifold perspectives of the research objective, and provides indepth validation of the underlying constructs (Corbin and Strauss 2008, Orlikowski 1993).

FCI pursues a high-growth strategy, prompting managers to engage in vigorous searches for new opportunities while simultaneously defending existing market advantages against industry rivals. The firm’s competitive repertoire includes new product development (Banbury and Mitchell 1995), new customer acquisition (Ferrier et al. 1999), new market entry (Ferrier et al. 1999), product pricing (Smith et al. 2001), and advertising (Vilcassim et al. 1999). These were further verified as competitive actions through industry and trade publications. We have categorized the firm’s actions under the typology suggested by Grimm et al. (2006) as entrepreneurial actions that derive from novel combinations of firm resources, Ricardian actions that exploit scarce firm resources, and deterrent actions that capitalize on the firm’s market position.

3.2.3. Data Collection and Analysis. Primary data collection took place through semistructured interviews with 14 managers within the organization, providing us with an “insider’s” view of the phenomenon (Chen et al. 1993). Participants included the Chief Financial Officer; Chief Operating Officer; Corporate Controller; Director of Engineering; Director of Global Sales; Director of Human Resources; Director of Information Technology; Director of Production; Director of Purchasing; Customer Service, Shipping and Receiving and Scheduling; Director of Quality Control; Director of Marketing; General Manager; and Network Administrator. Each interview, lasting one to two hours, was taped for accuracy and then transcribed to text, which was used to conduct the data analysis.

The text of our interviews is quite extensive, numbering more than 200 single-spaced pages. Thus, providing examples of managerial quotes within the text of this paper is prohibitive, given space limitations. Therefore, we have provided paraphrasing of the managers’ quotes. Each paraphrase appears in italicized text and is hyperlinked to the managers’ actual quotes, which are contained in our supplemental document.<sup>1</sup>

Data collection, coding, and analysis were conducted iteratively (Corbin and Strauss 2008) and were focused to seek information within the research context. Interview questions were somewhat loosely structured, allowing managers flexibility in responding. In addition to interviews, one researcher attended an investor’s conference, where managers were observed in discussions with securities analysts and current or potential investors regarding the firm’s competitive activity. Thus, as recommended by Chen et al. (1993), both insiders and outside informants were used in assessing the impact of competitive actions on firm performance. The analysis of the data occurred through three coding processes: open coding, axial coding, and selective coding (Orlikowski 1993). Examples of our coding processes can be found in Table I in our supplemental document found at ISR’s online supplements website.

Data collection and analysis were conducted to the point of theoretical saturation, when previously identified concepts were repeated in the data; no new concepts were found (Corbin and Strauss 2008). Emergent concepts were grouped into concepts and categories, and categories were then integrated to form the theoretical framework (Corbin and Strauss 2008).

3.2.4. Validation. Validation took place in four stages. First, an objective researcher not involved in the data collection reviewed the data analysis (Gioa and Chitipeddi 1991). Second, following Eisenhardt (1989) and Santos and Eisenhardt (2009), we compared our findings with relevant extant literature and theory, including organizational information processing (Galbraith 1974; Gattiker and Goodhue 2004a, b; Jarvenpaa and Ives 1993), the AMC perspective (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007), decision making (Huber 1981, Newell and Simon 1972, Simon 1960), organizational memory and learning (Stein and Zwass 1995, Walsh and Ungson 1991), cognition and distributed cognition (Boland et al. 1994, Walsh 1995), and real options (Kogut and Kulatilaka 2004, McGrath et al. 2004). Literature comparison sharpens analytic generalizability and provides an additional layer of theoretical relevance by examining the similarities and differences in the derived theory versus existing literature. Third, securities analysts’ opinions were used to confirm managerial interpretations of impacts of competitive actions on firm performance (Chen et al. 1993). Finally, study participants (managers) reviewed the findings.

## 4. Process Model of IS, Competitive Action, and Firm Performance

The process model of IS, competitive action, and firm performance (see Figure 1) illustrates how IS contribute to the conception, enactment, and execution of competitive actions and thus, how IS impact firm performance. The model describes a process that incorporates four distinct categories related to our research question: IT-enhanced organizational information processing and competitive action, information-driven competitive action decision, execution/abandonment of competitive action, and firm performance. Furthermore, the nature of the model and the relationships between the concepts and categories suggest that the process is nonlinear, with the option of feedback loops among different categories in the model.

## 4.1. Summary of Findings

Following Mintzberg and McHugh (1985) and Ferrier (2001) in the conceptualization of strategy as process, our grounded theory explains the relationship among IS, competitive actions, and firm performance as embedded within a competitive dynamics process. This process view comprises the formation of competitive action, decisions on competitive action, the execution or abandonment (demotivation) of competitive action, and the impact of competitive action on firm performance, or in other words “the sequential patterns of competitive moves    and how such patterns relate to firm performance” (Ferrier 2001, p. 858). A summary of our findings and details on each concept and category in the theoretical model are presented below.

4.1.1. Conceiving: IT-Enhanced Organizational Information Processing and Competitive Action. IS provide a requisite platform for enhancing awareness of the internal and external environment, facilitating a cohesive understanding of motivations and firm capability to act; they also reduce uncertainty of the consequences or benefits of action. IS augment organizational memory by facilitating information acquisition and sharing, knowledge exchange, and interaction among decision makers and serve as a requisite platform to synchronize disparate managerial cognitions regarding conceiving competitive actions or responses.

## 4.2. Explanation of the Theoretical Model

In the following sections, we provide a detailed exposition of our theoretical model (see Figure 1). We explain the fundamental concepts and relationships inherent in the process model, and compare our findings with extant literature (Eisenhardt 1989, Santos and Eisenhardt 2009).

4.2.1. Conceiving: IT-Enhanced Organizational Information Processing and Competitive Action. The following sections provide an overview of each of the concepts that explain the conceiving stage of competitive action.

Information Sources, Idea Generation, and Competitive Actions. IS such as a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) enterprise resource planning (ERP) system, BlackBerry multifunctional wireless devices, data/information repositories, and the Internet are used by FCI’s managers to enhance the firm’s informational capability, which can be regarded as a special kind of firm-level capability (Gimeno 1999). Internal information sources include employees who provide customer information and databases that house results from R&D. External information sources include the Internet for researching new end uses of products, trade organizations or publications, information from vendors on rivals’ activities, governmental sources, and information from customers. Information systems provide the conduit and platform for essential information when conceiving of competitive action or response.

IS serve as a gateway to new ideas and as the conduit for distributing and acquiring internal and external information relevant to new product development.<sup>2</sup>

The AMC perspective (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007) suggests that competitors will engage in an action/response only if they are aware of the competitive environment and have the motivation and capability to act or respond. If managers interpret that firm performance is likely to be affected by an action or response, they become motivated to act or respond (Lant et al. 1992), and managers thus begin to assess their capability to respond. IS enhance FCI’s managers’ awareness of the consequences or benefits of action, including responses from rivals (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007).

IS provide a vital information channel that increases awareness of rivals’ moves, motivates managers to conceive of a competitive response, and helps managers determine the firm’s capability respond.

Tacit and Explicit Knowledge Resources on Competitive Actions. Organizationally distributed knowledge resources are a necessary component when contemplating competitive actions. Knowledge is defined as “the intersection of information, experience, and theory” (Hatala and Lutta 2009, p. 7) and is both tacit and explicit in nature. FCI’s knowledge resources are distributed among key organizational participants and hold an assessment of human, plant, and equipment resources, marketing, capital, and R&D available for a particular competitive action or response.

Inside organizations, individuals think, learn, and interpret independently. However, when individuals begin to take into account their interdependencies, coordinated efforts emerge (Boland et al. 1994). IS play a vital role in coordinating tacit and explicit knowledge, helping FCI’s managers in conceiving of competitive actions most appropriate for FCI.

Distributed cognition (Boland et al. 1994, Hollan et al. 2000, Hutchins 1991) is a concept that recognizes individuals as autonomous agents who recognize their interdependencies. Cognitions are schemas or knowledge structures held by individuals and groups to make sense of information and environments (Walsh 1995). FCI’s IS are used as a mechanism to coordinate the tacit and explicit knowledge resources that are distributed among the cognitions of FCI’s managers (Boland et al. 1994). Coordination of tacit and explicit knowledge resources held by FCI’s managers is necessary across the entire competitive activity process, as “[t]he composition of a TMT [top management team] shapes the lenslike cognitive structure that defines its members’ collective field of vision” (Ferrier 2001, p. 863). Furthermore, IS facilitate a “cross-understanding” (Huber and Lewis 2010, p. 7) about who knows what (awareness) about the competitive landscape, motivations to act, and the firm’s capability to act. Individual managers and relevant IS artifacts that go into the successful completion of a competitive action must be understood in unity, as managers and FCI’s information systems form a collective cognitive system (Hutchins 1991).

IS serve as the mechanism for aggregating and disbursing tacit and explicit knowledge resources distributed among managerial cognitions within the competitive dynamics process.

Managers at FCI utilize the modern IS as a repository of current and historical information that can be easily accessed by key organizational participants to extend organizational memory. Our interpretation of organizational memory incorporates the knowledge held by individuals, the organizational knowledge in historic files, and the documentation of unique manufacturing processes that FCI has continued over time. The term “organizational memory” implies that organizations have the capacity to acquire, retain, and retrieve information (Walsh and Ungson 1991). However, Simon (1991) posits that we should not objectify organizations as thinking and learning entities: people think and learn, not organizations. Various managers within the organization acquire information and knowledge based on different interpretations of what is needed to address problems and make decisions. When these disparate interpretations are shared, individual interpretations and knowledge transcend the individual level and live on for later retrieval and use (Croasdell 2001, Walsh and Ungson 1991). Croasdell (2001) suggests that retentiveness—the preservation of organizational memory—and connectedness—the accessibility of organizational memory—define the value of organizational memory. However, the individual’s abilities with regard to retentiveness and connectedness are quite limited. Thus, managers at FCI rely heavily on IS to provide both retention and connectedness with regard to the firm’s competitive activity. FCI’s firm-specific knowledge, embedded in the routines and practices of the organization, creates a non-imitable resource that in turn generates rents through Ricardian actions or provides the basis for deterrent competitive actions (Grimm et al. 2006).

## IS augment human firm-specific knowledge embedded in FCI’s competitive practices.

Managers see that the organizational routines and processes embedded within FCI’s organizational memory are a potent source of competitive advantage (Barney 1991) but cannot be efficiently utilized in crafting competitive actions in the absence of effective IS.

Information on Drivers of Competitive Actions. We can examine the concept information on drivers of competitive action in the context of the need construct inherent in prevailing organizational information processing theory (Galbraith 1974, Thompson 1967), where increasing uncertainty creates the need for increased information processing capability in firms. Managers at FCI interpret their organization as a first mover and market leader (Ferrier et al. 1999, Smith et al. 2001); thus, managers do not believe that uncertainty plays a major role in their actions. Managers have strong assumptions about their own organization and about rivals in the industry (Zajac and Bazerman 1991), which could influence their competitive strategies or create “blind spots,” situations where managers do not have a realistic view of competitive situations (Grimm et al. 2006). We find that FCI’s managers rely extensively on IS to reduce uncertainty by providing a realistic view of the consequences of their own competitive activity as well as awareness of rivals’ actions (awareness) within the competitive landscape.

FCI’s product quality is well known, bringing a competitive advantage that is hard to imitate by rivals because of costly resource investments and is sustained by contractual protection of FCI’s proprietary manufacturing processes. Kroll et al. (1999) suggest a positive relationship between product quality and relative market share. FCI has been very successful in cultivating a significant product quality gap between itself and its rivals; thus, managers perceive quality as a significant motivating factor in initiating competitive actions and defending its market position. Managers who have access to plentiful resources available through superior performance of their firm may demonstrate greater competitive responsiveness (Jayachandran and Varadrajan 2006). FCI’s superior quality leads to improved resource endowments, as enhanced financial, human, and physical capital can be effectively used to act on and to respond to rivals’ actions.

IS are used as a competitive weapon embedded within competitive actions/responses that rely on information about product quality, including acquiring new customers, entering new markets, and deterring rival firms.

We also find that access to proprietary information held in the organizational memory supported by FCI’s information systems drives the competitive activities of this firm.

Access to historic information housed in IS drives new product ideas.

Managers envision IS not simply in a supporting role but as mechanisms that provide opportunities for competitive actions. This finding emphasizes the idea that the relationship between IS investments and competitive actions might be circular or longitudinal, where current investments in IS may lead to future competitive actions. This also implies that assessing investments in IS through the lens of the AMC framework may provide a more meaningful analysis of both current and future returns on such investments.

Investments in IS drive competitive activity by opening up new opportunities.

Information Flexibility Related to Competitive Actions. Information flexibility is defined as anytime, anywhere access to and dissemination of information and knowledge on competitive actions contemplated by FCI or on actions of its rivals. FCI migrated from a traditional module-based ERP system to a SaaS ERP system and adopted the BlackBerry communications system because of the flexibility these technologies provided in overcoming temporal and spatial limitations (capability). These IS hasten the completion of the competitive action or response process that FCI follows, as the speed at which the process can be addressed is immediately relevant to the speed at which information and knowledge are provided, shared, and disseminated among the managers.

FCI’s information and communications systems provide the mechanisms for flexible access, availability, and the operation of a platform for a collective view among the managers of how information and knowledge should be accessed and utilized, given specific competitive actions. The collective interpretations representing the degree to which various IS facilitate the flow of information necessary to formulate and enact competitive actions are tightly integrated with the degree of effective interdependence among managers.

Information flexibility is realized through global availability of systems, enforcement of system use policies, and the integration of information systems and units.

Perceptions that FCI’s particular information needs are met through the specific combination of its IS and definite ways in which they should be used are widespread. Such perceptions are significant, as they emphasize the importance of organizational context in understanding the role of IS in the competitive process.

Information Flow and Competitive Action. Hatala and Lutta (2009) suggest that information sharing and acquisition are crucial for firms’ competitiveness and “require a free flow of information among members that is undistorted and up-to-date” (p. 5). Although information flow has been recognized in a minor role in studies utilizing organizational information processing theory (Smith et al. 1991, Bento and Bento 2006), we find that information flow is a critical antecedent to competitive decision making. Information flow constitutes communicating, sharing, connecting, and diffusing relevant information to key decision makers involved in conceiving of competitive actions. FCI’s managers believe that the progression of a competitive decision is unlikely in the event of ineffective information flow.

Competitive decisions depend on information flow facilitated by effective use of IS.

The effective formulation of information flow includes the transfer and analysis of sensory data related to the competitive environment (awareness) from the boundary of the organization. It enables decision makers to emphasize the information needed to begin the competitive decision process (Huber and Daft 1987, Knight and McDaniel 1979). Information flow directly impacts awareness of competitive alternatives, motivations toward a particular action or response, and assessment of the firm’s capability to endorse a particular course of action (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007).

Although information systems are seen primarily in this study to facilitate information flow among FCI’s managers, reliance on information systems for effective information flow can produce negative effects as well. For example, individuals may be inadvertently (or purposefully) omitted from distribution lists or not given access to certain data and information. FCI’s IS are used to address these types of problems by providing vehicles for effective information aggregation, transfer, and access.

IS are purposefully used to provide an effective platform for the necessary components of information flow.

4.2.2. Enacting: Information-Driven Competitive Action Decision. The literature suggests that decision making is largely nonrational (Simon 1955), influenced by individual cognitions (Hodgkinson et al. 1999, Walsh 1995) and individual management skills and experience (Thomas and McDaniel 1990, Ferrier 2001). However, FCI’s managers engage in a highly rational and cohesive competitive decision-making process purposefully facilitated by their use of IS. Fourteen different managers were interviewed independently during our data collection process. Therefore, our findings with regard to a highly rational decision process are compelling and not the consequence of rationalization a posteriori of past decision processes.

Information Collection and Dissemination on Competitive Actions, Evaluation of Competitive Action Repertoire, and Choice of Competitive Action. Managers are motivated to engage in actions that will result in positive impacts on firm performance. Thus, at the decisionmaking stage of the competitive dynamics process, managers begin to evaluate potential consequences of a competitive action or response. With competitive decision making comes risk, as the execution of a competitive action can have serious and farreaching consequences. Hence, managers at FCI utilize IS to extend the bounds on human decision-making abilities (Newell and Simon 1972).

IS are used to reduce the risk of decision outcomes and to aid managers in making final judgments about the best course of competitive action.

Several perspectives on decision making are present in the literature. The theory of bounded rationality (Simon 1955) implies that environmental complexity, time and space constraints, and human computational limitations all work to constrain the decision maker against rational decisions. Furthermore, environmental competitive threat, perceived external control of the organization, and the uncertainty of strategic issues affect the rational abilities of the decision maker (Mintzberg and Waters 1982, Dean and Sharfman 1993). Thus, individuals in an environment of risk and uncertainty will impose a structure that leads to a “satisficing” decision. Satisficing is defined as “setting an acceptable level or aspiration level as final criterion and simply taking the first acceptable move (Newell and Simon 1972, p. 681). Competitive decisions rely on the collective interpretations of managerial decision makers. Larson et al. (2004) suggest that information that is shared among key individuals will have a stronger effect on decision making than information held by individuals. However, it has been suggested that variations in cognition and experience may frustrate decision-making consensus within managerial groups (Ferrier 2001), reducing competitive decisionmaking speed and efficiency (Hambrick et al. 1996).

Our research extends current literature on decision making by revealing that IS facilitate a highly rational process of competitive decision making. Simon’s (1965) three-stage decision-making model assumes that at the choice stage, decision makers may reevaluate alternatives. However, we find that enhanced awareness of the internal and external environment provided through IS may change the entire dynamic of the decision process. Zajac and Bazerman (1991) and Grimm et al. (2006) point out that competitive decisions are unique strategic decisions, as these decisions require firms to consider potential actions of rivals in the industry. The role of IS in awareness is significant at the decision-making stage, as the extent to which a firm’s managers comprehend the consequences of their own competitive choices as well as rivals’ actions within the competitive landscape (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007) is directly impacted. IS provide opportunities for feedback loops, creating channels through which new information on rivals’ actions may be obtained, motivations for competitive action may be reevaluated, and additional knowledge from within the organization can be obtained, thus enabling a better than “satisficing” decision.

IS provide the platform for renewed awareness, motivations, and capability during the decision-making stage of the competitive dynamics process that may influence decision outcomes.

Decision makers have “gut instincts” about carrying out competitive activity. However, FCI’s managers use IS to bring a collective and rational dynamic to the competitive decision process, wherein the “numbers don’t lie” (FCI manager); thus, they are less likely to act based on competitive tension or on the strain between competitors that results in rivalrous action (Chen et al. 2007).

Rational and collective competitive decisions are dependent on the access, speed, accuracy, and objectivity provided by IS.

The choice of competitive action to pursue may be driven by firm-specific capabilities and/or resources. Price decreases are considered a classic form of competitive action (Vilcassim et al. 1999), but managers at FCI use superior quality as an avenue for inverting this competitive action. Quality derived through FCI’s proprietary manufacturing processes and unique knowledge of the firm’s engineers motivates a price increase, challenging the status quo of the market (Ferrier et al. 1999, Miller 1990).

IS use is embedded within pricing decisions that communicate product quality to the competitive environment.

This move follows Porter’s (1980) view of competitive differentiation via superior product quality, thus enabling the firm to execute a competitive action by charging premium prices in the marketplace.

Organizational and Competitive Factors Moderating Choice. Managers’ interpretations of their organization and environmental events influence how companies act and respond. Although the factual evidence provided through IS plays a major role, “   human judgment is needed to interpret the findings and determine their relevance for the future” (Barnes 1984, p. 129). Although results of our analysis reveal the rationalizing influence of IS on competitive decision making, our findings deviate from a purely rationalistic model by identifying several factors that moderate the rational choice of competitive action taken by FCI managers. The competitive dynamics literature indicates that certain characteristics of a firm and its position in its competitive environment will influence the actions that managers take (Miller and Chen 1996). Managers at FCI perceive their organization as a leader in its industry. This collective perception colors awareness and influences the managers’ motivations to act.

Firm managers interpret FCI as an innovator (Banbury and Mitchell 1995), a first-mover (Ferrier et al. 1999) with competitive differentiation (Caves and Ghemawat 1992), which moderates their collective choice of competitive action.

We have also found that even under the rationalizing influence of IS embedded within the competitive decision process, managers’ perceptions about potential outcomes of a particular course of action will moderate decision outcomes (Chen et al. 2007, MacMillan et al. 1985, Smith et al. 1989).

Managerial perceptions of consequences of action moderates collective choice of competitive action.

Management style will moderate managers’ choice of competitive action. Managers agree that FCI has evolved toward a flat organizational structure, which influences the competitive decision-making process. IS facilitate the dynamic decision style used by managers, providing flexibility in acquiring and sharing information, given a particular competitive action decision scenario.

Organizational management style facilitates a dynamic competitive decision process that moderates collective choice of competitive action.

Managers at FCI believe (awareness) that competitive advantage depends on FCI’s ability to recognize and seize growth opportunities (capability) that capitalize on its unique quality characteristics to gain customers and market share (motivation). Ferrier (2001) suggests that competitive actions a firm chooses over time (sequence) represent its strategy. FCI’s vigorous quest for growth has a moderating influence on its choice of action.

Organizational growth strategy influences collective choice of competitive action.

Superior product quality is both a competitive advantage and a source of pride for managers that strongly influences their competitive choices.

Commitment to quality moderates the collective choice of competitive action.

Firm culture may also moderate managers’ collective choice of competitive activity. In particular, shifts in organizational culture may create disconnects, confounding the competitive decision-making process. We find evidence of shifts and cultural adjustments embodied in FCI’s implementation and utilization of information systems.

The incorporation of IS into competitive organizational routines results in changes in firm culture that moderate collective choice of competitive action.

Even within the factors moderating choice, IS continue to play a vital role. Subjective components such as managerial “gut feeling,” managerial style, and organizational factors are integral to any strategy formulation process (Barnes 1984). However, once such subjective judgments have been formulated, they must then be communicated and disseminated (development of common awareness and motivating factors) to the various individuals responsible for achieving strategic goals.

4.2.3. Executing: Execution or Abandonment (Demotivation) of Competitive Action. At this stage of the competitive dynamics process, managers are actively pursuing a chosen course of action. In the case of new product development, they are announcing the new product in the marketplace, actively promoting it to potential customers, and explicating various ways in which it can be used. However, at this stage, managers may become demotivated to follow a particular course of action. Abandonment may be caused by a sudden economic or environmental shift, lack of adequate or timely information, or even a purposeful abandonment to mislead rivals.

Information and Competitive Action Execution. Managers use IS in identifying where, when, and how actions or responses will best help improve relative firm performance. For example, IS-enabled research mechanisms are used to identify competing standards in the industry. Managers then become active members of bodies that set product standards. By influencing industry standards in its favor, FCI is able to alter resource recombinations required to produce products at the expense of rivals that may find it difficult or impossible to carry out such recombinations (Galunic and Rodan 1998). In describing scenarios of successful execution, managers refer to accomplishments, achievements, and—interestingly— exploitations of rivals.

IS are used to recognize new market and customer opportunities and to acquire market share at the expense of rivals.

Information and Competitive Action Abandonment (Demotivation). The abandonment of competitive action has not been investigated in the literature. Thus, little is understood about why some actions that were originally conceived and chosen as a viable firm-level action—via high levels of awareness, motivation, and capability—were not eventually carried out. Although successful execution with positive impact on firm performance is the desired outcome, managers may become demotivated to carry out certain competitive actions, which emphasizes the day-to-day reality of competitive decisions. As managers become demotivated to act, they become aware of a sudden market or economic development that would result in the competitive action having a negative impact on firm performance. In such a situation, managers will no longer feel the need to act, as it is in the best interests of the firm to abandon the action. The abandonment of competitive action emphasizes the critical role played by IS in providing timely and easily accessible information in the context of competitive activity.

The real options body of literature (for a review, see McGrath et al. 2004) may help shed some light on action abandonment. Certain actions give managers the option of taking another direction should additional information be obtained in time to avoid negative consequences to the firm; however, information that could allow the firm to take other options may come too late or not at all.

Managers will have options with regard to the execution of competitive actions when decisions are not fixed or when additional information has been obtained (Amram and Kulatilaka 1999). However, all possible consequences of executing competitive actions cannot be known. Managers at FCI rely on IS to generate market projections and forecasts when making competitive decisions, which may reduce uncertainty of action; but it may also give the illusion of certainty (Amram and Kulatilaka 1999), bringing a sense of false comfort with regard to competitive decision outcomes.

Abandonment of action may unavoidably adversely affect firm performance, depending on how far the firm has progressed with the action. In an example of an entrepreneurial action, FCI’s managers abandoned an action designed to capitalize on a new market opportunity. Capital investments had already been made, and rivals had become aware of FCI’s actions. It is important to note that the managers’ decision to abandon the competitive action was not a strategic shift, where investments in time and money could be reapplied.

McGrath et al. (2004) point out that knowledge is not static, and managers are continually learning and adding to what they know about motivations and capabilities for competitive action. In this example, managers chose to execute a competitive action contingent on what they expected the market to accept (Kogut and Kulatilaka 2004). However, demotivation criteria came in the form of a sudden shift in what the market would accept, so the abandonment conditions were not known until this information became available. Although a priori abandonment criteria can sometimes be specified, such criteria may only become known with time as more information becomes available (McGrath et al. 2004).

## IS provide the platform for continual learning about (de)motivations and capabilities for competitive action.

A form of learning has occurred through abandonment, contingent on having IS that provided demotivation information. Absent information systems, FCI would have suffered a greater loss by continuing to pursue a competitive action that would be detrimental to its performance. Becoming demotivated to pursue certain competitive actions emphasizes the integral role that IS play in the competitive process. However, even with the highly integrated presence of information systems in FCI’s competitive process, managers can never have complete information.

Although the abandonment of competitive action is often caused by factors outside the control of FCI’s managers, abandonment is sometimes an intentional competitive move that the managers at FCI label a “bombshell.” Contrary to the position taken by Chen and Miller (1994), who suggest that firms can best “    achieve their own objectives by making nuanced and discreet moves that fail to elicit retaliation   ” (p. 86), a bombshell occurs when a rival has been led to believe that the firm is making a competitive move, prompting the rival to make significant investment in preemptive response. In one example, FCI’s managers were able to capitalize on a “blind spot” of a major rival, prompting the rival to act without accurate information. Information is the weapon used in this kind of attack, and managers proactively used IS in the context of this most unusual type of competitive activity to create visibility of a phantom action.

## IS provide the conduit for timely abandonment criteria and the vehicle by which to capitalize upon blind spots of rivals.

Through purposeful abandonment, FCI profited both in enhanced firm reputation and financially by regaining lost customers. Deceitful manipulation to gain strategic advantage is not uncommon (Crawford 2003). Interestingly, the deceived party is generally more harshly criticized for gullibility than is the deceiver for misrepresentation. Indeed, the reputation of FCI’s rival suffered as a consequence of a bombshell, whereas FCI’s reputation was enhanced. IS played a critical role in providing the platform and vehicle for passing information among industry participants and stakeholders, resulting in both the deception of and the eventual gain for FCI.

These examples of abandonment emphasize the potential irreversibility of competitive actions (Chen et al. 2002). Chen and MacMillan (1992) suggest that “   the greater the cost of reversing a move, the more likely it is that [the] attacker will stay put” (p. 545). In the first example, FCI suffered financial loss because it had invested resources in a competitive action that had to be abandoned because of industry forces beyond its control. In the second example, FCI was able to use IS to learn in a timely fashion that further pursuit of an action would not be in the best interests of the firm, and to present a phantom (deceptive) action to a major rival to erode its competitive position.

4.2.4. Firm Performance. We find that through the competitive actions and responses in which FCI’s managers engage, IS will have an unquestionable impact on market and/or financial firm performance. Additionally, FCI’s managers use IS to evaluate the impact of execution of competitive actions on firm performance.

Information, Market Performance, and Financial Performance. IS play a clear role in each of the aforementioned concepts and categories that make up the firm’s competitive process. For each of the identified competitive actions or responses and realized impacts on firm performance, managers see that information systems play an integral and critical role. However, to substantiate the managers’ views, we have used objective accounts of the effects of competitive actions on firm performance from securities analysts (Chen et al. 1993). A Jeffries & Company, Inc. analyst recognizes a newly formed market presence in the cell phone industry through FCI’s innovation in products (new product development) and predicts strong growth based on FCI’s current market performance data in the automotive market. PiperJaffray predicts strong sales growth and margin expansion because of FCI’s acquisition of a major new customer in the automotive market. Roth Capital Partners reports that FCI’s first quarter revenues increased 156 percent year over year, largely because of its entry into the Chinese broadband market.

## 5. Study Implications

## 5.1. Managerial Implications

Managers considering IS investments should consider systems that will enhance awareness, motivation, and/or capability at the competitive dynamics level. Viewing IS through the lens of the AMC framework can reduce uncertainty of action, decreasing the likelihood of the powerless abandonment of a competitive action that may negatively impact firm performance or increase opportunities for purposeful abandonment, which may positively impact firm performance. Furthermore, IS can be used to facilitate a highly rational competitive decision-making process by providing an objective view of competitive circumstances and mitigating the influence of moderating factors such as managerial perceptions.

Competitive decision making is fraught with peril, as such decisions require preemptive knowledge of competitors’ moves. Furthermore, the complexity of firm-level competitive decisions is compounded by the temporally and spatially disbursed nature of today’s organizational structures. Our findings imply that competitive decisions rest on effective information flow among decision makers, directly affecting awareness of the competitive environment, motivation to act or respond, and capability of the firm to act or respond. By consciously evaluating the ways information systems can be used to reach maximum effectiveness in information flow, managers can avoid bottlenecks and disconnects across the competitive dynamics process that may thwart opportunities for competitive advantage.

Our findings are explanatory, but they are also prescriptive. This research has uncovered the notion that current IS investments can provide a foundation for future competitive actions. Competitive advantages will evolve when managers envision IS as a resource that provides competitive opportunities rather than viewing IS in a service role. By understanding their embedded nature within the competitive dynamics process, managers can proactively use IS to orient the competitive activity of the firm toward positive impacts on firm performance.

## 5.2. Research Implications

Numerous studies have sought to explain the relationship among IS, competitive advantage, and firm performance. However, most studies have tended to focus on discrete factors leading to competitive advantage and performance impacts without capturing a process view of this complex relationship. By conducting a field investigation and using a grounded theory approach, we were able to discern how managers collectively view the process of conceiving, enacting, and executing competitive actions and responses, and the integral role played by IS in that context. The process model depicted in Figure 1 can be used to explain, evaluate, or anticipate the role of IS in the competitive dynamics process followed by a firm. By using interview data we were able to gain insight into the information systems used, how they were used, why they were used, why investments in IT were made, as well as results of use or purchase of systems in the actions taken by managers to maintain their firm’s leading competitive position. Additionally, we gained insight into factors moderating competitive choices, including organizational structure, culture, strategy, and managerial perceptions and style. Furthermore, this paper uniquely addresses action abandonment and the role of IS in that context, a phenomenon that has been overlooked in extant research. Although managers may utilize every mechanism at their disposal to successfully execute a competitive action, outcomes cannot always be anticipated. Moreover, this research uncovered purposeful abandonment, a unique type of competitive action that until now has remained unexplored. By examining real organizational processes, we were able to discover why some actions that were conceived and chosen were not eventually carried out, and the role of IS within that context

This research contributes to both IS and competitive dynamics streams of literature. Competitive dynamics has primarily examined visible, detectable competitive actions carried out in the marketplace, but never actually identifies the process by which managers carry out firm-level strategic competitive activity. This paper is the first to measure the processes by which such actions are conceived, how choices are made, how actions are implemented, and what the impacts on firm performance are. Most IS research has assumed a linear relationship between IS and firm performance, such as the influence of a particular kind of system on firm performance. Furthermore, current knowledge is largely limited to the single IS/user relationship, when most individuals employ multiple systems (Kane and Alavi 2008). This research provides a process model for understanding the complex relationship among multiple, organizationwide IS, competitive action/response, and firm performance. As firms’ managers rely heavily on various digitized mechanisms to congregate, coordinate, communicate, and acquire and share information and knowledge, firm-level strategic actions become increasingly inseparable from IS. We find that managers interpret IS as embedded in (1) factors that influence awareness of the competitive environment; (2) factors that provoke or encumber motivation to undertake action; and (3) factors that influence capability to take action (Chen 1996, Chen et al. 2007).

## 6. Limitations and Future Research

Interestingly, some of the limitations of this study provide fertile ground for future research. The grounded theory process model depicted in Figure 1 is so intimately tied to the data that the resultant theory is likely consistent with empirical observation (Eisenhardt 1989); however, large-sample, statistically generalizable studies are needed to test specific aspects of our model. Our process model was derived through an in-depth examination of the competitive dynamics process carried out within one organization (Fairbank et al. 2006). Thus, the competitive nature of this organization and its particular utilization of information systems in the process of conceiving-enactingexecuting competitive actions might not be indicative of every firm. Large-scale studies will sharpen generalizability and further our understanding of this very complex phenomenon. We propose a large-scale study that examines the link among information flexibility, action repertoire complexity, action abandonment, and firm performance.

This study emphasizes the need for rich and interpretive studies in the organizational setting that examine competitive dynamics in the context of the process in which competitive actions/responses are conceived, enacted, and executed. The vast majority of competitive dynamics studies examine visible, detectable competitive actions carried out in the marketplace. This trend in contemporary competitive dynamics research has tended to follow the general notion that competitive actions are externally observable (e.g., Smith et al. 1991, Ferrier 2001). As a result, nondetectable actions, or actions that are not readily observable by rival firms, have not been addressed. This is a significant omission, given that Chen and Miller (1994) suggest that firms can best “   achieve their own objectives by making nuanced and discreet moves that fail to elicit retaliation   ” (p. 86), and Chen and MacMillan (1992) propose that competitive actions that do not elicit response bring the most significant performance gains, a perspective that indicates that nondetectable actions may bring the greatest competitive advantage. Furthermore, “visibility” suggests that actions that bring competitive advantage and superior market position are always externally directed (Ferrier 2001). However, FCI’s ability to produce high-quality products (Kroll et al. 1999) results from internally focused efforts to establish and protect proprietary manufacturing processes, enabling the firm to attain and sustain competitive advantage and superior performance. Additionally, extant competitive dynamics research ascribes intentionality to competitive actions (purposeful and planned actions taken to defend or improve a firm’s competitive position; Schumpeter 1934) but never actually measures intentionality or motivations. Additional research is needed to develop a better understanding of competitive actions as firmlevel (i.e., collective) intentional actions undertaken to bring competitive advantage and enhance firm performance. Similarly, the AMC framework and the empirical research that depends on it makes a great leap between the “awareness” with which a firm sees actions carried out in the marketplace and new competitive opportunities, and the “tension” experienced from interacting with rival firms. Yet this research has not fully tapped into exploring or measuring true cognitive awareness. Indeed, no research has addressed how awareness, motivation and capability really play out in decision-makers’ minds. Finally, if an action is executed (successful or not), it is assumed that it is because of some firm resource, although the empirical/causal link between resource and actions—guided by managerial awareness and motivation—is never actually measured. Research that furthers understanding of these complex phenomena is needed.

IS are being used to extend the traditional cognitive, temporal, and spatial boundaries on competitive decision making. Additionally, managers are utilizing IS to objectively evaluate competitive alternatives and make competitive choices. However, more research is needed to fully understand the rationalizing influence of IS on the competitive decision process, as such decisions require firms to consider potential actions of industry rivals.

It is important that researchers probe further into the notion of unavoidable as well as purposeful action abandonment uncovered in this research, as abandonment can have serious consequences for firms. Furthermore, as yet, we do not fully understand the influence of abandonment in managerial cognition or organizational memory, which may sway future competitive decisions.

Findings from this study emphasize the collective nature of managers when carrying out firm-level competitive activity, leading toward a social network perspective. Research has emphasized the importance of social relationships for acquiring and sharing information (Granovetter 1973; Burt 1976, 1992) and knowledge (Floyd and Wooldridge 1999, Larson et al. 2004). The authors plan a future study to examine managerial social networks in the context of competitive dynamics.

Finally, the concomitant presence of technology in today’s competitive processes provides a real opportunity for IS and competitive dynamics researchers to develop new theories of competition and identify new forms of competitive actions that may be evolving, new methods of attack and response, and conversely, ways competition is shaping developments and uses of IS.

## 7. Conclusion

Through a field study conducted using the grounded theory method, we found that IS play a much more integral role in the competitive advantage/firm performance relationship than projected in many current studies. IS are embedded in the competitive dynamics process followed by our focal firm and play a crucial role in keeping FCI in its leading competitive position.

IS are embedded in competitive actions that impact firm performance.

This paper addresses several recognized gaps in extant literature: the mixed results of cross-sectional studies investigating the impacts of IS on firm performance (Fairbank et al. 2006); the exclusion of how and why investments in IT affect firm performance (Sambamurthy et al. 2003); and the omission in competitive dynamics literature of how and why firms engage in certain strategic moves (Ketchen et al. 2004). Additionally, these findings serve to increase the understanding of the relationship among digital systems, competition, and firm performance (Ferrier et al. 2007).

Although significant progress has been made in identifying factors associated with competitive advantage, competitive dynamics and IS researchers have tended to concentrate on structural variables and have made few obvious efforts to identify the actual competitive dynamics process or the critical role of IS associated with conceiving, enacting, or executing competitive actions or responses. Most studies in competitive dynamics and IS rely on statistical methods of discovery and secondary data, tending to ignore differences across firms. This research takes into account the intentions and interpretations of key organizational players, the competitive action/response process, and the organizational context in which competitive activity takes place; thus, the IS/competitive advantage/firm performance relationship can be more accurately explained. Research built on assumptions and untidy causal propositions naturally give rise to a serious “black box” problem, which may severely limit future advancement of IS and competitive dynamics research domains. This paper, squarely focused on the role of IS and competitive dynamics, provides a vital look inside the black box. Indeed, we measure the processes by which actions are conceived, how choices are made, how actions are implemented, and their impact on firm performance.
