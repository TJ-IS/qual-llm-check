## Abstract

The coordination of a social event requires a number of time-bounded tasks (exact time, location, activities of the event) to be determined prior to the event. On university campuses, Facebook is increasingly used to organize ad hoc physical gatherings within social groups. The coordination takes place via Facebook event pages and a ‘wall’ that represent online, shared, interactive spaces. This paper explores how such online interactive spaces facilitate the temporal coordination of social events. We content analyze Facebook’s event pages and walls to understand how social group behavior is different from prevailing theories of group task progress and media use. The results suggest that, similar to many work groups, social groups exhibit differential interactive behaviors before and after the midpoint of when the event is created on Facebook and when the offline activity is going to happen. However, the results differ in the sense that the interactive behavior is highest before rather than after the midpoint. We also found that the involvement of the creator of the event pages is associated with higher interactive behavior of the social group. We discuss the findings and derive implications for the design of online tools for social event management.

## Introduction

Social events include activities like birthday parties, site-seeing trips, charity events, or any non-work-related gatherings of two or more people. As the events grow in the number of members and complexity of activities, keeping track of who is and who is not coming and concomitantly allocating tasks and resolving conflicts can become cumbersome as well as a source of anxiety.

People are increasingly using social networking technol ogies such as Facebook.com and Evite.com to organize ad hoc physical gatherings within social groups (Booher et al., 2007; Golder et al., 2007; Joinson, 2008). The technologies represent online shared interactive spaces that afford a rather wide range of social interactions and potentially a large and wide range of membership (Jones et al., 2004). According to a Pew Internet & American Life Project (2007) survey, ‘Three out of four social networking teens (72%) say they use the sites to make plans with their friends.’ Despite popular use, little is known about how social groups use social networking technologies for social event coordination.

What makes the question of how people coordinate social events using Facebook interesting is that the technology lacks the structures and processes to support the coordination processes of a group. Conventional temporal coordination mechanisms include social presence and process structures for scheduling, synchronization, and allocation of different activities (McGrath, 1984, 1991). However, Facebook.com represents asynchronous technology with no explicit process structures for temporal coordination. How do social groups overcome the limitations of the technology to accomplish event management? The exploration of this question will help generate theoretical and empirical understanding of information technology (IT) support for temporal coordination in social groups.

## Background

Facebook is one of the most popular social networking sites. Such sites are commonly characterized as online shared interactive spaces, in which a group of people use a repertoire of technological features (e.g., forums, newsgroups, messaging, etc.) to carry out a wide range of social interactions (Jones et al., 2004; Horowitz, 2005). The academic studies about Facebook have mostly examined such issues as identity (Stutzman, 2006; DiMicco and Millen, 2007), privacy and information disclosure (Gross and Acquisti, 2005; Jones and Soltren, 2005), self-presentation (Walther et al., 2008, 2009), and social capital (Ellison et al., 2007). A few studies have examined temporal patterns in Facebook use. One study conducted on a US college campus suggests that Facebook use is lightest during the weekends and heaviest during study times, suggesting that Facebook use parallels schoolwork and computerrelated activities (Golder et al., 2007).

The use of Facebook to organize social events has grown with the use of other Facebook applications (Pew Internet & American Life Project, 2007). In Facebook, members can create event pages and post messages on a wall. Access to pages and the wall can be restricted to a predefined social group. In addition to these shared spaces, members can also send private messages and ‘pokes,’ which are contentless messages.

The event pages function as invitations and online sources of information about the event, and enable sharing, storage, and retrieval of key information about the event. The creator (administrator) of the event is a role that Facebook assigns to the person(s) who constructs the event pages. The creator (or designated administrators) can change the event pages at any time. Figure 1 provides an example of an event page comprised of different subsets of information; Event Info, Time and Place, Contact Info, Description, and Confirmed Guests.

The wall is Facebook’s discussion board for the event pages. The wall facilitates ad hoc discussion and problem solving about the event among members as well as between the event creator and the members. Ideas, inquiries, and responses to inquiries are interwoven with various social remarks on the wall, and it can be turned off by the creator at any time. Table 1 compares Facebook event pages with Evite, another social networking technology that has event coordination capabilities. Compared to Evite, Facebook offers more choice in allowing creators to control the guest list with three distinct access options. Facebook’s notification feature is similar to Evite’s, because notifying members about important issues is at the discretion of the creator. However, Evite does not have a discussion board similar to the Facebook wall.

## Theoretical framing

In his seminal work, McGrath (1991) conceptualized group coordination processes to involve task production (problem solving and task performance) and social support (member support and group well-being). The group member’s familiarity with the task, other members, and media all affect how challenging it is to coordinate a group (McGrath, 1991). The more familiarity a group member has with the task, members, and media being used, the less pressing the coordination demands (McGrath, 1991).

With Facebook, familiarity with the task, members, and media should be high and not pose coordination challenges. Social events and their scheduling are commonly reoccurring tasks. Studies have found that a majority of ‘Facebook friends’ are individuals who know each other from the offline world (Ellison et al., 2007; Lewis et al., 2008; Mayer and Puller, 2008). Furthermore, media familiarity is high, as most users use Facebook daily (Ross et al., 2009).

However, Facebook’s lack of synchronicity poses coordination challenges for scheduling and allocation of different activities. Asynchronous technology environments suffer from ambiguity in schedule deadlines, conflicting temporal requirements and interests, and suboptimal allocation of time to tasks (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001). McGrath and Hollingshead (1994) noted that computermediated asynchronous technologies are not well suited to supporting tasks such as negotiation, conflict management, and coordination in which joint outcomes are important. Carte and Chidambaran (2004) similarly argued in their Capabilities-based theory of Technology Deployment that asynchronous technologies that limit normal language and speech patterns will prove more useful at the beginning of group work, while synchronous technologies afford support for later stages. Carte and Chidambaran (2004) based their temporal IT use conjectures on the midpoint transition of Gersick’s (1988, 1989) punctuated equilibrium model. Carte and Chidambaran (2004) suggested that different bundles of capabilities of online group technologies are used and others neglected before and after the midpoint transition of a group’s activities.

In the punctuated equilibrium model, Gersick (1988, 1989) argued that team members coordinate by pacing their interaction. Gersick observed that groups demonstrate an observable transition in pacing roughly at the temporal midpoint, when half of the group’s time toward task completion had elapsed. The groups became markedly more intense in their interaction after the midpoint. The greater interactivity in turn allows greater synchronicity, that is, more simultaneity in action and greater shared understanding (Dennis et al., 2008).

The punctuated equilibrium model, specifically the notion of midpoint transition, has been empirically validated in different task contexts. Other studies found that the half-way point marks a transition, but that the increase in activity is more gradual closer to the final deadline (Waller et al., 2002) than in Gersick’s original studies. Other studies found that even when the deadlines shift, the midpoint still serves as a punctuation point, helping to provide a semi-structure around which the group evaluates its work and makes choices to meet the pending deadlines (Waller et al., 2002). With groups that already know each other, the punctuated equilibrium model captures the group task progress (progress of group problem solving activities) rather than group development (psychological and emotional aspects of group maturation) (Seers and Woodruff, 1997; Chang et al., 2006).

Although Gersick’s (1988) original model was developed in a face-to-face context, others have replicated the model in online contexts in which project teams pursue a shared goal that can be achieved only by working together (e.g., Jarvenpaa et al., 2004). Michinov and Michinov (2007) found the midpoint transition in an online learning forum, but with some modifications. At the midpoint and thereafter, an online learning group showed a decline in taskoriented communication, motivation, and positive mood, not a marked increase as in the original Gersick model (1988, 1989). Michinov and Michinov (2007) speculated that the groups may have resorted to the increasing use of synchronous communications (phone and interactive chat) after the midpoint, but the use of these media was not verifiable by the researchers.

It is important to note that all the above research related to work groups in which members came together to complete a work task such as to make a decision within a predefined time span. For example, in Gersick’s (1989) laboratory study, all members could see a clock, and when one team member realized half-time had passed s/he informed the rest of the group. All successful teams made a time comment at or around the midpoint of the allotted time. Furthermore, these teams had a coherent goal to strive toward. Such a coherent goal is not necessarily present in social events. Although events are conceptualized as tasks, they may comprise a number of varied purposes, which may obfuscate a clear sense of goal coherence in interaction.

## Exploratory research questions

On Facebook, the event pages and wall are the locus of interaction between the event creator and other social group members who are coordinating a social event. The postings on the wall relate either to the event (task production) or to maintaining a social relationship with members (social support). Here, we focus on postings as a form of group interaction. It is through the group’s use that the technology can influence group coordination processes (Kane and Alavi, 2008).

Question 1: How do groups pace their postings on a Facebook event wall? Does the pacing exhibit a midpoint transition? How groups pace their postings may depend on what the postings are meant to convey. On Facebook event wall, postings may convey information transmission, or conveyance, of the logistics of the event. Postings may also convey convergence, or development of a shared understanding of joint issues and resolution of any conflicts that might arise (Dennis et al., 2008). The research generally suggests that conveyance tends to be more pronounced early on and convergence later in a group’s coordination (Carte and Chidambaran, 2004). Convergence is more impeded by asynchronous technology than conveyance. Convergence requires ‘the ability to support individuals working together at the same time with a shared pattern of coordinated behavior, while conveyance processes have a lesser need for synchronicity’ (Dennis et al., 2008: 576).

However, conveyance may also be challenged with Facebook. Conveyance requires members to provide responses to previous communicative exchanges (Herring, 1999). The responses to previous messages should be timely (Kalman and Rafaeli, 2005; Walther, 2006) as members become frustrated with longer than expected waiting times to their inquiries (Rintel and Pittam, 1997). In established social circles, expectations of quick response times are somewhat relaxed (Walther and Bunz, 2005).

Nevertheless, the asynchronous nature of the technology may diminish the likelihood of a response as well as subsequent postings. Response latencies are understood as the difference in time between consecutive postings (Kalman and Rafaeli, 2005). The large response times between postings make it difficult to anchor group members to a common theme, such as linking tasks to certain times (Herring, 1999). They can also decrease members’ motivation to use the wall as issues become more urgent and the wall looses utility.

With Facebook, rapid responses may only be possible if members exhibit ‘pull behavior’; that is, if they monitor the wall for discussion. Users are not notified of activity on walls via any push-based features. As their use of Facebook event pages grows, they have an increasing number of events to monitor. Furthermore, event pages do not support strands of discussion: All topics are discussed on the same wall, which creates a high level of fragmentation; and important questions can become buried in unrelated postings at any time.

Gersick’s group development model theorizes that groups also vary the pacing of socio-emotional activities during the group’s lifetime. In studying face-to-face groups, Gersick (1988) found that groups had a period of socialization at the onset of the event, which persisted until the midpoint. After the midpoint, less social content is exchanged.

In the literature on computer-mediated communication, a different pattern regarding socio-emotional activities has emerged. Because there is a lack of visual cues, computer-mediated teams need more time to develop relationships and, hence, socio-emotional communication prevails throughout the group’s communication (Walther, 1992; Chidambaram, 1996; Wilson et al., 2006). Social groups are also likely to value socio-emotional information such as jokes, as it helps to promote the commitment and longevity of an online group beyond the specific task (i.e., event) (Arguello et al., 2006). Admittedly, excessive socialemotional information might hinder event coordination (task production), particularly when it distracts attention from outstanding task-related inquiries or shifts the topic of discussion.

Yet, socio-emotional communication might take on particular importance in asynchronic and pull-based contexts such as Facebook. We conceptualize social postings to represent those postings that are not directly related to the event and exhibiting or invoking a social relationship. Social postings may indicate that members are actively checking the event page. Social postings also promote more back and forth between members. This interactivity might help to overcome the asynchronous character of the technology. If members are returning to the wall for social posts then they may also participate in more logistical discussions.

Question 2: How does the role of a leader affect interactive use of Facebook for event coordination?

Gersick (1988) depicted successful groups as ‘self-organizing’ teams and did not focus on how specific roles such as a ‘leader’ influence the group’s task production and social support. Research on online environments has implied that leadership is an emergent task shared among all members rather than a formal role or identity tied to a specific member (Kayworth and Leidner, 2002). In their review, Bell and Kozlowski (2002) identified the two primary leadership functions in virtual teams to be performance management (i.e., production) and team development (i.e., social support). They specifically argued that the leader’s role was not to monitor and control but rather to motivate a self-organizing group to actively participate and work toward achieving its goals. Others have described leadership in online environments as a set of tasks that are dynamically executed by different members, decentralized, and emergent (Reeves et al., 2008; Weisband, 2008). Those who emerge as leaders contribute content and undertake community building more than the other members (Butler et al., 2007). Research on non-work related online communities (Bock et al., 2008) has found that emergent leaders have a significant impact on others’ participation.

In the few studies of online communities that have examined the role of formal leaders, such leaders may be referred to as ‘owners,’ ‘administrators,’ ‘hosts,’ or ‘wizards.’ Naturally, as the level of responsibility increases, the importance and impact of the formal leader on group dynamics increases. This role is further defined and reinforced through community structure and rules (e.g., Butler et al., 2008).

In our study, leaders take the form of Facebook event page creators (or designated administrators), who are the only ones able to edit the background information of the events, event descriptions, and privacy and openness features. The leaders (creator and administrators) may play a key role and may have a critical impact on the postings of the rest of the social group.

## Methodology

To explore the first research question surrounding pacing, we analyzed the frequency of postings through the study of the response latency of wall postings and the activity around the midpoint. To explore the second question, we analyzed how the creator’s postings affected the group’s interactivity.

## Facebook events

We analyzed 294 Facebook events to understand how social groups used the event pages to organize events and resolve problems associated with them (e.g., transportation, timing of the event, assigning responsibility for the supplies). All the event pages and the wall postings were digitally saved for manual data coding. Previous content analyses of web content support the use of manual coders and small samples (Schultz, 1999; Trammell et al., 2006). Prior samples have ranged from 43 to 358.

To examine how the type of event may impact pacing, we examined two types of events: trips and parties. Exploring two different categories of behavior is consistent with prior research such as Cha et al. (2007). These two categories of trips and parties, built into the event tool, are expected to exhibit coordination capability. The event pages’ other categories such as Education and Causes are primarily publicity devices.

We gathered event pages and walls for 83 trips and 211 parties between spring 2007 and spring 2008, selecting a full year of events in order to minimize any seasonal bias. Events that took place in the summer months were rare, and we saw event spikes around weekends. Our selection process involved identifying events after which roughly a week had elapsed since they had taken place, in order to guarantee no activity occurred subsequent to our analysis.

We selected an event for further analysis if (1) it involved a trip or a party and (2) if the wall of the event had a minimum of four different individuals who contributed to discussion. This second step was necessary to ensure that there was the potential for some problem solving (convergence) among multiple members. We excluded from our study community events that targeted entire communities, as we are interested how social groups use Facebook to organize events.

Our initial search for events was limited to a university network in Midwestern USA (hereafter University network). We expanded the search for events outside of the University network to Facebook ‘Global’ network to ensure that the results were not idiosyncratic to that university. By spring 2007, Facebook had expanded beyond school-based networks allowing members without university email addresses to register. Facebook ‘Global’ network is accessible to all Facebook members. These events do not require affiliation with a particular network and they were open events, meaning that anyone could read the wall of the event. The sample is almost evenly divided between the University and Global network. One hundred and fiftyeight events are from Global network and 136 are from University network.

The events were found by searching for Trips and Parties. We only included events that were in the English language. Most such events on Global Network originated in the United Kingdom or Canada. We sorted the events chronologically, gathering them through time until we reached 300 events from University and Global networks. Six events were deleted for a variety of reasons, including use of multiple languages in postings.

Our sample did not include any closed events either in University or Global networks. Open events represent a convenience sample but we have no reason to believe that closed events would be necessarily coordinated in different ways than open events. A study of open events should help to shed light how the social groups are affected by asynchronous technology and may overcome it. Studies of blogs have excluded content that are password protected (Trammell et al., 2006).

## Coding

Given the exploratory nature of the study, we sought a coding scheme that captured the common features of postings and provided data to examine the research questions. The coding scheme required a manual coder to assess the types of activity before and after the midpoint, but the actual calculation and determination of the midpoint did not require a human coder. The coding scheme underwent multiple iterations, as we derived it empirically based on Facebook event pages and walls. Two independent raters compared coding at each iteration of the scheme to test the replicability of the coding. The coding scheme was refined until the degree of agreement between the two coders was acceptable for an exploratory study (Kappa Coefficient: 0.696). One coder was unfamiliar with the specific research questions under investigation. The coding scheme is included in the Appendix.

Our initial analysis of the event pages indicated that group coordination was largely spurred by inquiries on the wall, as it was the shared interactive space. Thus, we examined postings in terms of inquiries and responses to those inquiries. The unit of analysis was an inquiry or a response that represented a single posting by a single Facebook member on the wall at a particular point in time. An inquiry/response could be one sentence or multiple sentences that were part of the same posting. We numbered each unique inquiry as well as the responses to that inquiry, and recorded the time of postings in the event life (see Table 2).

We further broke down the type of inquiries into simple inquiries (conveyance) and complex inquiries (convergence). Simple inquiries corresponded to requests for information such as the clarification of logistics. Simple inquiries were coded if one or more of the following applied: (1) information seeking, (2) short response, (3) no high-level group involvement, and (4) not critical for the event to continue. An example of a simple inquiry is: ‘YEAH!!!! Can I bring Sether?’ The complex inquiries involved some form of joint problem solving: ‘How can we be sure that everyone shows up on time?’ Complex inquires were seen to require more convergence than simple inquiries. Others (e.g., Jones et al., 2004) have used message length as an indication of a type of message. Our study relied on actual content analysis for simple and complex inquiries.

The determination of the midpoint for each event required us to record the time span between the first posting on wall and the actual event. We also recorded changes in time because changing the end point of the event also changes its midpoint. These often had to be inferred from the context of the conversation. We calculated the midpoint by assuming a Tinitial ¼ 0 at the time of the first posting and Tfinal at the time of the event. The midpoint was calculated as (Tinitial-Tfinal)/2. This is different from previous calculations of the midpoint that have relied on a 10–60-s time scale (Gersick, 1989; Waller et al., 2002) derived from hour-long experiments. For the purposes of our study, this approach did not make sense because in the context of asynchronous technology, time between postings can be large and highly variable. Fine-grained time analysis adds little value beyond demonstrating the highly varied nature of the data. Thus, we implemented a simple midpoint calculation to capture a macro scale trend. Zaheer et al. (1999) suggest that the timescale ought to have an objectively or socially constructed nature. Our analysis chose the former; the objective is understanding the pattern of behavior in terms of an already established midpoint.

Social postings were coded if the postings exhibited or invoked a social relationship. An example of a social posting is: ‘just really thought i’d never have to see flannery again :p.

We determined the creator’s involvement by summing the number of creator/administrator postings. Additionally, we captured the number of postings and the number of unique people who made postings.

We also captured a number of other variables. The size of the event was recorded; this included the number of invited and the number confirmed to the event (no information was available regarding actual attendance). The date and type of event, either party or trip, was recorded. We coded postings that directed members to use other media as phone, email, and other.

Consistent with Gersick (1989), we also coded statements that made direct references to time. Postings concerning when something should be done or how long an action would take, or references to finishing on time were also coded as time comments (Waller et al., 2002). These types of messages included things like: ‘8 days left!’

## Data analysis

We controlled for three variables in the quantitative analysis: (1) whether the event was from Global or University network, (2) size of the event, (3) whether the event was a trip or a party. The control variables proved insignificant based on the Tests of Between-Subjects Effects. Hence, for the remainder of the analysis, we collapsed trips and parties, and the events from the two networks.

Besides standard descriptive statistics, we used the Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test as a non-parametric test because the sample of postings on the walls could not be assumed to be normal. Results were similar to traditional paired t-tests, and hence, we present the quantitative results from traditional t-tests. We calculated the average response latencies of posting via methods used by Kalman et al. (2006).

The average response latency between postings on the wall was 1.7 days (41 h). The average response latency of nearly 2 days is quite surprising given that members report logging into Facebook multiple times a day (Pew Internet & American Life Project, 2007). The aggregate timing pattern of postings on the wall were, however, similar to those found for email and Google Answers (Kalman et al., 2006). A study of Usenet messages found an average response time of 1 day, with 90% of the responses created in less than 3 days (Jones et al., 2004).

The temporal distribution of the postings was highly skewed, with a stretched out and rapidly diminishing thin line to the right (see Figure 3). This might mean that unless follow-on postings appeared on the wall within a couple of days, the wall became inactive.

In examining the postings before and after the midpoint, we found that the patterns were, indeed, different. There was a distinctly observable change in Facebook use before and after the midpoint. However, rather than intensifying after the midpoint, as was the case with Gersick (1988), there was a significant drop in postings after the midpoint. For all of the events, an average of 7.903 postings occurred before the midpoint and 2.603 occurred after. There were significantly more postings before the midpoint than after, using a matched pair two-sample t-test (t ¼ 17.93; df ¼ 297; P ¼ 0.000).

Question 2: How does the role of a leader affect interactive use of Facebook for event coordination?

In addition to creating the event and setting the initial guest list, the creator (administrator) could engage by (1) posting on the wall, (2) editing the details of the event, (3) messaging members about changes to the event, reminders, and clarification. The second method could be inferred by carefully examining the discussion on the wall and the event details. The third method, messaging, was indiscernible. Thus, our focus was predominantly how the leader acted through the wall, and, in so far as it was evident, editing of event details.

Of the 294 events, 191 had creators that posted on wall (so called active creators). The mean number of creator postings for events with active creators was 2.37 postings. Creator posts were significantly higher before the midpoint than after the midpoint (t ¼ 6.974, df ¼ 188, 0.000). Response ratios, or the number of response to inquiries, were also greater when a creator was involved. ## Discussion

We began our exploratory study in an attempt to understand the increasing popularity of Facebook for event coordination, despite the technology’s asynchronicity. Prior studies had found that sometimes members demonstrate creative uses of technology to overcome its limitations (Walther and Tidwell, 1995). To explore whether this was the case with Facebook, we undertook an exploratory study to examine patterns of use on Facebook: (1) How are the postings paced on Facebook wall? (2) How does a leader affect interactive use of Facebook for event coordination?

We frame the discussion about pacing and the role of the leader in terms of synchronicity. Watson-Manheim and Be´langer (2007) suggest that simple inquires require relatively less synchronicity than complex inquiries. We related simple inquiries to conveyance. Dennis et al. (2008) argue that conveyance is affected less by asynchronicity than convergence. However, we found no difference in response rates for simple and complex inquiries. This may suggest that the technology was not sufficiently synchronous for responding to even simple inquiries on the wall. The lack of synchronicity did not, however, limit the popular use of the event pages as static text message boards. The event pages provided a convenient source of information about the event. However, there was not necessarily much interactivity on Facebook walls.

Facebook event pages and walls exhibited less synchronicity in terms of responsiveness than that reported with other asynchronous technologies (Kalman et al., 2006). Not only were long delays of several days typical, but many inquiries never received a response on the wall. Jones et al. (2004) attributed delayed response times within asynchronous technology to having too many issues under discussion concurrently. Information overload is not a probable explanation for our results, given the relatively few number of inquiries per event.

However, unawareness that an outstanding inquiry existed may be an explanation. Currently, no feature exists that alerts and consolidates wall activity for social group members; only a small box on the right hand corner of the screen indicates whether an event is happening that day. This box is even easy to overlook. Most group members in the social group may be unaware that an inquiry is waiting for a response. Particularly complex inquiries, by nature, require more social group members to participate in issue resolution or, at a minimum, to be aware of its existence and resolution.

Even if the group members are aware of an inquiry, they may not be motivated because the social group’s behavior is not goal-directed, at least not to the extent found with many work groups. Given the ratios of unique posters to invitees to an event, many invitees do not participate in discussions on the wall even if the discussion pertains directly to them. Possibly, the presence of other communication channels, such as mobile phones that are more synchronous, may lead to less use of the wall. In this sense, the event page and the wall loses its original intent of serving as the locus of communication and coordination for the event.

## Midpoint transition

The Facebook wall was primarily used in the early phases of event coordination. The majority of postings occurred prior to the midpoint; generally, discussion became less frequent closer to the time of the event. Research has consistently found marked increases in activity prior to deadlines, including increases in bargaining prior to deadlines (Lim and Murnighan, 1994). In online learning environments, however, studies have not found this phenomenon to be evident (Michinov and Michinov, 2007). Gersick found that a distinct midpoint was due to explicit group discussion surrounding the amount of time remaining and/or elapsed, and that there was an increase in time comments near the midpoint. In our study, only a handful (eight) of the events contained such explicit pacing markers; and none of them bore any relation to the midpoint. This type of shared realization of time may be difficult to generate when members (1) are not physically present, (2) do not posses knowledge of whether other group members are viewing the event page, and (3) are probably not viewing the page concurrently.

All in all, Facebook social group members exhibited little evidence that they overcame the limits of asynchronous technology and devised methods of conveyance and convergence to accomplish the timing and scheduling of events. This was despite the fact that members were at least somewhat familiar with the social groups, the task was familiar, and the technology was familiar. The results suggest that a critical boundary variable that may need to be included in the discussions of the technology synchronicity is the level of committal (see Dennis et al., 2008). When committal is low, the technology may only be peripheral to the coordination or provide very limited functionality (e.g., static event information). In the overall use of Facebook, the creation of and access to event pages may remain as a non-committal activity; event pages and wall are not the primary driver of traffic (Joinson, 2008).

## Pattern of social activity inconsistent with Gersick’s findings

Gersick (1988) noted a period of socialization at the onset of an event, which persisted until the midpoint. At the midpoint, the group became markedly more task-oriented. This pattern was not apparent in our study of Facebook event pages. Event pages were created by and for individuals in the same social network, who had probably interacted on and offline. Even if social group members were not ‘Facebook friends,’ their invitation to the same event pointed to the existence of some mutual friend. Provided that the majority of discussion on the event pages occurred prior to the temporal midpoint, it stood to reason that the number of social postings might also be greater prior to the midpoint. However, no such difference was found. We speculate that, because personal information about others attending the event is readily available on member profiles, depending on privacy settings, such personal information might have eliminated the need for periods of intense socialization. Finally, the types of social postings exemplified ‘inside jokes’ between members, which is clearly different from the socialization in Gersick’s (1989, 1989) studies.

## Creator involvement

Creator postings were both more numerous and more frequent prior to the midpoint than after. The decline in the level of group member postings coincides with less frequent creator postings after the midpoint. In Facebook, a creator is a formal role that did not exist in Gersick’s (1988, 1989) studies. The creator (administrator) possesses a number of important powers and may be more than one person. S/he is the only group member who can edit the content of the event. The criticality of leader corroborates previous research. Focusing on leadership characteristics and sources of power, Bock et al. (2008) found that an involved leader in non-work-related online communities motivated group members. Bock et al.’s study of leadership in a virtual environment made an underlying assumption of an involved and responsive leader. In our study, the high level of responsibility and authority granted to the creator seemed to dampen member motivation, especially if the creator was not responsive. Giving almost complete control to the creator, Facebook’s event pages did not promote participative leadership.

## Implications

We provide preliminary implications to theory with additional implications to the redesign of Facebook.

Implications for the theory of social group problem solving and IT use

Conventional models assume that groups solve problems within a predefined time span. Time is linear, and groups carefully appropriate time to meet the time constraints. Prior studies on group problem solving teams have found that task groups use semi-structures such as temporal midpoints to promote task progress in the group. Semistructures are socially constructed markers that help to organize and allocate time to task activities but at the same time provide flexibility for emergent behaviors. Midpoint transition is one such socially constructed marker. Gersick (1988, 1989) found that the groups used a midpoint of a group’s life as a way to pattern the group interaction to meet the time constraints of the task deliverable. The midpoint transition has been found to help groups with specific deliverables and deadlines organize their work (Waller et al., 2002).

This study suggests that the midpoint cannot be conceptualized in the same manner when new channels of communication are introduced, synchronicity is diminished, and social presence is reduced. Thus future research must reconsider the conceptualization of midpoint and or explore other semi-structures in social groups.

## Contribution to computer-mediated communication research

The current paper contributes to research on computermediated communication and organization of ad hoc events and gatherings. Various information and communication tools are being designed to plan, organize, and attend meetings and social group gatherings (Kowitz et al., 2005; Ng et al., 2006; Booher et al., 2007). IT designers have predominantly a tool-based view of event scheduling systems. That is, they see the tool as enhancing the way inviters and invitees process and distribute event-related information. Orlikowski and Iacono (2001) note that observed usage and behavioral patterns are not necessarily those that had been anticipated in the design of technology systems. Although the IT tools available through Facebook can improve the efficiency of information flow, they do not appear to readily facilitate interactive discussion about events with a large number of people. Our results suggest that the tool view of Facebook event pages and walls does not reflect member practice. A more embedded view of the social group’s overall communication environment is needed to understand temporal coordination behaviors (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001). Other information systems scholars have offered similar arguments about IT and temporal coordination (e.g., Lee and Liebenau, 2000, Lee and Whitley 2002; Shen et al., 2005).

## Redesign of Facebook

Currently, much of the problem solving that is attempted on Facebook event pages involves inquiries that are not responded to. Furthermore, event pages are dominant in the early stages of planning an event and quickly loose their utility. Thus, a redesign of the event pages should seek to increase the responsiveness of members as well as expand overall usage into later parts of the event planning life cycle.

A lack of synchronicity makes checking the event page inconvenient and inefficient for members. Members must check each event page independently because there is no automatic notification mechanism for postings, discussion, or changes on the wall. Only the creator of the event can send notifications regarding the event. Facebook’s minifeed is a newsfeed intended to update members about what activities are occurring within their social networks. Some topics members are notified of via the minifeeed include postings on other friends’ walls, uploading of photographs, and editing profiles. Adding updates about event activity (edits and wall postings) would decrease members’ monitoring costs.

Many postings on event page walls are irrelevant, containing little task or socio-emotional content. The creation of a system whereby members can designate their postings as highly important and inform invitees of these postings could also increase synchronicity and use. Member feedback systems can highlight which posts are of particular importance by allowing members to rate each post. These systems have proven valuable in other contexts such as message boards (Thurman, 2008).

The final details of the event are located in the description, but only the creator and appointed administrators have control over the description. A creator may not update the details as needed. Allowing members to change the description allows members to become more vested in the event and increase their participation. Allowing initial event descriptions to have ‘open time’ might also increase participation. Currently, Facebook forces the creator to select a specific time rather than allowing the time to emerge gradually as the invitees reach a consensus. Our preliminary analysis suggests higher levels of posting activity for those events whose time had to be negotiated. The open time provided a shared goal among the social group members around which to rally.

## Conclusion

This study was exploratory and has a number of limitations. The Facebook technology is rapidly evolving and uses are ever expanding. However, we limited our focus on Facebook wall and event pages in studying coordination of social events. We excluded communications such as messages sent by the creator outside of Facebook wall and event pages. Our exclusion of messages should not be a significant factor, as others have found them infrequently used (Golder et al., 2007). Only open events were analyzed. The extent to which Facebook event pages and wall facilitated the success of the offline event was not studied. Facebook is available in 54 languages. The same patterns of behavior may not be found across cultures and languages. These limitations provide opportunities for future research.

Even with these limitations, this study encourages others to examine group coordination in social networking sites. We know of no prior study that has examined event coordination in open shared interactive spaces. By grounding our exploration in existing formal theories of group task pacing and media use, we suggest that social group behavior may exhibit important differences from work group behavior. We related the observed behaviors to the asynchronous character of the technology. However, future researchers should explore alternative explanations such as the informality and the lack of goal orientation of Facebook environment that may create different expectations and desires for event coordination from the members’ point of view.

## About the authors
