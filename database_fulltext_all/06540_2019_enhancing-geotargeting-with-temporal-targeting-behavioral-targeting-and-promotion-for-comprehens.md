# Enhancing geotargeting with temporal targeting, behavioral targeting and promotion for comprehensive contextual targeting

Shaohua Lian, Tingting Cha, Yunjie Xu

School of Management, Fudan University, 670 Guoshun Road, Shanghai, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Geotargeting Temporal targeting Behavioral targeting Online-to-offline Field experiment

## A B S T R A C T

Ofline stores rely on customers who are geographically close to the store. However, how to target at online users to draw them to the ofline store remains a challenge. This study investigates how to enhance geographical targeting by a suite of other targeting strategies, including behavioral targeting, temporal targeting, and use of discount in an online-to-ofline commerce context, to form a more comprehensive contextual targeting strategy. Our randomized field experiment in the restaurant industry reveals that nearer distance, right timing, matching with users' preference for service type, and users' recent visits to food websites all increase the ad click-through rate. Furthermore, a preference for on-site service intensifies the negative effect of distance, and the recency of food-related website browsing mitigates the distance efect. These findings show how diferent targeting stra tegies complement and enhance each other in afecting online users' responses to ad of an ofline store.

## 1. Introduction

The internet enables bricks-and-mortar retailers to find online users and bring them to ofline stores [17]. As people spend time online using personal computers (PCs) and mobile devices, retailers can obtain in formation such as their location, time, and browsing history to target an ad at those who are likely to visit an ofline store [17]. This marketing practice is known as the online-to-ofline (O2O) commerce, which has been demonstrated to be an efective way to improve the sales of an ofline store [26,37].

How to target at users to generate a higher ad response rate in the context of O2O commerce? Because the means for tracking users' whereabouts on mobile devices are precise and convenient, a number of studies have explored how to personalize advertising based on mobile users' geographical information (K. [28]). This target method is called geographical targeting (i.e., geotargeting). Geotargeting is considered as an efective method [10,14,30,33,42]. The fast increasing body of geotargeting applications (Yuxin [5]) testifies its usefulness. A consistent finding of extant research is that users within a certain radius of the ofline store are regarded by the firm as better targets for a mobile promotion than those at a further distance [10,14,30]. The rationale of geotargeting is that distance incurs a transaction cost for users, hence the closer is a user to the ofline store, the lower is the travel cost [45,48]. Moreover, users in the proximity of a store at the promotion moment are also more inclined to impulsive purchase [10].

Can firms further enhance geotargeting for a better performance? One direction is to enlarge the radius of geotargeting. To help users overcome the cost of distance, firms can ofer a discount to distant users [11,42], so that distant users become more willing to come. However, this practice incurs a cost. Another direction is to complement geotargeting with other targeting strategies. A general practice in previous studies is to target an ad indiscriminately at all users within a specified radius [10,30,45], as if they were equally receptive of the promotion. To tap on other dimensions of targeting, a few exceptional studies by Luo et al. [30] and Molitor et al. [33] have suggested an interaction between temporal targeting and geotargeting. Promotion at the right time and right place is found to be more efective. Other study has considered the interaction between location (i.e., city) and weather [33]. Unfortunately, we have found few study that integrates geotargeting and behavioral targeting, although behavioral targeting has long been regarded as an efective targeting strategy because it considers users' preference and characteristics [3]. There is even fewer study that tests the composite efect of geotargeting, temporal targeting, and behavioral targeting together.

The purpose of this paper is to empirically examine how geotargeting can be enhanced by a suite of other targeting strategies, including behavioral targeting, temporal targeting, and use of discount in O2O commerce, to form a more comprehensive contextual targeting strategy. We argue that to better catch the “fish in the pond” of a geographic radius, firms may simultaneously adopt multiple targeting strategies. Not only each individual strategy contributes to targeting efectiveness, but also their interactions with geotargeting ofer added value to help firms overcome the negative efect of distance to users. A comprehensive contextual targeting strategy is efective because purchase decision is not only triggered by location match, but also need match and preference match [20].

To test our model, with the cooperation of a local restaurant, we pushed coupon ads to online PC users near and far. Because the restaurant ofered only on-site service and no delivery service, we could gauge how diferent targeting strategies jointly afect users' click of online ads. With a between-subject factorial field experiment, we analyzed how distance afects users' click of ad and how temporal and behavioral variables produce a main efect and moderate the efect of distance. Our findings show that a higher click-through rate for the restaurant ads stems from a nearer distance, the right timing of ad delivery, matching with users' preference for service type, and users' recent demonstration of consumption interest online. Furthermore, users who prefer on-site service are more sensitive to distance, while users who demonstrate a recent interest online are less sensitive to distance. We did not find the interaction efect between geotargeting and temporal targeting in this context.

This study makes a few interesting contributions to the geotargeting literature. First, we propose a set of behavioral variables to characterize users of the restaurant industry, including users' preference for on-site service (i.e., dining out) or delivery service and their recency of browsing food-related websites. Second, we show that behavioral targeting can enhance geotargeting. Finally, while previous targeting research has focused on mobile users, this study employs PC users as the study population. We demonstrate that when the location of PC users is available, geotargeting and other targeting strategies can be applied to PC users as well.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the literature related to geotargeting, temporal targeting, and behavioral targeting. We articulate the need for comprehensive contextual targeting and propose our hypotheses. Section 3 describes our experimental design and the data. Section 4 reports estimation results. Finally in Section 5, we provide a discussion on theoretical contributions, managerial implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Geographical targeting

Geotargeting refers to the practice of delivering personalized content or ads to online users based on their geographical location. Advertisers can obtain a PC user's location through an IP address, or a mobile user's location through GPS. The location-based services enable advertisers to deliver a text message, email alert, or app notification to mobile devices when a user enters the vicinity of a firm's location (a.k.a. geofencing) [10] or its competitors' locations (a.k.a. geoconquesting) [11].

Studies have indicated that the distance between a store and a user has a notable impact on a series of user decisions, such as ad clicking, coupon redemption, or purchase intention. According to the first law of geography by Tobler [43], “all things are related, but near things are more related than far things.” Geographic proximity increases the relevance to users and thus improves the efectiveness of online adver tising [34]. Transaction utility theory in economics also supports this intuition [42]. Travel cost to ofline stores includes monetary cost and opportunity cost of time [12]. Thus, whether a user reacts to an online ad depends on the tradeof between the travel costs and the attrac tiveness of the ad.

Table 1 outlines the relevant research on geotargeting. According to the definition of the geographical location, we can classify these studies into two categories. One category focuses on location type, such as private or public locations. The other category focuses on distance, which refers to the geographical distance from the focal store to the location where a user receives online ads. This study adopts the second definition of location.

The main efect of location on user behavior is consistent and evi dent. When locations are classified as a store's own location, a nearby competitor's location, and a benchmark in-between, Fong et al. [11] found that competitive locational targeting is more efective than targeting at the benchmark location even with deep discounts. Companies may use competitive targeting to generate incremental sales without cannibalizing profits. When locations are classified as in-store or elsewhere, Ketelaar et al. [24] demonstrated that when the activity at users in-store location is congruent with an ad, the ad is more efective. However, outside of the store, mobile ads attract more attention than do point-of-sale online ads. When location is defined as distance to home, Ghose et al. [14] demonstrated that nearby stores or those appear at the top of the screen are much more likely to be clicked. These two efects are stronger on mobile phones than on personal computers. When location is the distance from user to store, Molitor et al. [33] revealed that distance is negatively correlated to coupon choice. Luo et al. [30] found that geographical proximity and short promotion leadtime both increase purchase. In addition, promotion lead-time has a negative efect for proximal users but an inverted-U efect for distant users. Fang et al. [10] revealed that location-based mobile promotions not only increase product sales in the contemporaneous period but also induce future sales.

## 2.2. The need for comprehensive contextual targeting

How can geotargeting be further enhanced with other targeting strategies? We propose that geotargeting in O2O contexts can be enhanced by a suite of other targeting strategies to form a comprehensive contextual targeting strategy. In this study, contextual targeting refers to the practice when a firm employs a comprehensive set of targeting strategies based on users' location, time, behavior, and other contextual factors. The ideal of precision targeting in the mobile age is “the delivery of the right information to the right person at the right location and time” ([45], p. 1338). Once this goal is achieved, the situational relevance of the commercial information is considered to be high to a user [45]. In the marketing literature, this is known as contextual marketing [23], which emphasizes the importance of context besides content. Moreover, the contextual marketing theory suggests that temporal and spatial targeting may have an interactive impact on consumer behavior. For instance, the research by Luo et al. [30] and Molitor et al. [33] has demonstrated the nuanced interaction efect between time and location.

Unfortunately, existing research on geotargeting has not integrated it with users' behavior characteristic. Most efort has been focused on the “right location and time”, (e.g., [30]), but not the “right person”. Hence, a more comprehensive coverage of contextual factors with regard to the “person” dimension is necessary.

The research on personalization in the Information Systems literature ofers a suitable theoretical lens for a comprehensive contextual targeting strategy. The core tenet of personalization is to meet the preference of a user [20]. An individual's preference for a product can be either preexisting or constructed in situ [41]. Consequently, meeting one's preexisting and situational preference is the key to the efective ness of a promotion. Ho and Lim [20] diferentiated three types of matching in a contextual personalization. Location-matching refers to the proximity of a consumer to the place of purchase. Taste refers to idiosyncratic preference of an individual [19]. Taste is one's general inclination, hence represents one's stable preexisting preference. Tastematching is therefore the personalization of an ofer to match one's stable preference in a consumption context. We regard taste and preference as synonyms. In contrast, need is a personal circumstance in which something is required rather than desired [20]. Need is situational and dynamic. For instance, a meal at meal time is a need. Need-matching is the personalization of an ofer to match one's need of the moment.

Drawing on the typology of matching in personalization research [20], we seek to identify contextual factors that can be applied to complement and enhance geotargeting for a better performance. Concurring with the literature, we expect that the right timing of promotion enhances geotargeting because temporal targeting enhances needmatching. We further propose that matching of consumers' preference for service types (e.g., on-site vs. delivery service in the case of restaurant service) and recency of food-related website browsing behavior are taste-matching strategies to complement and enhance geotargeting. Finally, discount serves as a known strategy to overcome the distance efect.

## 2.3. Temporal targeting and its interaction with geotargeting

Temporal targeting refers to the advertising practice to identify an appropriate time to launch an ad. In online contexts, an advertiser's ad delivery time is a user's ad exposure time. Time is an indispensable dimension for improving the eficiency of advertising [31]. The role of time becomes even more crucial in the mobile context, where retailers can reach users anytime and anywhere [25,47]. The right timing often suggests a match to receivers' dynamic situational need. For instance, when one is in a shopping mall, a promotion of movie ticket might stimulate an impulsive purchase or facilitate a planned purchase of the trip [30]. Consequently, a customer is more likely to click an ad or to buy a product. Otherwise, disrupting users at the wrong time not only lowers the persuasiveness of an ad but also leads to consumers' negative attitude [4]. In short, temporal targeting appeals to the situational need-matching.

Studies have examined the contextual efects of time on consumer behavior in personalization [40]. For example, real-time targeting has been demonstrated to trigger users' purchase intentions in in-store promotions [21]. Coupons that target at customers at the time of purchase can increase sales [18]. Other studies have focused on the interaction between time and other factors, such as advertising intent, brand image [4], types of ad content [15], and spatial proximity [30]. The sales efects of employing temporal targeting and other targeting techniques together are typically less straightforward. For example, Molitor et al. [33] found that users are more sensitive to distance in the mornings compared with other time of the day. Goh et al. [15] found that the temporal proximity efect depends on the geographical proximity of a campaign.

In this paper, we propose that consumer decision stages serve as a guideline for choosing the right timing for temporal targeting. Consumer decision-making process comprises four distinct stages: information search, alternative evaluation, purchase decision, and postpurchase evaluation [39]. In the first two stages, consumers browse websites to search, compare, and evaluate diferent products before deciding on which product to purchase. We call the first two stages the pre-decision time. Research suggested that ad exposure at the pre-decision stage improves brand image and purchase intention [4]. This is because the pre-decision stage is the time when a consumer sees the need, and starts planning for the need. Thus, we expect that the ad exposures at the pre-decision time would yield a higher click-through rate.

Hypothesis 1. Ad exposure at the pre-decision time increases the probability of ad clicks for an ofline store.

In the context of meal services, when online users encounter an ad from a local restaurant at the pre-decision time, they have suficient time to schedule their travel to the ofline restaurant. Consequently, they are less concerned with the distance. By contrast, if they are exposed to the ad at the meal time, they may not be able to schedule suficient time for the travel. Even if they are close to the ofline store, they may ignore the ad because of other arranged plans. Furthermore, the need to eat becomes urgent at meal time, thus making a distant choice unattractive. Consequently, they become more sensitive to the distance efect. Therefore, we hypothesize that an interplay exists between ad exposure time and a user's distance to the ofline store.

Hypothesis 2. Ad exposure at the pre-decision time attenuates the negative efect of distance on ad clicks for an ofline store.

## 2.4. Behavioral targeting and its interaction with geotargeting

Behavioral targeting is a technique in which historical user behavior is leveraged to select the most relevant ads for users [3]. It is an application of modern statistical and machine learning methods for online advertising. Learning from a user's online behavior, especially with implicit feedback (i.e., sites visited and their demonstrated interest in a particular type of content), behavioral targeting seeks to deliver ads that a particular group of users is more likely to consider as appealing. Because behavioral targeting addresses the “right person” dimension, it is a valuable complementary component to other advertising techniques [6].

Besides a potentially higher response rate to online advertising [16,32], behavioral targeting has other advantages too. It may also create a greater utility for receivers by delivering a more relevant content. Furthermore, behavioral targeting has the potential to have a synergy with other targeting strategies to form contextual targeting [29].

## 2.4.1. User preference

The goal of behavior analysis is to use historical data to infer users stable product interest. Users' product preference is a crucial factor that influences their likelihood to click online ads [9,47]. By analyzing users' historical activities, retailers can construct user profiles and personalize ads, content, and recommendations accordingly [1]. For example, frequent browsing of car-related websites may indicate that a user is more likely to click a car ad.

Users' preference for meal services can be classified into on-site service and delivery service. Users' preference for two types of service can be inferred from their browsing history of food websites. We divide food websites into three categories. The first category is websites that provide information of restaurants that provide only on-site services. Users need to decide whether to travel to an ofline store for on-site service. The second category is websites that provide information of restaurant that provides only delivery service. Users can order a meal on these websites and have it delivered to their house. The third category is other food websites, such as websites providing recipes, sharing cooking videos, or providing information on local cuisine, but not meal service.

We assume that browsing websites in the first category is indicative of the preference for on-site service at ofline restaurants. Browsing websites in the second category is indicative of the preference for meal delivery services. We use the proportion of the two types of websites to represent the degree of users' existing preference for dining out or delivery service. A higher proportion of website visits in the first category indicates one's interest in dining out, hence it is positively related to the probability of ad clicks of a restaurant which provides on-site service. Reversely, a higher proportion of websites in the delivery service ca tegory is negatively related to the probability of ad clicks.

Hypothesis 3. A higher proportion of website browsing in on-site (delivery) service category is positively (negatively) related to probability of ad clicks for an ofline store.

A higher proportion of website visits in the on-site service category suggests that these users are more experienced in dining out at restaurants. They are also more experienced in making decisions regarding which restaurant to choose. Experienced decision makers tend to pay more attention to relevant and important information [7], that is, they are more sensitive to major decision factors. Distance is regarded by most diners as a major decision factor [35]. Therefore, onsite service consumers are more likely to be sensitive to it. Conceivable, every time when one considers a restaurant listed in a website, one would check its location, plan the travel route, and gauge the travel cost. In contrast, a higher proportion of web browsing in the delivery service category suggests that these users prefer staying at home and waiting for delivery. Because they do not travel to an ofline store, distance suggests only an expected delivery time which is not in their control. Thus, these users are not more sensitive to distance besides the main efect of distance. Based on this inference, we propose the interaction efect of user preference and distance:

Hypothesis 4. A higher proportion of website browsing in the on-site service category exacerbates the negative efect of distance on ad clicks for an ofline store.

## 2.4.2. Recency

Recency is a widely recognized indicator of user behavioral [22] because it suggests users' recent interest. Recency is the time that has elapsed since a user's last purchase of a product or last visit to a website. Studies have shown that recency is a useful to predict users' response likelihood [38]. The likelihood of recent users to engage in future transactions is higher than users who have not made a visit or purchase for a long time [44]. The rationale is that recency indicates one's recent preference. It suggests that the user may have a recently unmet need, or a recently developing interest. In this study, we use the number of days from the last visit to a food website to the first ad exposure time to represent the extent of a user's recency of interest. This knowledge of user ofers a firm an opportunity to remind lapsed users that it has been a while since their last visit or transaction and to ofer them an incentive to rekindle their patronage. Because recency suggests a recent preference, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5. A user's recency of visiting food websites positively afects the probability of ad clicks for an ofline store.

Recency may also enhance geotargeting. For a repeating need such as meals, a recent instance not only indicates that one has an interest in food-related information, but also suggests one's cognitive familiarity with the occasion. This phenomenon is known as the “recency efect” in cognitive psychology, which suggests that recent items may still be available in the short-term memory and thus are more likely to be recalled [36]. When recency is a signal of recent individual preference and interest, at least in a short-term sense, it implies that the user has a high involvement in such consumption activities. According to the motivation theory, an intrinsic motivation moderates the efect of one's perception of efort and cost [8]. Marketing research also indicates that consumers with high product involvement are less sensitive to cost [46]. Therefore, recency attenuates the negative efect of distance on ad click. Based on the inference, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 6. A user's recency of visiting food websites mitigates the negative efect of distance on ad clicks for an ofline store.

## 2.5. Discount and its interaction with geotargeting

Discount is a conventional promotion strategy to attract users from a further distance, and to mitigate the efect of travel cost. The rationale is straight forward. Because distance is a cost, a compensation of the travel cost neutralize the negative efect of distance. Previous research has found this strategy to be efective [11,42]. Therefore, we consider discount as an important component of the suite of contextual targeting and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7. The depth of discount mitigates the negative efect of distance on ad clicks for an ofline store.

## 3. Research methodology

## 3.1. Experimental design

We conducted a randomized field experiment to test the hypotheses. The advantage of a field experiment includes both generalizability and theoretical rigor. The collaborating restaurant served customers with midpriced local cuisine in a downtown area of a large city. Assisted by a network service provider (NSP) in China, we randomly pushed ad de signs to our subjects, who were PC users in the vicinity of this restaurant. The ad was delivered in two periods. The first was from August 3–5, 2017, and the second was from August 9–15, 2017. The weekend in-between was skipped. The exact location of the PC was provided by the NSP which implemented the local network and had the exact lo cation of each router. We tagged each ad with a unique identification number to make it traceable. When a user clicked an ad, it was recorded by a server. Because of the NSP's privacy policy, we cannot confirm whether users had redeemed our coupons in the restaurant. Thus, this study only focuses on the click-through rate of online ads.

The key manipulation of the experimental design is distance. Our experiment was a $2 \times 3 \times 2$ between-subjects factorial design with three variables: distance, discount, and advertising background. We identified two geofenced groups of PC users. The first group was between 500 m and 1000 m away from the restaurant, and the second group was between 2000 m and 2500 m. Subjects in the near and far geofences were randomly drawn from the total population in these fences. Users in the first group could walk to the restaurant, whereas those in the second group might need to take public transportation or drive to the restaurant. We included only users who had used the NSP services for at least 6 months. This filter ensured that the subjects had been there long enough to be familiar with the proximal area. We also included the restaurant address in the coupon ads. The shopping mall where the restaurant was located was well-known in the proximal area. Therefore, users seeing the ad would be aware of the distance from their location to the restaurant.

We utilized three levels of discount rate, namely 65%, 75%, or 85% of the original price. We also employed two versions of ad design which difered only in background. One version of the ad used signature city landmarks as a background (Appendix A.a), by which we hoped to arouse users' interest for local cuisine. Another version used a less known building (Appendix A.b). Ad background serves as a control variable in data analysis [13].

For each subject in our geofences, we randomly selected an ad from the six versions (discount × background) and delivered it to the web page the user was browsing. In total, the number of unique users who browsed our ads was 18,402, among which 9116 users were exposed to the ad only once.

## 3.2. Data

## 3.2.1. The variables

For geotargeting, we identified two geofenced groups of PC users. Users in the first group were nearer to the restaurant than those in the second group. We coded the variable Distance for the two groups as zero and one, respectively.

For temporal targeting, we created a variable MealPD to represent whether the ad delivery time was during the pre-decision time for meals. We assume that the regular pre-decision time for meals is 8:00 to 11:00 for lunch and 13:00 to 18:00 for dinner. We set MealPD to one if the ad delivery time was within the two periods, and zero otherwise.

For behavioral targeting, we counted the frequency of browsing diferent types of food websites for each user and calculate the proportion of websites in the first and second category separately. We labeled these two ratios as ShopRatio and HomeRatio. A higher ShopRatio indicates a preference for dining on-site and a higher HomeRatio indicates a preference for delivery service. In addition, we defined a variable Recency as the number of days from a user's last visit to a food website to the time of first ad exposure.

Finally, we defined Discount as the ratio of the discounted price to the regular price. A smaller Discount means a deeper discount rate.

## 3.2.2. Control variable

To control the variance explained by these factors that could influence the probability of ad clicks, we included them as control variables in our model. First, we created a variable Background for diferent background versions. We set Background to one for the version with signature local landmarks as a background, and zero otherwise.

Second, we created three variables to describe users' browsing history of food website. SiteNum represents the number of unique food websites that the user had visited. SiteFreq represents the users' frequency of visits. SiteHHI represents the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index of food websites, which indicates whether users' browsing was concentrated on several food websites, or dispersed over many sites. These variables were calculated after the experiment. We obtained the browsing histories of users from July 19–August 15, 2017. Based on users' one month of browsing histories before the first ad exposure, we calculated these variables in our research model.

Unfortunately, we could not obtain the demographics, food preference, and previous experience in the restaurant as control variables. We intentionally excluded subjects who were within the 500 m of the restaurant, because they were more likely to be frequent visitors, and their experience could have a disproportional efect. Nevertheless, the lack of these variables may threaten the internal validity of the experiment. To alleviate this threat, we randomly selected subjects from a large population at each geofence, and randomly assigned the subjects to diferent groups. The randomization process helped control for users unobservable heterogeneity. We compared the near and far groups on their control variables (i.e., SiteNum, SiteFreq, SiteHHI), no significant diference was found, suggesting that randomization was successful.

## 4. Empirical result

We used a logit model to analyze users' ad click behavior in our field experiment. We modeled users' ad click probability as a function of distance, ad delivery time, user preference, recency, discount, their interactions, and control variables. The estimation results are presented in Table 5. Model 1.0 comprises only Distance and control variables. In this model, the coeficient of distance is negative and significant, which suggests that a smaller distance leads to an increase in the probability of clicking. This finding reconfirms the distance efect in extant geotargeting research. Model 1.1 is the main efect model with all contextual targeting strategies. Compared to Model 1.0, the model fitting of Model 1.1 has been substantially improved (likelihood ratio test, $p \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting that temporal targeting, behavioral targeting, and discount significantly complement geotargeting. The coeficient of MealPD is positive and significant, which suggests that ad exposure at pre-decision time increases the probability of clicking. This result sup ports H1. For user preference, the coeficients of ShopRatio and Home-Ratio are both significant but with opposite signs, as hypothesized in H3. As we have expected, a higher ShopRatio implies a preference fo dining on-site, thus producing a higher ad click probability. Conversely, a higher HomeRatio implies a preference for a delivery service, thu producing a lower ad click probability. Finally, the coeficient of recency is negative and significant, which means that a user with a more recent visit to a food website is more likely to click the ad. This also supports H5, and reconfirms the findings in marketing literature [44]. The coeficient of discount (reverse coded) is significantly negative. This finding implies that a stronger economic incentive could increase users' click probability.

Notably, some control variables in the main efect model also significantly influence the probability of clicking coupon ads. The coeficient of SiteFreq is positive and significant, whereas SiteNum is insignificant. A plausible reason is that SiteFreq is more indicative of ones' consumption interest, whereas the number of unique food websites is not. The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index of food websites (SiteHHI) also demonstrates a significantly positive efect on ad click probability. A possible reason is that a concentrated browsing pattern suggests that users are inclined to goal-directed searching instead of browsing. Thus, they are more ready to make a purchase. The coeficient of Background is positive but insignificant, which indicates that the two versions of ad background do not have a substantial efect on ad click.

We then added the interactions of distance with five independent variables to our model one by one. The results are shown in Models 1.2 to 1.6 in Table 5. First, the coeficient of the interaction between distance and MealPD is negative but insignificant, thus rejecting H2. Second, the coeficient of the interaction between distance and Sho pRatio is significantly negative, which supports our hypothesis H4. In contrast, the coeficient of the interaction between distance and HomeRatio is insignificant. This result suggests that users who prefer on site service are more sensitive to distance. Third, the coeficient of in teraction between distance and recency is negative and significant. This result suggests that users who have recently visited food websites are less sensitive to distance, which supports H6. Finally, discount has a significantly negative interaction efect with distance. Because discount is negatively coded, the result suggests that a deeper discount helps to mitigate the distance efect. The result supports H7. Overall, comparing Model 1.1 and Model 1.6, the likelihood ratio test is also significant, suggesting the interaction efects are substantial.

## 4.1. Further analysis

To examine the robustness of the main model, we further conducted another estimation based on all 18,402 users who were exposed to our coupon ads at least once. To control the efect of ad exposure frequency, we included a new variable ExpFreq in the model. In addition, we excluded the variable MealPD from the previous models because MealPD does not apply to users with multiple exposures. We still used browsing history before the first ad exposure to calculate all other variables.

Table 6 reports the estimation result of the new model. A key finding of this model is that exposure frequency has a negative efect on ad clicks, suggesting that multiple exposures are not efective in this context. Meanwhile, most results of hypothesis testing are consistent with those in the main model, except that the coeficient of the interaction between distance and recency becomes significant only at the α < 0.1 level.

## 5. Discussion and implications

With the rapid growth of location-based services, geotargeting has become an increasingly powerful tool for precise targeting. However, few studies have attempted to examine the efect of a comprehensive suite of contextual targeting strategies and the interactions between distance and other contextual factors. This study aims to take the research one step closer to a comprehensive contextual targeting strategy by investigating how temporal targeting, behavioral targeting, and discount could complement and enhance the efect of geotargeting in an O2O context. The variables that we focus on are discount, ad delivery time, user preference for on-site or delivery service, and the recency of the last visit to food websites. We integrate these variables into a unified framework and apply a logit model to test all hypotheses.

The results first reconfirm the main efect of various targeting strategies, including the efect of geotargeting, temporal, behavioral targeting, and discount. In particular, our results suggest that a smaller distance and the right delivery time at user's pre-decision stage efectively drive the probability of ad clicks. Matching of a user's product preference (i.e., on-site service) and recency also boosts ad clicks.

This study reveals some new interaction efects with geotargeting.

First, users' behavioral characteristics derived from their historical behavior can improve the efectiveness of geotargeting. For online users who have demonstrated a preference for on-site service in their web browsing behavior, they are more sensitive to the distance of an ofline restaurant. In contrast, online users who prefer delivery service are not more sensitive to distance. For online users who have recently visited food websites, they are less sensitive to distance.

Unexpectedly, this study also reveals that temporal targeting and geotargeting may not have an interaction efect. A plausible reason in the meal service context is that meal is such a frequent consumption activity and a constant need, promotions at any time seem appropriate. In contrast, a more occasional decision such as going to a cinema demands more planning and is more sensitive to the time of ad exposure [30].

This study makes a few interesting contributions to the literature. First, we propose the notion of comprehensive contextual targeting and a suite of targeting strategies that may complement geotargeting. Particularly, we explore the complementary efect of geotargeting, temporal targeting, behavioral targeting, and discount. We observe that complementing geotargeting with temporal and behavioral targeting does enhance the overall efect of contextual targeting.

Second, we introduce the personalization literature as a theoretical lens for contextual targeting. We confirm that besides locationmatching, taste-matching and need-matching are important dimensions to explore [20]. Temporal targeting, if the ad exposure time is suggestive of one's need of the moment, taps on need-matching. Behavioral targeting provides one way to implement taste-matching. These matching strategies not only ofer a psychological mechanism to explain the efect of targeting, but also ofer guidance on how to enrich the conventional distance and discount strategies in contextual targeting.

Third, in the context of O2O ecommerce, we propose the potential synergistic efects between geotargeting and behavioral targeting. We diferentiate user's preference for on-site service and delivery service. This preference classification is not unique to restaurants. Many other type of services such as fruit and vegetable shopping, pet service, and laundry service ofer both on-site service and on-door (i.e., delivery) service. An understanding of user's preference helps firms to draw different service radius based on user type. For instance, users who prefer delivery service are generally less responsive to an ad, but meanwhile they are not sensitive to distance. Consequently, a marketing campaign for such users can cover the population in a larger radius. We also find that recency attenuates the negative efect of distance. This interaction efect has not been explored in the literature before.

The implications of our study to practitioners are straight forward. First, since there is a suite of contextual targeting strategies in an advertiser's hand, the simultaneous application of these strategies helps reduce user's irritation of unwanted ad, and saves an advertiser's ad cost. The interaction efects between geotargeting and other factors also provide alternative means to overcome distance. For instance, the conventional way to attract distant users could be discount. Recency provides an alternative strategy to discount. A firm may precisely target at distant users with recent online behavior. This would improve the targeting efectiveness at a lower cost. Regarding user preferences, because users who prefer on-site service are more sensitive to distance, retailers may consider ofering these users additional discounts to ofset their travel cost. However, retailers do not need to ofer distance-related incentives for users who prefer delivery.

This study shall be interpreted with its limitations. First, we set distance in our experiment as a factor with only two levels. This might limit the generalization of our conclusions. Future work can use a more precise scale of distance to examine its efect in a broader range. Second. based on users' browsing history, many other behavioral vari. ables can be constructed besides recency and preference for service type. Future work may explore other dimensions of user's taste and need, such as food type and restaurant environment. Third, we used a restaurant as a focal industry; thus, generalization of the results to other industries shall be treated with a caution. Finally, the underlying psychological mechanism by which temporal and behavioral targeting af fect geotargeting requires further exploration. Other research methods, such as surveys or interviews, could be valuable research directions to further explicate users' decision mechanisms.
