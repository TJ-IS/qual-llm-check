# Dialectic decision support systems: System design and empirical evaluation<sup>☆</sup>

Suprasith Jarupathirun <sup>a,1</sup>, Fatemeh “Mariam” Zahedi <sup>b,⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Wisconsin Milwaukee, School of Business Administration, P.O. Box 742, Milwaukee, WI 53201, United States <sup>b</sup> School of Business Administration, P.O. Box 742, Milwaukee, WI 53201, United States

Available online 2 May 2006

## Abstract

Many complex and unstructured decisions are hindered by a lack of clear understanding of various underlying assumptions and perspectives involved in the decision process. At present, the traditional decision support systems (DSS) pay little attention to the elicitation of underlying assumptions and perspectives in dealing with complex issues. We argue that the Socratic dialectic inquiry is an effective method for dealing with unstructured problems that are complex and require the involvement of different perspectives in DSS. In this paper, we propose a design for Dialectic Decision Support Systems (DDSS), in which dialectical processes are integrated with traditional DSS in order to provide support for individual decision makers. We then formulate a conceptual model for identifying factors that contribute to the efficacy of DDSS in comparison to traditional DSS. The empirical test of the model supports the superior efficacy of the DDSS and identifies factors that contribute to it. The contributions of this research are in generating support for stimulating critical thinking, dealing with complex decision issues and identifying creative solutions. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Dialectic inquiry; Devil's advocacy; Intelligent agent; Assumption elicitation; Dialectic decision support systems

## 1. Introduction

Researchers have known that individual decision makers are often incapable of making the best decisions when the problem is complex. Socrates–one of the most influential philosophers in ancient Greece–argued that not all men are capable of seeking the “truth knowledge” or dealing with problems without the help from others [58]. Using inquiry dialogues, Socrates helped his students discover by themselves that they failed to have the answers (or the “truth knowledge”) they believed they had ([58], p. 106). Simon's [61] bounded rationality theory explains the role of human's limited cognitive processes in discouraging decision makers from pursuing the optimal solution. Tversky and Kahneman [67] argue that decision makers are open to serious errors and biased decisions because they use “rules of thumb” or heuristics for making decisions.

The underlying motivation for developing decision support systems (DSS) has been to provide support by complementing the decision makers' cognitive resources. However, empirical studies indicate that less motivated decision makers are willing to settle for inefficient decisions by using more comfortable but less effective strategies in order to conserve their cognitive effort/energy [66]. Furthermore, while understanding underlying assumptions in various decision options plays an important role in tackling complex and unstructured decisions [45], traditional decision support systems (DSS) have paid little attention to the elicitation of underlying assumptions and perspectives in dealing with complex issues.

In organization studies, structured conflict approaches–the dialectic approach and devil's advocacy–are used to broaden a decision maker's perspectives in group decision making when dealing with unstructured tasks [13]. Following the Socratic dialectic approach in philosophy, dialectic inquiry provides a critical perspective to the decision-making process and, thus, improving decision quality [14]. Based on a multiple perspective view, Chae et al. [8] argue that the dialectic approach could be effectively used to deal with wicked problems for which there is “no knowable correct answer” (p. 23). Empirical studies have found the dialectical approach to be effective in helping decision makers in unstructured tasks [57]. These findings lead naturally to two questions. First, can one design a DSS with a dialectic component (dialectic DSS or DDSS)? Second, what, if any, are the factors that contribute to the successful use of DDSS in making complex decisions?

In answering these two research questions, this paper first proposes a DDSS design, in which traditional DSS is integrated with a dialectical approach and advanced technologies to help decision makers improve their decisions for unstructured tasks. The design draws on the Socratic school of thought, the Hegelian triad, the research on dialectic inquiry, and advanced technologies in intelligent systems to provide a knowledge base and intelligent interface for engaging decision makers in the dialectic process. This design heeds the call by [60] that DSS designers should incorporate emerging IS technologies in DSS to enhance the systems' capabilities to better address the needs of decision makers. Secondly, drawing on theories in task-technology fit, self-efficacy, goal setting, and technology acceptance model, we develop a conceptual model for evaluating the efficacy of the proposed DDSS.

The increasing complexity of decisions encountered by individual and organizational decision makers and the global nature and far-reaching interdependencies of decision consequences have created a mandate for a more potent class of DSS. While adding a dialectic component to a system has been proposed in artificial intelligence and in dealing with conflicts (see, for example, [21]), this study is the first to develop DDSS in business contexts and could contribute to the creation of support tools in making complex and difficult decisions and in finding creative ways in dealing with new circumstances.

## 2. Dialectic approach and its applications

In this section, we briefly discuss theories and research on dialectics that inform the development of the DDSS design.

## 2.1. Critical thinking through a dialectic approach

Critical thinking or critical reasoning is a major topic in several disciplines with the focus on enhancing the effectiveness of human judgment and decision making [22]. Critical thinking has been defined as the ability to think with a goal in mind, and capability to clarify underlying assumptions of beliefs and decisions by asking pertinent questions [24,29]. Critical thinking is a skill needed for many complex problems, in which implied assumptions, regularly held beliefs, and normal procedures cannot be successfully used to solve a decision problem. Critical thinking is used to question old ways of problem solving and to search for alternative assumptions, beliefs, or procedures to arrive at innovative solutions. Critical thinking involves reflective and healthy skepticism, requires deepening the background knowledge about the decision problem, and calls for a greater cognitive effort in selecting facts and assumptions to use [47]. Although there are different schools of thought about approaches to teaching critical thinking [22], there is a consensus that critical thinking skills can be taught and acquired [29]. Expanding on Type I and Type II errors in statistics, Mitroff [49] identifies Type III error in decision making as “solving the wrong problem precisely” (p. 15). He emphasizes the importance of critical thinking in avoiding Type III errors in dealing with wicked decision problems.

Teaching and use of critical thinking date back to ancient Greece, when philosophers such as Plato and Socrates taught their students to think critically by questioning beliefs and reasons used by themselves and by those who had different perspectives\the dialectic approach [56,63]. The dialectic approach considers existing alternatives and their underlying explanations and assumptions, as well as other possible alternatives and explanations that could be elicited through the interaction of opposing views [49].

## 2.2. The practice of the dialectic approach

According to Rychlak, Socrates required his students “to think for themselves\to be critical, exact, and tenacious in pursing any point under consideration” using a dialectical inquiry ([56], p. 29). Socrates took an opposition role by asking a series of questions. His intention was that when students could not respond to Socrates' questions with a strong argument, they would come to self-realization of new knowledge.

Hegel, one of the most influential modern philosophers, formulated the fundamental principle of his dialectical philosophy based on the Socratic school of thought. According to Stace's interpretation [63], the negation principle is that “to deny that a thing belongs to one class is to affirm that it belongs to some other class” (p. 33). Hegelian dialectical approach is developed as a triad system that describes the flow of thought (Fig. 1).

The three members of a triad are thesis, antithesis, and synthesis. Thesis is an initial affirmation that explains itself as a positive assertion. Antithesis is the opposite of thesis that denies what the thesis affirms. Antithesis is deduced from thesis. The confrontation between thesis and antithesis produces a contradiction that results in reconciliation, leading to synthesis, which is a combination of best thesis and antithesis. Churchman [10] observes that in Hegelian inquiring systems, the designer undertakes with full conviction “to construct a ‘case’ for a point of view, in effect in defense of a thesis” (p. 171). The relevant items of information are those that are evidence relative to the thesis and lend “positive, zero, or negative credence to the thesis” (p. 172). Then, the observer questions why the thesis is true and wonders if “another conviction wouldn't do just as well,” leading to the antithesis, which Churchman calls the “deadliest enemy of thesis” (p. 172). The synthesis is the result of conflict between the thesis and antithesis. It is not a compromise, but has a higher or broader view of reality. It is a “superproposal” that encompasses the conflicting thesis and antithesis in it. The process is not mechanical, but involves dynamic interactions for self-realization [7].

In group decision making, the diverse cognitive abilities, perspectives, and creativities possessed by group members could remain dormant and never come to the full realization when group thinking occurs [33]. For achieving the full potential of a group's diverse resources, it is suggested that a structural conflict such as dialectical inquiry should be embedded in group decision making [44]. A “healthy” conflict during decision making increases exchanges of different ideas and information and promotes critical evaluation or critical thinking among decision makers [36]. Dialectical inquiry and its variation in the form of devil's advocacy embed such a “healthy” conflict in order to alleviate group thinking in group decision making and to improve decision performance. Harrison and Bramson [30] outline seven strategies that the dialectical thinker uses: open argument and confrontation, asking dumb– smart questions, participating from the sidelines, suspending opposing ideas, speculation and Fantasy, proposing far-out solutions and negative analysis.

In dealing with complex and wicked planning problems, Mason [44] advocates the dialectic approach to stimulate structured cognitive conflicts in group decision making. His dialectic inquiry involves a plan (thesis) and a counter-plan (antithesis) in order to form a new plan with expanded perspectives (synthesis). Mitroff and Emshoff [50] use the dialectic approach to develop their strategic assumptions analysis model for the analysis of business strategies. Their strategic assumptions analysis (SAA) model includes four basic steps: assumption specification, dialectic, assumption integration, and composite strategy creation. During the “assumption specification” step, the stakeholder-analysis technique is used for identifying assumptions. The dialectic step generates counter-assumptions and plans. Using the pool of assumptions related to both original and counter-plans, the belief assessment procedure (BAP) is used to classify acceptable assumptions, based on their degree of certainty and importance, for the purpose of developing a new plan. Cosier [13] summarizes the dialectical inquiry technique to include the two main processes:

1. original strategy → analysis → identification of assumptions → proposed strategy;

2. original strategy and proposed strategy → analysis → identification of counter-assumptions → proposed counter-strategy.

Ironically, while a structured conflict approach in decision making can improve the quality of decisions, the tension caused by conflicts during the decisionmaking process could negatively impact the decision consensus and its acceptance by members [2]. To achieve decision quality, consensus and affective acceptance, researchers suggest that the group nourish “healthy” and depress “unhealthy” conflicts in the process [37]. Priem and Price [54] identify two types of conflicts in group decision making: cognitive conflicts and social conflicts. Cognitive conflicts are task focused and have positive effects on group decision acceptance [35]. Social conflicts are personal and thus involve interpersonal relationships and personal disagreements among decision makers in a group [54]. Social conflicts have negative effects on decision acceptance and the future effectiveness of decision outcome [35,54].

Business applications of the dialectic approach have mostly taken place in group settings without any IT support and have required engaging in organized conflicts. However, there are circumstances in which engaging in structured conflicts may be neither possible nor desirable for decision makers. Unlike group decision making, individual decision making is unlikely to incur unhealthy conflicts (social conflicts), since it does not involve social interactions in the decision process. However, it is difficult to engage in selfinduced cognitive conflicts, which may be caused by a lack of diverse perspectives and by the limited skills of the individual decision maker. To overcome these limitations, we suggest incorporating the dialectic approach into DSS to assist individual decision makers in their complex decision problems, thus reducing the need to engage in organized group conflicts.

## 2.3. Incorporating the dialectic approach into DSS

Valacich and Schwenk [68] have studied the effectiveness of dialectical inquiry in both face-to-face and computer-mediated environments. They have reported that some of their subjects (undergraduate students) did not engage in dialectic process to generate an “optimal” solution. This could be the result of additional cognitive efforts and skills needed for initiating and engaging in the dialectic process. This point of view is supported by Fasolo's [23] findings that decision makers can better deal with conflicting information when decision aids are used. Thus, we argue that the use of DDSS can help expert and novice decision makers engage in the critical thinking needed for dealing with difficult and complex problems.

## 2.4. The Argument structure

Incorporating the dialectic approach into DSS requires following a structured argumentation. Mason and Mitroff [45] propose a structure for arguments during the dialectic decision making-process to ensure that thesis, antithesis and syntheses are systematically examined. In this study, we argue that before decisions can be reached, assumptions should be first validated and proved. The argument structure is used as a systematic process of reasoning for accepting or rejecting claims.

The argument structure consists of data, assumption, warrant, backing, and rebuttal [45], as shown in Fig. 2. Mason and Mitroff [45] suggest that decision makers first provide their assumptions, then list data, warrant, backing and rebuttal arguments to support and validate their claims or assumptions. Invalid assumptions, therefore, should be dropped from the assumption pool used to derive the final decision. In strategic decision making, Mason and Mitroff [45] define assumptions as assertions needed for infusing certainty in reaching a conclusion. Assumptions that do not have support are invalid and should be dropped.

Data is defined as “grounds” for supporting assumptions. Data represent “the evidential support for the claim” and “may be factual statements or claims from other arguments” (p. 214). The word used for facts is “given that.”

Warrants consist of “rules, principles, premises, or inferences that act as a bridge between the data and the claim” (p. 215). In other words, warrants are the underlying reason for interpreting why data support the assumption. The warrants are the reasoning components that show why the assumptions should be accepted or are worthy of consideration [11]. “A warrant is the ‘because’ part of an argument” (p. 215).

Backing supports the warrant and “is always ‘waiting,’ ready to come to its defense” (p. 215). Backings normally refer to established or generally accepted principles or categorical statements of facts and are prefaced by the word “since” in the argument. Examples of backing include “statistical principles, laws, categories in a taxonomical system, definition in a language, or accepted methods of calculation” (p. 216).

Fig. 2. The argument structure.

Rebuttals refer to cases, conditions, and circumstances where the assumption does not hold, and the operative word for it in the argument is “unless.” Rebuttals may negate any parts of data, warrants, backings or present counter assumptions. Rebuttals are important in establishing the strength of an assumption. If strong rebuttals exist for an assumption, it may be judged to be relatively weak. Cogan [11] suggests only assumptions with strong support should be included in the decision-making process.

## 3. The design of dialectic decision support systems

Traditionally, DSS is designed to support decision makers to improve decision outcome for semi-structured tasks. However, the need for a DSS design with a stronger emphasis on the early stages of decision making has already been recognized. Todd and Benbasat [66] suggest that DSS should guide decision makers to select the most appropriate decision strategy. Belardo et al. [5] propose strategic DSS, which incorporates multiple conceptual models to support senior managers and to train managers to include different perspectives in dealing with complex strategic decisions. Their system allows decision makers to incorporate different perspectives into the decisionmaking process.

Dialectic decision support systems take a different approach to influence decision makers by assisting them on how to think critically rather than what to think. We argue that by providing guidance for critical thinking, based on the dialectic approach, the system is more effective in helping decision makers solve unstructured tasks that are complex and have no “cut-and-dried” formulation. In addition, DDSS could help decision makers overcome their limitations and unwillingness to engage in structured conflicts.

## 3.1. The design framework of DDSS

In legal arguments, dialectic approaches are either fixed or dynamic, depending on the nature of underlying premises. In fixed premises, the opposing sides do not alter their premises and defend their positions to win their case. In dynamic premises, a dialogue protocol is used to mediate the process and help opposing sides resolve their legal conflict by modifying their premises [28]. The design of DDSS in this study is similar to the dynamic-premises approach used in legal arguments. In our proposed DDSS design, we adopt the strategic assumptions analysis in order to add a dialectical thinking process to the DSS. We incorporate the argument structure in the system for the evaluation of underlying assumptions of thesis, antithesis and synthesis. The focus of the DDSS is not automatic generation of alternative assumptions or alternatives. The focus is on assisting the individual decision maker (DM) to think dialectically and arrive at the appropriate synthesis.

The design of the DDSS consists of four modules: DSS, the dialectic knowledge base, visual aids, and the intelligent interface interacting with the system, as shown in Fig. 3.

## 3.2. Intelligent agents

Intelligent agents are used for interfacing among main modules and their communication process. That is, each module communicates through its intelligent agent with other modules. Each intelligent agent (IA) has its knowledge base (not shown on Fig. 3) for interaction with other modules and the decision maker (DM). The interface IA checks the DM's responses by providing a dialogue process for the DM to check if her response is relevant to the problem domain and to the question asked. It then sends the response to the appropriate module. If the DM takes too long to provide a response or asks for assistance in answering a question, the interface IA provides hints, examples, and suggestions to assist the DM to respond to a question.

## 3.3. Dialectic knowledge-base module

The DDSS design contains a dialectic knowledgebase module, which supports the DM in the dialectical thinking process. This module is usable across knowledge domains because it involves the process and is not limited to specifics of a domain. This module has two main components: the dialectical inquiry structure and the dialectical dialog model.

The dialectical inquiry structure reflects the flow of the dialectical inquiry process. Following the strategic assumptions analysis model of Mitroff and Emshoff [50], the dialectical inquiry structure involves three processes: (i) assumption surfacing, (ii) counter-assumption surfacing, and (iii) assumption integration. In addition, for each process, the argument structure is used to evaluate the importance, the truth, and the validity of assumptions. As shown in Fig. 4, at the beginning of the process, the DM identifies the alternative or choice that the DM believes to be the best. Then, the DM identifies the assumptions that support the alternative. The DM evaluates assumptions using the arguments structure, which ensures that the DM has data, warrant, backing and rebuttal arguments to support the claims of the elicited assumptions. After the assumptions of the initial alternative are evaluated and ranked based on the importance, truth, and validity criteria, the DM identifies counter assumptions, which are used to construct counter-alternatives. The pool of assumptions and counter-assumptions are ranked, evaluated and used to develop a set of synthesized alternatives. The generated alternatives from the dialectic process are then sent to the DSS module for the final stage of the decision-making process.

The dialectical dialog model controls the flow of the dialectic process. It consists of a set of questions and a set of rules for interacting with the DM in order to elicit and clarify the perspectives and underlying assumptions in the decision process. ## 3.4. DSS module

The DSS module is specifically designed for a particular problem domain, such as financial investment analysis or marketing strategy. The DSS module assists the DM to evaluate alternatives for making the decision. For example, the DSS module could include a query application to retrieve and summarize the relevant data and an analysis application to analyze and evaluate each alternative based on different decision-making-models.

According to DiBiase [19], decision makers use visualization aids to explore problems and to verify assumptions and hypotheses. Visual representations are found to assist individuals to better assimilate and understand new concepts [38]. Therefore, DDSS should provide visual representations for each step in the dialectical analysis. ## 4. The evaluation framework of dialectic DSS

In this section, we develop a conceptual model for evaluating the efficacy of DDSS. The structure of the model is presented in Fig. 5. Construct definitions and scale references are reported in Table 3.

Simon [62] identifies three phases for decision making: intelligence, design and choice. While all phases of the decision-making process are important to the success and quality of a decision, we argue that the intelligence phase should be examined first because the intelligence phase is the foundation for the following phases. As when building a house, if decisions are based on a shaky foundation (assumptions), the quality of decision is at risk. According to Mitroff and Emshoff [50], assumption elicitation and assumption challenging are critical steps to successfully and effectively solve complex problems and find innovative solutions. As a result, it is reasonable to evaluate the success of DDSS in terms of its success in the first phase of decision making. Consequently, the quality of assumptions made in the decision process constitutes a measure of DDSS success and is a dependent variable in our conceptualization of the efficacy of DDSS. The other dimensions of DDSS success include intention to use DDSS and satisfaction. There are two types of satisfaction: satisfaction with the DDSS technology and satisfaction with the decision.

## 4.1. Task-technology fit, its antecedents, and its impacts

The first set of hypotheses in Fig. 5 (H1–H5) deals with antecedents of the DDSS success that are related to the capability of the DDSS in helping decision makers overcome their limited cognitive resources and skills. Decision makers are required to have broader perspectives when solving unstructured tasks. In group settings, Jehn [34] argues that the fit between the level of conflict and the nature of a task affects the group outcome. Jehn reports that conflict has a detrimental effect on the functioning of a group when a routine task is performed and is beneficial to group outcome when a non-routine task is involved. Priem et al. [55] suggest that the cognitive conflict through a dialectical inquiry approach has a positive effect on satisfaction with decision outcome and decision process.

Based on the task-technology fit (TTF) [71], we argue that DDSS is congruent with complex and unstructured tasks. Implicit assumptions in the traditional DSS may narrow decision makers' perspectives or hide damaging biases in the decision process. Using DDSS, on the other hand, the DM can enforce a structured conflict in the decision-making process to develop new ideas and to critically evaluate all perspectives and alternatives [49].

Empirical studies of TTF find that the performances of individuals improve when capabilities of the technology match with characteristics of the task [27]. Based on the TTF theory [27], we posit that decision makers using DDSS will perform better than those using traditional DSS for complex, non-routine decisions.

In this study, we differentiate TTF from two perspectives: the DDSS designer's perspective and the DM's perspective. TTF with the designer's perspective is defined as the congruence of the designed capabilities of DDSS and task requirements [71], whereas the DM's perspective or perceived TTF is defined as the congruence among the perceived capabilities of DDSS, task requirements, and the competence of decision makers with the decision task and the system [43]. In this study, we focus on the DM's perspective by using the “perceived TTF” construct.

Self-realization of knowledge, thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, requires self-consciousness ([56], p. 242). According to Hegel, self-consciousness combines “the fact that I know” and “what I know” to realize awareness of its own being ([56], p. 242). In another words, to engage in the dialectic process, the DM should know the facts and have the knowledge related to the problem domain.

In the social cognitive theory, self-efficacy or the perception of one's own abilities to perform a task is found to influence individuals' outcome expectations and later their behaviors toward accomplishing a task [12]. Individuals often either overestimate or underestimate their actual abilities in performing a task [4]. Stone [64] finds that most individuals overestimate their self-efficacy at first and later adjust their self-efficacy to reflect their actual abilities. Marcolin et al. [43] link user competency with perceived task-technology-user fit. Their study finds that the actual knowledge and selfefficacy are different.

Self-efficacy, nevertheless, is a useful indicator for the perception of individual ability [43]. Marcolin et al. [43] imply that self-efficacy is more appropriate to evaluate the degree of the task-technology fit in the context of perception or subjective measurement. For decision makers to perceive fit between DDSS and task, they have to believe that they can use the technology effectively and know how to solve the problems effectively. If decision makers are not confident in using the technology and in their knowledge of the task, they will not view the use of technology to be appropriate to the task. Therefore, we hypothesize that self-efficacy influences how decision makers perceive the fit between the technology and the task.

Furthermore, to engage in dialectical thinking, we hypothesize that decision makers have to be openminded about multiple perspectives. According to Dermen et al. [18], open-minded people are innovative, ready for new ideas, flexible and have foresight. In group decision making, Amason and Sapienza [3] find that openly interacting, critiquing and accepting new ideas, methods and perspectives in a group are positively associated with cognitive conflict. The cognitive conflict is needed for a healthy dialectical thinking or critical thinking [54]. Different structured conflict methods such as dialectical inquiry and devil's advocacy to promote cognitive conflict usually involve at least two parties debating and exchanging ideas. Without open-mindedness, decision makers are likely to develop affective conflict instead of cognitive conflict [3].

Based on a meta-analysis of prior studies in IS, DeLone and McLean [17] conclude that system and information quality and system quality impact user satisfaction, a conclusion that has been supported by subsequent studies [46]. In the context of DDSS, beliefs related information and system quality are reflected in the perceived task-technology fit, in that the DDSS provides all the needed information and functionality for making a decision about a complex problem. Bhattacherjee [6] finds that user satisfaction is influenced by the perception of the technology capability and expectation of the outcome when using the technology. Hence, it is reasonable to expect that perceived TTF influences DM satisfaction.

Davis and Kottemann [16] report that when decision makers believe the technology to be superior to other technologies, they would also believe that the use of the technology will result in better performance. Without the proper feedback of the actual performance, they warn that the perceived decision quality is not accurate. The study also supports the presence of a relationship between the belief of superior technology and the perceived decision quality. A higher level of fit between the decision task and the DDSS technology leads to a better performance. In the case of using DDSS, the performance measure is the quality of the elicited assumptions, which are produced using the dialectic components of the system. Therefore, the hypotheses regarding perceived TTF are as follows:

(H1). The higher the DM's task self-efficacy, the higher perceived TTF.

(H2). The higher the DM's DDSS self-efficacy, the higher perceived TTF.

(H3). The higher the DM's open-mindedness, the higher perceived TTF.

(H4). The higher perceived DDSS TTF, the higher satisfaction.

(H5). The higher perceived DDSS TTF, the higher the quality of elicited assumptions.

## 4.2. Goal difficulty and goal commitment, their antecedents, and their impacts

The second set of hypotheses deals with the role of decision makers' motivation to expend effort in using the DDSS and performing the task. According to attributional theory [70], the factors that determine performance can be classified into internal factors and external factors. Internal factors are ability and effort, while external factors include task difficulty, luck, and assistance from other people or technology. Keeping external factors constant, performance is a function of ability and effort.

While ability in terms of self-efficacy was discussed above, in this section, we focus on effort as another critical factor influencing performance. Motivation theorists have developed several models, which identify factors influencing the decision maker's behavior to spend cognitive and psychological effort, sustain various types of attention, and focus on the task at hand. Among the motivation theories, goal setting is a well-established theory in organizational behavior [40].

In this study, we use goal setting theory to manifest decision makers' intensity and duration of attention in broadening their perspectives during the decisionmaking process. In goal setting research, goal is a major factor in motivating people to spend effort in accomplishing tasks, and there is a positive relationship between goal level and task performance [40]. Mento et al. [48] observe that 90% of the goal setting studies report challenging goals (high goals or difficult goals) lead to better task performance. Hence, individuals goals influence their levels of performance. A higher goal level motivates individuals to spend more effort to achieve the desired decision performance.

However, the effect of goal level is insignificant if decision makers have no commitment to maintain attention and effort to meet the goal over the entire period in which the decision task is performed [30]. An individual goal commitment is defined as the willingness to extend effort over time and staying focused on the process until the goal is accomplished [30]. Locke et al. [40] propose that commitment is influenced by a number of factors, including incentive and expectancy of success. Similarly, Hollenbeck and Klein [31] suggest that if the goal is not attractive (less incentive) and is difficult to attain (low expectancy of success), individuals are unlikely to commit to the goal. Taylor et al. [65] find that commitments to assigned goals are reported to be higher among individuals with easy goals than those with difficult goals. As a result, decision makers who perceived their goals to be easy are more likely to have a high commitment to achieving the goals. Hence, we posit that the perceived difficulty of the goal negatively influences the extent of decision makers' commitment in achieving it.

Self-efficacy has been found to impact perception of goal difficulty. In their comprehensive review of the selfefficacy literature, Marakas et al. [42] report on a number of studies that have found strong associations between self-efficacy and goal-related factors. Locke et al. [40] argue for the association of self-efficacy and goal difficulty and, in a later study, observe that self-efficacy is related to expectancy of success. Furthermore, Hollenbeck and Klein [31] report that people with high self-efficacy view goals as easier than those with low self-efficacy. Thus, we argue that self-efficacy has an influence on the perception of goal difficulty.

Decision makers are more satisfied with performance when they achieve their goals [40]. Studies in organization behavior find a strong relationship between goal commitment and job satisfaction [41]. Davis and Kotteman [16] argue that individuals who exert effort to achieve a goal expect a high quality outcome. Individuals are more confident of achieving the desired results when they exert more effort [53]. The above arguments could be summarized in the following hypotheses.

(H6). The higher the DM's task self-efficacy, the lower perceived goal difficulty.

(H7). The higher the DM's DDSS self-efficacy, the lower perceived goal difficulty.

(H8). The higher perceived goal difficulty, the lower goal commitment.

(H9). The higher goal commitment, the higher satisfaction.

(H10). The higher goal commitment, the higher the quality of elicited assumptions.

4.3. Perceived usefulness and ease of use, their antecedent, and their impacts

The continued use of DDSS is another indication of its success, which is captured by the “intention to use” construct. The technology acceptance model (TAM) is a popular model for conceptualizing the antecedent of use or intention to use a system [15]. Empirical studies over the years have confirmed the relationships of the constructs in the TAM [1]. In the TAM, the perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness impact the individual's intention to use directly. Furthermore, perceived usefulness has an indirect impact on the intention to use, mediated by ease of use. Following the TAM, we posit that perceived DDSS ease of use and usefulness influence the intention to use the system.

Venkatesh and Davis [69] identify general computer self-efficacy as an antecedent of perceived ease of use.

Agarwal et al. [1] confirm the existence of the direct effect of self-efficacy on perceived ease of use. Furthermore, the fit between task characteristics and the functionality of information technology appears to have an effect on the perceived ease of use [20]. Goodhue and Thompson's [27] model, on the other hand, identifies ease of use as one of the dimensions of TTF. They argue that at the organization level, an information system is perceived to be more effective and appropriate for a task when the system has good characteristics of system quality and information quality: reliability, currency, compatibility, and ease of use. In other words, they use the perception of users about system and information quality as a surrogate indicator of the actual TTF. Goodhue [26] concludes that ease of use is one of many important traits of a good system. He proposes that perceived task-technology fit influences the utilization of the system through the perceived usefulness or the relative advantages of using the information system. Based on Goodhue and Thompson [27] and Goodhue [26], we therefore argue that perceived ease of use influences perceived usefulness through perceived task-technology fit. The arguments regarding the intention to use DDSS could be summarized in the following hypotheses:

(H11). The higher the DM's task self-efficacy, the higher perceived ease of use.

(H12). The higher the DM's DDSS self-efficacy, the higher perceived ease of use.

(H13). The higher perceived ease of use, the higher perceived TTF.

(H14). The higher perceived TTF, the higher perceived usefulness.

(H15). The higher perceived usefulness, the stronger intention to use DDSS.

(H16). The higher perceived ease of use, the stronger intention to use DDSS.

## 5. Empirical study of DDSS

The proposed model was tested by developing a prototype for the DDSS and collecting data to assess factors that impact its efficacy. The empirical analysis is exploratory and is intended to refine the construct measurement, to assess the experimental design, to evaluate the research model, and to test the DDSS prototype for the future improvement. The data were collected in a controlled and blind lab experiment, in which the DSS with the dialectic component was the “treatment” and the DSS without the dialectic component was considered as the “control.” The experimental design was full factorial 2 levels of goal difficulty × 2 levels of stimuli (treatment and control). The participants were assigned high or low goal difficulty level in order to provide adequate variability in the perception of goal difficulty. The prototype is a web-based DDSS, which was programmed using Java Script and Microsoft agents. It included the dialectic, interface, visual aids, and DSS components.

## 5.3. Impact of the Dialectic on System Efficacy

We coded the presence or absence of dialectic in the DSS by a binary variable (1 = present and 0 = absent) called “dialectic.” The correlation values of the dialectic variable with efficacy constructs (assumption quality, decision satisfaction, technology satisfaction, and intention to use) were 0.36, 0.34, 0.38, and 0.36, all with p-values of 0.000. This indicated that the presence of the dialectic component had positive influence on the perceived efficacy of the system. Furthermore, we estimated four regression models (not reported here due to space limitations). Each regression had one efficacy measure as the dependent variable and a dialectic variable as the sole dependent variable. The results of this regression provide support that the dialectic component has a positive significant impact on the perceived efficacy of the system.

## 5.4. Model estimation

The conceptual model was estimated using two methods: structural equations modeling (SEM) and partial least squares (PLS). This approach was taken to ensure the robustness of results with respect to the estimation method. In the SEM method, we used with the MLM estimation algorithm to deal with the nonnormality of data. Fit indices of the SEM model are reported in Table 6. The normed chi-square was well below the recommended threshold 3 and the CFI is 0.91, which is better than the recommended threshold 0.90. The TLI and RMSEA are 0.90 and 0.06, respectively, which are at the recommended threshold. GFI and AGFI are below the recommended threshold. PLS has the average $R ^ { 2 }$ value of 0.38. The two estimations show a reasonable fit for the model.

The results of the model estimation are reported in Table 6 and Figs. 6 and 7.

The two estimations led to similar significance levels for 14 hypotheses. Two hypotheses were not supported by either of the two estimations, and two hypotheses had marginally conflicting support. H1 was marginally supported by SEM and marginally rejected by PLS (tvalues of 2.59 and 1.93, respectively), indicating a lack of strong support for the relationship between task selfefficacy and perceived TTF. However, the association between technology self-efficacy and TTF was well supported in both estimations (H2). H3 was not supported in either of the two methods; hence, we did not find support for the impact of open-mindedness on TTF.

H4a and H4b were strongly supported by SEM and PLS, indicating that perceived TTF impacts technology satisfaction and decision satisfaction. H5 was also strongly supported in both SEM and PLS results, showing that perceived TTF influences perceived assumption quality.

## 5.5. Discussion

The results of testing the DDSS prototype using controlled, blinded lab experiments show that the use of the dialectic component has a positive impact on perceived efficacy of DSS, as measured by perceived assumption quality, technology satisfaction, decision satisfaction, and intention to use (the results obtained by four regression analyses). Furthermore, the conceptual model was tested using SEM and PLS, the results of which indicated robustness of the model with respect to the estimation method. The conceptual model had a satisfactory fit in both estimation methods. Furthermore, the test of the model's hypotheses pointed to the important and pervasive role of perceived TTF in outcome measures of system efficacy. Furthermore, we found that self-efficacy constructs, particularly technology self-efficacy, have a significant mediated impact on the outcome measures of DSS efficacy. We did not find any support for the influence of open-mindedness. However, we found that goal difficulty and goal commitment impact decision satisfaction and perceived assumption quality.

Hence, the factors that influence the perceived successful use of DDSS could be categorized into three groups: self-efficacy, perceived TTF, and goalrelated factors. A fourth category of factors relate to ease of use and perceived usefulness of the system, which mediate the impacts of self-efficacy and perceived TTF on the outcome measures. Organizations contemplating the development and use of DDSS should be aware of the significant role of these three categories of factors in the planning and deployment of such systems.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

This paper posed the question whether DSS integrated with a dialectic approach called dialectic DSS (DDSS) could be designed to help decision makers in dealing with complex, unstructured problems. It also asked what factors contribute to the successful deployment of such systems.

Drawing on the literature on the dialectic approach, we argued that DSS equipped with a dialectic component and knowledge-base could support decision makers in assumption elicitation during the intelligence phase of the decision-making process. Such a system could be particularly useful in making complex and unstructured decisions. Relying on the theories and literature in dialectic and devil advocacy as well as approaches in intelligent agents, we proposed a design for the development of DDSS. We then used the design to create a web-based DDSS. The successful development of the DDSS prototype indicated the feasibility of the proposed design.

In order to test the efficacy of the proposed DDSS, we relied on a number of theories, including technology acceptance model (TAM), task-technology fit (TTF), and goal setting to develop a conceptual model. In this model, the outcome measures for the efficacy of DDSS are identified as perceived quality of assumptions, technology satisfaction, decision satisfaction, and intention to use. The model identifies factors that could influence the successful deployment of DDSS. Using the DDSS prototype, we carried out a controlled, blinded lab experiment to test the conceptual model and to identify factors that contribute to the successful deployment of such systems in terms of perceived efficacy of the system.

Our findings suggest that the use of a dialectic component in DSS contributes to the positive perceived outcomes. Furthermore, the test of the model using two estimation methods (SEM and PLS) indicated that four groups of factors could influence the outcome measures. The first factor was related to a decision-maker's self-efficacy in technology and the decision task. Of the two, technology selfefficacy plays a more important role. The second factor is perceived task-technology fit (TTF). Decision makers who view the DDSS technology as being a good fit for the decision type will have a more favorable perception of the outcome measures. The third factor is related to goals and the extent of a DM's commitment to the goal. Finally, the fourth set of factors relates to ease of use and perceived usefulness of the system, which have a mediating role in the process.

With the increased complexity of decisions and their global and far-reaching consequences, there is an acute need for support systems designed to identify creative solutions and to think “outside-the-box” in dealing with difficult issues and identifying innovative strategies. The contributions of this research lie in promoting of an integrated approach in DSS for dealing with novel situations and complex decisions, when the decision maker lacks resources for or access to group dialectic and devil advocacy processes. Similarly, the dialectic component can be integrated in group DSS to counter “groupthink” and to ensure that decision makers' underlying assumptions are elicited and adequately challenged before making a final decision. Finally, DDSS could be used for teaching critical-thinking skills to novice decision makers, the way Socrates questioned his students in promoting self-discovery of new knowledge.

Fatemeh “Mariam” Zahedi has been Wisconsin Distinguished Professor at the School of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee since 1995. She has received her doctoral degree from Indiana University and teaches MIS courses in the graduate and PhD programs. Her present areas of research include intelligent DSS, behavioral studies in web design, software components and maintenance, intelligent systems and data mining. She has extensive journal publications,

including Management Science, Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, Information and Management, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Machine, and Cybernetics, IIE Transactions, IEEE Transactions on Professional Communications, European Journal of Operations Research, Operations Research, Computers and Operations Research, Interfaces, Software Maintenance and Evolution, Review of Economics and Statistics. She is the author of two books, has consulting and managerial experience in IT, and serves on the editorial boards of a number of journals.
