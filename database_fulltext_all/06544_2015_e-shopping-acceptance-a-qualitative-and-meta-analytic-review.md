# E-Shopping Acceptance: a Qualitative and Meta-Analytic Review

## Abstract

A literature review of consumers’ acceptance of e-shopping reveals that trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence are the concepts most often used to adapt the technology acceptance model to e-shopping. This study presents and discusses measures of these concepts, their interrelationships, and their relationships with the TAM nomological network. An integrated model of e-shopping acceptance including the above-mentioned concepts is elaborated and tested using meta-analytical path-analysis. While this integrated TAM contributes significantly to the understanding of e-shopping, the time may have now come to place more emphasis on achieving a better understanding of the techno-marketing antecedents of its components.

Keywords: e-shopping, Technology Acceptance Model, trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, social influence

## Introduction

Increasing numbers of consumers are accepting electronic commerce as a viable alternative to traditional shopping in their search for information and purchase of goods and services. According to eMarketer (2014) [1], worldwide B2C sales will rise by 20.1% in 2014 to reach \$1.500 trillion. This growth is mainly due to the increasing number of online and mobile users in emerging markets, the development of more advanced payment and shipping methods, major brands opening digital storefronts, and digital retailers expanding into new markets. To maintain and take advantage of this growing consumer interest in online shopping, retailers are investing more and more in the adoption of e-commerce and are searching for new ways to attract customers to their websites or their mobile apps, to encourage them to buy their products and services, and to enhance their loyalty. The overall challenge for retailers is then to attract the attention of the digital natives (consumers who have grown up in the digital world) and persuade them to spend more, as well as to attract digital immigrants (consumers who are presumed to resist new technology or at least have trouble accepting it) to this way of shopping [2]. Understanding why consumers shop and buy online is thus of primary importance for achieving e-commerce success.

The main objective of this paper is to synthesize a dozen years of prolific and versatile empirical research on the factors influencing consumers’ acceptance of online shopping. Numerous research articles use the well-known Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) as the core theory for investigating variables influencing e-commerce use. It is therefore thought that the time has come to confirm that TAM is actually a useful base model for explaining online purchasing and assessing how it has successfully been adapted and extended to e-commerce. More specifically, the aim of this study is to account for variables contributing to the explanation of customers’ online shopping behavior, which, along with TAM’s perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, have been identified in e-commerce literature as being influential antecedents of online shopping acceptance. Since trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence have been the variables most often examined in TAM studies so far, they are the variables that will be considered here.

This review article is unique in that it differs from previous information technology acceptance reviews in its scope, object and method. While other reviews concentrate on the basic relationships of the TAM model [3-6], a specific relationship with it [7-9], or a particular methodological concern [10,11], this research proposes a comprehensive but parsimonious summary of the way TAM has been adapted to eshopping by integrating additional variables. To the best of our knowledge, no other systematic review of e-commerce acceptance has as yet been published in leading journals. To obtain complementary and more complete insights into how trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence affect e-shopping acceptance and how they should be used in research studies, a mixed methods approach sequentially combining a qualitative review and a meta-analysis is used [12].

This article proceeds in two stages. The first stage presents a qualitative review of TAM’s extension to online shopping, with trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence as its main determinants. The qualitative review focuses on how these variables are measured, how they are related to TAM variables, and how they are interrelated. The review reveals the existence of inconsistent and even contradictory results that lead to the formulation of specific research questions about the marginal contribution of the supplementary variables to explaining e-shopping and their place in the TAM perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence founded on theoretical rather than empirical considerations. The model is based on meta-analytic results and modeled with path analysis. It is well known that meta-analysis is suited for quantitatively summarizing what has been observed in a stream of literature. The benefits of conducting metaanalyses to cumulate research findings in the field of information systems in general and of TAM in particular have been demonstrated in recent years. For example, Straub and Burton Jones [13] support our approach by noting that “…meta-analysis has not been used to refine TAM into its essential, parsimonious set of variables, including critical antecedents” (p.227).

## Background: TAM and its extensions for B2C e-commerce

The research model most commonly used to predict or explain information technology usage is the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), initially proposed by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [14]. A person’s attitude “represents a summary evaluation of a psychological object captured in such attribute dimensions as good-bad, harmful-beneficial, pleasantunpleasant, and likable-dislikable” ([15], p. 28). In TRA, attitude toward a behavior is assumed to be proportional to a weighted sum of the evaluations of salient beliefs that individuals have about the expected consequences of that behavior. Similarly, subjective norms are considered to be proportional to the weighted sum of the evaluations of their normative beliefs, which are the “…extent to which important others would approve or disapprove of their performing a given behavior” ([16], p. 195). Consequently, these beliefs have to be elicited for each behavior under scrutiny. In the context of a particular information technology, beliefs and attitude relate to the usage behavior of that technology [14].

As mentioned above, a first version of TAM was proposed by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [14] (TAM with attitude in Figure 1). This initial version will be referred to, when necessary for clarity, as TAM with attitude throughout the rest of the article. The aim of TAM is to explain why people use or do not use information technology in a job context. As in TRA, the basic assumption behind TAM is that individuals use or intend to use a particular information technology based on their attitude towards its use. However,

TAM differs from TRA in several important ways. First, in TAM, since subjective norms are not considered as an important direct antecedent of usage in an organizational context, they are set aside. Second, salient beliefs are not elicited for each technology; instead, general beliefs about the consequences of using a particular information technology are considered to be valid for any type of information technology. In TAM, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are the two general beliefs that play a key role in shaping attitude toward using any form of IT. Third, in TRA, attitude toward the behavior fully mediates the effect of beliefs on intention, in contrast to TAM, in which perceived usefulness (PU) affects intention directly and also indirectly through attitude. Attitude is thus conceptualized as a partial mediator of beliefs. Fourth, in TRA, attitude is proportional to a weighted sum of belief evaluations and no causal relations are assumed between them. As in TAM, perceived ease of use (EOU) is argued to be a direct causal antecedent of usefulness. A general predominance of usefulness over ease of use in directly explaining intention or usage is confirmed in several reviews of TAM [3,4,6].

## TAM in e-commerce research

A transactional website is both a store and an information technology platform: individuals who use it are consequently both consumers and IT users [17,18]. Therefore, it is plausible that TAM beliefs could be complemented or even replaced by other salient beliefs relating more to the consumer per se. In fact, TAM is a model of information technology acceptance by workers in a job-related context and the appropriateness of its extension to online shopping acceptance by consumers may be questioned [19].

Many attempts have been made to extend TAM and to adapt it to the context of Internet technologies in general and of B2C e-commerce in particular. To synthesize those attempts in e-commerce, a bibliographic search following a systematic procedure (later described in detail) was conducted and a subset of 102 references using TAM to TAM with attitude. In relation to TAM and e-shopping, the most frequent additional variables studied in this research are trust (37/102), perceived risk (16/102), enjoyment (28/102), and social influence (18/102).

## Trust

E-commerce literature measures trust in different ways (Table 1). In most of our sampled references, it is measured as a set of specific beliefs about the trustworthiness of the vendor, the service provider or the website [20-38]; in two cases, as a feeling of confidence and security about online transactions [39,40]; and, in two other cases, as a mix of trustworthiness in the vendor and a feeling of confidence in the transaction [39,41,42]. Note the difference between the object of trust and the object of TAM beliefs: ease of use and usefulness are beliefs about a behavior, i.e. the use of Internet technologies to shop, while in most cases trust is related to an entity, i.e. the web vendor.

In a TAM extension, Gefen, Karahanna, and Straub [21] demonstrate the direct effect of trust, usefulness, and ease of use on intended use. The same results are obtained by [22,28,34,36,37]. The direct positive significant effect of trust and usefulness, but not of ease of use, on intention is also present in [26,32,38,40-47]. In six of the twelve studies including attitude and trust, trust, together with usefulness and ease of use, contributes significantly to the formation of a positive attitude toward e-shopping [24,29- 31,35,35,48], while in three others, attitude is formed by trust and usefulness, but not ease of use [25,48-50]. Finally, usefulness and ease of use but not trust are significantly linked to attitude in [33,47], and only the relation between ease of use and attitude is confirmed in [51].

There are many inconsistencies in the relationship between trust and TAM beliefs. No direct link between trust and usefulness or ease of use is tested in [20,24- 26,29,31,33,34,37,40,42,43,46,49,51,52]. However when this link is tested, it is widely agreed that a positive path from trust to usefulness exists [21,22,27,28,30,36,39,41,44,45,48,53], even though two exceptions indicate that the path is from usefulness to trust [32,35]. Finally, several studies show a significant positive path between ease of use and trust [21,30,32,35,48,54], with a few exceptions where the path is from trust to ease of use [22,39,45,53].

## Perceived risk

In the context of e-commerce, perceived risk is conceptualized as a consumer’s belief about the potential losses or other negative outcomes from transacting on the Internet [55,56]. While perceived risk is inherent in any commercial transaction, two dimensions of perceived risk are particularly acute in online transactions: threats to security and threats to privacy. Security risk is perceived as higher when shopping on the Internet due to the use of the Internet itself (e.g., risk of intrusion, risk of credit card fraud), the virtual nature of an e-store (a poorly protected site), and a higher perceived threat to privacy than shopping in a physical store (e.g., risk of interception, risk of sharing information with a third party, and unauthorized use of personal information

Perceived risk is a direct negative antecedent of intention [22,30,33,42,48,58-61], of actual e-shopping usage [27], and of attitude [30,33,48,62,63]. No effect of perceived risk on intention or on attitude is found in [60]. In 10 of the 16 sampled studies where risk is present, no causal link between TAM beliefs and perceived risk is hypothesized or tested. Nevertheless, three studies indicate a negative effect of perceived risk on usefulness [58,61,64]; two, a negative effect of usefulness on risk [60,65]; and one, a causal link between ease of use and risk [58]. Some studies investigating the link between trust and risk find that trust is an antecedent of risk: the greater the trust in the e-shop, the less the perceived risk [22,42,48,59].

## Enjoyment

In 1992, Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [68] extended basic TAM to include the concept of enjoyment. They demonstrated that enjoyment, defined as “the extent to which the activity of using a computer is perceived to be enjoyable in its own right” (p. 1113), is an important direct determinant of intention to use a job-related IT. Enjoyment (ENJ) is associated with the concept of playfulness, which, according to Webster and Martocchio [69], may be conceptualized as a trait (a long-lasting characteristic of an individual) or as a state (a temporary situation). When conceptualized as a state, playfulness is defined as a person’s belief about the extent to which interacting with the web focuses attention, stimulates curiosity, and procures enjoyment [70]. Enjoyment is also associated with the concept of flow, a positive psychological experience that includes enjoyment and loss of self-consciousness [71]. Table 3 presents measures of enjoyment and playfulness used in our sample of studies. It can be concluded that some studies measure playfulness and enjoyment in a very similar manner [31,72-74].

Using TAM as the reference model for a study on factors affecting the intention to use an electronic supermarket, Henderson, Rickwood, and Roberts [75] find that enjoyment is the most important predictor of this intention. In their study, no significant direct links are found between usefulness or ease of use and intention. In several ecommerce studies, enjoyment and usefulness, but not ease of use, are significant direct antecedents of the intention to use e-shopping [18,26,41,73,76,77] or of having a positive attitude towards using it [25,49]. These studies suggest that enjoyment could perhaps replace ease of use as a direct antecedent of intention or attitude in an e-commerce acceptance model, while other studies find the direct effect of ease of use on intention [28,60,78,79] or on attitude [31,74,80,81] to be significant.

Some confusion reigns about how enjoyment is linked to usefulness and ease of use. In many studies, no causal link between enjoyment and usefulness or ease of use is hypothesized or tested [18,26,28,31,41,65,75,80,82-84], while in others, enjoyment significantly affects [78,79] or is affected by ease of use [27,47,60,74,76,81]. Finally, in two studies, enjoyment affects usefulness [27,49].

## Social influence

Venkatesh et al. [86] view social influence as a generic concept encompassing the concepts of subjective norm, social factors, and image, defining it as “the degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe he or she should use the new system” (p. 451). Table 4 lists the measures of social influence that we found in our review.

According to TRA, subjective norms are a direct positive antecedent of intention. In the case of e-shopping acceptance, this relationship is significant in [29,33,40,50,51,61,73,75,87,88] but, even though hypothesized, not significant in [24,30,79,89,90]. Two studies [29,91] find that social influence has a positive impact on attitude. Finally, some authors trace a significant path from social influence to usefulness [29,33,61,73,79,91] and even to enjoyment [73] or risk [50].

## Research questions

The above qualitative review reveals that, when referring to TAM, authors use “basic TAM” or “TAM with attitude” as their core model, generally without justification. The review also brings to light many inconsistencies or even contradictory results as to how trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence affect attitudes and intentions towards using e-shopping, are related to usefulness and ease of use, and are interrelated. The rather chaotic situation of TAM research censured by Benbasat and Barki [19], which may be due to substantive and/or methodological reasons, is clearly confirmed in the electronic shopping context, which in turn raises several research questions:

(1) In the context of e-shopping, is TAM with attitude a better explanatory model of intention to use e-shopping than the basic TAM model?

(3) In the context of e-shopping, does the inclusion of trust, perceived risk, and enjoyment positively contribute to the explanation of the intention to use eshopping?

## Towards an integrated model of e-shopping acceptance

According to the literature that seeks to extend TAM to make it more suitable to e-commerce, a model integrating trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence in TAM is proposed. To build this model on a theoretical rather than an empirical basis, after discussing the conditions necessary for this integration, a first model consistent with TRA serves as a starting point. Then, following reasoning similar to that applied by Davis, additional relationships are added to the model to form an integrated model more suitable to the study of the online shopping TAM model.

Before new attitudinal beliefs such as trust, perceived risk, and enjoyment are integrated in the TAM model, it should be ensured that they are introduced in a manner consistent with their theoretical foundations. Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are behavioral beliefs about a technological behavior, i.e. shopping online, which also applies to perceived risk in this context. It is thus natural to consider this risk as another salient belief about using technology to shop online and to treat it accordingly. Enjoyment refers to the hedonic dimension of e-shopping, which is perceived to be enjoyable as such [92]. Here, the referred theory is the motivational model adapted to information technology by Davis, Bagozzi, and Warshaw [68] in which extrinsic motivation through enjoyment and intrinsic motivation through usefulness conjointly and directly affect the intention to use an IT [93][92]. Because enjoyment is a belief about the same object (e-shopping), as is usefulness, ease of use and perceived risk, it may be considered as a fourth salient belief about shopping online and treated as such.

In our view, the issue of trust is different. The measures presented in Table 1 indicate that in most cases trust (in fact the perceived trustworthiness of or confidence in the vendor) is operationalized as a belief in the online store and brand rather than in using the technology to shop. Benamati, Fuller, Serva, and Baroudi [35] propose to treat trust as a trusting attitude modeled in parallel to the attitude towards online shopping. This study suggests conceptualizing trust as a belief component of a general attitude toward the web vendor and, thus, in the spirit of [35], placing it at the same level as attitude toward online shopping in the model.

When considering how to integrate new variables into the model, another subquestion arises: are the existence and the direction of causalities between these variables and between these variables and TAM beliefs theoretically grounded or dependent only on the context of the studies or on the research method used? The theory of reasoned action posits that a person’s attitude towards an object is directly proportional to the total number of beliefs about that object times the strength of those beliefs [16,94]. Attitude is thus theoretically operationalized as a formative construct or a composite in which salient beliefs are attitude components explaining attitude attribute dimensions captured separately. This formulation assumes no causal relations between beliefs. As an illustration, take the following example of the assumed causal relation between trust and ease of use and consider the equally persuasive logical arguments developed by respected scholars in Pavlou [22] and Gefen et al. [21]. To support the hypothesis that trust affects ease of use, Pavlou [22] uses the following argument: “In the e-commerce context, trust would reduce the consumer’s need to monitor the web retailer’s actions and check every detail, making on-line transactions easier” (p.111). Inversely, to support the hypothesis that ease of use affects trust, Gefen et al. [21] argue, quite ambiguously, that “if more effort is placed in configuring the Web site so that it is usable and navigable, users will conclude that it is both easy to use and that the e-vendor is investing in the relationship” (p. 66).

The introduction of causal relationships between beliefs could then be explained by the almost universal use of structural equations in recent TAM research rather than by well-established theoretical grounds. In fact, in structural modeling, no covariation is permitted between endogenous variables or between exogenous and endogenous variables. Since TAM treats usefulness as a dependent variable of ease of use, then no other variables are mathematically permitted to covariate with it. In fact, if a covariation exists, it has to be indirectly expressed through ease of use or through a “mathematically artificial” causal link to usefulness. It is worth noting that Davis[95] himself initially justified the causal link between ease of use and perceived usefulness on the basis of an argument that was more circumstantial and data dependent than theoretical: “From a causal perspective, the regression results suggest that ease of use may be an antecedent to usefulness rather than a parallel direct determinant of usage” ([95], p. 334). To support the causality between ease of use and usefulness, Davis et al. [14] logically argue that: “Effort saved due to improvement in EOU (Ease of Use) may be redeployed, enabling the person to accomplish more work for the same effort” ([14], p. 987). The transposition of the argument to the context of e-shopping is at least questionable.

Since TAM is rooted in TRA, social influence, if reintroduced in TAM, should be treated the same way as the subjective norms, i.e., as a direct antecedent of intention as in TAM2 [96] (see Figure 1). Based on the previous discussion, we propose to develop an initial model in line with TRA (see Figure 2, TRA extended model for e-shopping) having the following characteristics:

(1) To avoid the ambiguity of causal relations between ease of use, usefulness, risk, and enjoyment, these variables are treated as exogenous variables and modeled as attitude components towards e-shopping;

(2) trust is also considered as an exogenous variable representing a trusting attitude component towards the e-merchant or the website and is modeled in parallel to the attitude towards the behavior of e-shopping [35];

(3) attitude towards e-shopping, trust in the e-merchant and social influence are considered to influence conjointly the e-shopping intention;

(4) all exogenous variables are allowed to covariate.

Following the logic underlying TAM, we also propose a second model (see Figure 3, TAM extended for e-shopping). Based on an approach similar to that used by Davis et al. [14] to develop TAM, the assumption that attitude fully mediates the effect of usefulness is set aside, allowing a direct causal link between usefulness and intention. To explain this causal link, Davis et al. [14] argue that usefulness affects intention in a job context, “…over and above whatever positive or negative feelings may be evoked toward the behavior per se” ([14], p. 986). This argument can be transposed to the context of online shopping by maintaining that the intention to shop online may be motivated by reasons of efficiency that are not fully captured by a positive attitude toward that behavior. For example, the intention to buy online may be motivated by necessity, a positive outcome of being able to acquire a product that is not or not easily available in a physical store. In our sample of studies and in the TAM tradition, this causal relation has been demonstrated to be significant in [25,29,30,33,49,67,74,88,97-101].

Because our aim is to formulate a global model that adequately represents the foundation of past literature on TAM extensions to e-shopping, we tentatively introduce a direct causal link between enjoyment and intention, such a link being significant in [25,51,74,75,81,102], but not tested or not significant in [31,47,49,73,84,98]. As for perceived risk, it can be argued that whatever the positive or negative attitude toward online shopping, the perspective of a negative outcome in terms of privacy violation or fraud may deter the intention to shop via online technologies. In our sample of studies, the relation is significant in [30,33,103], but not in [62,67].

Finally, a causal link between social influence and attitude toward e-shopping is also introduced. In a meta-analysis of TAM to investigate the role of subjective norms in multiple contexts of IT usage, Schepers and Wetzels [8] find this link to be significant. Theoretically, this causal relation is justifiable because of the recognized effect of social influence on attitude change [104] through compliance, identification or internalization [105]. ## Methodology

The above inconsistencies and sometimes contradictory conclusions justify the use of a meta-analysis, which is indeed what Straub and Burton-Jones [13] strongly Identification and selection of studies

First, a broad set of academic databases<sup>1</sup> was searched to identify references to the technology acceptance model using the keywords: TAM, technology acceptance model, technology acceptance, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, and electronic commerce. The period of investigation ended on December 31, 2012. Only peer-reviewed academic or scientific journals and conference proceedings were selected. The search was completed by consulting the specialized search engine Google Scholar and the bibliography of references in order to find references that could not have been previously identified. For the present article, a subset of 109 independent studies pertaining to online purchasing and suited for meta-analysis of correlations was utilized: studies on egovernment and e-banking were excluded. To be eligible, the selected studies had to present quantitative results in the form of correlations and use the TAM variables, i.e. perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and at least one dependent variable: attitude, intention to use or actual use. These 109 studies derive from 102 articles and conference papers since some of them present results of more than one study. All these studies were used for the above qualitative review. The bibliographic search, the selection of the studies, and the coding for the meta-analysis were carried out by the first author and a research assistant. The largest part of the coding was done face to face. When a problem of interpretation rose, it was dealt with immediately.

## Meta-analysis procedures

To respond to the research questions in a meta-analytical way, a two-step procedure was followed. The first step consists in calculating the mean correlations between the model’s eight variables (ease of use, usefulness, risk, enjoyment, trust, attitude, social influence, and intention); no attempt is made to suppress outliers. Because only three correlations between social influence, trust, risk, enjoyment and actual use were found, the meta-analysis does not include the actual usage variable. The second step aggregates the bivariate correlations in a correlation matrix that serves as input for path analysis. This procedure has been utilized for meta-analytic path analyses in information technology management by [8,106,107].

To compute the 28 mean effect sizes (correlations), the Hedges-Olkin’s random effect model with no corrections for artifact, except for sampling error, was chosen for its adequacy to the purpose of our study, i.e., to synthesize past literature.

In our view, the appropriate course of action depends upon the researcher’s objective. For example, if the objective is to summarize the existing data to determine “what is” rather than “what might be” no corrections should be applied ([108], p. 132).

The Hedges and Olkin method has been utilized for meta-analysis in the information technology field in general [9] and for TAM in particular [3]. Based on the recommendation of [109], the following procedure is applied. For each effect size (correlation): (1) the original correlation is transformed into Fisher’s z(r); (2) the individual z(r) are weighted by the inverse variance (sample size -3) to take sampling error into account; (3) the weighted average and associated Z and confidences interval are calculated from transformed effect sizes using a random model because sources of variation other than sampling error are assumed; and (4) the results are back-transformed to their original form for ease of interpretation.

To answer research question (1), the two versions of TAM are compared; one in which attitude at least partially mediates the link between PU, EOU and BI [14] and one without attitude [95]. To address questions (2) and (3), social influence, and then trust, risk, and enjoyment are successively introduced as direct parallel antecedents of intention to assess their individual and conjoint direct contribution to explaining the intention to use e-shopping. Research questions (1), (2) and (3) are treated with block ordinary least square (OLS) regression analysis, while question (4) is investigated via structural equation models (SEM) estimated with IBM SPSS AMOS 20 using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation. Block regression was chosen instead of SEM to answer research questions (1), (2), and (3) because statistical comparison between square correlations coefficients $( \Delta { \sf R } ^ { 2 } )$ is not available in SEM.

Testing a model using a correlation matrix obtained via meta-analysis as input correlations. Because path analysis in structural equation modeling and block regression require a unique sample size, we use the more conservative minimum cumulative sample size rather than the harmonic mean as suggested by Viswesvaran and Ones [110]. The problem of correlations calculated from heterogeneous studies also has to be addressed. Heterogeneity refers to the fact that in meta-analysis the correlations are aggregated across studies with different characteristics in terms of settings (e.g., studies from different countries, studies concerning different technologies, field versus laboratory studies), respondents (e.g., students or consumers with or without experience), measures of variables, and method (e.g., cross-sectional or longitudinal). Those characteristics may be tested as moderators of the correlations and be the object of comparisons across subgroups.

The purpose of this article is to test the suitability of models of general applicability to represent the existing literature rather than to identify potential moderators of the correlations. Thus, following [110] (p. 879), the strategy of submitting three matrices to block regression and path analysis has been chosen: a matrix of the lower 90% confidence intervals of the meta-analyzed correlations, a matrix of the mean correlations, and a matrix of the upper 90% confidence intervals of the correlations. The behaviors of the models under the three conditions are then compared for assessing model robustness. Computations of the meta-analytically derived correlations are made using the Lipsey and Wilson SPSS macro [109].

## Discussion

One of this study’s aims was to assess the research community’s contribution over the last twelve years or so to provide a better explanation of the intention to shop online by extending TAM. In this respect, it can be concluded that the contribution has been significant and valuable, producing a 7% increase in the explanatory power of TAM with attitude and a 16% increase in the explanatory power of basic TAM. These results correspond to findings by Legris et al. in 2003 according to which:

the results from the meta-analysis tend to support the view of some researchers … who suggest that TAM should be modified to include other components in order to explain consistently more than 40% of system use ([6], p.202).

The integrated model proposed here replaces TAM’s causal link between ease of use and usefulness by a covariation and treats all beliefs as covariates, thus significantly deviating from TAM. However, in our view, the benefits obtained in terms of the clarification of the links between beliefs should take precedence over strict compliance with the original model, especially as the search strategy used in studies extending TAM is usually ex post facto and cross-sectional, which does not allow for the clear demonstration of precedence between beliefs. However, this does not mean that beliefs cannot be causally interconnected [112] but rather that the theoretical demonstration of those causal links is missing.

The above results suggest that TAM with attitude is preferable to basic TAM when studying e-shopping. On its own, TAM with attitude explains 45% of the variation in e-shopping intention, which is almost 9% higher than for basic TAM. It can therefore be argued that the role of attitude is probably more central to explaining consumer behavior than to explaining technological behavior in a job context. It may also be principal beliefs in the formation of an attitude toward using an IT in a job context, attitude captures other very important beliefs (such as enjoyment and perceived risk) in an e-shopping context. Accordingly, since attitude does more than partially mediate the effect of usefulness and ease of use on intention, it should not be discarded.

In this review, trust is the variable most often introduced in TAM and one that is results, its status in relation to TAM and its direct influence on e-shopping behavior are debatable. When trust is the only added variable, its direct mean effect on intention is important (β=0.29, p<0.001 in the Extended TRA), but when usefulness, perceived risk, and enjoyment are also taken into account in directly explaining intention to use, its relative influence significantly declines $( \beta { = } 0 . 0 9 , \ \mu { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ . Additional studies should be undertaken to clarify the role of trust in the evaluation of online shopping by consumers. Applying an approach similar to that used by Davis et al. [14], it would be useful to systematically elicit generic salient beliefs about web vendors, which will undoubtedly include trust [113]. The relationship between attitude to shopping online and attitude toward the web vendor should also be further investigated (as in Chen et al. [114] ). From a marketing perspective, trust is a sine qua non condition of any future relationship between a vendor and its consumers [115]. In building consumer trust, a company’s reputation or brand image is as important as the experience the company can offer through its web interface. Since trust is not constant over time [116], its dynamic nature should also be considered at the different stages of the decision-making process: i.e., from the recognition of a need to the final purchase.

Despite initiatives by various stakeholders such as banks and credit card issuers to mitigate the risk to consumers in the event of fraud, non-delivery or identity theft by offering refunds, insurance or assistance for online transactions, risk remains pervasive and perceived risk plays a significant role in negatively and directly affecting attitude (β=-0.16, p<0.001) and purchase intention $( \beta { = } { - } 0 . 1 6 , ~ \rho { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . To more clearly understand this impact, the perception of risk should be analyzed, taking its various facets into account. Featherman and Pavlou [58] identify seven facets of the perception of risk (performance risk, financial risk, time risk, psychological risk, social risk, privacy risk and overall risk), which may have different impacts on the intention to purchase online. The higher the perceived risk, the higher the complexity of the decision-making process, and the higher the probability of failure in performing a task on a website. It is also important that the study of perceived risk integrate not only these facets, but also others such as cultural and situational factors. For example, Pilara Alcantara et al. [117] have shown that the perception of risk varies between countries.

The direct impact of enjoyment on intention (β=0.26, p<0.001) and on attitude towards e-shopping $( \beta { = } 0 . 2 5 , \ \rho { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ is also worthy of note. In fact, how to generate enjoyment through usability and content should be a major concern for designers of eshopping solutions. As mentioned by Guo and Poole [118], researchers tend to simply refer to enjoyment instead of flow when exploring the experiential side of shopping, yet a state of flow involves more than enjoyment because it includes a focused immersion and even a temporal dissociation [116]. In our study, three articles include some aspects of flow in their measure of playfulness [65,74,79] (see Table 3). Although the concept of playfulness, initially called micro-computer playfulness [69], derives from IT literature, the concept of state of flow is considered to be more appropriate in dealing with a shopping behavior. From a marketing point of view, where consumers’ experience is central to explaining their behavior, the experiential side of e-shopping captured by the concept of flow, or the related concept of cognitive absorption, and their role in explaining e-consumer behavior merit further investigation. Indeed, cognitive absorption is defined as a state of deep involvement that online users experience as they participate in an e-shopping context activity and includes the tendency to lose track of time [119,120]. Some studies have also shown that cognitive absorption plays a key role in shaping intention to return to a website [121].The whole idea of mixed-use systems, combined hedonic and utilitarian use and mixed motive systems (e.g., intrinsic/extrinsic motivations), is gaining considerable traction in TAM and systems-use studies [122,123] and also merits further investigation.

Although the direct impact of social influence on the intention to buy online is relatively modest $( \beta { = } 0 . 0 8 , \rho { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ , its impact on the attitude toward purchasing products and services online is more significant $( \beta { = } 0 . 1 5 , \rho { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Given the current popularity of social networks, marketing initiatives to develop a positive electronic “word of mouth” about buying online may contribute positively to visibility on the Internet, to the vendor’s reputation, brand image and product and, ultimately, to sales and loyalty [124,125] .

## Conclusion

This article does not claim to close the debate on the relevance of TAM as a fundamental theory to explain e-shopping. However, it appears to be clear that the extended version of TAM that includes trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social customer (e.g. his or her innovativeness, age or gender), and of the e-merchant (e.g. its responsiveness) and, on the other, online purchase behavior. The time has come to concentrate e-shopping research on more distal antecedents of buying behavior to clarify their effect on consumer perceptions and subsequently make more concrete recommendations on how they could be taken into account in impacting trust, perceived risk, enjoyment, and social influence. Because online purchasing is both a technological act and an act of consumption, IT and marketing researchers would benefit from pooling their efforts to advance knowledge in this area and to be able to provide common recommendations transferable to the business community.

## E-Shopping Acceptance: a Qualitative and Meta-Analytic Review

Main results

\- Inconsistencies and contradictions in the way researchers have adapted TAM to e-shopping

\- An extended TAM for e-shopping is tested meta-analytically

TAM with attitude is better than basic TAM when studying e-shopping acceptance

\- After attitude, enjoyment is the stronger direct predictor of e-shopping acceptance

\- Usefulness, risk, trust and social influence follow in that order

\- E-shopping acceptance research should now concentrate on more distal antecedents
