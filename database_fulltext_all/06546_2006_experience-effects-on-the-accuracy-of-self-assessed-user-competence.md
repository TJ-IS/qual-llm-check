# Experience effects on the accuracy of self-assessed user competence

Jane I. Gravill <sup>a,\*</sup>, Deborah R. Compeau <sup>a</sup>, Barbara L. Marcolin

<sup>a</sup> Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, Ont., Canada N6A 3K7 <sup>b</sup> Haskayne School of Business, The University of Calgary, Calgary, Alta., Canada T2N 1N4

Received 4 November 2004; received in revised form 13 August 2005; accepted 16 October 2005 Available online 28 November 2005

## Abstract

Self-managed learning is the normal way that users learn to work with software within organizations. To be effective, selfmanaged, learning requires individuals to self-assess their IT knowledge; accurate self-assessment helps them optimize the capabilities they possess and be aware of those they do not. This study demonstrated that, in general, individuals did not accurately self-assess their knowledge of the software they used. However, we also found that the accuracy of self-assessment increased with greater experience in, and better understanding of, IT domains.

Organizations need to recognize the self-assessment problem to facilitate effective software learning and to gain the most from their software investments.

‘‘He who knows best, knows how little he knows’’, Thomas Jefferson.

For organizations to use their information technology (IT) effectively, employees need to engage in ongoing user learning in order to keep pace with changing technology. New learning requirements result from changes in both this and the tasks that individuals undertake for their work. Managing the learning process presents many issues and challenges for both organizations and employees [7,46] and one of the most challenging phases of the process is in assessing training needs [38]. It requires indepth assessment of the individual, the task, and the organization to determine what training is required [26,27,19]. It is difficult to design training content or determine the most appropriate mechanism for delivery without first understanding users’ needs. Moreover, it is difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of training if training requirements are not clearly understood.

A second challenge relates to the locus of responsibility for maintaining IT skills. Research on user training and learning consistently shows that selftraining has become the most common means by which users learn. Increasingly, organizations are relying on e-Learning programs or training portals that require employees to self-select themselves into training programs to meet new knowledge demands and keep pace with the changing occupational competency profiles of existing or new roles within the organization.

Thus it makes sense to revisit the training and learning literature from a self-management perspective; i.e., the means by which people ‘‘engage in actions designed to change or maintain [their] own behavior’’ [6]. Selfmanagement is a theoretically strong, empirically proven method of increasing positive and decreasing negative behavior [11]. Both Social Cognitive Theory [5] and Goal Setting Theory [18] provide support for the effectiveness of self-management, which involves four related processes: assessing current capability; assessing future needs; setting goals; identifying supports for, and barriers against, developing competence. Thus, needs assessment – comparing current capabilities to future needs – is central to self-management.

Such learning is important in organizations and a key factor of it is an individual’s ability to assess his or her capabilities [24]. It follows that computer users need to be able to be accurate in assessing their capabilities in the IT domain. Our study applied a user competence cube framework [20] and examines the implications of aligning the components of the cognitive dimension in a self-managed learning situation, in order to understand the individuals’ ability to self-assess their knowledge.

Recent research suggests that users may not be able to assess their own skills accurately, and found that users have a tendency to overestimate their IT knowledge. Overestimation is a tendency that is common in many areas of human functioning, yet detrimental to learning [17]. Hence, even though employees may be motivated to learn or have established learning goals, they may not be effectively self-managing their learning. If they overestimate their knowledge, they will not select themselves for training. If they underestimate their knowledge, they will not utilize or enhance their capabilities, potentially decreasing organizational efficiency. Thus, low levels of self-awareness and misaligned self-assessments are detrimental to effective self-managed learning and thus to organizational performance.

Our study considers previous findings about the accuracy of competence assessments and seeks to understand these by addressing two questions:

1. What is the nature of self-reported (subjective) knowledge assessments and how do they relate to the individuals’ demonstrated capabilities (objective knowledge) within a specific software domain.

2. Under what conditions do people overestimate or underestimate their knowledge in this context?

Learning theories and models suggest that level of experience produces varied learning outcomes throughout the process [3,15]. Thus, in addressing the second question we primarily focused on how several aspects of a user’s experience influenced how accurately they selfassessed their competence within a specific software domain.

## 1. User competence—the cognitive dimension

This study applies the cognitive dimension of a user competence cube (UCC), as shown in Fig. 1. This framework is appropriate, as it identifies the dimensions of user competence and the role of the knowledge types involved. The cognitive dimension consists of declarative, procedural, and strategic outcomes that represent incremental stages in the learning, or knowledge acquisition, process. Previous UCC research examined declarative knowledge and suggested that the remaining two types be examined. Our study focused on the first two stages in the learning process, declarative and procedural knowledge.

Fig. 1. User competence cube [20].

Initial learning results in information that is acquired in the form of declarative or verbal knowledge. Declarative knowledge is defined as the early phase in the learning process. In it the individual cognitively encodes a set of facts about a skill. It is typically measured by a paper and pencil test and is often referred to as the ‘know what’ or ‘know that’ type of knowledge [2]. For example, declarative knowledge allows individuals to identify what functionality exists within a particular software product, understand that each function performs specific activities, and understand where the functions are located. Declarative knowledge is not entirely conceptual in nature; it is an initial stage where individuals form the knowledge that is the foundation for later learning stages. In our study, declarative knowledge was primarily measured by examining what respondents knew about the capabilities of a software package. Context influences the assessment of knowledge and was thus an important consideration in interpreting results.

Later, individuals develop procedural knowledge and meaningful cognitive structures begin to form; knowledge is better organized, and individuals begin to link the actions required to perform activities. This phase is also called the knowledge organization stage. It helps individuals answer ‘know how’ questions as they learn which aspects of a domain link with others to conduct the required tasks (e.g. How can I use Excel to create a chart to display annual revenues? How can I display the chart in landscape mode? etc.). Procedural knowledge is most often measured by a hands-on test to evaluate the extent to which individuals understand the linkages required to perform tasks. Even later in the learning process, individuals develop sufficient knowledge to address the ‘know why’ questions; this is the strategic knowledge, which was outside the scope of this work.

Based on the UCC framework, we examined selfassessments of competence in both declarative and procedural knowledge within the specific domain of spreadsheet software. Previous research had tended to focus on one or the other of these forms of knowledge rather than both. What was not clear was where to put self-assessed knowledge. Do self-reported assessments of knowledge reflect declarative or procedural knowledge? How aligned are these knowledge components? We attempted to determine whether individuals’ judgments of their IT software capabilities was based on their perceived level of declarative or procedural knowledge. In this way, we tried to better understand what is and is not being measured when individuals are asked to judge their own capabilities. Furthermore, we wanted to understand how a user’s IT experience influenced their ability to assess their own competence.

## 2. Conceptual model and hypotheses

The conceptual model for this study is presented in Fig. 2. The model illustrates the relationship between our three knowledge constructs – self-assessed knowledge, declarative knowledge, and procedural knowledge – and the influence of experience on this relationship.

Self-assessed, or subjective, knowledge represents the subject’s perception-based knowledge. Declarative and procedural knowledge are based on knowledge test scores and represent the subject’s demonstrated, or objective, knowledge. Procedural and declarative knowledge do not overlap because they represent different aspects of knowledge. The subjective, self-assessed knowledge construct is represented by the oval in the center of the model, which is illuminated. The shaded area where the construct boundaries overlap represents accurate selfassessment: the self-assessed and the demonstrated knowledge constructs are aligned. The larger the discrepancy between the self-assessed and demonstrated knowledge, the smaller the overlap, and the less accurate the self-assessment. This model illustrates the similarities and dissimilarities in the construct domains [31].

The concept of experience has a long tradition in IS research. It has been found to positively influence individuals’ use of computers [36]. The technologyacceptance literature also shows that experience has positive implications for technology use [39,41,12].

Individuals who know more about a target IS (gained, for example, through exposure over time) are expected to perceive the system as easier to use; and thus are expected to believe that it is more useful. Moreover, experience has been found to moderate the effects of some technology adoption variables [13,35,40,42]. We built upon initial conceptualizations of experience and extended the work by examining how three kinds of computer experience affected how accurately individuals self-assessed their knowledge.

## 2.1. Alignment of assessments

Our first hypothesis considered the alignment of the subjective and objective knowledge measures. We asked how similar the objective, or demonstrated, knowledge was to the self-assessed knowledge. If selfassessed and demonstrated knowledge were aligned, then each subject should have similar mean scores on each of their knowledge dimensions. However, previous research using a declarative knowledge test had found that self-assessed and demonstrated measures of knowledge were not consistent. In a study of spreadsheet and word processing capabilities, subjects tended to overestimate their knowledge relative to their scores on declarative knowledge tests, especially for word processing. Outside the IS domain, research in psychology found evidence of both an above-average effect (a tendency to overestimate one’s capabilities, especially relative to the average) and a below-average effect (a tendency to underestimate one’s capabilities) [16]. Underestimation effects were found in domains of greater difficulty and overestimation effects in ‘easier domains. Subjects were found to believe they were worse than their peers when they considered more challenging domains. Thus, Hypothesis 1 was:

H1. Subjects’ knowledge self-assessments will be significantly different from both their demonstrated declarative and procedural knowledge.

## 2.2. Experience

Research suggested that experience leads to knowledge and experience positively influences computer use [37]. In our study, experience was conceptualized in three ways: number of years of experience, breadth of work, and anchoring or proximal experience. Years of spreadsheet experience represented the length of time that individuals had been exposed to the target software was consistent with prior research. Individuals with a longer history of using a software package had had more opportunity to understand its range of capabilities and to develop their perspective of their own knowledge of it. We proposed that those with a greater number of years of experience will be more aware of the functionality available, and thus will be more aware of their level of knowledge of this functionality. However, we recognized that a quantitative measure, such as years of experience, does not capture the more complex nature of experience and thus that the effect may not be strong.

H2. Subjects with greater years of spreadsheet software experience will self-assess their knowledge more accurately than subjects with less experience.

However, we felt that experience was more than simply length of use of a technology, we therefore examined other aspects of IT experience. Breadth [25] captures the degree to which individuals have been exposed to technology. We believed that individuals who had been exposed to a wider variety of IT gained greater understanding of any software package: they had a broader and richer frame of reference [44] from which to draw in estimating their own knowledge in a particular domain. Previous research on learning suggested that developing knowledge incorporated the notions of discrimination and generalization (i.e.. learning the boundaries or the rules). Exposure to multiple types of software may thus help individuals discover those items common to several packages and those unique to one. In doing so, the individual’s mental schemas are enhanced [21]. This illustrates how broad exposure to IT can help develop capability in a particular technology. We also believed that exposure to a variety of technologies forced users to consider what can be known about technology, and that this would stimulate them to be more accurate in their self-assessments. As an example, consider the database (DB) features in Microsoft Excel<sup>TM</sup>. A user who has only ever been exposed to it has developed an understanding of its functionality. A user who has been exposed to mainframe and other microcomputer database packages in addition to this, and who has studied database concepts, will have a richer understanding of what is implied by the DB features. Thus other experiences should help a user understand the functionality and more correctly estimate his or her capabilities. Therefore, we proposed that exposure to a wider variety of technological domains would allow individuals to be more accurate in self-assessing their knowledge.

H3. Subjects with greater breadth of IT experience will self-assess their knowledge more accurately than those with narrower IT experience.

We also examined the influence of anchoring experiences that set the immediate context for knowledge self-assessment. This addressed the recency effect (completing a software test just prior to self-assessing knowledge). Previous research had found that subjects knowledge self-assessments were more accurate when they were completed just after a declarative knowledge test of their ability (i.e., their self-assessments were more aligned with their score on the declarative knowledge test). The researchers argued that the declarative knowledge test was a stimulus forcing individuals to be more reflective in considering what they did and did not know. This suggested that the accuracy of self-assessments was influenced by the context in which they were made. In our study, we therefore varied the order of self-assessment and actual knowledge tests to check the findings of earlier research and to explore the effect in a different software domain (MS Excel versus WordPerfect and Lotus). We did this to determine whether, as expected, the knowledge assessments of subjects self-reporting after the anchoring experience would be more closely aligned with their demonstrated knowledge scores.

H4. Subjects who complete the self-assessment after the declarative and procedural knowledge tests will selfassess their knowledge more accurately than those who complete the self-report first.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Study design and experimental method

In this repeated-measures design, each subject completed a background questionnaire measuring breadth of ITexperience, years of spreadsheet experience, and other individual variables. They also completed knowledge self-assessment and tests of both declarative and procedural knowledge in a controlled environment. Upon arrival at the experiment site, subjects were randomly assigned to treatment group 1 or 2. Both groups were first given the background questionnaire; then group 1 was given the self-assessment knowledge measure followed by the declarative and procedural tests while group 2 was given the declarative and procedural knowledge tests followed by the self-assessment knowledge measure. This design is illustrated in Fig. 3.

## 3.2. Subjects

Subjects werevolunteers from four largeorganizations located in a medium-sized metropolitan area from the financial, retail, consulting, and distribution sectors. Subjects were screened to ensure that they used Microsoft Excel<sup>TM</sup> at work. All four organizations had standardized on this as their spreadsheet software package of choice.

Notices were posted on the organizations’ intranets indicating that researchers needed volunteers to participate in a study designed to further understand the effectiveness of the self-managed learning process. The notice emphasized that we wanted volunteers with a wide range of experience and expertise. This was intended to reduce the possibility that only highachievers would volunteer to participate. We succeeded in obtaining a study sample of subjects with a wide distribution of experience and knowledge.

## 4. Measures

## 4.1. Dependent variables

## 4.1.1. Self-assessed knowledge

This was measured by six items that asked subjects to indicate, on a seven-point Likert scale, their familiarity with different dimensions of spreadsheet functionality (editing, graphing, formulas, macro functions, database functions, printing). These items were chosen by reviewing the main categories of functionality in Excel [45]. Scores for this and other knowledge measures, were converted to percentages. Scales for this and all other measures are given in Appendix A.

## 4.1.2. Declarative Knowledge

This measure consisted of 30 items, focusing on the same categories of knowledge as the specific selfreported measure. It was a pencil and paper, multiplechoice test. The question topics were chosen randomly from a list of tasks available for the Microsoft Office User Specialist (MOUS) certification examinations (see http:// www.microsoft.com/traincert/mcp/mous/default.asp).

This examination tests the full range of skills required to be certified as a specialist user of MS Excel software.

## 4.1.3. Procedural knowledge

This measure consisted of 22 items from an authorized Microsoft Excel MOUS certification test provided by NetLearn Solutions Inc. (www.netlearnsolutions.com) covering the same categories of knowledge as given for the declarative knowledge. The 22- item scale tested subjects’ knowledge of the six major functional categories from various perspectives to produce an overall procedural knowledge test score for each subject. The overall test was divided into two sections representing different levels of proficiency. The first, called ‘proficient’ in the Office 97 and ‘core’ in later certifications, covered creating, modifying, printing, formatting, and creating worksheets, and applying ranges, using functions, draw, and charts, and saving spreadsheets as HTML files. The second, ‘expert’, level covered formatting worksheets, using lists, printing workbooks, auditing a worksheet, using advanced functionality, using macros, importing and exporting data, using templates, using multiple workbooks, and using workgroup functions [23].

## 4.1.4. Accuracy of self-assessment

This is the degree of alignment (or similarity) between the individuals’ self-report score and their score on either the declarative or procedural knowledge test. We did not use difference scores (subtracting one variable from another) as it has been shown that they are statistically problematic [8].

## 4.2. Independent variables

It may seem troublesome to use self-reported years of experience and experience breadth measures when our theory suggested that users may not know what they think they know. If individuals are not very good at estimating their capability, then how can they estimate their experience? We argue that judgments regarding experience and knowledge are separate, involving different cognitive processes. The years of experience measure required subjects to provide factual information—their number of years experience. The experience breadth measure required a series of yes or no responses to questions about their use of several software packages. Thus, the experience-related questions are more factual and less evaluative. Research suggests that factual measures are less susceptible to distortion. One research study [28], for example, shows that stimuli with simple, plausible causes were perceived more accurately than stimuli with complex causal process they were subject to greater personal bias (i.e., selfassessing ability in using the software package).

Another research study [43] also supports the possibility of differential accuracy between experience and ability self-reports. In a comparison of paper-based and computer-based surveys, the authors found that measures that asked for more factual information (such as experience) were less susceptible to change when the administration method was varied than measures that were more evaluative in nature. Thus, we believe that self-reports are appropriate for the more factual breadth and years of experience measures, and that these reports are not as subject to change as the more evaluative measures used to examine self-assessed knowledge.

## 4.2.1. Years of experience

This was measured by the number of years using spreadsheet software. A median split was conducted to produce a binary variable representing lower and higher levels of experience. This single item measure was used to examine how experience affected the alignment between subjective self-assessments of knowledge and the objective knowledge measures. It is the typical experience dimension used in extant research.

## 4.2.2. Experience breadth

This was assessed using an updated version of the user competence breadth measure which asked whether the subject had ever used each of 26 different technologies. As with years of experience, a median split was conducted to produce a binary variable for the analysis.

## 4.2.3. Experience anchoring

This was a binary variable reflecting treatment group 1 or 2 (the order in which the knowledge tests were completed). This variable indicated how the anchoring experience was manipulated by administering the knowledge test prior to self-assessment.

## 4.2.4. Control factor—social desirability bias (SDB)

Subjects also completed the balanced inventory for desirable responding (BIDR) measure [29,30]. This measure was included to control for any social desirability bias that may have been present in subjects self-reported knowledge assessments. This bias indicates whether subjects have a tendency to misrepresent themselves and respond in a biased manner. Evaluating one’s own knowledge can be a sensitive issue, so we had to rule out the possibility that results were impacted by SDB (leading to either over- or underestimation).

The BIDR measure consisted of 40 items in total and consisted of two subscales. The first (20 items) measured impression management; that is, the tendency for subjects to deliberately misrepresent themselves. The second (also 20 items) measured self-deception; that is, the tendency for subjects to give responses that are honest but positively biased. Internal consistency tests have been conducted on both the self-deception and image management scales resulting in coefficient alphas that range from 0.68 to 0.80 for self-deception and 0.75 to 0.86 for image management. Validity tests also indicate that the 40 BIDR items are concurrently valid in correlating with other SDB measures, such as 0.71 with the Marlowe–Crowne scale [22] and 0.80 with the multidimensional social desirability inventory [33].

## 4.3. Pilot study

We conducted a pilot study within a medium-sized organization and also pre-tested the declarative knowledge measure. The data obtained in these studies were used to improve the study design and experiment procedures.

The declarative knowledge pre-test was conducted on 11 subjects who were doctoral students, from various disciplines, at a Canadian university. The mean score out of 30 for the pilot test was 16.8, the median was 17, the mode was 16, with a standard deviation of 2.9. Pretest scores ranged from 12 to 22, out of a total of 30. We replaced two questions in the declarative knowledge test because the pre-test data analysis showed that these questions had low reliability. We also replaced these questions because they were oriented toward version 2000 of Excel and it was our intention to include functionality resident in both Excel 97 and 2000 software versions. We changed the wording of one question because it was too similar to a previous one, however the question topic remained the same. Other questions were reworded to improve clarity, based on feedback from the pre-test subjects. We also conducted power calculations using the pre-test data to determine the appropriate sample size for our experiment.

## 5.3.5. Social desirability bias

The absence of such bias is confirmed if the Social Desirability Bias scale is uncorrelated with the target variable [9,10]; i.e., the knowledge self-assessment construct. Regression results indicated that the bias results for image management and self-deception were not significantly related to subjects’ overall knowledge self-assessment scores $( p = 0 . 3 3$ for self-deception overall, $p = 0 . 6 3$ for image management overall). This validated the knowledge self-assessments and study results by ruling out an alternative bias explanation and confirmed that subjects were not misrepresenting their overall scores.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Hypothesis 1—knowledge construct alignment

Our findings indicated that there is limited alignment, either in terms of correlations or mean similarity, between the subjective knowledge self-assessment and demonstrated objective declarative and procedural knowledge constructs. This is consistent with previous research. Prior confidence and knowledge miscalibration results suggest that individuals have difficulty selfassessing their knowledge [1,32,34]. Specifically, our research findings illustrate that knowledge self-assessments are not consistently aligned with demonstrated declarative and procedural knowledge and confirm that accurate knowledge self-awareness is a problem in the IT context.

Our study further showed that subjective selfassessments tend to be more closely aligned with demonstrated procedural than with declarative knowledge. It is interesting that respondents’ self-assessments were more closely aligned with procedural than declarative knowledge, even though procedural knowledge comes later in the learning process.

We do not contend that either demonstrated declarative or procedural knowledge test scores more accurately reflect a user’s knowledge than subjective self-reported, or self-assessed, knowledge, in all contexts. However, our results indicate that researchers should be aware of the possible discrepancies between self-assessed and demonstrated knowledge constructs when designing and conducting their research studies.

## 6.2. The influence of experience

Overall, the three knowledge constructs – selfassessed, declarative, and procedural knowledge – were not aligned, but that the degree of alignment was influenced by experience and the type of knowledge examined. Effects were only observed for two types of experience and the patterns of the effects differed, depending on the type of experience examined.

## 6.2.1. Hypothesis 2—years of experience

The between-subjects effect of years of experience was significant, indicating that subjects with more years of experience scored differently on the knowledge tests. However, the interaction itself was not significant. Subjects with more years of experience were not found to be more accurate in their self-assessment.

## 6.2.2. Hypothesis 3—breadth of experience

Subjects with greater breadth of experience were more aligned in their knowledge self-assessment. Therefore, the knowledge self-assessment of individuals with wider IT-related experiences may be more representative of their demonstrated abilities. Also, the self-assessments of subjects with a greater breadth of IT-related experience were more closely aligned with their procedural knowledge than their declarative knowledge; the judgments of less experienced subjects were more aligned with declarative knowledge.

## 6.2.3. Hypothesis 4—anchoring experience

Varying individuals’ self-awareness with an anchoring stimulus resulted in a closer alignment between selfassessed and declarative knowledge, rather than between self-assessed and procedural knowledge.

## 7. Further analysis and alternative explanations

## 7.1. Underestimation

In our analysis, we saw that all groups underestimated both knowledge constructs, with the exception of declarative knowledge for both high experience groups (years and breadth). This is an intriguing finding: although research has found below-average effects or underestimation when compared to an average, it is generally acknowledged that most people exaggerate their skills, traits, and abilities. We considered five possible explanations for why we consistently observed under-estimation effects.

## 7.1.1. Specific questioning rationale

First, specific questioning elicits different responses than general questioning; indeed general questioning apparently leads to greater overestimation. We used specific questioning in our study. Our knowledge selfassessment measure consisted of six specific questions regarding subjects’ knowledge of the software domain. Therefore, our type of questioning may have contributed to underestimation. Further analysis of our study data supports the argument that general questioning leads to overestimation. Subjects in our study overestimated their general knowledge assessments and underestimated their specific assessments.

## 7.1.2. Difficulty of knowledge tests rationale

We ruled out the possibility that our knowledge tests were too easy. The declarative knowledge test results ranged from 3% to 90% with a mean of 47%, and the procedural knowledge test results ranged from 13% to 91% with a mean score of 54%. Thus, there is no evidence that a floor effect on the knowledge test artificially created the appearance of underestimation.

## 7.1.3. Gender rationale

Prior research has observed a difference in user competence scores for men and women, finding that women reported being less effective in using computing than did men. Our sample had a 63% female content and thus the underestimation tendency might reflect a gender imbalance in the sample.

In order to examine this possibility, we conducted another ANOVA, using gender as a between-subjects factor. As with previous analyses, the within-subjects effect of knowledge type was significant. The betweensubjects effect was also significant, and a comparison of the group means showed that women scored lower on the three knowledge measures than men. But the interaction (Fig. 6) showed that it is on self-assessment that women primarily scored differently than men $( p < 0 . 0 0 6 )$ . For women, the self-assessment score was the lowest of the three, while for men self-assessment was the highest. Thus, it appears that men tended to overestimate their capabilities, whereas women tended to underestimate theirs. Moreover, self-assessments were more aligned with declarative knowledge for women and with procedural knowledge for men. Thus, the higher percentage of women in our study could have contributed to the underestimation results.

## 7.1.4. Different technology domains rationale

Previous work and our study have shown that technology matters when assessing the learning process in the IT context. Different software domains have resulted in different over-or underestimation effects, even when similar declarative knowledge measures were used. It has been suggested that the broad offering of menu displays in spreadsheet software have made users aware of portions of the software that had functionality they did not use. Thus, the difference in technology may also explain part of the difference in the type of effects observed. Outside IS, studies have found underestimation effects in domains of greater difficulty, and overestimation effects in the domains rated as ‘easier’. Subjects were found to believe they were worse than their peers when they considered more challenging domains.

Practically then, these results emphasize the need to consider the nature of the technological domain in addition to the type of knowledge when interpreting or comparing results.

## 8. Limitations of the study

Three limitations of our study should be noted. First, the research model was only tested in one software domain, therefore generalizations to others cannot be assumed. Second, the subjects who participated in our research study were volunteers, and we cannot be certain the study sample is representative of all employees. More importantly, the sample size was small, and this led us to adopt a critical value of p < 0.10 for examining significance in our results. Third, the use of the Microsoft Office User Specialist (MOUS) certification is a comprehensive and validated test. However, it is not an unbiased knowledge measure because users in the workplace may not aim (or need) to acquire full knowledge of the package to complete their work tasks.

## 9. Implications

As discussed earlier, self-management has become the dominant process through which users develop their IT skills. Our findings suggest that breadth of an individuals’ IT-related experience should be considered when facilitating effective self-managed learning. Subjects with greater breadth of experience in a wider variety of technology domains were able to self-assess their knowledge for a specific software package. Individuals need to be aware of their abilities, in order to use and further develop them. However, longer exposure to a software domain does not necessarily result in a better understanding of capabilities within a domain.

Many organizations are turning to e-Learning alternatives and are relying on web-based learning or competency portals. They expect employees to selfselect themselves into various training programs and be responsible for updating their skills to meet the everchanging competency profiles of existing or new employment positions. The benefits of this process will not be reaped unless employees who require assistance are identified and given the additional tools they need.

Organizations should encourage employees to gain exposure to a broader variety of technologies than may currently be prescribed by job-related requirements. Exposing employees to a variety of technological domains has positive implications for learning.

Our study results suggest that knowledge and awareness of abilities may be gained and transferred from one or more IT domains. If procedural knowledge can be gained and transferred, then a high level of declarative knowledge may not necessarily be the first step in the learning process.

## A.2. Procedural knowledge measure

The procedural knowledge test was based on a Microsoft Office User Specialist examination for Excel software; it consists of 2 parts with 11 questions each for a total of 22 procedural knowledge test questions. The first part measured mid-level procedural knowledge and the second part measured more advanced procedural knowledge.

For the procedural knowledge test, subjects were directed to the workstation where the testing program was pre-launched. They were shown how to press the ‘Start’ key to begin. Subjects were instructed to answer the test questions by reading the instructions on the screen and click on the ‘Next’ key (located at the bottom of the screen) when they had completed each of the 22 questions. When the test was completed, a results window popped up and subjects’ test scores were recorded.

For each question, subjects were provided with a partial spreadsheet and asked to complete a specific task.

Examples of questions from the first basic test asked subjects to demonstrate how to: use the Auto Sum feature to add a series of number, fill an identified cell block with a specific color, create a chart with various features from a series of numbers and text provided, draw an object to specification, hide particular rows/ columns, set page margins to specified width.

Examples of questions from the second advanced test asked subjects to demonstrate how to: find a specific comment in a worksheet, create and name a new worksheet in a current workbook, use a custom list to select specific information, create a chart collection based on information provided, add a text box with specific parameters and content, use the merge and center function to manipulate text, create a database to match a specified design, rotate text in a chart, create multi-sheet formulas, etc.
