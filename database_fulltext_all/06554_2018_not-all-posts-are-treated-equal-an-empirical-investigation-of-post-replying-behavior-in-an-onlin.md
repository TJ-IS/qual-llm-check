# Not all posts are treated equal: An empirical investigation of post replying behavior in an online travel community

Jiaming Fang<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Lei Chen<sup>a</sup>, Xiaoyuan Wang<sup>a</sup>, Benjamin George<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Management and Economics, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, China <sup>b</sup> Beacom School of Business, University of South Dakota, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online community participation Post replying behavior Feedback dynamics Norm of reciprocity Social exchange behavior

## A B S T R A C T

The post replying behavior in online communities (OCs) has garnered little consideration, even though the feedback behavior represents the central social dynamic of OCs and greatly determines the vibrancy of OCs. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap, this study aims to identify major sharing post-related variables that explain the heterogeneity in the post replying behavior in knowledge sharing OCs. The research model is validated through a panel dataset assembled from an online travel community. The results reveal that sharing post length and vividness, contributors’ expertise and degree centrality, and members’ social interactions have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant associations with the number of replying posts.

## 1. Introduction

Online communities (OCs) are collective groups of individuals who utilize computer-mediated environments to interact around a shared interest [59]. Online reviews or user-generated contents (UGCs) in OCs have become one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential information sources for consumers to make decisions [2]. It is believed that the success of an OC is dependent on the ability to attract community members to proactively participate and contribute in meaningful discussion [12,63]. Indeed, Pai and Tsai [49] report that OC participation and engagement can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhance loyalty intentions. However, a consistent <sup>fi</sup>nding in the study of UGC system is that most users do not participate in and contribute to the systems they visit. The “silent groups” in OCs, usually known as lurkers, comprise the majority of community members [58]. The lack of participation in OCs makes the majority of such communities fail at growing into vibrant social media forums beyond merely existing [39].

Participation behavior in OCs has inspired a number of research streams, engendering important insights into its antecedents (e.g., [31,35,63]), and its rami<sup>fi</sup>cations (e.g., [49,62]). One notable common feature of these studies is that the majority focused on psychological factors associated with the content sharing (e.g., posting product information and sharing experiences). However, the activities performed by one member in an OC require both initiative contribution as an information source, and others’ feedback on the contribution [35]. Surprisingly, little attention so far has been given to the feedback dynamics (i.e., the post-replying behavior) in an OC, even though the feedback behavior is deemed as the key social dynamics of an OC [4,12].

There is obviously a di<sup>f</sup>erence between the motivations driving an individual to reply to a post and the motivations driving people to share knowledge in an OC. Earlier research suggests that establishing reputation and gaining approval from other peers [31,48] and developing a sense of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the community [39,52] are the two primary motivations for people to contribute knowledge in an OC. Unlike knowledge contribution behavior, the post replying behavior may not obtain any direct monetary return or external rewards [74]. Prior research reports that the most important drivers of responding behavior in online discussion groups consist of the controversiality of the content, the readability of the post, and the status of the post author [53]. Therefore, factors facilitating content sharing in OCs are rather distinct from the factors impelling people to comment on a post.

Extending current OC research to investigate the feedback behavior in OCs also has important managerial implications. Prior research reports that the contribution behaviors in OCs are largely decided by the feedback that they would receive from other members. Joyce and Kraut [33] observe that responses to community members’ postings by other members increase their likelihood of contribution. Similarly, Jin et al. [31] and Kim and Sundar [35] reveal that individuals are more willing to contribute in a community when they perceive positive feedback of their postings. According to these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, insu<sup>fi</sup>cient feedback from other members may hinder the contributors from realizing their initial purposes and discourage the contributors to contribute in the future. Consistent with this view, Cheng et al. [12] report that the contributors who receive no feedback are likely to leave a community. Furthermore, the dynamic communication processes (e.g., providing comments on posted contents) can also promote members’ engagement in an OC [7,56,65]. Taken together, besides attracting community members to contribute, the survival and success of an OC is dependent on creating a community ambiance that enables the contributors get feedback on how well their contributions are perceived by others in the online community. Obviously, both people’s contribution and dynamic social interaction behavior deserve our explicit attention.

Having noted all this, little attention has been paid to the factors driving community members’ post replying behavior. To our best knowledge, there is no research that directly models heterogeneity in the post replying behavior in OCs. This lack of research prevents OC operators from identifying and proposing well-grounded strategies to attract community members to post replies. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this important gap, the purpose of the present study is to explore which sharing posts characteristics can explain the di<sup>f</sup>erences in the replying post number of a sharing post in OCs. There are various types of OCs that serve a wide range of purposes for their users, such as social media based communities [38], online collaborative communities [74], social Q&A communities [31], transactional online communities [59,60], and knowledge sharing communities [15,75]. This research is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally concerned with post replying behavior in knowledge sharing OCs. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from the social media based communities that is centered on tight social linkages, knowledge sharing OC is generally based on loose social relationships between users that shares a common interest. In knowledge sharing communities, members take their own initiative to facilitate knowledge contribution and dissemination, which is clearly di<sup>f</sup>erent from the knowledge contribution behavior in the social Q&A community where a member’s knowledge contribution behavior is triggered by other members’ questions. Knowledge sharing OC is also dissimilar to the online collaborative community such as Wikipedia in that the sharing content in the collaborative community is the results of member collaboration while the knowledge contribution in knowledge sharing OCs can always be clearly attributed to a solitary member. Fi nally, the sharing behavior in knowledge sharing OC is guided by the social exchange protocol; while in transactional virtual communities, knowledge contribution is guided mainly under the principle of eco nomic exchange [60].

The norm of reciprocity is salient in the knowledge sharing OCs as the sharing content in the knowledge sharing OCs is treated as a public good that is free to all OC members. Prior research reports that when the OC members regard knowledge as a public good that belongs to the OC, they feel obligated to reciprocate [1]. High quality posts (informative, entertaining and relevant) results in high levels of perceived functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and OC members are likely to post replies because they have a sense of obligation to repay the contributor and the online community. Central to the social exchange theory is the idea of norm of reciprocity. Commitment is also recognized as an important form of the reciprocation [32]. As such, we in this study use social exchange theory to justify users' obligation towards the contributor. At the same time, we use organization commitment theory to rationalize users' obligation towards the OC. On the other hand, people participate in OCs are not just for functional and hedonic needs, they also attempt to develop social relationships with other people inside the OCs [13]. In general, individuals are more inclined to endorse those who are part of a group that they like and care about [25]. As such, in addition to the perceived functional and hedonic value which may stimulate the post replying behavior, social network factors of the contributor should also a<sup>f</sup>ect the post replying behavior. Social capital theory are thus used. Together, we in this study use social exchange theory, organization commitment theory, and social capital theory to provide complementary perspectives on people's motivation to post replies in knowledge sharing OCs.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, drawing upon work by Hovland et al. [27], we propose <sup>fi</sup>ve sharing post-related characteristics from three distinct levels (i.e., contributor features, content features, and receiver social interaction features) as being the antecedents of post replying behavior. We validate the proposed linear panel data models by using the data assembled from a major Chinese online travel community (OTC). Tourism represents one of the most information-intensive industries. In today's social media-obsessed world, online consumer-generated content has become a major information source for travel consumers and plays an instrumental role in their destination selection [40,77]. Online travel communities, a typical type of the knowledge sharing OCs, wherein customers can easily communicate their opinions, share experience, and rate destinations, become increasingly prevalent as a credible information source since it provides prospective tourists with reliable destination recommendations and suggestions [14].

The current research di<sup>f</sup>ers from previous studies in three main ways. First, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to focus on post replying behavior in knowledge sharing OCs and empirically establish the link between the characteristics of a sharing post and its replying post number. Although providing asynchronous feedback on previous postings is crucial for an OC to be truly vibrant, we are not aware of any prior research explicitly focusing on feedback behaviors and discussing sharing post factors that are likely to have an e<sup>f</sup>ect on the feedback behaviors. Second, this study is also the <sup>fi</sup>rst to apply social exchange theory, commitment theory and social capital theory to the context of post replying behavior in OCs. The empirical results attest to the felicitous application of these theories. Third, our study is one of the rare studies to gather real world post replying data from an OC and quantify the di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects of sharing posts’ characteristics on the number of replying posts by using econometric analysis, which allows us to gain a more reliable quantitative insight, compared to previous survey research or laboratory studies. Taken together, this study not only extends the OC participation literature by exploring how the characteristics of sharing posts and contributors might shape post replying behavior, but it also contributes to OC operators’ ability to develop better strategies in building and maintaining a vibrant and successful OC.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the literature on perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of OC participation, and make an introduction to social exchange theory, commitment theory, and social capital theory. Section 3 presents the research hypotheses. Section 4 <sup>fi</sup>rst describes the empirical method, and then presents the results of data analysis. We conclude the paper with a discussion of the results, implications and limitations.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Perceived benefits of OC participation

Community members' proactive participation is the key anteceden of determining community growth and ensuring the community’s longterm success [24,63,74]. Prior OC literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed diverse bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and needs of OCs toward members that motivate them to participate in various types of OCs in general (e.g., [24,32,43,75]). In particular, Vogt and Fesenmaier [64] expand the view of information search, beyond a functional need, and report that functional, hedonic and social needs are the three main bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in<sup>fl</sup>uencing participation and attitude towards an OC. Functional needs refer to motivated e<sup>f</sup>orts that are directed at a purpose. Hedonic needs involve the pursuit of enjoyment. Social needs are associated with the search for status expression and social interaction.

Following the work of Vogt and Fesenmaier [64], Wang et al. [66] propose that members participate in OCs in order to satisfy three fundamental needs: functional, social, and psychological needs. Wang and Fesenmaier [65] and Chung and Buhalis [14] further claim that an OC provides functional/information acquisition, hedonic, psychological, and social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to members. These bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or needs contribute to increasing the levels of participation and creating positive attitude toward the OC. Kang [34], in addition to these four bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, incorporates monetary bene<sup>fi</sup>t as a predictor of member participation. Tsai and Pai [63] also con<sup>fi</sup>rm that social, hedonic, and utilitarian community attributes signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly a<sup>f</sup>ect proactive participation through community identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and relationship satisfaction. Similarly, Kuo and Feng [36] reveal that hedonic, social and utilitarian (learning) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in <sup>fl</sup>uence online brand community participation. Although existing research also proposes some other bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as access to expert advice [37], enhanced reputation and professional status [31], relational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as a sense of belonging (e.g., [78]), sense of well-beings [15], social identity [16], norm of reciprocity [10], and community commitment [4], all of these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be generally subsumed under one of the functional, social-psychological, and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Taken together, the existing OC literature reveals a range of explanations for why individuals participate in OCs in general. These studies o<sup>f</sup>er a sound understanding of why people contribute their knowledge in OCs. However, little research has devoted attention to the feedback behavior in OCs. The severe scarcity of current literature in revealing possible antecedents leading to the replying behaviors in OCs hints a strong need that motivates this study.

## 2.2. Social exchange theory

Social exchange theory, <sup>fi</sup>rst developed by the sociologist George Homans [26], is a model for interpreting society as a series of interactions between people that are based on estimates of costs and bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts. It posits that all human relationships are shaped by the use of a subjective bene<sup>fi</sup>t-cost analysis model, consciously or subconsciously [6,26]. Social exchange process brings grati<sup>fi</sup>cation when people obtain fair returns for their expenditures. The theory indicates that individuals who receive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts perceive moral obligation pay back the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the other party to ensure ongoing supportive exchanges [32]. Nowadays, the OC becomes an important form of social interaction [32,69]. Participation in OCs is regarded as social exchange involving voluntary actions by individuals and organizations, through which intangible resources, such as information, knowledge and social-psychological support, are exchanged [24,31]. The social exchange theory suggests that participants in OCs expect mutual reciprocity that justi<sup>fi</sup>es their expense in terms of time and e<sup>f</sup>ort spent contributing their knowledge [61]. As such, social exchange theory, as one of the most in<sup>fl</sup>uential theories, is frequently used to examine the participation behavior in various community types.

Central to the social exchange theory is the idea of norm of reciprocity. Reciprocity is related to a sense of mutual indebtedness and obligation, and states that people tend to help those who have helped them by returning equivalent bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [32].

## 2.3. Commitment theory

Commitment is regarded as a strong focus for OC in the social ex change literature [24,31]. Commitment is conceptualized as a psycho logical state that characterizes an individual’s relationship with an or ganization [44]. Individuals with commitment to an organization are willing to make su<sup>fi</sup>cient e<sup>f</sup>orts to bene<sup>fi</sup>t the organization and to maintain relationships with the organization [76]. Many scholars have also found that commitment is instrumental in online environments and they have applied the commitment theory in di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of OCs such as online discussion community [4,5], social media-based OCs [11], online brand communities [36], and online education communities [76]. Commitment is regarded as a complex and multidimensional construct consisting of three components: a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment, nor mative commitment and continuance commitment [5,44]. A<sup>f</sup>ective commitment refers to an individual’s a<sup>f</sup>ective or emotional attachment to an organization [44]. Individuals with a high level of a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment tend to engender a sense of belonging and are willing to participate and contribute. Normative commitment re<sup>fl</sup>ects an individual’s sense of personal obligation to an organization, and individuals with a high level of normative commitment feel that they ought to help the organization develop [44]. Continuance commitment refers to the consequence of an individual’s perceptions of the costs of leaving an organization. Individuals with a strong continuance commitment will remain in the organization and keep loyalty.

## 2.4. Social capital theory

The central proposition of social capital theory is that relationship networks constitute a valuable resource for the conduct of social a<sup>f</sup>airs [46]. The theory suggests that social capital captured from embedded resources in social networks [42] facilitates cooperation and mutually supportive relationships in communities for mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [51]. Social capital is in general de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by individual or social unit” [46]. It describes the ability of an individual to access resources embedded in his social network [57]. Social capital actually is the social relationship that an individual owns, which can be a productive asset facilitating some forms of social action [31,73]. Individual social capital accumulates over time, and the accumulation of social capital can lead to several positive consequences such as increasing the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency of ac tion, encouraging cooperative behavior within the groups, and engaging in citizenship behavior.

Prior research proposes three dimensions of social capital: structural dimension, cognitive dimension, and relational dimension [46,51]. Structural social capital refers to the interpersonal con<sup>fi</sup>gurations of linkages between people within the network and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the overall pattern of connections between people [46,57]. Relational social capital is the social bonds between people. It refers to resources rooted in the interpersonal relationships created through a history of social interaction [46,57]. Cognitive social capital refers to resources that relates to shared representations and systems of meaning between parties [46]. Cognitive social capital represents the common understanding that fa cilitates social interaction among people within a social network [57]. Social interaction increases social capital [31,57], and an OC is a favourite place for content sharing and social interaction, as such, OC is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for promoting social capital accumulation. The three dimensions of social capital have also been applied to the OC context [13,57,67].

## 3. Research hypotheses

A sharing post’s attributes determine its e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. This study postulates that OC members’ evaluation of a sharing post’s attributes facilitates their post replying behavior. According to Hovland et al. [27], three building blocks (information/content, source/contributor, and receiver) in<sup>fl</sup>uence people’s information evaluation. As such, we postulate that variables representing the attributes of content, contributor, and receiver levels will drive post replying behavior.

## 3.1. The influence of content features

The existing research suggests that consumers mainly rely on the textual content of sharing content when evaluating products and services in the online context [45,77]. The e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of textual content is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the form of helpfulness and ease of comprehension [28]. In the context of knowledge sharing OCs, helpfulness and ease of comprehension are important attributes of sharing content as the pursuit of functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are important motivations driving OC members to read the a post. In light of the information diagnosticity theory [19], a piece of information is perceived as helpful if it helps the user reduce uncertainty in decision making. In the OC context, a helpful post can satisfy an OC member’s innate functional and/or hedonic needs and facilitate decision making. Information depth, which is usually represented by the information length in words, can increase information diagnosticity to help users obtain information without additional search cost [45]. On the other hand, information vividness not only can improve information diagnosticity but also can help users to better comprehend the information [30]. As such, in this study, we focus on two characteristics of sharing content: length and vividness.

Post length generally re<sup>fl</sup>ects the extensiveness/depth of information or knowledge o<sup>f</sup>ered in the sharing posts [20]. Posts with longer length indicate that there is greater involvement of the contributor and greater likelihood of presenting a detailed description [9]. As such, this post can be more likely to satisfy OC members’ functional needs. For instance, in the context of OTC, a longer travelogue typically includes more advice, travel tips, and destination information. As such, a longer piece of travelogue helps to convey relevant information to members that aids them understanding and evaluating the quality performance of destinations, and thus contributing to the alleviation of readers’ un certainty about destinations’ quality.

According to social exchange theory, individuals will experience the need to reciprocate the other peers’ support and assistance [31]. Norm of reciprocity is one of the central factors that is associated with user participation in OCs [13,67]. Applying the reciprocity notion to the context of OC post replying, this study postulates that one reason compelling OC members to post replies is that these members have a feeling of obligation to pay back the contributor. Members’ perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts received from the sharing posts are reciprocated in the form of post replying as the replying behavior can promote the recognition and reputation of the contributor. Accordingly, OC members perceive more functional needs are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled by the longer post, these members are more likely to feel obligated to repay the contributor by way of post replying. Supporting this argument, Jin et al. [31] observe that individuals, due to the norm of reciprocity, will respond to other members’ knowledge contribution behaviors in an online social Q&A com munity. We therefore propose the following:

Hypothesis 1a. There is a positive relationship between post length and the number of replying posts.

In this study, information vividness refers to the extent to which sensory information is provided by a sharing post. The vividness of a sharing post signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases the information richness, visual richness, and attracts attention. A vivid product exhibition exposes consumers to more information cues about a product and stimulates more sensory channels than a pallid product presentation [30]. Evoking vividness through pictures is a common practice in online context.

In the context of OCs, vivid information can not only increase the perceived diagnosticity of the presented information but also enhance perceived hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Visual depictions of products or services can compensate for a lack of haptic information online and increase the ability to imagine and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in product assessments [50,68]. As such, when presented with pictures in sharing posts, OC members should be more certain of the sharing content compared to if no pictures are presented. As a supporting argument, Weathers et al. [68] report that information with pictures plays a positive role in lowering the performance uncertainty for experience products in the online context. As such, vivid sharing content is likely to satisfy members functional needs. Further, vivid presentation can also increase the ap peal of information and improve member engagement, and thus vivid content generally is more emotionally attractive [47]. Jiang and Benbasat [30] observe that increased vividness in product presentations enhances consumers enjoyment. Therefore, members in an OC are more likely to acquire hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the vivid posts. Collectively, vivid sharing content results in higher perceived functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to members. According to social exchange theory, OC members feel indebted to repay the contributor of the sharing post. As such, OC members are likely to reciprocate in the form of post replying.

In addition, vividness creates feelings of social presence by con veying the perceived presence of communicating participants [21], which is likely to lead to a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment [29]. From the perspective of commitment theory, a<sup>f</sup>ective commitment contributes to community citizenship behavior and content provision in the form of posting replies or starting new knowledge sharing [5]. Community citizenship behaviors are the prosocial discretionary behaviors contributing positively to the e<sup>f</sup>ective functioning of communities without formal rewards, which include both social encouragement and social control [5]. Social encouragement occurs when a member promotes desirable behaviors by recognizing others who contribute valued and interesting messages (information and knowledge). Relating this notion to the OC context, post replying is an important social encouragement behavior when an OC member realizes that a sharing post she/he has read is informative and entertaining (high quality). By replying post, the member expects the contributor and other OC members can continue to engage in such desirable behavior (i.e., contribute high quality sharing posts).

Taken together, it seems that, OC members are likely to post replies to the vivid sharing posts. Thus, the following is hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1b. There is a positive relationship between the vividness of sharing content and the number of replying posts.

## 3.2. The influence of contributor features

Prior research [17] observes that the perceived helpfulness of online review relies not only on review content but also on accessible cues of the contributor such as contributor credibility [70]. When a contributor possesses speci<sup>fi</sup>c practical expertise and domain knowledge, then his sharing content is considered as more credible and relevant than other contributors [55]. As such, a contributor’s expertise is an important consideration when evaluating the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of sharing knowledge. According to social exchange theory, the perceived e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of sharing knowledge is likely to drive OC members post replies due to the norm of reciprocity.

Further, knowledge sharing OCs are online social networks in which people with similar interests or goals interact to share knowledge, and engage in social interactions. Nowadays, many OCs have introduced social networking features to disseminate the knowledge and meet the users’ growing appeal of social interaction. People thus treat OC as a place to seek social support, friendship and a sense of belonging [13]. Therefore, post replying behavior in OCs should be determined both by personal cognition factors and by social network factors. Preceding research reports that social network plays a critical role in driving an individual’s participation behavior in OCs [13,31]. The degree to which an individual connects with others re<sup>fl</sup>ects his attraction to the group and his interactions with group members [31]. Social capital theory suggests that social capital inheres in the relationships between persons and among persons, and it is created through social interactions [57]. Contributors possessing higher levels of social capital are likely to receive more replying posts due to the gratitude, respect, trust, and friendship that members have toward each other. Contributor degree centrality captures a contributor’s overall social interaction linkages with other OC members. As such, contributor degree centrality is also an important attribute of a sharing post a<sup>f</sup>ecting post replying behavior. Together, in this study, we focus on two contributor-related variables: expertise and degree centrality.

Contributor expertise refers to the degree to which a contributor is capable of making valid assertions [17]. A contributor’s expertise is based on the accumulation of skills, competences, or knowledge through experience [70]. Social cognitive theory [3] holds that individual actions could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by observing others within the context of social interactions, and people tend to engage in behavior that they expect will result in favorable results. Contributors with substantial expertise, compared to newcomers, possess more experience about how to write sharing content to get favorite results, such as extensive attention and positive feedback from other peers. At the same time, the experienced contributors are generally a<sup>fi</sup>liated to larger social networks and have more social interaction linkages with other OC members. They are motivated to attract more attention and recognition from community peers in order to maintain their reputation and promote community identity, and negative word of mouth from other members will greatly ruin their reputation [31]. Thus, these ex perienced members are likely to write posts that directly match the interests and expectations (e.g., information acquisition or hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) of community members. Additionally, prior research [54] demonstrates that experts’ reviews are more trustworthy and reliable than those of layman; individuals are likely to agree more with experts and adjust their attitudes in accordance with expert opinions [70]. In the OC context, the ranking level is a manifestation of the expertise of a contributor. In line with these notions, in comparison to a post shared by a newcomer, members are more likely to perceive that a post shared by a high-ranking peer matches their functional or hedonic needs. As such, they perceive more functional and hedonic gains from the posts by the high-ranking contributors. As we have stated above, these in dividuals are likely to reciprocate the received bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in the form of post replying, ensuring ongoing supportive exchanges. We therefore propose the following:

Hypothesis 2a. There is a positive relationship between the ranking level of a contributor and the number of replying posts.

In social network analysis, centrality is used to identify the most important/active people at the center of a network or those that are well connected [22]. Degree centrality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of links associated with a node, and can be evaluated by in-degree centrality and out-degree centrality within a directed network. In-degree centrality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of ties directed to a node, and outdegree centrality refers to the number of ties that the node directs to others [22]. Individuals who exhibit high in-degree centrality in a social network indicate their importance and popularity to the community [8], while individuals who exhibit high out-degree centrality are often said to be gregarious and extroverted actors. Degree centrality captures the structural dimension of social capital. Social capital is determined by accessibility, that is, the presence or absence of social interaction linkages between members [46]. The extent to which an individual connects with others reveals his attraction and popularity to the group and his interactions with group members [31].

As we have stated above, the post replying behavior can promote the recognition and reputation of a contributor. It is believed that individuals are more likely to support those who are part of a group that they like and care about [25]. In the OC context, when a contributor shares a new piece of content, it will be fed to his followers. As such, contributors possessing higher in-degree centrality tend to receive more replying posts. Further, from the perspective of social exchange theory, because these contributors with high in-degree centrality are active and important players in their social networks, members, in order to reciprocate their positive experience, are likely to invest time and e<sup>f</sup>ort helping the contributors by replying to their posts for mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. As such, for the contributor who has high in-degree centrality will have a higher likelihood to receive replying posts.

Moreover, social cognitive theory [3] holds that portions of an in dividual's knowledge acquisition are associated with social learning. In other words, an individual is likely to choose to replicate others’ behavior by observing their actions within the context of social interactions. OCs provide all the necessary steps for social learning to occur [31]. As such, individuals with more social learning opportunities are more likely to know how to write a piece of sharing content to gain attention of other members. The out-degree centrality of an OC member represents the opportunities for social learning as in OCs anything new about the followed members will be fed to them. An individual possessing high out-degree centrality has more opportunities to learn from other peers about how to write an eye-catching travelogue, and re produces what s/he has learned. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that the sharing content of contributors with high out-degree centrality is more likely to be regarded as e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness. According to the norm of reciprocity, members have a propensity for posting replies to reciprocate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts they receive from the high quality sharing posts. Thus, the out-degree centrality of a contributor is likely to be positively related with the replying post number. Collectively, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 2b. There is a positive relationship between the degree centrality of a contributor and the number of replying posts.

## 3.3. The influence of receiver features

Prior research suggests that an individual’ demographic and social interaction characteristics may a<sup>f</sup>ect his evaluation of service or product [23]. Although in Hovland et al. [27]'s model, information receivers’ di<sup>f</sup>erence in demographic characteristics such as gender, race and ethnicity, education, and marital status could in<sup>fl</sup>uence their information evaluation, we cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd any theoretical basis to support that these demographic characteristics may a<sup>f</sup>ect post replying behavior. Therefore, in this study, we focus on the receivers’ social interaction characteristic. OCs are online social networks in which people with similar interests or goals come together to share knowledge and engage in social interactions. As such, besides the functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts such as relationship maintenance and interactivity among OC members on replying post behavior should be considered. Further, the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence model [16] proposes that a member s participation decision relies on other members’ participation behaviors. In other words, members may decide to participate only if they observe high levels of interaction among other members [34]. In the case of OCs, the extent of social interaction is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the breadth and depth of the interactivity in replying posts. In general, the average length of replying posts encapsulates the breadth and depth of the social interactivity [45]. As such, we in this study select the average length of replying posts as a receiver level variable that may a<sup>f</sup>ect OC members’ subsequent replying behavior.

Interactivity increases an individual’s sense of involvement and social presence because it o<sup>f</sup>ers the opportunity to communicate among individuals [21]. A sense of high involvement and social presence helps to form positive cognitive and emotional experience [21,79], which in turn leads to higher levels of perceived psychological and social bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. In addition, the social interaction content in replying posts usually endorse, complement and extend the knowledge of sharing posts which increases OC members’ perceived functional and/ or hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. According to social exchange theory, enhanced perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are likely to lead to the post replying behavior because of the norm of reciprocity. In knowledge-sharing OCs, members’ participation is largely determined by the amount of attention that they will receive from other participants [31]. Replying the sharing post can attract more OC members’ attention, and thus satisfy the attentiongetting need of the contributor and social discussion participants. Further, as a community citizenship behavior, OC members may reply posts to encourage the OC participants to engage in deep social discussion, as the survival and success of an OC depend upon social interaction and exchange between users. Taken together, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 3. There is a positive relationship between the average following post length and the number of replying posts.

On OTCs, a contributor’s gender is usually presented together with his travelogue, making it possible to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the post reply behavior. The heterogeneity of destinations is mainly re<sup>fl</sup>ected in locations and types. Due to di<sup>f</sup>erences in cultures and the environments, compared to non-exotic destinations, prospective travelers perceive greater uncertainty (for example, economic risk and safety risk) as well as curiosity before they visit exotic destinations. As such, there should be more replies to the travelogues about exotic tourism destinations. The types of destinations also have an impact on the number of replying posts. ## 4.4. Endogeneity and identification

Although the above model estimation results have shown the ex pected impacts of content level, contributor level and social interaction level variables on replying post number, the econometric model used might su<sup>f</sup>er from endogeneity issues arising from omitted variable and simultaneity. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the replying post number can be a<sup>f</sup>ected by the ranking of a contributor (Ranking), but also can a<sup>f</sup>ect it. Further, in the knowledge sharing OCs, although OC members can arbitrary increase their followees, the followee number in<sup>fl</sup>uences replying post number because of the social learning. Thus, a contributor, through social learning from his followees, studies how to write an eye-catching sharing post and gets many replying posts, which in turn drives the contributor’s temptation to reproduce what s/he has learned, e.g., how many word/picture numbers should be included in a travelogue. As such, the dependent variable (replying post number) and content level independent variables (i.e., Pictures and PostLength) might be mutually in<sup>fl</sup>uenced. In addition, a possible unobservable omitted variable of popularity (view number) of other travelogues in the same destination may potentially lead to the presence of ViewNumber endogeneity. Taken together, we treat our four major independent variables (i.e., PostLength, Pictures, Ranking, and ViewNumber) as potentially endogenous variables. On the contrary, we consider the followee number (Followees) as an exogenous variable because it is mainly determined by the innate qualities and personality traits of a contributor such as extraversion, sociability, and openness [18]. We also treat the Followers as an exogenous variable that mainly determined by the quality of a contributor's posts.

To deal with the endogeneity of Pictures, we use the picture number of the last travelogue written by the contributor as an instrument. Similarly, we use the length of the last travelogue written by the contributor as an instrument for PostLength. The picture number and length of the last travelogue should be correlated with the picture number and length of the current travelogue as picture number and post length re<sup>fl</sup>ect a contributor’s writing preferences and habits which cannot be changed substantively in the short period. However, these instruments are impossible to be related with the replying post number of the current travelogue. As such, these variables could serve as reasonable in struments.

Moreover, to address the potential simultaneity between replying post number and a contributor’s ranking we use the total number of the contributor’s shared travelogues prior to the current travelogue as an instrument. A contributor's total number of shared travelogues prior to the current travelogue would have an association with the ranking score, because the previous number of travelogues published by a contributor is one of important determinants of the contributor’s current ranking score. However, the previous total number of shared travelogues is unlikely to directly a<sup>f</sup>ect the replying post numbers of the current travelogue. Hence, we believe the previous total number of shared travelogues could serve as a valid instrument for the ranking score.

## 5. Discussion and implications

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

Post replying behavior creates complex social feedback e<sup>f</sup>ects, which in<sup>fl</sup>uences contributors’ knowledge sharing behavior in future [4,12]. However, not all sharing posts in an OC are treated equal: some posts are followed by tens of hundreds of replies, while some other posts fail to receive any reply. The existing OC participation research focuses primarily on identifying factors associated with OC participa tion in general. Few studies thus far have examined the factors relating to post replying behavior. As such, the present study seeks to advance our understanding of post replying behavior in knowledge sharing OCs by proposing and testing a model that explores the impact of sharing posts’ characteristics on replying post number. Through the lens of social exchange theory, commitment theory, and social capital theory, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed <sup>fi</sup>ve sharing post-related variables from the three distinct levels that may be associated with the heterogeneity in replying post number—issues not yet investigated in the extant literature. We vali dated the proposed models by using a panel dataset assembled from a popular OTC. The empirical results of this study lead to some important <sup>fi</sup>ndings that had not been revealed by previous studies.

The results suggest that the contributor level, content level, and receiver social interaction level characteristics of a sharing post are associated with the number of replying posts. At the level of content, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that picture numbers and sharing post length have a positive impact on the number of replying posts. The results indicate that the helpfulness and ease of comprehension of sharing posts can help increase other OC members’ willingness to post replies by satisfying these members’ innate functional and hedonic needs. As for the characteristics of contributors, the results reveal that the in-degree centrality of a contributor (follower numbers) has a relatively large e<sup>f</sup>ect on the number of replying posts, which suggests that the social capital a contributor accumulated in a community can help them to gain an advantage in obtaining more replying posts from the community members. As expected, ranking or expertise can also help a contributor reap more replies because s/he is more likely to know how to present information to match members’ bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and needs. Finally, this study also shows that receiver social interactions can drive the post replying behavior. Social interactions in OCs can not only complement and extend the knowledge of sharing posts, satisfying OC members’ functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, but also increase their commitment and the sense of belonging, which enhances the likelihood of post replying behavior.

The results also indicate that these characteristics have di<sup>f</sup>erential e<sup>f</sup>ects on stimulating post replying behavior. Follower numbers and picture numbers have the largest impacts, followed by the post length and social interaction extent. The expertise of contributors shows a small but signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect. The results stress the roles of vividness of a sharing post and social capital of a contributor on OC members’ replying behavior. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings thus underscore the dual value of knowledge sharing OCs: Ful<sup>fi</sup>lling OC members’ functional/hedonic needs as well as social needs.

Contrary to our expectations, the number of followees is negatively related to the number of replying posts. A potential explanation is that the social learning behavior probably occurs mainly in low-ranking contributors. Social learning occurs by observing, remembering, and imitating behavior seen in others. As we have stated above, followee numbers re<sup>fl</sup>ect social learning opportunities. Table 3 shows that a higher ranking is associated with a lower followee number, which indicates that, compared to high-ranking contributors, low-ranking contributor have a stronger motive to learn from other members to write high quality sharing posts and ultimately increase the replying post number of their sharing posts. Social cognitive theory suggests that observational learning mainly occurs in OC members in their early stages. High-ranking contributors generally possess su<sup>fi</sup>cient capacity, experience and knowledge to write eye-catching sharing posts, which reduces their motive to link to other OC members. According to social exchange theory, the sharing posts by high-ranking contributors are likely to receive more replying posts as OC members receive functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the high quality sharing posts and they are inclined to reciprocate in the form of posting replies that can promote the recognition and reputation of the contributors (i.e., the norms of reciprocity). Taken together, the number of followees is negatively associated with the number of replying posts.

## 5.2. Theoretical contributions

The current research makes important theoretical contributions to the existing OC participation literature. First, to our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to focus exclusively on investigating the feedback behavior in knowledge sharing OC contexts. Although the ability for users to provide feedback is a crucial feature of today’s OCs, and the feedback behavior is the central social dynamics in OCs, prior studies (e.g., [7,12,35]) have just investigated the consequences of feedback behavior on members’ contribution behavior, no empirical research has been conducted on identifying factors driving the feedback behavior. We advance this knowledge base by empirically identify variables that explain how the characteristics of a sharing post a<sup>f</sup>ect post replying behavior. This study provides useful knowledge for our initial understanding of factors leading to post replying behavior. Although the variables we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study may be not exhaustive, they shed light on the feedback behavior and serve as a precursor for recognizing other factors that are crucial to understand the social dynamics in OCs. By adding to the understanding of the feedback behavior in OCs, this study contributes to an OC operator’s ability to develop better strategies in building and maintaining a vibrant and successful OC.

Second, we enhances the existing OC participation literature by drawing theoretical support from social exchange theory, commitment theory and social capital theory to explain the heterogeneity in replying post number in knowledge sharing OCs. Although these theories have been widely applied to understand the knowledge contribution behavior in OCs, it is entirely new to use these theories to investigate how the characteristics of a sharing post a<sup>f</sup>ect post replying behavior. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we emphasize the importance of social exchange and commitment in explaining the reason why content level factors might a<sup>f</sup>ect post replying behavior. From a social exchange perspective, post replying is regarded as a reciprocal behavior arising from members’ perceived functional and hedonic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts receiving from the sharing posts. From the perspective of commitment, post replying is considered as a social encourage behavior from OC members who expect the contributor and other OC members can continue to engage in such desirable behavior as contributing high quality sharing posts and engaging in deep conversations online. On the other hand, we highlight social learning and social capital in explaining the reason why contributors’ degree centrality will a<sup>f</sup>ect post replying behavior. We thus contribute by extending the applicability of these theories to the post replying context. The empirical results show that these theories provide suitable theoretical lens for us to understand the underlying mechanism about how the characteristics of a sharing post may a<sup>f</sup>ect replying post behavior.

Third, our study is one of the rare studies to gather real world post replying data from an OC and quantify the relative impact of characteristics of sharing posts on driving post replying behavior, thereby providing a more complete account of the replying behavior. The panel data analysis used in this study allows us to gain a more reliable quantitative insight, compared to cross sectional surveys or laboratory studies. The econometric analysis also enables us to gain a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between the characteristics of sharing posts and post replying behavior.

In addition, although previous research argues that the social capital facilitates an OC member's participation by cultivating a sense of belonging (e.g., [78]), we extended this line of research and revealed that social capital can help contributors gain an advantage in obtaining replies from the OC members, which could further increase the sense of belonging and the possibility of contribution in the further [12]. Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>nding of this study provides one viable explanation about how the sense of belonging forms in an OC.

## 5.3. Managerial implications

Our research may eventually help OC practitioners recognize what factors relating to sharing posts drive OC members to post replies. This study also helps to address the practical needs of OC managers and community developers who care about why members provide a feedback response in their community, not why they participate in OCs in general. Our results empirically suggest that the level of vividness of a sharing post are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to the number of replying posts.

Evoking vividness through pictures, a common communication practice, can not only compensate for a lack of haptic information online but also reduce the decision uncertainty. For example, in OTCs, photos and pictures contribute to instilling a sense of social presence and facilitating the understanding about the destinations. OC operators can control the presence and quality of product pictures, thus a<sup>f</sup>ecting content vividness. In order to encourage the contributors to upload more high quality pictures, OC operators should provide the optimal image-uploading technology to help contributors make the uploads run smooth and fast in addition to a set of community policies and rules for governing members’ postings. For example, OCs can o<sup>f</sup>er some express upload tools that make pictures upload easy and e<sup>fi</sup>ciently. At the same time, OCs should also employ the latest technologies to speed up webpage load times whose contents include many pictures. OC opera tors can enact some rules and policies for encouraging picture sharing behavior. For instance, contributors uploading more pictures can gain more score helping to upgrade their ranking in the community. Picture copyright protection rules and policies against unfriendly or malicious behavior should also be well established and enforced.

This study also highlights the importance of the social interaction between members. High level of interactions facilitates other members’ participation decision. Social interaction can foster warm and close personal relationships, and enhance the sense of engagement [35]. Thus, OC operators have to think carefully about how to encourage social interactions between contributors and repliers, and among repliers. Community operators can formulate some rules and regulations to increase contributors’ intention to interact back and forth with the repliers. Moreover, operators should also re<sup>fi</sup>ne the website design to increase the convenience for the members to reply the sharing posts. To our knowledge, very few OCs at present possess the features especially designed for promoting mutual interactions among repliers.

Finally, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that sharing reviews posted by the contributors who possess high degree centrality especially the high outdegree centrality can obtain more replies, compared to the low outdegree centrality contributors. Therefore, OC operators should encourage high out-degree centrality members to engage in content sharing. Some measures can be taken besides providing extra incentives to encourage these members to share knowledge continually. For example, OTC operators can work together with the destination operators to invite these members to head for destinations for experience, and thus inspire the members to write more sharing travelogues.

## 6. Limitations and future research

Several limitations of the study warrant careful consideration. First, we only used the average length of replying posts to represent the extent of social interactions. The average following post length may not able to capture the dynamics of social interactions fully. Future research could overcome this issue by collecting a more comprehensive set of relevant data from the social interaction level. Second, our research focuses on observed post replying behavior and thus limit our’ ability to investigate the psychological processes underlying the behavior, which may weaken the theoretical contributions of this study. As such, further questionnaire research or laboratory studies are required to provide additional insight into the underpinning mechanisms of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Third, the econometric model used in this study is exactly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and thus it is impossible to carry out the test for the exogeneity requirement of the instrumental variables. Future research could try to identify more excluded instrumental variables to check the instrument exogeneity condition by testing overidenti<sup>fi</sup>cation restrictions. Moreover, the data used in this research are collected from a Chinese OTC, and majority of its visitors are Chinese. Considering that the cognitive di<sup>f</sup>erences can be found among di<sup>f</sup>erent national cultures, it is unclear that whether the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this research can be generalized to other OTCs located in the distinct cultural countries such as TripAdvisor and VirtualTourist or even the other knowledge sharing

OCs. To overcome this limitation, future research can conduct similar studies in the di<sup>f</sup>erent OC contexts. Finally, future research should delve more deeply into potential cultural in<sup>fl</sup>uence on post replying behavior. Thus, another meaningful extension to this research is to conduct cross-cultural comparison studies.
