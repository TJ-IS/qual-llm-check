# Irony, critique and ethnomethodology in the study of computer work: irreconcilable tensions?

Teresa Marcon\* & Abhijit Gopal<sup>†</sup>

\*School of Business and Economics, Wilfred Laurier University, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada N2L 3C5, email: tmarcon@wlu.ca, and <sup>†</sup>Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada N6A 3K7, email: agopal@ivey.uwo.ca

Abstract. To broaden discussion of critique in the field of information systems beyond current approaches, we look outside the core management discourse and examine the critical element in ethnomethodological research on computer-based work environments. Our examination reveals a form of critique that is above all without irony, seeking always to be respectful of the competence of research subjects, and informed by an in-depth understanding of participants’ practices. We argue that ethnomethodology is an often unrecognised critical approach that attempts to speak from within a community of practice and deliver critical insights that are responsive to the kinds of practical problems of interest to practitioners.

Keywords: ethnomethodology, critique, irony, computer work

## INTRODUCTION

Is ethnomethodology critical? Some ethnomethodologists have argued that ethnomethodology is ill-equipped to serve the emancipative and interventionist aims of critique. Ethnomethodol ogy cannot change the world; it leaves everything as it is (Sharrock & Anderson, 1991). Bogen & Lynch (1990) have similarly suggested that strategies other than ethnomethodology, such as informing people of their legal rights, would prove far more effective in addressing the inequalities of the world. Yet, for some, ethnomethodology and critique are close allies. As Anne Rawls (2002) notes: ‘Ethnomethodology cannot be indifferent to political, ethical or theoretical critique because that is essentially what it is. Ethnomethodology seeks to reveal the way in which taken for granted social practices maintain the appearance of things’ (p. 54).

We follow the thread of the debate in the academic community and explore the critical potential of ethnomethodology with a view to broaden the possibilities for critique in the field of information systems (IS). Ethnomethodology takes an interest in how human beings make sense of the world in interaction with others and in relation to the obiects they encounter

Although this theoretical lens has rarely been used in IS (Ross & Chiasson, 2005, is an exception), the revolutionary impact and continuing popularity of ethnomethodology in Human Computer Interaction and Computer Supported Collaborative Work (Heath et al., 2000) suggests that this approach holds significant promise for the study of information technologies and IS. Moreover, the ethnomethodological attention to the production of information itself – evident, for example, in Garfinkel’s (1952) detailed accounting of the relation between suicide statistics and the practices through which they are produced – further suggests that this theoretical perspective may be particularly well suited to this area of research. Given space limitations and our focus on understanding the critical potential of ethnomethodology, we are not able to do justice in this paper to the richness of ethnomethodological research or to its relevance to the field of IS. We proceed on the premise that ethnomethodology is indeed relevant to the IS enterprise in spite of its invisibility in this domain to date. Beyond the promise of applicability, our reading of ethnomethodological research points to a critique that, grounded in the activities of members of a community of practice, holds the potential to be responsive to the kinds of practical problems that are of interest to practitioners. This practical orientation in our view renders ethnomethodology a promising critical research approach for the IS research community.

We begin with a presentation of key concepts in Garfinkel’s (1967; 2002) ethnomethodology. We then rehearse the main arguments in a debate that seems to point to irreconcilable tensions between ethnomethodology and critique. In the last part of this paper, we attempt to reframe the question of the critical potential of ethnomethodology along different lines, taking a broader view of critique that allows a reading of ethnomethodology as critical. Turning our attention to ethnomethodology’s commitment to a situated analysis of human practices, we reflect on the praxical potential of this approach to critique.

## WHAT IS ETHNOMETHODOLOGY?

Ethnomethodology originated with the work of Garfinkel (1952; 1967) in the 1950s and 1960s. A student of Talcott Parsons, Garfinkel developed a fundamentally different approach to address the classical Durkheimian question of ‘what holds society together’ (Collins, 1994, p. 186). Rather than following in Parsons’ footsteps and looking for an explanation for the orderliness of the social world in a system of internalized norms, Garfinkel (1967) turned to the world of practice. Through empirical observation, he sought to discern the logic or rationality of sense-making in everyday interactions. A principal outcome of these inquiries has been to highlight the methodical nature of sense-making practices. This is reflected in the terms ethno and methodology themselves, as per Rawls (2002) explanation: ‘ “Ethno” refers to members of a social or cultural group (or in Garfinkel’s terms, members of a local social scene) and “method” refers to the things members routinely do to create and recreate the various recognizable social actions or social practices. “Ology” as in the word “sociology” implies the study of, or the logic of, these methods. Thus Ethnomethodology means the study of members methods for producing recognizable social orders’ (p. 6).

Garfinkel’s original work has been appropriated widely both within and outside classical sociology, resulting in a diverse body of research that draws on multiple philosophical and methodological orientations. It is in fact misleading to regard ethnomethodology as an entirely homogenous field. Ethnomethodology can more accurately be characterized as a movement that has grown and evolved over time (Flynn, 1991), spawning multiple research traditions that evince both commonalities and significant tensions (Figure 1). Our discussion takes place within the confines of one particular strand of ethnomethodology, also known as mundane reasoning analysis, represented by the work of Garfinkel. The precepts of other strands of ethnomethodology and their compatibility with critique, broadly defined, may be equally deserving of consideration but are not addressed in this paper. We offer a brief introduction to the principal ideas in ethnomethodology, following Garfinkel. More extensive discussions of ethnomethodology and its diversity can be found in a number of sources including Button (1991), Heritage (1984), Maynard & Clayman (1991) and Sharrock & Anderson (1986).

## The intelligibility of human activities

Ethnomethodology takes an interest in the intelligibility of human activities, from the most commonplace to the unusual. It inquires into just how people manage to make sense of the world, to know what is happening around them and what it is that they are doing (Heritage, 1984). Making sense is the most common and unremarkable activity. It passes unnoticed, part of the background of our daily lives, as an embodied competence that is nevertheless methodical. Attempting to step outside the taken for granted acceptance of everyday facts known in common, ethnomethodology reveals in fine-grained analyses the remarkable and methodical organization of human activities which lends meaning to our words and actions and in the process constitutes the social order as we know it.

For ethnomethodology, the meaning of objects or actions does not come to us in the moment of apperception based on a system of typifications, given a priori (Husserl, 1982) or internalized through learning and socialization (Berger & Luckmann, 1967; Schutz, 1962). Rather, meaning is routinely achieved or accomplished through the embodied practices of knowledgeable actors, through language and actions as employed within the flow of everyday life. Thus, while ethnomethodology displays a phenomenological sensibility (Maynard & Clayman, 1991), taking an interest in how it is possible for us to constitute the meaning of objects and actions in the existential flow of our lives, it is not oriented towards consciousness as a mental, cognitive or private process. In concert with the Wittgensteinian view, ethnomethodology regards sense-making as a consensual and normatively grounded practice that is intersubjectively available and public, taking a non-essentialist, non-cognitivist, non-mentalist and non-ironic approach to knowledge, consciousness and the self (Watson, 1998). It is worth noting that the work of Cicourel (1973) subsequently diverges from the original formulation o ethnomethodology, reintroducing an interest in cognition and in the process giving rise to a popular strand of ethnomethodology which Sharrock & Hughes (2001) label ‘distributed cognition’ (Figure 1).

It is in the course of acting and making sense of the events of our lives, as we interact with others, that we participate in the constitution of society and the social structures that sociology traditionally designates as micro and macro. Contrary to the way ethnomethodology has often been depicted, this is not a micro sociology, useful only in illuminating the dynamics of human interaction (Hilbert, 1990). Ethnomethodology gives equal consideration to agency and structure (cf. Giddens, 1984; Bourdieu, 1977). For ethnomethodology, social facts and socia structures are not mere constructions existing in the minds of acting agents. Rather, following Durkheim’s injunction to treat social facts as objects, social facts are material or endowed with a certain ‘facticity’ (Garfinkel, 2002, p. 122); they are, for all practical purposes, ‘really’ there (Pollner, 1974). Indeed, ethnomethodology is drawn reluctantly into the agency and structure debate and its related forms, i.e. the distinctions between subjectivity and objectivity, value and fact, micro and macro, etc. (Sharrock & Anderson, 1991). For ethnomethodologists, the distinction between agency and structure does not reflect a division in the empirical world. Rather, the polarity of agency and structure and the need to privilege one over the other arise from artificial divisions that have been drawn and perpetuated as analytical matters within social science. For an excellent discussion, see Sharrock & Watson (1988) and also Latour (1996).

For ethnomethodology, social facts are brought into being through an emergent process that gives rise to a society known in common. Yet the constitution of the social world is not something that takes place invisibly or by accident. Ethnomethodology proposes that this process is both methodical and observable. Furthermore, this methodicity relies on a situated and consensual understanding of language and action that rests on an embodied morality or a normative foundation of taken for granted social facts. In the next two sections, we explore the normative bedrock of intelligibility and the methodocity of sense-making practices.

## The accountability of human practices

The profound insight of ethnomethodology, one which, in our view, has a critical import, has been to illuminate the consensual basis of human reasoning practices. At the heart of ethnomethodology is a concern with describing the background of normative or moral expect ancies (Garfinkel & Sacks, 1970) implicated in all forms of practical reasoning in local settings, including those inhabited by social scientists. This moral ground is not composed of agreed upon universal values (Jayyusi, 1991). Rather, it consists of an embodied morality, a set of mutual expectations that are taken for granted by the members of a social group and collectively sanctioned when breached. Such normative expectations are often unstated, being simply what everybody knows within a form of life (Wittgenstein, 1968).

Careful examination of human interaction allows us to see that practical reasoning is predicated on just such a background of normative expectations. Key to this insight is attention to language as used in naturally occurring talk. Garfinkel’s (1967) early work focused on making evident the indexical and reflexive properties of language as it is used in everyday living. In line with Wittgenstein’s (1968) reformulation of linguistic philosophy, ethnomethodology stresses the indexicality of language. To say that language is indexical is to say that the meaning of a linguistic expression is not unequivocal but rather depends in part on the context in which it is uttered (Audi, 1999). Pronouns, to make use of an obvious example, do not have unequivocal meanings as they may refer to different people or objects depending on the context. The ambiguity of language extends beyond pronouns. As Wittgenstein (1968) and Garfinkel (1967) demonstrated, language used in the normal course of affairs displays a ‘natural incompleteness’ (Coulon, 1995, p. 17) and inevitable ambiguity. Linguistic expressions always point to (i.e. provide an index to) something else which must be understood in turn, whether implicitly or explicitly, in order to arrive at meaning. If subjected to careful scrutiny, all explanations, no matter how detailed, ‘leave something out, need fudging, or are replete with inconsistencies’ (Maynard & Clayman, 1991, p. 397) and thus potentially require further elaboration. Attempts to remedy the ambiguity of language through clarification or the use of more specific terms in turn lead to other ambiguities because these efforts also rely on language.

Faced with such a problem, how is it possible that people nevertheless routinely make use of language to arrive at meaning and make sense of the world? Garfinkel (1967) argued that, given the irremediable indexicality of language, making sense must require something more. This something more is an embodied competence that brings into play a set of shared and often unstated normative expectations. Relying on such expectations, members of a social group attempt to make sense of talk in conversation by reserving judgement prospectively (e.g. by asking for clarification) and applying sense retrospectively (e.g. by reconsidering the meaning of what was said previously) (Garfinkel, 1967) in light of what everybody knows, continually solving new puzzles through meaning-in-use (Wittgenstein, 1968).

Equally central to ethnomethodology is the notion of reflexivity. This term is often associated with philosophical self-reflection or a kind of introspection (but see Lynch, 2000, for a more comprehensive treatment of different kinds of reflexivity). In ethnomethodological parlance, however, reflexivity has a specialized meaning: it refers to those human practices that serve simultaneously to describe social structures (e.g. rules, institutions) and to constitute them, endowing them with meaning (Coulon, 1995). Garfinkel took an interest in Mannheim’s documentary method of interpretation which involves the search for ‘an identical homologous pattern underlying a vast variety of totally different realizations of meaning’ (Mannheim cited in Garfinkel, 1967, p. 78). Garfinkel noted that social actors routinely invoke a consensually accepted fact or pattern, such as for example a rule, to explain their activities, in the process reaffirming or reconstituting the existence of this same fact or pattern (a process which Giddens, 1984 and Bourdieu, 1977 term reproduction). The particular and the pattern stand in a mutually constitutive or reflexive relation whereby the particulars of a pattern are invoked as evidence of the pattern’s existence and, in a reciprocal but always incompletely specified (i.e. indexical) relation, the pattern grants meaning to the particular. This does not simply mean that we reproduce consensually accepted patterns with a fixed meaning by affirming or invoking them. Because meaning is context dependent, the same fact, pattern or rule may be used at different times as justification for different or even contradictory actions and statements (e.g. Wieder, 1974). The meaning of a known fact, rule or pattern is subject to constant negotiation; it is embedded in the particulars of the situation and subject to a consensual process of meaning making grounded in a common background of normative expectations. Ethnomethodologically speaking, rules and other social facts are resources (Garfinkel, 1967) that people use reflexively and contingently in interaction.

Achieving intelligibility in interaction with others thus is predicated on practical reasoning and the indexicality and reflexivity of language. Human activities become intelligible through a constant ‘push[ing] and prodd[ing]’ (Dourish & Button, 1998, p. 415). We can interrogate other people, and in turn be interrogated by them, to make sense of a situation and we routinely expect others to participate in this process. When the possibility of engaging in such activities is absent or limited, intelligibility is potentially at risk. This is quite evident, for example, in the interactions between users and computer systems. Computer interfaces are inscribed in systems; they are ‘static, atomic, and unexaminable’ (p. 416). The possibility of asking specific questions to resolve problems is limited and the number of context specific messages that can be inscribed in systems a priori is relatively small. People, of course, are aware that machines cannot be expected to participate in a consensual process of sense-making in the same way as human beings. Nevertheless, as Dourish and Button (1998) show in using ethnomethodology to inform interface design issues, practical reasoning pervades people’s interactions with machines and is the basis for their attempts to make sense of computer messages. Our occasional frustration with computer systems is a reflection of the implicit expectations tha underlie routine interactions.

In the absence of a breach in our expectations (Garfinkel & Sacks, 1970), the normative grounds of intelligibility may pass unnoticed. The normative grounds of intelligibility, however, are not invisible. They can be uncovered in the reasoning practices of members of a social group. These reasoning practices are available for inspection in the accounts that people produce (Garfinkel, 1967). Accounts are descriptions that members provide for each other that tell what they and others are doing. In order to count as an account of the facts, such descriptions must be seen as reasonable and relevant to the situation at hand – in other words, as ‘visibly-rational-and-reportable-for-all-practical purposes, i.e. “accountable” ’ (Garfinkel, 1967, p. vii). In addition, the reasoning practices that people employ in their accounts display a visible methodicity. These methodical practices of practical reasoning, explored in the next section, are one of the central concerns of ethnomethodology.

## The study of people’s methods for making sense of the world

Ethnomethodology is the empirical study of the methods that people use to make sense of the world, in the process giving rise to society in its particularity and manifest orderliness. These methods are known as ethnomethods (Garfinkel, 1967). As competent users of ethnomethods, ordinary members of society routinely display a methodical knowledge of how to accomplish tasks, from the most commonplace – for example, taking one’s turn in a conversation (Sacks et al., 1974); conversational analysis is the branch of ethnomethodology that takes a particular interest in the manner in which mundane conversations are organized, see Figure 1) – to the most vital, like performing the work required in coordinating the movement of trains to avoid collisions (Luff & Heath, 2000) or determining the appropriate response to calls to a police emergency line (Benson, 1993). Ethnomethodology seeks to uncover, through empirica observation, the embodied competence that people routinely employ, a competence that is methodical and observable even if largely unnoticed by those who perform it. It is a compe tence that occurs largely at a pre-reflective level, being embodied or incarnate (Garfinkel, 1967).

The methodicity of human practices should not, therefore, be equated with a calculus of performance such as is suggested by information processing theories (Simon. 1957) or with a system of societal norms that are internalized and unproblematically applied, as Parsons proposed (Collins, 1994). Methodicity does not merely consist of the sequential application of norms or rules, perhaps in the form of very complex decision trees. As Wittgenstein (1968) and Garfinkel (1967) demonstrated, applying a rule is never simply a matter of achieving congruence with an unequivocal principle. Rather, rules as linguistic statements are irremediably indexical and used reflexively within the situation, taking their meaning from the particula contexts in which they are embedded even as they are reflexively reproduced as facts known in common.

Attending to human practices in the context of the indexicality and reflexivity of language entails an emphasis on investigating the methods rather than the rules that people use to accomplish routine or unusual activities. Benson (1993), for example, points to the methodicity of police discretion as a means of enforcing the law and maintaining order even in the presence of information technologies that aim to systematize police work according to a set of rules. As he notes, ‘[p]olice officers do not simply enact the law of the land as though they were pre-programmed automata wandering the streets of cities and towns arresting anybody and everybody who could be seen as having broken the law’ (Benson, 1993, p. 87). Enforcing the law in practice cannot be equated to the unequivocal application of a set of rules. Laws are resources that can be invoked in the everyday work of police officers according to the circumstances surrounding incidents. Rather than trying to establish whether the police officers are enforcing the law according to the rules, ethnomethodology would enjoin us to consider the methods that are used in applying the law. Law enforcement has a discernable methodicity or a kind of logic; police officers routinely (though not in every case) employ certain tactics in applying the law such as, for example, allowing forms of illegality that facilitate intelligence gathering or condoning speeding violations within a certain range. A focus on ethnomethods rather than rules helps us understand how work is actually carried out. It shows us that police discretion is not a nuisance to be remedied through stricter policies or codification of procedures in computerized systems; it is ‘an essential feature of practical policing’ (Benson, 1993, p. 89) that makes police work both possible and effective.

The routine use of ethnomethods by groups of individuals accounts for the orderliness and predictability of our lives. Yet, the order that emerges from our use of ethnomethods is not something we can count on without reservation. Though ethnomethods may operate in disparate local circumstances (for example, taking one’s turn in conversation at home or at work) and thus appear to be context free, they are always context sensitive (Sacks et al., 1974). Our use of ethnomethods is always contingent on the circumstances of particular interactions, on the actions and accounts of others and on the material objects and structures we come upon within the flow of existence. This is one reason why ethnomethodology insists on producing contextually grounded case studies, rather than generalizable theories. It is also the means by which ethnomethodology preserves the possibility of change, independent action and agency, as the outcomes of interaction are never totally determined a priori bu rather responsive to local conditions. In an Austinian sense, utterances and gestures are performative acts that, being subject to the doctrine of the Infelicities, can be ‘happy’ or ‘unhappy’ rather than merely ‘true’ or ‘false’ (Austin, 2001).

Without slipping into a naïve non-realism, ethnomethodology maintains an open-endedness towards the future which admits the possibility of thinking and acting otherwise (Giddens, 1984). This open-endedness, together with the recognition of the consensual nature of sensemaking and the role that people play in continually constituting and reconstituting the socia order, opens a space for reading ethnomethodological studies as critical.

## IS ETHNOMETHODOLOGY CRITICAL?

Given this potential, why is ethnomethodology generally regarded as falling under the umbrella of interpretivism (e.g. Burrell & Morgan, 1979) and why does controversy surround a reading of ethnomethodology as critical (e.g. Bogen & Lynch, 1990)?

An answer to the first question may lie in the diversity of ethnomethodology and particularly in the manner in which ethnomethodology has been enrolled in a prolific array of rather loose adaptations which Holstein & Gubrium (1994) label interpretative practices (Figure 1). Such elaborations take ethnomethodology into close proximity to other interpretative approaches that are generally concerned with subjectivity and the actor’s point of view. Denzin (1969), for example, has proposed a synthesis between ethnomethodology and symbolic interactionism. While ethnomethodologically inspired modifications and extensions collectively travelling under the ‘sign’ of ethnomethodology may well merit consideration in their own right, they often bear little resemblance to Garfinkel’s project and the efforts of those who have followed closely in his footsteps (e.g. Bittner, 1967; Lynch, 1999). Given the diversity of positions within the ethnomethodological domain (Flynn, 1991), a variety of readings is no doubt possible.

The debate in the academic community on the compatibility of ethnomethodology and critique, however, does not appear to be rooted in the diversity of ethnomethodology. This debate takes as its principal pivot the juxtaposition of ironic and non-ironic accounts. In the next section, we distinguish ironic and non-ironic research approaches and articulate what moti vates the ethnomethodological commitment to avoiding irony. Subsequently, we explore the manner in which the concept of irony is connected with critique and embroiled in the confron tation between supporters and opponents of ethnomethodology as a critical endeavour.

## Ethnomethodology and irony

Ethnomethodologists seek to maintain a close alignment with members and produce research reports that are not ironic, that is, that do not unfairly privilege the analyst’s version of reality. The analyst’s view of the world is privileged when researchers employ methodologies that do not adequately account for the practices and conceptions of research subjects in their everyday life or, in Schultz’s terms, within the ‘natural attitude’. Irony, or more properly methodological (or analytic) irony (Watson, 1998), involves a two-step process. In the first step, researchers, as experts, conduct empirical investigations armed with a scheme of knowledge (theories and methods) that does not adequately take account of the conceptions and proce dures of research subjects (what people actually do and say in the everyday flow of living). By setting up a scheme of knowledge that produces particular kinds of findings based on certain rules and assumptions, the researcher is often able, in step two, to show that the knowledge of practitioners is wrong or deficient and should therefore be disregarded. Methodological irony sets up an unfair competition between the knowledge of researchers and that of lay members. The outcome of the research is in effect ‘rigged’ in favour of the researcher through methodological procedures that produce certain ‘facts’ and not others (see also Law, 2004).

Methodological irony is in a way akin to some definitions of Socratic irony, as in: ‘a pretense of ignorance and of willingness to learn from another assumed in order to make the other’s false conceptions conspicuous by adroit questioning’ (Merriam-Webster Online Dictionary). When methodological irony is at play, the analyst, armed with theories and methods, ‘knows better’, like Socrates. Nevertheless s/he invites lay members into dialogue through questionnaires or interviews, for example, in order to show, by careful reasoning, why their common sense understanding is in fact wrong (we are grateful to one of the reviewers for pointing this out).

According to Watson (1998), irony is characteristic of both the hypothetico-deductive epistemology of functionalism and non-functionalist methodologies such as symbolic interaction ism or dramaturgy. Ironic treatments accord a privileged status to the analyst’s scheme of knowledge, inevitably downgrading local schema through various strategies – by relegating them to the realms of the subjective or belief, for example – while simultaneously legitimating the researcher’s scheme through claims to objectivity, rigour and so on. In some critical perspectives, for example, an act of theoretical fiat deems social actors to be operating under ‘false consciousness’ and thus mistaken in their understandings of the situation. In other cases, ironic accounts may represent social actors as engaged in doing something other than what they can be seen to be doing. For Goffman, for example, social actors are playacting rather than engaging in conversation. Objecting to an ironic stance, Watson and the ethnomethodologists align themselves with members of the local setting.

Taking serious notice of members, however, should not be misconstrued as an attempt to promote a form of epistemic egalitarianism (Lynch, 1997) that assigns value to local, common sense or marginalized forms of knowledge. While ethnomethodology recognizes the competence of the members of any social setting, this is not equivalent to suggesting that the knowledge of some members (e.g. marginalized groups) is more valuable or legitimate than those of others (e.g. people in positions of authority). The telling point in Watson’s argument, which he adopts from Garfinkel’s deconstructive reading of traditional sociology, is that a priori schema (or theories) applied in concert with certain kinds of methodological operations which obscure the indexicality and reflexivity of language while implicitly relying on them, effectively obscure the intersubjective procedures of social agents (see Cicourel, 1964 for an illuminating explication). An ironic analysis loses sight of the phenomenon in its embodied detail. If we accept the ethnomethodological view of sense-making as irremediably situated and consensual, this is equivalent to losing the phenomenon in its entirety.

Watson (1998) argues that an ironic perspective ‘works to falsify the phenomenologica integrity of that which it is designed to force into view’ (p. 206). Attempting to force the taken for granted into visibility, making our familiar world strange, is one of ethnomethodology’s principal concerns. Ethnomethodologists attempt to produce adequate accounts (Garfinkel, 2002) of reasoning practices, relying on the analyst’s competence (membership) in the use of the language that is particular to a setting; in Wittgenstein’s (1968) terms, the ethnomethodologist must be able to adequately participate in the language games of a particular form of life. In accordance with the overall project of avoiding irony, ethnomethodologists bracket existing theoretical frames of reference, a policy known as ethnomethodological indifference (Garfinkel, 2002). Analysts must also refrain from privileging any particular version of reality or the views of any particular group, producing accounts that are non-partisan (Bogen & Lynch, 1990). These practices are meant to direct analysts towards a ‘unique way of investigating how socia order is constituted’ (Lynch, 1997, p. 372).

A commitment to non-partisan accounts, which follows from the above methodologica considerations, has often been misconstrued as laying claim to a value-free social science or as a position of indifference to social issues (e.g. McHoul, 1988). Both these depictions of ethnomethodology are misleading. In regards to value neutrality, ethnomethodology displays a reflexive awareness of its own project, a characteristic it shares with various critica approaches. Practised in the shadow of a radical referential reflexivity (Pollner, 1991), ethnomethodology identifies all accounts as versions, including its own. The term ‘reflexivity’ in this context is used in a philosophical sense to denote a questioning of one’s own preconceptions and procedures. Referential reflexivity, in particular, is concerned with the preconceptions that colour representations of the world (Lynch, 2000). The use of the term here is thus not equivalent to the reflexivity of language discussed earlier in the paper.

Ethnomethodology recognizes that it is impossible to lay claim to an absolute objectivity, given that researchers also rely on practical reasoning. Yet, while ethnomethodologists cannot claim ‘to know better’ (Garfinkel, 1996, p. 6), they seek to escape this vortex of reflexivity – that is, the possibility of infinite regression – and its immobilizing effects through an eminently practical orientation (Lynch, 2000). Ethnomethodologists recognize that the research reports they produce rely on practical reasoning and thus cannot be said to be objective in a strict sense; these accounts can in themselves become sites of investigation as instances of sense making. These investigations of ethnomethodological studies in turn can also be analysed, and so on, potentially leading researchers into a cycle of infinite regression that admits no possi bility of developing any kind of practical knowledge. As Lynch (2000) notes, however, ‘there is no particular advantage to “being” reflexive or “doing” reflexive analysis, unless something provocative, interesting or revealing comes from it’ (p. 42).

Elsewhere, Lynch (1997) has also effectively argued against a reading of ethnomethodology as an indifferent discipline. He argues that a commitment to avoid granting epistemic privilege to particular accounts does not imply a value-free sociology, nor does it indicate the absence of social concern on the part of the analyst. This is not to say that Lynch is proposing that ethnomethodology could be actively employed as a tool for social advocacy on behalf of marginalized or powerless groups. To do so would be to privilege a particular version of reality over others and slip into irony, inevitably risking the integrity of the phenomenon.

A commitment to eschewing irony by bracketing a priori assumptions, including commonly accepted concepts such as psychological motivations or power relations, is a methodological requirement for conducting ethnomethodological studies. Social theories commonly associ ated with the tradition of critique generally proceed from a priori assumptions, often positing the existence of a power differential that privileges particular groups and subjugates others. The critical tradition is also commonly associated with an emancipative intent that seeks to liberate social agents from the fetters of misconception (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991). The aims and preconceptions of many strands of critical social theory stand in opposition to the eth nomethodological commitment to avoid irony. It is perhaps unsurprising then to find that some ethnomethodologists are at odds with the idea of enrolling ethnomethodology in the project of critique. The crux of the debate is the topic of the next section.

## Ethnomethodology and social critique

Several academics have perceived an affinity between ethnomethodology and other critical positions. Perhaps most notably, McHoul has sought to extend ethnomethodology in the direction of deconstruction (McHoul, 1994) as well as a political linguistics (McHoul, 1988). Similarly, Chua (1977) has argued for a Marxist appropriation based on the value that ethnomethodology brings to this perspective by laying bare the process by which ideology is constructed and maintained in society. While not explicitly connected to ethnomethodology, Dorothy Smith’s (1974) work on the social construction of documentary reality is deeply influenced by an ethnomethodological sensitivity that links the reporting and accounting procedures of formal organizations to the obviousness of social phenomena.

The work of McHoul (1988) towards an ethnomethodologically inspired political linguistics has led him to an illuminating exchange of opinions with two prominent ethnomethodologists (Bogen & Lynch, 1990). In a critical re-reading of Coulter’s (1973; 1979; 1983) work on the sociology of mind, McHoul (1988) attempts to shift the grounds of Wittgenstein-inspired ethnomethodology towards political analysis and commitment. While generally appreciative of ethnomethodology as a means of social analysis, McHoul detects something missing in Coulter’s strictly descriptivist stance. Coulter’s texts are concerned with analysing conversations between mental health professionals and patients in order to uncover the practical reasoning that sustains psychoanalytic talk and the medicalization of mental illness. According to McHoul, missing from ethnomethodological analyses of conversations reported in Coulter’s texts is an appreciation for the underlying power relations that traverse these linguistic exchanges. McHoul draws on Foucauldian ideas to re-analyse a conversational fragment from Coulter’s (1979) book, coming to a different set of conclusions about the goings-on in this setting. In particular, McHoul argues that the patient in this setting can be seen to strategically employ conversation as a form of resistance. He proposes that an ethnomethodologically inspired political linguistics would put ethnomethodology to the task of cataloguing conversational strategies that may be usefully employed in acts of resistance or transgression.

In response to McHoul’s proposition, Bogen & Lynch (1990) argue that McHoul confuses the ethnomethodological commitments to descriptivism and to bracketing a priori schema of interpretation (i.e. the practice of ethnomethodological indifference) with the claim to a valuefree and indifferent sociology. Further, they argue in a crippling critique that McHoul’s re-reading of the conversational fragment is based on a priori assumptions about what is going on in the situation and also about its eventual outcomes, performing a transcendental or ironic analysis. In slipping into irony and moving away from the attempt to provide a non-partisan account, McHoul risks losing sight of the phenomenon in its embodied detail.

Overall, Bogen and Lynch do not find McHoul’s proposal that ethnomethodology could be put to the task of developing an arsenal of conversational strategies of resistance to be tenable. The situated and contingent nature of human interactions precludes the possibility of developing such a collection of strategies. Moreover, Bogen and Lynch suggest that the individual featured in the conversational fragment ‘is quite able to exploit the options presented by the local interactional situation without having to be instructed by academic analysts’ (p. 513). The ethnomethodological appreciation for the knowledgeability of social agents is evident in this statement.

While sympathetic to the project of social critique, Bogen and Lynch see McHoul’s attempt to press ethnomethodology into the service of social critique to be ‘ill-fated’ (p. 506). Irony characterizes McHoul’s re-reading of the data contained in the segment. It gives primacy to the analyst’s a priori scheme, going beyond the understanding visibly displayed by social agents and coming into conflict with the ethnomethodological orientation. In the background is also a tension between the emancipative aims of a political linguistics that would seek to inform socia agents about strategies not already known to them and the ethnomethodological perspective that grants these same social agents an embodied competence and seeks not to instruct but to learn from them (Garfinkel, 2002).

Given what appear as irreconcilable tensions, can ethnomethodology be critical? Our own view is that it is possible to reconcile ethnomethodology and critique by reframing the project of social critique along broader lines.

## ETHNOMETHODOLOGY AS CRITIQUE

We have explored the role that the concept of irony plays in shaping ethnomethodological investigations. We have also pointed to the importance of irony in the debate about ethnomethodology and social critique. We now turn briefly to the idea of critique in IS before returning to the question of whether ethnomethodology can be critical. We will argue that ethnomethodology can indeed be critical. Subsequently, we reflect on the praxical potential of a critical ethnomethodology. Finally, we make use of an existing ethnomethological investiga tion of an information system to illustrate the potential we see.

## Critique in IS

In the field of IS, the idea of critique has traditionally been associated with the project of Critical Theory (e.g. Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991), and particularly with the work of Jürgen Habermas (Brooke, 2002). In keeping with such perspectives, a focus on systems of domination and a broadly emancipative aim are generally regarded as the distinguishing marks of critical research (e.g. Howcroft & Truex, 2001). In recent years, the IS community has begun to recognize other modes of critical engagement. Brooke (2002), in particular, has called for the IS community to move beyond a singular focus on Habermasian discourses, pointing to the promise of a Foucauldian perspective as one possible avenue to critique. From the perspective of Actor Network Theory Doolin & Lowe (2002) have further argued in favour of a broade critical agenda that would include ‘act[s] of revelation’ (p. 74) that show us ‘how things could have been otherwise’ (p. 75). Critique thus need not entail a negative view of power or explicitly emancipative and interventionist aims (e.g. Foucault, 1980). These efforts as well as those of others in the IS community (e.g. Marcon et al., 2004; Avgerou, 2005; McGrath, 2005; Walsham, 2005) and in other scholarly domains (e.g. in organization studies – Alvesson & Deetz 1996; Kincheloe & McLaren 2000) point to a growing appreciation for the variety of possible modes of engagement with critique.

## Reconciling ethnomethodology and critique

In keeping with this broader agenda, we believe that it is possible to reconcile ethnomethod ology and critique. Ethnomethodology is critical when conceived as an act of revelation that makes visible the constructedness of existing social arrangements. Such a task need not begin by positing a system of domination and thus slip into methodological irony. Rather, the requirement to avoid irony directs the researcher to inquire into the everyday practices of all members of a setting without privileging the understanding of any particular group.

Lynch (1997) argues that, through its policies and maxims, ethnomethodology directs our attention to the manner in which ‘humble accounts’ (p. 372) (i.e. those of ordinary members of society) as well as authoritative ones (e.g. those of governments or institutions and even researchers) relentlessly constitute the social order. Ethnomethodology shows that all of us, as producers of official, authoritative or simply humble accounts, are inevitably implicated in the production and reproduction of the social order including the institutional and politica structures. As Rawls (2002) notes in the introduction to Garfinkel’s book: ‘The fact that persons may be required to reproduce the very social forms that oppress them is a much more powerful and frightening vision of tension and inequality than the idea that independent individuals exist in some sort of primeval struggle against society’ (p. 56). For Rawls, ethnomethodology is far from indifferent: ‘Ethnomethodology cannot be indifferent to political, ethical or theoretical critique because that is essentially what it is. Ethnomethodology seeks to reveal the way in which taken for granted social practices maintain the appearance of things’ (p. 54).

Ethnomethodology illuminates the contingent process through which social structures are kept in place. It shows us the situated nature of this process and the multiplicity of participants and acts that contribute to the ongoing constitution and reconstitution of existing socia arrangements. Acts of revelation have the potential to displace the obviousness of what we take for granted and thus bring the legitimacy of existing structures into question (cf. Doolin & Lowe, 2002). Like ethnographic work, they can have the quality of criticality: a call to question our assumptions and to reflection (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993).

In ethnomethodology, this critical task can be performed explicitly by augmenting a description of just how the social order is constituted in practice with a clear statement tha points out the inadequacy of taken for granted conceptions (e.g. Suchman, 2000) or, as is more often the case, implicitly by allowing the reader to come to his/her own conclusions based on a detailed account of situated practices (Garfinkel, 1967). The lack of an explici critique, however, does not mean that critique is not at play as the critical import may nevertheless be perceived by the reader. As de Certeau (1984), among others, has noted, the ‘reader’ is not a passive receiver. Like ethnography, ethnomethodological studies are ultimately communicative acts subject to sense-making and evaluation by their readers (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 1993).

As readers, we noted the critical import of ethnomethodology in a plethora of ethnomethodological studies conducted in institutional environments by Garfinkel (1967; 1986) and his adherents (e.g. Bittner, 1967; Sacks, 1972; Pollner, 1974) and more recently in the application of ethnomethodology to IT-enabled environments (e.g. Benson, 1993; Suchman, 2000). We noted also that, within this research tradition, critique takes a particular form that, in our view, suggests the possibility of a close connection to praxis.

## Ethnomethodology and praxis

The debate over the critical potential of ethnomethodology led us to the issue of methodological irony. Our awareness of this issue in turn guided our attempt to re-read ethnomethodology in light of a broader conception of critique that includes also acts of revelation that show us how the social order is constituted. This does not mean, of course, that ordinary people engage their daily lives with the explicit intent of constituting a social order. Given its commitment to avoid ironic accounts, ethnomethodology turns its attention to practical sites of action where members are doing something – performing their jobs, for example – for a purpose other than the constitution of the social order. Close investigation of routine activities nevertheless reveals a social order that is embedded and continually constituted in everyday interactions. Macro level structures are thus not ignored by ethnomethodology in spite of its focus on situated practices, as the macro is embedded in the micro (Hilbert, 1990).

The embeddedness of social structures in everyday activities provides an opportunity for a critique that, being grounded in the knowledge of participants in a setting, may provide a useful avenue to praxis. As we attempt to illustrate through an example in the next section, taken for granted social structures that might not seem immediately relevant to the accomplishment of everyday tasks are, at least at times, inextricable from the practical problems that are encountered in accomplishing tasks. A critique that takes place at the confluence of social structures and practical problems in our view has the potential to be responsive to the interests of practitioners. It provides insight into a problem that makes the accomplishment of practical tasks difficult or ineffective, while concretely implicating social structures such as systems of differentiation in the problem and potentially also its amelioration. It is here that issues of fairness and justice that may appear too abstract to be of interest to practitioners can perhaps be made relevant and more actionable.

Our interest in this paper is to suggest the praxical potential we glimpse in ethnomethodol ogy rather than fully explicate it. Bogen & Lynch (1990) as well as others (e.g. Sharrock & Anderson, 1991) have argued that ethnomethodology is a descriptivist project that cannot change the world, leaving everything just as it is, a charge that has also been levelled at Critical Theory (e.g. White, 1995). Nevertheless, there is certainly merit in such statements. Yet, as we see it, such impediments do not negate the revelatory function of ethnomethodology or the potential for praxis that a situated analysis may offer. The connection between critique and praxis is a complex topic and it is not our intent to treat it here in a substantive manner. Nevertheless, the practical orientation that ethnomethodology brings to its investigations is, in our view, promising as an avenue to critique.

It should be noted that it is also possible to use ethnomethodology for purposes other than social critique. Ethnomethodology, for example, has been used to inform system design by understanding how people use technologies in practice without necessarily leading to a critique of social arrangements (e.g. Heath et al., 2000). This is obviously not the case with critical approaches that begin by positing dominant and dominated groups and which place social critique at the very core of any investigation. Ethnomethodology thus is not always placed directly in the service of social critique but it can be used in this fashion.

One researcher who has employed ethnomethodology as a critical lens in relation to technologies is Suchman (2000). In the next section we make use of an investigation involving an information system to illustrate in concrete terms the critical import of ethnomethodology and its relevance for IS research.

## Ethnomethodology, critique and IS: an example

Taken for granted conceptions of knowledge and routine work are at the centre of Suchman’s (2000) study. At least at the level of discourse. knowledge (or professjonal) work is associated with the exercise of subjective judgement and interpretation. Routine work, on the other hand, is deemed to consist of objective tasks requiring little mental activity. The dichotomy knowledge/routine and its related pair subjective/objective are generally taken as the basis for the assignment of differential rewards and identities.

Suchman makes use of ethnomethodology to inquire into the adequacy of taken for granted conceptions of knowledge and routine work. The study is set in a legal firm where the work of coding documents is shared by junior lawyers and temporary workers. The work involves coding documents related to legal cases and entering the information in a computer system to facilitate subsequent retrieval. Within the organization, this shared work of coding is considered to consist of subjective coding based on judgement and interpretation as performed by junior lawyers, and objective coding requiring copying information which is the task of document coders.

The distinction between subjective and objective coding is one that is relevant to the everyday work of coding. It thus provides a practical site for inquiring into the distinction between knowledge and routine work. In keeping with ethnomethodology’s focus on understanding how people make sense of what they and others are doing in everyday interactions, the investigation must take account of what people actually do and say to arrive at meaning through a consensual process of sense-making. To this end, Suchman studies both the material practices of junior lawyers and coders and their accountability. Material practices refer principally to what lawyers and coders actually do with the documents and to how problems that arise in the course of the work of coding are resolved by making use of available resources and background knowledge. Accountability points to the fact that what workers do must make sense to others. To investigate the accountability of an activity is to inquire into how practitioners go about making sure that their coding is seen as ‘sensible and responsible’ (p. 32) in light of their particular identities.

Suchman makes visible the co-presence of activities that the organization deems subjective (requiring judgement and interpretation) and objective (requiring copying information) in the work of both junior lawyers and document coders. By laying bare the actual reasoning practices in the work of lawyers and coders, Suchman disturbs common assumptions about the degree of difficulty and the level of knowledge required to be a coder, a task that is commonly regarded as routine work and rewarded accordingly. Though the work of the two groups is not identical, the distinctions that are perpetuated by the classification of junior lawyers and coders into knowledge and routine workers are thus shown to rely in a significan way on differences in identity and reward rather than only on differences in the materia practices of the two groups.

Suchman’s analysis disturbs the obviousness of taken for granted conceptions of knowledge and routine work. It reveals the heterogeneous nature of the work of lawyers and coders and points to the unrecognized competence that document coders bring to their work. While this is in itself an important point, Suchman further shows how the organization of the work of coding according to the subjective/objective dichotomy has practical implications for the effective functioning of the organization. In particular, Suchman describes the problems that arise from the requirement that the coding performed by temporary workers be objective. Providing a description of a document, for example, is a subjective task that requires both background knowledge and interpretation given the complexity and variety of documents. This subjective task is nevertheless the responsibility of document coders who, as accountable members of the organization, must produce objective coding. Paradoxically, such descriptions are often not useful because they do not necessarily reflect the interests of lawyers. The troubles that enter into the task of generating objective document descriptions when what is in fact needed is a subjective description oriented to the process of building a legal case thus have a material import for the effectiveness of the organization and the IS that support it and it is at such sites of everyday practice that the social structures that order our world become issues for the purpose of getting something done.

A detailed account of work practices in Suchman’s study reveals how the dichotomy between subjective and objective work is (1) currently constituted through the daily practices of both junior lawyers and coders as an intrinsic part of doing the work itself; and (2) relevant to the practical problems experienced by the organization. In the close connection between what may appear as abstract moral issues involving fairness and practical problems that are of immediate concern to practitioners lies the potential for a critique capable of energizing action. This potential is suggests by Suchman’s conclusion that an organization ‘interested in identifying more effective working orders . . . abandon simple distinctions in favor of an appreciation of the specific requirements and possibilities that the work, and the heterogeneity of organization members, actually presents’ (p. 45).

## CLOSING REMARKS

Ethnomethodology is a research tradition that, in its practical orientation, has much to offer a critical social science. Yet the passage from a descriptivist project to critique must be undertaken with caution in order to preserve the analytical orientation that ethnomethodology demands. In its insistence on a contextually grounded analysis that eschews a discourse based on universal values or totalizing schema, ethnomethodology turns our attention to ‘standards of practice and methods of judgment that are endogenous to the lived-and-used orders of everyday life’ (Bogen, 1999), in other words to the very sites where embodied values and morality are woven into the relentless constitution of society (Jayyusi, 1991).

The debate about the critical potential of ethnomethodology reflects the care to avoid irony that must be exercised in conducting ethnomethodological research as well as a particular conception of what counts as critique. In our own account, which is merely a version, we have attempted to shift the grounds of this debate to shed some light on a research tradition that is little understood in IS and, in our view, offers a fruitful avenue to critique and praxis.

As readers, we saw in the work undertaken by ethnomethodologists over the last fifty years and in recent ethnomethodological studies of work in IT-enabled environments, a sympathetic critique of human practices that does not fail to take account of the considerable competence of members of society and at times also the potential for a critique that, being responsive to the interests of practitioners, may lead to alterations in existing practices, towards a different and perhaps more equitable order.
