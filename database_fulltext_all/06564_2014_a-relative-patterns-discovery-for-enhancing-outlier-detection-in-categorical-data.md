# A relative patterns discovery for enhancing outlier detection in categorical data

## a b s t r a c t

Outlier (also known as anomaly) detection technology is widely applied to many areas, such as diagnosing diseases, evaluating credit, and investigating cybercrime. Recently, several studies, based on frequent itemset mining (FIM), have been proposed to detect outliers in categorical data. For ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, these FIM-based studies pruned (ignored) the majority of data by either imposing a threshold or restricting the length of the pattern or both, and they further adopted the limited information to evaluate observations. In spite of high ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, such a pruning approach encounters the problem of distortion, i.e., the accuracy decreases to a low level of discernment or even causes the contrary judgment in certain cases. In this paper, we introduce the concept relative patterns discovery from a new perspective on association analysis. To ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently explore the relative patterns, we devise a hash-index-based intersecting approach (called the HA). Based on the knowledge of relative patterns, we propose an unsupervised approach (called the UA) to evaluate which observations are anomalous. Instead of using the limited information, our method can differentiate the features of observations without the problem of distortion. The results of the empirical investigation, conducted with eight real-world datasets on the UCI Machine Learning Repository, demonstrate that our method generally outperforms the previous studies not only in accuracy but also in ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. We also demonstrate that the execution complexity of our method is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly ef<sup>fi</sup>cient, especially in high-dimensional data. Furthermore, our method can represent a natural panorama of data, which is appropriate in controlled experiments for discovering more decisive factors in outlier detection.

## 1. Introduction

Outlier (also known as anomaly) detection technology [1,2] plays an important role in several applications, such as medical diagnosis, credit assessment, and the investigation of cybercrime [3–7]. According to the generally accepted de<sup>fi</sup>nition [8], an outlier is “an observation that deviates so much from other observations as to arouse suspicion that it was generated by a different mechanism”.

The majority of outlier detection studies [9–18] concentrated on this type of continuous data. Although these studies perform well in terms of continuous data, they cannot be applied to categorical data that represents a nominal scale of measurement. According to the study [19], the only statistic relevant in the categorical data is the number of items and the mode.

Several studies [20–23] based on frequent itemset mining (FIM) are proposed for outlier detection in categorical data. For the sake of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, these FIM-based methods pruned (ignored) the majority of the data by either imposing a threshold, or restricting the length of itemsets, or both. Intuitively, such a pruning approach most likely loses precision (discussed in Section 2). For testing the above proposition, we conducted several tests with the real-world datasets that can be accessed in the website of the UC Irvine Machine Learning Repository [28]. Based on the test results, we observed that such a pruning approach encounters the problem of distortion, i.e., the accuracy decreases to a low level of discernment or even causes the contrary judgment in certain cases. Indeed, the results of the related decision making, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the problem of distortion, most likely cause severe damage, such as the faulty evaluation of diseases and in the area of <sup>fi</sup>nance.

For improving accuracy, a straightforward solution is to reserve all frequent itemsets and then evaluate every observation by using these instead of using the limited length of itemsets or using the partial number of itemsets [20–23,34]. Nevertheless, such a solution is extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to achieve, especially in instances of large high-dimensional data. The reason is that the computation cost increases exponentially according to the number of items (also called attributes) and dimensionalities. Though the approach NDI [27], which provided the deduction rules, can speed up the process of itemset discovery, NDI encounters a similar challenge in that the number of deduction rules increase exponentially.

In this paper, we introduce the concept relative patterns discovery from a new perspective on association analysis. Different from the conventional studies of association analysis, the design goal of relative patterns discovery is to explore the relation between each two observations without the problems of distortion and enormous computation. Instead of yielding a vast amount of useless information (e.g., all frequent itemsets and association rules), it would be worthwhile to gain insight into the causes of the relation between observations. It is reasonable to consider that an observation is anomalous if this observation contains fewer characteristics (e.g., relative patterns) in comparison with other observations.

For outlier detection, initially we devise a hash-index-based intersecting approach, namely, the HA, to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently explore the relative patterns. Computing the intersecting set of two observations, the HA method only needs to run in linear time even in the worst case. Next, based on the knowledge of relative patterns, we propose an unsupervised approach (called the UA) to evaluate which observations are anomalous. Instead of using the limited (or partial) information, the UA method can differentiate the features of observations without the problem of distortion. In addition, the UA method runs in cubic time in the worst case. In contrast, a FIM-based approach suffers from an inherent exponential-growth problem in the complexities of time and space; in the worst case, these methods need to recursively traverse every itemset on the lattice structure [1,29,30]. As a result, a FIMbased approach runs in exponential time in the worst case, although they may perform well when the threshold (i.e., the minimum support) is high, which means discarding much of the itemsets.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the previous studies, the distortion problem, and the enormous computation problem. In Section 3, we introduce the concept of relative patterns and describe the proposed methods. In Section 4, we guide the design of empirical investigation and discuss the results of the analysis. Finally, we present this paper's conclusions in Section 5.

## 2. Related work

## 2.1. Previous studies on outlier detection

According to Han et al. [1], the current studies on outlier detection can be categorized into three types: supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised methods. The supervised method (e.g., the study [34]) modeled outlier detection as a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation problem. Given by domain experts, the class labels are used to train the classi<sup>fi</sup>er to identify outliers. The semi-supervised method is used for resolving the situation that only a small part of data has class labels. The unsupervised method (e.g., the studies [21–23]) aims for the condition that the class labels are either unavailable or unknown in advance.

Recently, many methods [20–24,34] have been proposed to identify the outliers in categorical data. Adopting both entropy and total correlation for outlier detection in categorical data, the method [24] is effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient; however, it would seem to be a misleading result. This is because this method [24] applied the defective measure (i.e., entropy and correlation) to the categorical data on a nominal scale, and therefore, the result is meaningless from the perspective of statistical relevance [14].

Several studies [20–23] adopted respective FIM-based approaches (i.e., FP-growth [25], Apriori [26], and NDI [27]) for outlier detection in the categorical data. Such a FIM-based approach is sound because of the number of itemsets used as a score measurement [19]. Similarly, the studies [20–23] applied their respective FIM-based approaches to determine patterns. Next, these researchers proposed a variety of evaluation approaches (i.e., based on the restricted length of patterns or the partial number of patterns) to identify the anomalous observations. Based on their experimental results, the studies [21–23] are fairly effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient for certain cases.

According to the survey [33], some constraint-based approaches, apart from the support-based approaches [25–27], have been proposed for selecting pattern sets. For example, Vreeken et al. [35] adopted the minimum description length principle (MDL) [36] to build a code table by selecting speci<sup>fi</sup>c pattern sets, which is called KRIMP. Through the MDL-based code table, the data can be well compressed [33]. Then, in KRIMP [35] a good classi<sup>fi</sup>er is constructed for the task of classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. To enhance KRIMP [35], Akoglu et al. [34] proposed COMPREX. Instead of using a single code table, COMPREX [34] designed multiple code tables that can ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently build the compressor for outlier detection. In experiments, COMPREX [34] achieved high accuracy (i.e., 1.00 ≥ AUC ≥ 0.70) in <sup>fi</sup>ve cases but was inaccurate (i.e., 0.70 ≥ AUC ≥ 0.00) in the other twelve cases. Moreover, in the initial phase, both COMPREX [34] and KRIMP [35] have to split data based on class labels; thus, they are only applicable for the case in which the class labels are available in advance. In sum, the previous studies pruned patterns by varied ways. For new data, it is not possible to determine beforehand the patterns that are useless. Therefore, a pruning approach not only loses the precision but also incurs the distortion problem.

## 2.2. The distortion problem

By de<sup>fi</sup>nition [8], an outlier is an observation that deviates signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly from other observations. A good outlier detection method should be able to discern the anomalous observations. In the following, we provide a dramatic scenario to demonstrate that a pruning approach becomes ineffective as a consequence of using the restricted patterns information. The issue of ineffectiveness, as occurs in the pruning approach, is called the distortion problem.

Example 1. Given a dataset with six observations, $O _ { 1 } = ( a 1 , b 1 , c 1 )$ $O _ { 2 } = ( a 2 , b 1 , c 1 ) , O _ { 3 } = ( a 1 , b 2 , c 2 ) , O _ { 4 } = ( a 2 , b 2 , c 2 ) , O _ { 5 } =$ $( a 1 , b 1 , c 1 ) , O _ { 6 } = ( a 2 , b 2 , c 2 )$ , each of which has 3 dimensionalities (i.e., a, b, and c), and each dimensionality has 2 attributes (e.g., a1 and a2 in dimensionality a). We can observe that the occurrence of every attribute is 3.

## 3. The proposed methods for outlier detection

## 3.1. The concept of relative patterns discovery

The relative patterns discovery, named RPD, is designed to explore the relation between each of two observations without the problems of distortion and enormous computation. In De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1, we formally describe a relative pattern, and the corresponding example is provided.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1. Suppose the data D is composed of κ observations and ℓ attributes (or called items). For each two observations, a relative pattern is a collection of attributes that occur in both of the two observations. The number of all relative patterns is between 1 (i.e., only the empty set {⊥} exists, that is, every observation has completely different attributes from each other) and κ × $( \kappa - 1 ) / 2$ (i.e., each two observations produce a uniquely relative pattern).

For example, observations $O _ { 1 } = ( a 1 , b 1 , c 1 )$ and $O _ { 2 } = ( a 2 , b 1 , c 1 )$ have the relative pattern {b1, c1}. In addition, we can observe that the duplicate observations yield the same patterns. Because the duplicate observations cannot produce any new relative pattern, we can reduce them for purposes of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. ## 3.2. Exploration of relative patterns

It is practical to use the operation of intersection to discover relative patterns, e.g., in Example 1, $O _ { 1 } \cap O _ { 2 } = \{ \ell \in O _ { 1 }$ and $\ell \in O _ { 2 } \} = \{ b 1 , c 1 \}$ . In the area of algorithm [32], there are sophisticated approaches to resolve the task of the search, including the sequential search, the binary search, and the hash-based search approaches. From the perspective of computation complexity, a hash-based search approach runs in constant time, which is more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than two other approaches (i.e., a sequential search approach runs in linear time, and a binary search approach runs in logarithmic time). Based on a hash-based search approach, Park, Chen, and Yu [37] developed an algorithm for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient large itemset generation. Inspired by the study [37], we adopt a hash-based search approach to explore the relative patterns, namely the HA. Instead of comparing each two attributes, we use a hash index that can directly determine the attribute between the two observations. In the HA method, the value of every hash index is unique so that the property of oneto-one correspondence holds. Therefore, the HA method can detect relative patterns correctly and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently.

## 3.3. Evaluation of anomaly

Based on the knowledge of relative patterns, we propose an unsupervised approach (called UA) to evaluate which observations are anomalous. The term “unsupervised approach” means that we neither use the class labels (usually provided by domain experts) to train data nor employ the information of class labels to compute the anomalous degree of observations. In fact, given new data, it is impossible to know in the beginning the types of class label to which every observation belongs, even for the domain experts.

To evaluate every observation, we propose two scoring metrics for the data with different dimensionalities such as the small scale and large scale. For the small-scale data, i.e., the number of dimensionalities is equal to or less than 10, we compute the score of every observation as Eq. (3), where variable κ means an observation; variable count denotes the occurrence of observation κ; variable si is the number of subsets (including the empty set) in the observation κ; variable v is the number of relative patterns that are a subset of observation κ; variable count denotes the occurrence of the relative pattern η; and variable $s i _ { \eta }$ is the number of subsets (including the empty set) in the relative pattern η.

$$
S O _ {\kappa} = (c o u n t _ {\kappa} \times s i _ {\kappa}) + \sum_ {\eta = 1} ^ {\nu} \Bigl (c o u n t _ {\eta} \times s i _ {\eta} \Bigr)\tag{3}
$$

Given the data of Example 1, we compute the score of every observation by Eq. (3), which is demonstrated in the following.

$$
\begin{array}{l} S O _ {1} = (\{a 1, b 1, c 1 \}, \{a 1 \}, \{b 1, c 1 \}) = (2 \times 2 ^ {3}) + (3 \times 2 ^ {1}) + (3 \times 2 ^ {2}) = 3 4; \\ S O _ {2} = (\{a 2, b 1, c 1 \}, \{a 2 \}, \{b 1, c 1 \}) = (1 \times 2 ^ {3}) + (3 \times 2 ^ {1}) + (3 \times 2 ^ {2}) = 2 6; \\ S O _ {3} = 2 6; S O _ {4} = 3 4; S O _ {5} = 3 4; S O _ {6} = 3 4; \end{array}
$$

For the large-scale data, i.e., the number of dimensionalities is larger than 10, the score of every observation can be obtained by using Eq. (4), where variable li is the length of the observation κ, and variable $l \dot { h } _ { \eta }$ is the length of the relative pattern $\eta .$

## 4.2. Evidence of the distortion assertion

To verify whether the distortion problem occurs in the FIM-based method, we perform two types of experiments. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst type of experiment, we give the same value of maximum length (ML) but different values of minimum support (MS). The results, in Table 3 and Fig. 5, indicate that in the majority of datasets, the FIM-based method is fairly inaccurate when the value of minimum support is high. In particular, the FIM-based method is unstable. For example, in the datasets CMC, the MS value of 0.25 is more accurate than that of 0.01, but the MS value of 0.01 is more accurate than that of 0.5. In the second type of experiment, we give the same value of minimum support (MS) but different values of maximum length (ML). The results, in Table 4, indicate that in half of the datasets, the FIM-based method is slightly inaccurate when the value of the maximum length is low.

These results also provide important insights into a pruning-based method. In the dataset TTT, given the relatively low value of minimum support, however, the value of AUC exhibits no apparent accuracy. This is because the value of the maximum length prohibits the discovery of the patterns for which the length is larger than 4. Even if we decrease the value of minimum support, it has the similar result. In sum, these results suggest that the FIM-based method can distort the truth and cause incidences of impaired judgment when pruning vast amounts of patterns.

## 4.3. Results of analysis

Our method belongs to the unsupervised type because we neither train a classi<sup>fi</sup>er nor score observations by using the class labels. Hence, we compare our method with the studies [21–23] that are also of the unsupervised type. Note that we select the study [21] as a comparative object because the other methods [22,23] are based on the scoring approach of the study [21]. According to the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings [23], we give the value of maximum length (ML) as 4. Moreover, we initially give the value of minimum support (MS) as 1%, and then we adjust the MS to a trade-off between accuracy and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. The values of MS and ML are only used for the studies [21–23]; our method operates in none of the restrictions.

From the perspective of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, Table 5 indicates that our method is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than the FIM-based method [21], especially when the number of dimensionalities is large. From the perspective of accuracy, Table 6 demonstrates that our method performs better in the cases of the CMC, LC, SLC, and TTT, and the precision of our method is similar to that of the FIM-based method [21] in the remaining cases such as the BCWO, CE, NU, and SH. ## 4.4. Research limitations and future work

In some cases, the pattern-based approach is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in terms of discernment. We consider that contradictory attributes and untypical õattributes are two possible justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the ineffectiveness. We now explain the term contradictory attributes. Table 8 describes the detailed attribute information for the dataset CMC. In the dataset CMC, many females, whether they are now working or not, use the same contraceptive method. Thus, the attribute “Wife is now working?” is most likely not adequate to evaluate the anomalies that are inconsistent. The term untypical attributes refers to the condition that the number of patterns is irrelevant to the representation of anomaly. For example, in the dataset TTT (a Tic-Tac-Toe game), the number of patterns is unrelated to either a win or loss. In sum, it is worth noting that the quality of detection can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the attribute usage in addition to the detection approach.

The result of the study [23] indicates a similar condition, that is, the lower value of minimum support brings about higher accuracy in a certain case but lower accuracy in the other one and vice versa. Thus far, many studies have provided various features that have achieved a certain usefulness in their respective cases; however, as discussed in Section 2, these studies are impractical for analyzing new (or unknown) data. For example, a FIM-based approach is incapable of identifying whether the inaccuracy is caused by the useless attributes or the pruned patterns. As opposed to the previous studies, our method can ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently represent the original appearance of the data without distortion. Hence, our method provides a fundamental principle to represent the full spectrum of patterns for the selection of typical attributes and the further examination of anomaly. Indeed, the technology of outlier detection is appropriate not only for providing the clues that need to be further analyzed by domain experts but also a <sup>fi</sup>nal decision.

## 5. Conclusions

This paper describes the distortion problem in a pruning-based approach and proposes a countermeasure. By empirical investigation, our method primarily possesses the following advantages. First, our proposed HA method can ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently obtain the complete information for relative patterns, especially for large high-dimensional categorical data. Second, our proposed UA method is effective in detecting anomaly in most cases. Third, without pruning patterns, our method can avoid either distorting the truth or causing a contrary judgment. For new data, this advantage would be helpful to support the decision maker because it is not possible to determine in advance the patterns that are useless. Finally, our method represents a natural panorama of data that can serve as a fundamental model and be used for controlled experiments. For example, using our method for new data, a decision maker can discover and select more decisive factors for outlier detection.
