# What drives trust transfer from web to mobile payment services? The dual e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived entitativity

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Web–mobile payment transition Trust transfer theory Perceived entitativity Mobile payment Mobile commerce

## A B S T R A C T

Drawing from trust transfer theory, this study investigates cognitive trust and emotional trust transfer from web payment (WP) to mobile payment (MP) services with the emphasis on the e<sup>f</sup>ect of web–mobile payment relationship, namely, perceived entitativity. By conducting an online survey (n = 491), our study shows two key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, cognitive trust and emotional trust can be transferred from WP to MP services, which, in turn, formulate intention to use MP services. Second, perceived entitativity not only increases cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP, but also enhances the transfer of cognitive trust and emotional trust from WP to MP services.

## 1. Introduction

Advancements in mobile technologies have facilitated the extension of online transactions from web to mobile environments accompanied by a shift toward diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation in online payment markets [1]. An increasing number of online payment providers, such as Google, eBay, and Alibaba, have begun to extend their incumbent web payment (WP) to mobile payment (MP) contexts [2]. The phenomenon is commonly known as web–mobile payment transition, which allows online payment providers to leverage the advantage of ubiquitous computing in mobile environments and to o<sup>f</sup>er real-time payment services [3]. MP services allow users to adopt mobile terminals such as smartphones to pay for goods, services, and bills [4]. Although MP services are highly useful, they are not widely adopted among users. The eMarketer reports that there are merely 7 % of users in India,<sup>1</sup> 8 % of users in Germany,<sup>2</sup> 13 % in the U.K.<sup>3</sup>, and 20 % in the U.S.<sup>4</sup> that have already been using MP services. The majority of online users still hesitate to use MP ser vices because of lack of trust and serious concerns, such as security (47 %), and privacy (15 %)<sup>5</sup> . Consequently, trust building in MP is crucial to the success of MP adoption and the web–mobile payment transition business.

Given the importance of trust building in MP, the information system (IS) research community has extensively investigated this sub ject (see Appendix A). Although previous studies have provided important insights into trust building in MP, we highlight two issues that will require further investigation. First, trust building in MP is primarily investigated from a cognitive trust perspective, while emotional trust was largely disregarded. Cognitive trust denotes a consumer’s rational expectations of MP services’ capability to provide reliable and accurate <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, while emotional trust means the consumer s emotional feelings of security and comfort toward relying on MP services [5]. MP services are pervasive and personalized technologies that enable users to pay for services anytime from almost anywhere based on their personal needs [4]. Prior research indicates that personalized technologies require people to make decisions based on trust not only according to the rational assessment of a technology’s integrity and reliability, but also derived from their emotional reactions of security and comfort to the technology [5]. Recent studies have also suggested that trust should be an interplay of cognitive trust and emotional trust [6–8]. Thus, understanding emotional trust can add valuable insights to trust building in MP in the web–mobile payment transition context.

Second, a very few of studies have examined trust building in MP by incorporating trust transfer from incumbent WP services and emphasizing the e<sup>f</sup>ect of web–mobile payment relationship. To delineate the web–mobile payment relationship, we adopt the concept of perceived entitativity, which denotes users’ perception of a source WP service and a target MP service being bonded together in a cohesive category that comprises perceived similarity and perceived business tie [9,10]. MP services are emerging technologies that are extended from incumbent WP ser vices [1]. Thus, trust transfer mechanism can take place in the web–- mobile payment transition process [3]. While trust transfer mechanism increasingly becomes an essential mechanism to build users’ trust in online environments, empirical evidence shows that trust transfer from incumbent web services to new mobile services may depend on the strength of web–mobile service relationship [11,12]. In the web–mobile payment transition context, it implies that even providers have invested a lot in maintaining a trustworthy image of WP services, it may no longer become a distinctive advantage for their extended MP services if users perceive a weak web–mobile payment relationship [1]. In fact, despite the successful implementation of MP from WP services, providers still struggle to propel users to trust their extended MP services. A possible reason may be that these <sup>fi</sup>rms fail to build a “close” relationship between WP and MP services. If the web–mobile payment relationship is weak, then trust transfer is unlikely to occur [2]. From this perspective, understanding the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived entitativity allows providers to manage trust transfer mechanism more e<sup>f</sup>ectively, and thereby optimize their return on investment.

Against these backdrops, our research objective is to investigate trust building in MP by considering a balanced cognitive-emotional trust perspective and the e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived entitativity. To achieve this objective, we <sup>fi</sup>rst refer to Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model and propose that cognitive trust and emotional trust are both important determinants of users’ intention to use MP services. Then, we build on Stewart’s [9,10] trust transfer theory and hypothesize that both cognitive trust and emotional trust in WP services can be transferred to MP services. Furthermore, perceived entitativity not only positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP, but also enhances the transfer of cognitive trust and emotional trust from WP to MP services.

Our work has several important contributions. First, researchers have begun to shed light on the topic of trust building in MP [13–16]. However, emotional trust building and trust transfer mechanism have not yet received adequate attention in academic IS journals. Our work is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate trust building in MP by considering a balanced cognitive-emotional trust perspective and incorporating trust transfer from incumbent WP services. Second, we point out the conceptual and operational inconsistencies of perceived entitativity in the extant literature. We re-conceptualize perceived entitativity as a second-order formative construct which comprises perceived similarity and perceived business tie. In view of perceived entitativity as web–- mobile payment relationship, we further examine its direct and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects in the trust transfer process from WP to MP services. Our work enhances our understanding of web–mobile payment relationship that shapes trust building in MP and also enriches trust transfer theory by explicating the complex roles of perceived entitativity.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst review prior studies on trust building in MP and specify key research gaps. We then proceed to introduce trust-based acceptance model [5], trust transfer theory [9,10], and perceived entitativity [9,10], which are the theoretical foundation of this study.

## 2.1. Trust building in MP

MP services can be generally classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into remote payment and proximity payment. In remote payment, payment activities are conducted by connecting to remote payment servers [4]. This MP solution facilitates transactions that can be performed remotely and are in dependent of users’ locations. For example, users may use remote payment to perform credit card repay and check out on e-commerce websites. In proximity payment, payment is conducted by using mobile devices on the spot through near-<sup>fi</sup>eld communication (NFC) or quick response code (QRC) technologies [4]. For example, users may use proximity payment to pay for vending machine bills, subway or public bus fees.

A majority of studies have been carried out to examine trust building in MP (see Appendix A). There studies uncover three major trust building mechanisms: knowledge-based mechanism, institutionbased mechanism, and trust transfer mechanism. Knowledge-based mechanism denotes that users’ trust in new MP services is derived from their prior satisfactory and bene<sup>fi</sup>cial interaction experience with MP services. For instance, Khalilzadeh et al. [13] and Lin [17] suggested that perceived security, relative advantage, and perceived ease-of-use positively a<sup>f</sup>ect trust in MP. Similarly, Zhou [4,18,19] argued that information quality, system quality, and service quality are important determinants of trust in MP. Institution-based mechanism focuses on building users’ trust in MP through a set of institutional assurances. For instance, Kim et al. [20] and Luo et al. [15] contended that structural assurance plays a key role in formulating users’ trust in MP. In a similar view, Oliveira et al. [21] and Zhou [18,19] reported that structural assurance and <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation exert in<sup>fl</sup>uences on users’ trust in MP. Trust transfer mechanism means that users’ trust in MP can be transferred from incumbent reputable WP services that have certain relationship with new MP services. For instance, Cao et al. [2] and Lu et al. [1] proposed that users’ trust in WP has a positive association with their trust in MP. Summarizing the three streams of research, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a very few of studies have been directed toward investigating users’ trust building in MP in consideration of emotional trust and trust transfer from incumbent WP services.

## 2.2. Trust-based adoption model

The IS research community has conceptualized trust in a variety of ways, such as (1) trusting beliefs, which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a trustor’s rational expectations of a trustee’s integrity, benevolence, and competence [22–26]; (2) trusting attitudes, which mean the trustor’s feelings of security and comfort [6–8]; (3) trusting intentions, which capture the trustor’s intention to rely on the trustee for certain behaviors [27,28]. Following these conceptualizations, Komiak and Benbasat [5] developed a trust-based adoption model which incorporates three types of trust to understand users’ intention to use online recommendation agents: cognitive trust, emotional trust, and intention to use. Cognitive trust, which is conceptualized as trusting beliefs, pertains to users’ rational assessments of a trustee’s attributes that can be relied on. Emotional trust, as a form of trusting attitude, refers to users emotional reactions of security and assure toward relying on the trustee. Intention to use is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as users’ trusting intention to rely on the trustee for a certain behavior in a given situation.

According to Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model, emotional trust is essentially di<sup>f</sup>erent from cognitive trust. First, cognitive trust captures users’ speci<sup>fi</sup>c trusting beliefs regarding the trustee’s trustworthiness attributes [28]. High levels of cognitive trust will be established if users are able to identify su<sup>fi</sup>cient reasons to trust. Emotional trust captures users’ evaluation of emotional feelings about relying on the trustee [6]. High levels of emotional trust are developed based on emotion, faith, and a<sup>f</sup>ective reactions toward the trustee [29]. Second, cognitive trust allows users to do things according to the rational assessment of a trustee’s integrity and reliability, whereas emotional trust enables them to feel comfortable and secure toward relying on the trustee even in the absence of available support [7]. Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model emphasizes that cognitive trust (i.e., trusting belief) a<sup>f</sup>ects emotional trust (i.e., trusting attitude) and subsequently formulates intention to use (i.e., trusting intention).

## 2.3. Trust transfer theory

Trust transfer theory indicates that users’ trust toward a trusted source object can be transferred to a relatively unknown target object, under the condition that the target object is found to be associated with the trusted source object [9]. According to the theory, <sup>fi</sup>ve categories of factors are important in understanding the trust transfer mechanism: trust in the source, source–target relationship, trust in the target, trusting intention toward the target, and boundary condition (see Fig. 1). When users decide whether to trust in a relatively unknown target object or not, they may derive from existing trust in a known source object that has a certain relationship with the unknown target object [10]. If users trusts the known source object and identi<sup>fi</sup>es a close source–target relationship, then trust transfer process is likely to occur, and the subsequent trusting intention will be formed [3].

Previous studies on trust transfer can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two major streams: research on multiple trusted sources and research on a single trusted source (see Appendix B). The first stream emphasizes that trust in the target object can be established through trust transfer from dif ferent trusted source object [28,30–36]. An important underlying assumption in this stream is that users believe strong relationships exist between the target object and various source objects. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stream show that scholars primarily identi<sup>fi</sup>ed di<sup>f</sup>erent trusted source objects, and few studies focused on the source–target relationship. Note that trust transfer is unlikely to occur if the target–source relationship is weak. Stewart [9, p.6] contended that trust transfer “is likely to occur derived from a communication process where the proof source and the unknown target place impacts on the trustor, or according to a categorization process where the perceptions of source–- target relationship prompts transfer”. The second research stream investigates how trust in the target may be e<sup>f</sup>ectively transferred from one speci<sup>fi</sup>c trusted source object, with additional focus on source–- target relationship [1,3,9,11,12,37–40]. In this research stream, trust in the source, internal relationship factors such as perceived similarity, consistent function, and external relationship factors such as perceived integration, perceived business tie, degree of association, and hyperlink a<sup>f</sup>ordance are regarded as important antecedents of trust in the target.

To further explicate trust transfer mechanism, recent research has focused on the importance of testing boundary conditions in conjunction with trust in the source, source–target relationships, trust in the target, and trusting intention toward the target. Sia et al. [28] showed empirical evidence which suggests that trust transfer mechanism is often condition-speci<sup>fi</sup>c. They conducted a laboratory study to investigate whether online trust is built similarly for users in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures through the same set of web strategies. They found that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of portal a<sup>fi</sup>liation on trust perceptions is stronger in the individualist culture, and peer consumer endorsement is stronger for subjects in the collectivist culture. Similar <sup>fi</sup>ndings are obtained by the trust transfer literature in which culture, purchasing experience, product type, a<sup>f</sup>ect infusion, and institutional mechanism are important boundary conditions to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of trust transfer mechanism [31–34,38]. Summarizing these studies, we further <sup>fi</sup>nd that (1) trust transfer is mainly examined from a cognitive trust perspective, while emotional trust is largely overlooked; and (2) most studies only investigate the direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of source–target relationship and disregard its possibility to be a type of boundary conditions.

## 2.4. Perceived entitativity

Perceived entitativity refers to the source–target relationship in which a source object and a target object are considered as bonded together in a cohesive category [41]. Although perceived entitativity is commonly employed to manifest the source–target relationship, its conceptualization remains inconsistent and diversi<sup>fi</sup>es into three streams (see Appendix C). The first stream suggests that perceived entitativity is characterized by the internal relationship and similarity among objects [30,42–45]. The second stream primarily employs per ceived entitativity to highlight the external relationship and tie among objects [3,46–49]. The third stream contends that perceived entitativity comprises both internal and external relationship factors [41,50–55]. To resolve the inconsistent understanding of perceived entitativity, this study re-conceptualizes perceived entitativity in the web–mobile payment transition context. We adopt Stewart’s [9,10] view to contend that perceived entitativity may be better treated as a multidimensional construct than a unidimensional one. We then de<sup>fi</sup>ne perceived entitativity as the web–mobile payment relationship, which denotes users perception of a source WP service and a target MP service being bonded together in a cohesive category that consists of perceived similarity and perceived business tie. Perceived similarity denotes the “internal” web–mobile payment relationship such that the two types of payment services have similar innate features [3,9]. Perceived business tie denotes the “external” web–mobile payment relationship in terms of sharing typical external cues [3,9].

We propose the multidimensionality of perceived entitativity based on three key concerns. First, this point of view helps to clarify the concept of perceived entitativity. The <sup>fi</sup>rst research stream shows that a variety of “internal similarities” may create perceived entitativity, while the second research stream points out that “external ties” are also important component of perceived entitativity. Thus, using the dimensions of perceived similarity and perceived business tie can help to consolidate the distinct conceptualizations of perceived entitativity in the literature. Second, the proposition of two dimensions of perceived entitativity is supported by the third research stream, where “internal similarities” and “external ties” are both regarded as essential components of perceived entitativity. Third, users are likely to associate new MP services with incumbent WP services in the web–mobile payment transition context. They tend to develop an overall perception of perceived entitativity by comparing the internal functional similarities (e.g., similar operation interface and familiar function steps) and external business ties (e.g., hyperlink a<sup>f</sup>ordance and service provider a<sup>fi</sup>liation) of the two payment services.

## 3. Research model and hypothese

Fig. 2 presents the research model and hypotheses. Building on Stewart’s [9,10] trust transfer theory and Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model, we operationalize trust in source as cognitive trust and emotional trust in WP, trust in target as cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP; source–target relationship and boundary condition as perceived entitativity; and trusting intention toward target as intention to use MP. We propose that cognitive trust and emotional trust can be transferred from WP to MP services, which in turn formulate users intention to use MP services. Furthermore, we argue that perceived entitativity may exert dual e<sup>f</sup>ects in the trust transfer process: direct and moderating. The direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived entitativity indicates that high levels of perceived entitativity may increase cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP directly. The moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect shows that perceived entitativity may strengths the transfer of cognitive trust and emotional trust from WP to MP services.

## 3.1. Core predictors of intention to use

Trust transfer theory suggests that trust in the target exerts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ect on trusting intention toward the target [9,10]. In this study, we examine two types of trust in the target: cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP. Cognitive trust in MP refers to users’ trusting beliefs that MP services have attributes that they can rely on [56,57]. Prior research suggests that transactions conducted in mobile networks and devices are more vulnerable than those conducted in traditional online environments [1] and thus present greater uncertainty and risk [4]. High levels of cognitive trust in MP mean that users believe the capability of MP to o<sup>f</sup>er truthful and authentic <sup>fi</sup>nancial services. Such trusting beliefs help users mitigate risks and assure the possibility of payment success when transacting online business [15]. As a result, users’ intention to use MP services will be increased. Thus, we hy pothesize the following:

## H1. Cognitive trust in MP is positively related to intention to use MP.

Emotional trust in MP refers to users’ trusting attitude that relying on MP for conducting payment is secure and comfortable [6,8]. Emotional trust captures users’ emotional feelings about relying on MP services. According to Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model, trusting attitude toward a behavior (e.g., emotional trust) can predict users’ trusting intention to perform the behavior (e.g., intention to use). In other words, if users feel assured and comfortable toward depending on MP services to pay for goods and bills, then they are likely to use MP services [7,8]. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

## H2. Emotional trust in MP is positively related to intention to use MP.

In line with prior research [6–8], emotional trust may be developed based on users’ cognitive trust. High levels of cognitive trust mean that users believe they can receive trustworthy <sup>fi</sup>nancial services from MP. Users with these cognitive beliefs are likely to invoke strong feelings of comfort and security about using mobile devices to conduct payment [6]. By contrast, low levels of cognitive trust suggest that users doubt if MP can provide promised services or care about their interest, thus leading to uncomfortable and uncertain feelings of using MP services. This idea is also consistent with Komiak and Benbasat’s [5] trust-based adoption model, which posits that trusting beliefs (e.g., cognitive trust) exert signi<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on trusting attitudes (e.g., emotional trust). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3. Cognitive trust in MP is positively related to emotional trust in MP.

## 3.2. Cognitive trust and emotional trust transfer

Cognitive trust in WP refers to users trusting beliefs of WP services capability to provide reliable and accurate <sup>fi</sup>nancial services [56,57]. It captures users’ cognitive beliefs of integrity, benevolence, and competence toward WP services. Cognitive trust transfer is a unique categorization process [9]. Categorization theory proposes that users’ cog nitive beliefs toward a source object could be extended to their cognitive beliefs toward a target object through category-based processing [39]. As a trust-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variant of categorization theory, Stewart [9] developed trust transfer theory in which cognitive beliefs are contextualized as cognitive trust. Trust transfer theory posits that users’ cognitive trust toward a source object can be transferred to cognitive trust toward a target object [9]. In the web–mobile service transition context, users are likely to view incumbent WP services as a source object and new MP services as a target object [2]. Users’ trusting beliefs are likely to be transferred from incumbent WP services to new MP services. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

## H4. Cognitive trust in WP is positively related to cognitive trust in MP.

Emotional trust in WP refers to users’ trusting attitudes that relying on WP to conduct payment is secure and comfortable [6,8]. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects users’ emotional feelings of comfort and security toward WP services. Emotional trust transfer is a unique emotion generalization process [6]. According to categorization theory, emotion generalization denotes that users’ emotional feelings toward a source object can be spread to their emotional feelings toward a target object through category-based processing [42]. Emotion generalization is a prevalent and common existence to guide users’ perceptions and behaviors [58]. A similar emotion generalization process may take place in the web–mobile payment transition context. When users hold the feelings of comfort and security toward hinging on WP services, these favorable emotional feelings may be transferred to related MP services. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H5. Emotional trust in WP is positively related to emotional trust in MP.

## 3.3. Direct efect of perceived entitativity

Trust transfer theory posits that source target relationship has a direct e<sup>f</sup>ect on users’ trust in the target [9,10]. In this study, we examine one type of source–target relationship: perceived entitativity. Perceived entitativity refers to the overall evaluation of the web–mobile payment relationship, which causes users to perceive the two types of payment services as being bonded together in one category [9,10]. It captures users’ assessment of the internal similarity and external business tie between incumbent WP services and new MP services [3,30,49]. Previous studies on trust transfer suggest that when users perceive a target object as sharing similar characteristics and external ties with a trusted source object, then they are more likely to categorize the two objects into one category and establish trusting beliefs in the target [42,49]. In the web–mobile payment transition context, users holding high levels of perceived entitativity will also categorize trusted WP services and new MP services in a cohesive category, thereby establishing similar trusting beliefs in the MP services. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

## H6. Perceived entitativity is positively related to cognitive trust in MP.

When interacting with new MP services, users are usually uncertain whether MP services can provide them reliable and secure <sup>fi</sup>nancial services [15]. High levels of perceived entitativity mean that users see a series of internal similarities and external ties between trusted WP services and new MP services [46,47]. These internal and external relationship cues with trusted WP services can help users alleviate transaction uncertainty when conducting payment services in unfamiliar mobile environments, thereby invoking emotional feelings of comfort and security toward new MP services [3]. In other words, perceived entitativity will invoke users’ emotional feelings of comfort and security about new MP services if such payment services are operated by the same company (i.e., high perceived business tie) and has a similar operation interface (i.e., high perceived similarity) with trusted WP services. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H7. Perceived entitativity is positively related to emotional trust in MP.

## 3.4. Moderating efect of perceived entitativity

Trust transfer theory suggests that source–target relationship may moderate the relationship between trust in the source and trust in the target [10]. From a trust transfer perspective, Stewart [10, p.187] argued that “a perceived relationship between two organizations may lead to adjustments in trust assessments toward both organizations”, and “when the relationships among organization members are perceived to be higher, this e<sup>f</sup>ect is likely to be stronger”. Chen et al. [38, p. 264] further suggested that “trust transfer is a dynamic process which operates di<sup>f</sup>erently under di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of boundary conditions (e.g., di<sup>f</sup>erent levels of source–target relationship)”. Trust transfer from a trusted source object to a new target object is a typical form of adjustments in trust judgments between the two objects [3,38]. Thus, source–target relationship is expected to enhance trust transfer from the source object to the target object.

Categorization theory also supports the view that source–target relationship may moderate the relationship between trust in the source and trust in the target [39,42]. According to categorization theory, users’ cognitive beliefs and emotional feelings can be transferred from a source object to a target object depending on the strength of source–- target relationship [39]. When faced with an evaluative task of a new target object, users <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to use a certain source category in memory to incorporate the target object on the amount of source–target relationship cues [59]. If they identify a close relationship between the source category and the target object, then they will be more likely to transfer cognitive beliefs and emotional feelings from the source category to the target object; otherwise, these associated cognitive beliefs and emotional feelings of the source category are less likely to be transferred to the target object [60]. In viewing cognitive beliefs and emotional feelings as cognitive trust and emotional trust, we also expect that source–target relationship determines the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of cognitive trust and emotional trust transfer from a source object to a target object. If users perceive high levels of source–target relationship, they are likely to view the two objects in a category and induce trust transfer. On the contrary, if users perceive low levels of source–target relationship, they tend to treat the two objects in distinct categories and then are unlikely to evoke trust transfer.

In the web–mobile payment transition context, high levels of perceived entitativity mean that users perceive a strong web–mobile payment relationship and categorize the two types of payment services in a cohesive category [3]. Such entitativity allows users to rely on internal similarity cues, e.g., proximate function steps, similar operation interfaces, and external business tie cues, e.g., hyperlink a<sup>f</sup>ordance, service provider recommendation, to associate new MP services with incum bent reputable WP services [2,12]. As a result, users’ cognitive trust and emotional trust toward using WP services are more easily transferred to new MP services. On the contrary, low levels of perceived entitativity denotes that users’ perceptions of similarity and business tie between WP and MP services are weak; and thus divide the two types of payment services into distinct categories [9,10]. Under this situation, users cognitive trust and emotional trust transfer from WP to MP services is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to take place. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H8. Perceived entitativity positively moderates the relationship between cognitive trust in WP and cognitive trust in MP.

H9. Perceived entitativity positively moderates the relationship between emotional trust in WP and emotional trust in MP.

## 4. Research method

We conducted an online survey in China to empirically validate our research model. Alipay, a popular Chinese third-party online payment platform, was employed as our research context. Founded in February 2004, Alipay enabled users to adopt wired terminals like desk compu ters to conduct payment during online transactions. Recent report suggested that Alipay had nearly 1 billion registered users and accounted for 54 % transaction value share in the Chinese online payment market. To extend their service scope, Alipay had released its MP service called Alipay Wallet as a separate brand in November 2013. These observations indicated that Alipay was an appropriate context for the current research.

## 4.1. Data collection

Our target population was current Alipay users. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we collected survey data among students and faculty members in several Chinese universities. The sample choice was made for two primary reasons. First, university students and faculty members have a higher possibility of using Alipay services than others because the majority of them have personal computers and often purchase online [61]. Second, they represent a large percent of high-education users, who tend to exhibit a great tendency of novelty seeking and using innovative technologies (e.g., Alipay Wallet services) [62]. We sent messages to all participants to invite them to attend this online survey. A concise description of the research objectives and the URL of the questionnaire were provided in the invitation messages.

We explicitly clari<sup>fi</sup>ed that “web payment service is a traditional online payment method which allows users to adopt wired devices like desk computers to conduct payment for goods, bills, and services” and “mobile payment service is a new payment method which allows users to use wireless devices like smartphones to conduct payment for goods, bills, and services” to ensure that respondents would have a clear understanding of WP and MP services. Meanwhile, we highlighted that the research subject of WP and MP services were Alipay and Alipay Wallet, respectively. Since this study examined users’ trust transfer and MP adoption in the web–mo bile payment transition context, the respondents should have certain WP and limited MP usage experiences. We implemented the following strategies taken from the trust transfer literature to address these issues [1,3]: (1) we used screening questions such as “have you ever used Alipay services” and “how long have you used Alipay services” to record respondents’ usage experience of WP services; (2) we collected the demographics information pertaining to usage experience, usage frequency, and transaction value of MP services to <sup>fi</sup>lter valid respondents; and (3) we adopted the survey website’s technical options to drop out repetitive submissions from the same Internet Protocol (IP) addresses and to eliminate abnormal questionnaires that were taken usually fast to our pilot test.

A total of 1500 questionnaires were distributed, and 604 completed questionnaires were obtained. Among the 604 completed questionnaires, 13 submissions were repetitive, and 16 submissions were taken usually fast to our pilot test. We dropped out the 29 invalid submissions, and there were 575 questionnaires remaining. Furthermore, when asked for Alipay usage experience, 23 people indicated that they had little experience in using Alipay services were eliminated, leaving 552 questionnaires. Finally, given that our target population was current Alipay users with little Alipay Wallet usage experience, we removed 61 respondents with prior–Alipay–Wallet usage experience and remained 491 respondents with non–Alipay–Wallet usage experience in the hypotheses testing stage. Table D1 presents the demographics of the respondents. Among the 491 respondents, 41.3 % were females, 91.2 % aged at 40 or below, and 78.4 % held bachelor’s degrees. All respondents had experience in using Alipay services and non-experience in using Alipay Wallet services.

## 4.2. Data measures

The majority of constructs were measured by adapting previously validated scales using a 7-point Likert scale (see Appendix E). To determine which constructs were re<sup>fl</sup>ective and which ones were formative, we followed the guidelines suggested by Jarvis et al. [63] and Petter et al. [64]: direction of causality between construct and indicators, interchangeability of the indicators, covariation among the indicators, and nomological net of the construct indicators. Using these guidelines, we operationalized all variables as <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs, except for perceived entitativity. Perceived entitativity was treated as a second-order formative construct with perceived business tie and perceived similarity dimensions, as de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the trust transfer literature [9].

Perceived entitativity was a relatively new construct without readily available scale. The scales for perceived entitativity were developed by following IS instrument development procedures, including item generation and scale development (see Appendix F). At the stage of item generation, we aimed to identify the initial pools of items for the proposed perceived business tie and perceived similarity dimensions of perceived entitativity from the trust transfer literature. Stewart [9,10] had developed well-established scales for perceived business tie and perceived similarity. These items were further adapted by Wang et al. [3] to measure web–mobile service relationship in the web–mobile service transition context. Based on Stewart’s [9,10] and Wang et al.’s [3] work, we developed a six-item scale for perceived entitativity that tapped its two dimensions: perceived business tie and perceived similarity. The items were worded to explicitly highlight the web–mobile payment relationship. At the stage of scale development, a panel of judges consisting of two associate professors and two doctoral students were asked to review these items to ensure content validity. All judges were provided with a three-part questionnaire: (1) part 1 presented the six items of perceived entitativity and its dimensions, (2) part 2 listed the conceptual and operational de<sup>fi</sup>nition of perceived business tie and perceived similarity, and (3) part 3 was a blank section to gather the judges’ feedback and suggestions regarding the scales. They were asked to carefully read these scales and sorted them into their intended dimensions. As suggested by Hoehle and Venkatesh [65], we asked the judges to rate how well each item was in relation to its intended dimension with a 5-point Likert scale from “1 = not at all” $\mathbf { t o } ~ ^ { \alpha } 5 ~ =$ completely”. Scales with lower scores were considered to have poorer content validity for their intended dimensions. Based on sorting results and judges’ feedback, we revised scales with unsatisfactory scores to improve their content validity.

The scales for cognitive trust in WP and MP were adapted from Lu et al. [1], the scales for emotional trust in WP and MP were adapted from Komiak and Benbasat [5], and the scales for intention to use MP were adapted from Lu et al. [1]. We also included demographic variables, i.e., gender, age, education, income, and WP experience, as control variables. Gender, age, education, income, and experience are important demographic variables that have been shown to a<sup>f</sup>ect users’ perceptions and usage intentions toward new technologies [66–69] and innovative MP services [14,70]. Recent studies also include demo graphic variables, such as age, gender, experience, as control variables when investigating trust transfer from a proof source to a target in online environments [38]. WP experience was chosen because a majority of respondents had never used MP services before or had limited MP usage experience. WP experience, serving as “indirect” MP usage experience, may have a potential role in MP adoption decisions [1].

To collect data from Chinese users, a professional translator translated the original English questionnaire to Chinese, and then two IS doctoral students translated it back to English. We discussed and resolved any inconsistencies in this process to assure the translation quality between the English and Chinese versions. In addition, we conducted a pilot study with a convenient sample of 20 online users to gather feedback on the questionnaire. Important feedback, including the wording of several questions and the layout, helped us improve the <sup>fi</sup>nal version of the questionnaire.

## 4.3. Common method variance

Multiple statistical techniques were employed to check common method variance (CMV). First, we applied Harman’s one-factor test on the measures [71]. The exploratory analysis accounted for 66 % of the variance, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor only explained 21 % of the variance, thereby indicating minimal concerns for CMV. Second, we followed

Kock and Lynn’s [72] full collinearity test to detect CMV. Table D2 shows that the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIFs) of vertical collinearity ranged from 1.60 to 2.12 and the VIFs of lateral collinearity ranged from 1.60 to 2.16. As none of the observed VIFs surpassed the 3.3 threshold, thereby providing evidence for minimal collinearity and CMV [73]. Third, we employed Malhotra et al.’s [74] marker variable technique and collected fashion consciousness as a marker variable in the survey. As expected, the average correlation between fashion consciousness and other principal variables was statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \beta = 0 . 0 7 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ , thereby con<sup>fi</sup>rming that CMV was unlikely to be a serious concern.

## 5. Data anlysis and results

We applied partial least squares (PLS) for data analysis and hypotheses testing. PLS is regarded as a suitable choice for predictionoriented models with formative constructs and their interactions [75], which is the case of the current study. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c software package was Smart-PLS 3.0 using the standard bootstrap resampling procedure with 1000 resamples [76]. We employed the two-step analytical pro cedure to analyze the measurement and structural models [76].

## 5.1. Measurement model

We assessed convergent validity and discriminant validity to ex amine the measurement model of <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs. Convergent validity was established with the average variance extracted $( \mathsf { A V E } > 0 . 5 )$ , composite reliability $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathbf { C R } > 0 . 7 ) _ { : } } \end{array}$ , and Cronbach’s alpha $( \mathrm { C A } > 0 . 7 )$ [77]. Discriminant validity was demonstrated when (1) item loadings should be higher on their intended construct than on other constructs, (2) the square root of each construct’s AVE should exceed the correlations between the construct and others, and (3) Heterotrait–Monotrait (HTMT) ratio con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval should not include 1 [76]. Tables D3, D4, and D5 show that our scales had su<sup>fi</sup>- cient convergent validity and discriminant validity. We assessed indicator weights, collinearity, and loadings to examine the measurement model of second-order formative constructs [64]. Table D6 shows that all indicator weights and loadings of perceived entitativity were statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Furthermore, the VIFs were smaller than the suggested cut-o<sup>f</sup> value 3.3, thereby indicating minimal concerns for collinearity [64].

## 5.3. Post-hoc analysis

To better understand the impact of perceived entitativity in the trust transfer mechanism, we further conducted hierarchical regression analyses with PLS by splitting perceived entitativity into two dimensions of perceived business tie and perceived similarity. Table D8 shows the results of post-hoc analysis. Overall, the analyses had two important <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, perceived business tie and perceived similarity played key roles in building cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP. Cognitive trust in MP was determined by cognitive trust in WP, per ceived business tie, and perceived similarity. Emotional trust in MP was predicted by emotional trust in WP, perceived business tie, and cognitive trust in MP. Furthermore, perceived business tie and perceived similarity positively moderated the relationship between cognitive trust in WP and cognitive trust in MP, as well as the relationship between emotional trust in WP and emotional trust in MP. Second, perceived business tie and perceived similarity placed di<sup>f</sup>erent emphases on di<sup>f</sup>erent trust building. Perceived similarity (i.e., “internal relationship”) played a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the formation of cognitive trust in MP, while perceived business tie (i.e., “external relationship”) ex erted a stronger impact on the formation of emotional trust in MP.

An explanation for the <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be that cognitive trust and emotional trust are derived from di<sup>f</sup>erent appraisal processes. As mentioned earlier, cognitive trust is rooted in users’ conscious calculation that MP has the capability to provide reliable and accurate <sup>fi</sup>nancial services [56,57]. High levels of perceived similarity help users establish the web–mobile payment relationship via internal cues, such as similar operation interfaces and familiar function steps $[ 3 , 1 2 ]$ . Such internal familiarity may play a more important role in the formation of cognitive trust in that it provides users with functional support and knowledge to use the corresponding MP e<sup>fi</sup>ciently, with great ease, and with few errors [5]. This result is in line with that of Wang et al.’s [8] study, in which functional support strategies (i.e., explanation facilities) are mainly found to enhance users’ cognitive trust rather than emotional trust in the context of online recommendation agents. In contrast to cognitive trust, emotional trust captures the a<sup>f</sup>ective bonds and feelings between a consumer and a MP service [6,8]. It may be rooted in users prior interaction with the MP service or emotional reactions to the service. Perceived business tie allows users to rely on external cues, e.g., hyperlink a<sup>f</sup>ordance and service provider a<sup>fi</sup>liations, to associate MP services with reputable WP services [3,40]. These external ties may exert more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the formation of emotional trust by o<sup>f</sup>ering emotional support to induce emotion generalization. For example, after completing several satisfactory shopping tasks with TaoBao and its WP service (i.e., Alipay), users may develop favorable cognitive trust and emotional trust. If TaoBao recommends users to adopt its newly introduced MP service, such a recommendation can quickly release a<sup>f</sup>ective information given the high business tie between the WP and MP services. However, the recommendation o<sup>f</sup>ers limited functional knowledge to assist users to use the new MP quickly and accurately. Thus, they may feel comfortable and secure about relying on the MP via emotion generalization rather than making a rational calculation regarding that the MP has capabilities to o<sup>f</sup>er safe and secure <sup>fi</sup>nancial services.

Furthermore, we note that four types of trust transfer processes may exist in the web–mobile payment transition context: (I) cognitive trust in WP → cognitive trust in MP; (II) emotional trust in WP → emotional trust in MP; (III) cognitive trust in WP → emotional trust in MP; and (IV) emotional trust in WP → cognitive trust in MP. We also ran the model by incorporating all the four types of trust transfer. The results showed that the former two relationships (I: $\beta = 0 . 3 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ;$ II: $\beta = 0 . 1 2 , ~ p < ~ 0 . 0 1 )$ were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, whereas the latter two relationships were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( { \mathrm { I I I } } { \mathrm { : } } \beta = 0 . 0 4 , p \ > \ 0 . 0 5 { \mathrm { ; } } \ { \mathrm { I V } } { \mathrm { : } } \beta = 0 . 0 1$ , $\textstyle p \ > \ \mathbf { 0 . 0 5 } )$ . These results are consistent with the trust transfer theory and categorization theory, which propose that (I) users’ cognitive trust (i.e., cognitive beliefs) in the source can be extended to cognitive trust in the related target; and (II) emotional trust $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , emotional feelings) in the source can be transferred to emotional trust in the target [9,10,39]. On the contrary, there is little theoretical support regarding that (III) cognitive trust in the source may in<sup>fl</sup>uence emotional trust in the target, and (IV) emotional trust in the source has an e<sup>f</sup>ect on cognitive trust in the target.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

Our primary objective is to achieve an in-depth understanding of what steps can be implemented to increase consumer trust and en courage consumer adoption of MP services. In particular, we examine trust transfer mechanism with a balanced cognitive–emotional trust perspective and investigate the direct and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived entitativity. Our empirical results show that both cognitive trust and emotional trust can be transferred from WP to MP services, which further facilitate users’ intention to use MP. This <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates that users who trust in WP are likely to trust in MP and have high likelihood of using MP services. Our empirical results also show that high levels of perceived entitativity not only lead to increased cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP, but also enhance the transfer of cognitive trust and emotional trust from WP to MP services. The direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of per ceived entitativity shows that when users can perceive a strong relationship between WP and MP services, they are likely to develop fa vorable trusting beliefs and trusting attitude about relying on mobile devices to conduct payment, given their initial trust in WP services. Meanwhile, the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect suggests that if users hold high levels of perceived entitativity between WP and MP services, then existing cognitive trust and emotional trust in WP services is likely to be ex tended to MP services easily.

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

This study has several key theoretical implications. First, our work enriches the MP literature by considering trust transfer mechanism. The extant MP literature mainly employs knowledge-based mechanism and institution-based mechanism to build users’ trust in MP. However, only a handful of studies have adopted trust transfer mechanism to build users’ trust in MP. One possible explanation is that most studies view MP services as emerging technology innovations in the mobile domain and largely neglect its relationship with incumbent WP services. Sun et al. [[12], p.471] posited that “the phenomenon of web–mobile ser vice transition has not been recognized and examined for a long time”. Our work addresses this call by incorporating trust transfer from in cumbent WP service to build users’ trust in new MP services in the web–mobile service transition context.

Second, our work extends trust transfer theory with a balanced cognitive–emotional trust perspective. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that aside from cognitive trust, users emotional trust can also be transferred from incumbent WP services to new MP services. The <sup>fi</sup>nding is an important contribution to the trust transfer literature. The trust transfer literature has documented that users’ cognitive trust will be transferred from a trusted source to a relatively unknown target [3,9,10,28]. However, to the best of our knowledge, limited research has examined whether emotional trust can also be transferred from a trusted source to a new target, despite academic suggestions that trust should involve an in terplay of both cognitive trust and emotional trust [5,6,8]. Our study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to address this theoretical gap in the trust transfer literature by theorizing and empirically testing emotional trust transfer in the web mobile payment transition context.

Third, our work advances trust transfer theory by identifying the direct and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived entitativity in the trust transfer process. To explicate what perceived entitativity is and resolve its inconsistent understanding in prior studies, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to re-conceptualize it with two important dimensions: perceived similarity and perceived business tie. The direct e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived entitativity enriches the trust transfer literature by showing that high levels of source–target relationship can lead to increased cognitive trust and emotional trust in target directly. The moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect of perceived entitativity adds to the trust transfer literature by addressing the call to identify important boundary conditions for the trust transfer process from the source to the target. With this new empirical insight, our work provides a plausible explanation for the prior <sup>fi</sup>nding that trust in the target has to be transferred from a closely relevant trusted source [28,32,79]. Our work demonstrates that trust transfer process is more likely to take place when users hold high levels of perceived entitativity between the source and the target.

Fourth, our work contributes to trust transfer theory by di<sup>f</sup>erent dimensions of perceived entitativity placed di<sup>f</sup>erent emphasis in dif ferent trust transfer process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the post-hoc analysis shows that perceived similarity (i.e., internal source–target relationship) exerts a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence in the cognitive trust transfer process, while perceived business tie (i.e., external source–target relationship) plays a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the emotional trust transfer process. While facilitating cognitive trust transfer, providers should focus on establishing the source–target relationship via internal cues, such as proximate function interfaces and similar operation steps. Such internal similarity increases users’ cognitive trust by o<sup>f</sup>ering them knowledge and functional supports to evaluate the new target accurately and e<sup>fi</sup>ciently [3,12]. On the contrary, when enhancing emotional trust transfer, providers may pay more attention to external relationship cues, e.g., hyperlink a<sup>f</sup>ordance, service recommendations, and provider a<sup>fi</sup>liations. These external ties promote emotion generation quickly in which users’ emotional trust associated with a trusted source can spread to a closely relevant target [3,11,40].

## 6.2. Managerial implications

This study o<sup>f</sup>ers several important managerial implications. Prior research identi<sup>fi</sup>es trust as a critical issue relevant to users’ adoption of MP services [1,15,80]. Thus, for many MP service providers, a traditional strategy would be to develop users’ cognitive trust, such as by providing many <sup>fi</sup>nancial features (i.e., perceived usefulness) and easy navigation/operation interfaces (i.e., perceived ease of use) or addressing security issues in mobile contexts. Our study recommends to MP service providers that they should focus not only on how users perceive MP (cognitive trust) but also on how they actually feel about it (emotional trust). In essence, our results suggest the greater importance of emotional trust in MP. To promote the adoption of MP, practitioners should at least <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to monitor the level of emotional trust among existing and potential users. The next step would be to <sup>fi</sup>nd e<sup>f</sup>ective approaches to increase users’ emotional trust in MP. Based on our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, this study proposes a possible strategy: trust transfer from WP.

The trust transfer strategy suggests that harnessing the power of existing trust in WP to promote MP adoption is helpful. Our empirical results suggest the prominence of prompting users to relate reputable WP services when they decide to use MP services. Thus, the trust transfer process can be activated. In this process, perceived entitativity may be a critical factor. The direct and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived entitativity imply that practitioners should remember to enhance perceived similarity and perceived business tie between WP and MP ser vices. They are recommended to allocate trust-building resources strategically according to such entitativity levels. They may initially conduct a survey to assess users’ perception of entitativity. If users hold low levels of perceived entitativity, MP practitioners should allot in creased resources to improve the web–mobile payment relationship and facilitate the transference of trust. For instance, MP service providers should create similar functions and operation interfaces to increase internal similarity or tag explicit labels to show external business tie between their MP services and reputable WP services. By contrast, if users hold high levels of perceived entitativity, then their cognitive trust and emotional trust will be transferred from WP to MP services easily. In this context, providers can manage the trust transfer process from WP to MP services e<sup>f</sup>ectively, thereby optimizing their return on investment in trust building in MP.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations and o<sup>f</sup>ers directions for future research. First, we recruit a relatively restricted sample of people from local universities to participate in our research. Although the respondents are users with certain experience of using WP services, future research may invite subjects from a more diverse population to increase the generalizability of the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, this study only considers perceived entitativity as a boundary condition factor. Boundary condition factors are important in the trust transfer process because they a<sup>f</sup>ect the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of the trust transfer mechanism. Thus, future research could examine more boundary condition factors and enrich trust transfer theory. Third, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that 37 % of the variance in intention to use MP is explained in the model. This suggests that there may be other important predictors overlooked, such as <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation, perceived controllability, perceived ease-of-use, perceive usefulness, and perceived risks. Future studies may incorporate the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these factors into the research model to better understand MP adoption. Forth, our study employs trust transfer theory and categorization theory to illustrate the cognitive and emotional trust transfer process. Although the two theoretical lenses are commonly employed to delineate the cognitive trust transfer process in the existing literature [39,42], they have not been widely adopted to uncover the emotional trust transfer process. Other than trust transfer theory and categorization theory, future research may also consider emotional theories such as mood congruence theory as a potential theoretical lens to further explore the emotional trust transfer process.

## 6.4. Conclusions

This study enhances our understanding of trust transfer mechanism in trust building in MP by introducing a balanced cognitive–emotional trust perspective and by examining the direct and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of perceived entitativity. Our empirical results show that both cognitive trust and emotional trust can be transferred from WP to MP services. Furthermore, high levels of perceived entitativity not only lead to increased cognitive trust and emotional trust in MP but also act as an important boundary condition that enhances the transfer of cognitive trust and emotional trust from WP to MP services. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings serve as a starting point for researchers to gain improved understanding of trust transfer mechanism and trust building in MP.
