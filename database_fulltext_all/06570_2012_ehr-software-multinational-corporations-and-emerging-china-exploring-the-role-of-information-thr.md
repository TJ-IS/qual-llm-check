# eHR software, multinational corporations and emerging China: Exploring the role of information through a postcolonial lens

Jose M. Alcaraz <sup>a,b,</sup>⁎, Miquel Domènech <sup>c,1</sup>, Francisco Tirado <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Barna Business School, Ave. John F. Kennedy 34 (Naco), PC 10124. Santo Domingo, Dominican Republic

<sup>b</sup> IESE Business School (IRCO), Spain

<sup>c</sup> Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona, Departamento de Psicología Social, Facultat de Psicologia, Edificio B, 08193, Bellaterra, Spain

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 3 July 2010 Received in revised form 19 January 2012 Accepted 22 January 2012 Available online 18 February 2012

## a b s t r a c t

This paper seeks to offer an alternative account of Human Resources Information software (eHR) informed by a critical/postcolonial view on information systems. In so doing, it aims to explore the possibilities for managing people that information brings when Human Resources Management practices are transferred from “developed” to “developing” countries. The paper relies on several qualitative indepth interviews with renowned Chinese Human Resources experts in Shanghai, and the examination of diverse eHR software-related documentation and functionalities. Critical discourse analysis was used to examine these sources. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that eHR information systems bring new governance possibilities that support and expand the discipline of Human Resources Management. The use of eHR software in people management gives a new momentum and increased dominance to key Western-originated practices, such as HRbased performance management. Information brings new ordering options that facilitate the transferability, mobility and standardization of HR values, discourse and practices and, ultimately, the construction of a global “generi<sup>fi</sup>ed employee”. The paper offers a <sup>fi</sup>rst critical analysis of eHR software, showing the need to understand the relevancy of the informating power of these systems for a postcolonial critique of ICT. It offers a view of the “micro-processes” that facilitate organizational transfer from the multinational corporation headquarters to the subsidiaries and across countries. In so doing, it challenges mainstream deterministic assumptions and apolitical approaches to this technology.

## 1. Introduction

Surrounded by old colonial houses in the former ‘French Concession’, Human Resources (HR) specialist Hilary Johnson received us.<sup>3</sup> Transferred from multinational corporation Honeywell's of<sup>fi</sup>ces in Atlanta to Shanghai, her mission is that of supervising the implementation of HR practices in China. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally: “To spread a philosophy which is involuntary, which for us in the USA is already assimilated and is a part of what we do in our daily life, but which is not so here in China”. In the computers of her department an information system for HR management (PeopleSoft) silently stores and manages data on each employee: identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, salary, years worked in the company, skills, training received, performance evaluations…

In the same way, a multitude of organizations in China are importing and integrating “modern” practices of HR and, in the process, implementing various software tools for HR management (eHR systems). The body of “HR practices” – job descriptions, competences management, recruitment, career planning, performance, compensation management, etc. – are now closely supported by a myriad of specialized information software systems brought along with the management practices.

The reforms of Deng Xiaoping at the end of the 1970s are directly responsible for the entry of “modern” techniques of management in China (Fernández & Underwood, 2006). Since then, the spectacular economic growth of the country, along with foreign investment and the inclusion of China in the WTO, made changes possible in the legal framework of the country which eventually permitted the institutionalization of some formal practices of recruitment, differences in salaries, and evaluation systems of performance (Cheung, 2008). In this context, Chinese HR managers (especially those that worked in MNCs) began to look towards Western practices of HR and their eHR systems as models of inspiration and work guidance. After that, those responsible for state-owned enterprises started to join these trends. The scholarly literature undertaken by Chinese academics leaves no doubt about the importance that HR management has for Chinese companies (Hang-Yue, Chung-Ming, & Foley, 2008). It also highlights the arrival of HR information systems — <sup>fi</sup>rst, those software packages in charge of administrative processes and later those that operate with several HR functionalities and modules (Caplan, 2005).

So, both HR practices and eHR software are becoming prevalent across the planet, and integrated globally, particularly in multinational <sup>fi</sup>rms (MNCs) and their subsidiaries (Heikkilä & Smale, 2011; Smale, 2008). eHR systems (also called Human Resources Information Systems, “eHR solutions”, or e-HRM) are standardized stand-alone programs or packaged programs that are often part of enterprise resource planning software packages (ERPs). They burst onto the stage in the 1980s as a response to the management needs of Personnel and Human Resources departments (Gueutal, Stone, & Salas, 2005; Walker, 2001). Under the eHR abbreviation is included specialized software that covers speci<sup>fi</sup>c areas (for example, training management), integrated software (for example, management of recruitment, training, performance and compensation), applications for managers and employees such as Manager Self-Service and Employee Self-Service programs (in which line-managers have access to and feed in data of their employees, and employees have access to the system to make consultations), etc. It can be af<sup>fi</sup>rmed that in a good number of Western countries, in <sup>fi</sup>rms with more than 500 employees, eHR systems have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant presence (Hendrickson, 2003; Meade, 2002; Pelegín, 2004). A growth tendency also seems to be observable in the Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region (Thompson, Ghee, & Fedric, 2007) and in emerging China (Caplan, 2005; Watson, 2004). For authors such as Hendrickson (2003), this software has converted itself into “the backbone of the HR profession”.

There are hundreds of eHR system providers – among them SAP, Oracle, PeopleSoft, Meta4, Workbrain etc. – although only a dozen of them control the majority of the market of MNCs. For example, the data on more than 60 million employees worldwide are managed with SAP software (SAP, 2006). eHR systems have not just speeded up and automated processes that had previously been based on paper. They also eliminate the disparate custom-made programs and sources of information (papers, spreadsheets, small scattered databases) that previously used to circulate in <sup>fi</sup>rms. Different kinds of information about people (on recruitment, training, evaluation, etc.) end up related, centralized and structured in a single digital environment. At the same time, these systems facilitate the use (inconceivable before) of enormous volumes of data, and their combination and subsequent distribution to diverse users worldwide. eHR systems have constituted themselves into the most important ally for the global integration of data on people (Alcaraz, 2004), and they play a key role in the global integration of HR practices (Heikkilä & Smale, 2011).

In the “<sup>fl</sup>at” globalized planet in which we live (Friedman, 2005), so intimately made possible by information and communication technologies (ICTs), a deeper look at technology transfer processes in the con text of MNCs is needed. The case of China, due to its emergence as a new super-power, is of particular interest. There, the management discourses and technology transfer processes are taking place in the wider scenario of an increasingly capitalist-oriented country, where extraordinarily rapid economic, social and cultural changes are taking place on a huge scale in all spheres. Examples include adherence to international treaties, the increasing presence of international companies (e.g. the arrival of virtually all the major corporate <sup>fi</sup>rms in the Shanghainese neighborhood of Pudong), the transformation of educational systems, new divisions of labor in workplaces, changes in local employment laws, the increasing role of consultancy groups (McKenna, Richardson, Singha, & Xua, 2010), the expansion of business schools, journals, books and management resources, the offering of training workshops and international conferences, the creation of specialized management associations, etc.

Certainly, the role of information systems in MNCs has long been acknowledged in functionalist positivistic-oriented work such as that of Smale (2008) and Kim, Park, and Prescott (2003), which has provided signi<sup>fi</sup>cant conceptual and practical insights on knowledge/technology transfer and “the global integration of business functions”. Kim et al. (2003) have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a number of “modes” which further this process: people-based integration modes (the transfer of managers and executives, such as Hilary Johnson, portrayed above, provision of training, etc.); information-based integration (through information systems); formalization-based integration (standardized work procedures, rules, policies, manuals, etc.); and centralization-based integration modes (decision-making authority lying in the business head of<sup>fi</sup>ce). For these authors, information-based integrating modes are perceived to be highly “effective” across all company functions and deserving of far greater attention from scholars

Although we agree with this latter statement, for us the time has come to extend that analysis to the (until now) ignored eHR software that is already at the heart of Human Resources. We believe that it is necessary to inquire about its role in these export/import and global integration processes but from quite a different epistemological and ontological perspective. For us, what is transferred in the processes of exportation is not merely a neutral, apolitical technology, but rather one of “world-making”, that is, one of micro-ordering processes that collectively serve to shape our aspirations and orient us towards ourselves and our environment (Chia, 2003). We believe that critical research on information systems and postcolonial theory can provide alternative critical accounts of those technologies and their transfer to “developing countries” such as China. This article is an effort to provide such an exploration

We shall begin by outlining the theoretical positioning of this paper by succinctly describing some of the main tenets of critical research on information systems and postcolonial theory. We then argue the need and pertinence of exploring critically the <sup>fi</sup>eld of eHR systems and we advance the key questions that remain unanswered whose analytical examination nurtures this paper. Next, the empirical exploration on which this research is based will be presented, as well as the method of analysis used. Then, the new and celebrated presence of Western HR practices and software will be described. Offering a critical view of the accounts of the interviewees and the documentary sources used, we will show that eHR soft ware brings not only new automation possibilities but also offers a new momentum to HR discourse and practices, bringing novel possibilities to create (and export) governance, visibility and inspection. In the following section we focus on Western HR-based “performance management” as a key factor in the emerging modes of people management in China, and we show how these are incorporated and “ampli-<sup>fi</sup>ed” in the eHR software. Subsequently, we explore how the possibilities and constrictions of information support the construction of a global “standardized employee”. In the discussion section we link our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to the literature previously referred to, highlighting what we believe are key issues to be contemplated in a critical approach to these systems and its transfer from “developed” to “developing” contexts, accentuating the political and asymmetrical nature of these import–export exercises. We conclude the paper by exposing the limitations of our study and outlining brie<sup>fl</sup>y items that could nurture future (critical) research agendas.

## 2. Theoretical overview

## 2.1. The contribution of critical research on information systems

The multiple theoretical approaches under the label of critical research on information systems have provided pertinent suspicious views on the allegedly inherently desirable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and consequences that information and communication technologies (ICTs) are supposed to bring to all, not just in the “developed” world, but even more so in “developing” countries (Avgerou, 2008; McGrath, 2005; Mitev, 2006).

Disregarding the functionalist/positivist imperative of subjugating all practical and analytical efforts to the imperative of performativity and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, critical information systems researchers usually escape from the instrumental rationality and discourses on ‘best practices’ that are embedded in much mainstream research on ICT. In contrast, they have directed attention to the political and ideological entanglement of information systems. Often drawing on the insights of Science and Technology Studies (Hackett, Amsterdamska, Lynch, & Wajcman, 2007), critical information system research has strongly questioned the predominant assumption of technological determinism (Leonardi, 2008). Some authors (e.g. Doolin & Lowe, 2002; Klecuri, 2004) have also portrayed the socio-technical embeddedness of ICT and the conceptual relevancy of processes such as ‘translation’ (Latour, 2005) in which the interests of some groups are inscribed in technologies. Among other effects, critical research on information systems has stressed the new possibilities of control, surveillance, “<sup>fl</sup>exibility”, empowerment for some (and de-skilling for many) associated with ICTs (Howcroft, 2009; Howcroft & Trauth, 2005). The ultimate purpose of (many) authors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with this trend is one of instigating social change instead of pursuing “ef<sup>fi</sup>cient solutions” (Brooke, 2002), and for this purpose they propose alternative discussions on ethics, power, domination and emancipation in the domain of ICTs

Within this myriad of critical contributions, those arising from postmodern/post-structuralist views are among the more fruitful frameworks to construct new knowledge and develop a critical agenda (Mitev, 2006). Among these, we believe that the multiple avenues coming from Shoshana Zuboff's in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Zuboff, 1989) continue to be relevant to our days and have the potential to inform a rich critical understanding of information systems (Brown, 2008) and technology transfer. Drawing partly on poststructural theory and the work of Michel Foucault, Zuboff showed early on how organizations have been strongly affected by work practices that depend intimately on the possibilities of information. As she showed, since the advent of information systems, work mainly consists of manipulating symbols, dealing with computer screens, reports, queries, graphs. Employees and managers interact (signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly) with others and with themselves through the “electronic text” — this becomes “the new medium in which events are both observed and enacted” (Zuboff, 1989). The duality of information technology – its capacity to automate and to informate (that is, to create new information and relations starting from previous information) – offers new possibilities of generating analysis, measurement, normalization, surveillance, and discipline (controlling others, controlling oneself) techniques.

Partly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the work of that author, and expanding it in a truly creative fashion, scholars such as Cooper (1992), Chia (1996) and Kallinikos (1995, 2006) have portrayed information as one of the key factors in the rationalization of work and society, offering signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research opportunities for further critical IS research. For these authors, information technologies can be conceived as ‘technologies of representation’ that expand primary organizing micro-practices (Chia, 1996), bringing formidable possibilities of ordering (Law, 1994), codifying, framing, standardization, “histori<sup>fi</sup>cation”, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, construction and visibility, in what Cooper (1997) calls the labour of di-vision.

As Alferoff and Knights (2008) have recently argued, software (such as Customer Relationship Management) expands Foucault's (1991) ‘governmentality’ project designed to manage aggregated populations through the capture and manipulation of data, by means of information technology's ability to translate a whole series of material and symbolic interactive events into a digitized set of numbers, categories or forms. For Kallinikos (2004. 2006) much of organizational life is translated into “computable" (that is. machine-representable and processable) settings. This exercise becomes the micro-texture of organizational operations, the micro-foundation of organizational life — the “architecture of the invisible” (Kallinikos, 1995). For this author, informating rides on software and hardware standardization (e.g. packaged software such as ERPs) that furthers the dissolvability of context-embedded operations and allows the mobility of tasks, services and operations across contexts, regions and countries.

Postcolonial theory offers, precisely, a sharp lens to examine critically ICTs and this “mobility”.

## 2.2. Critical research on information systems and postcolonial theory

Recent critical developments on information systems have been nurtured by postcolonial theory. This approach questions and tries to subvert the sovereignty of Western epistemological, economic, political, ethico-moral and cultural categories (Bhabha, 1994; Said, 1978; Thiong'o, 1986; Young, 2001). The postcolonial theorists' project aims to denounce the extended assumption that the world should be known and acted upon through Western “common sense” and technologies. For Prasad (2003:6) postcolonialism “starts with the recognition that the neocolonial world order of our times is extremely unfair and unjust, it is grounded in the belief that justice and human freedom are indivisible, and that achieving true freedom requires a global decolonization”. Under “postcolonial” approaches there is a rich mixture of literature in which terms such as colonialism, neocolonialism and imperialism are often employed interchangeably. Prasad (2003:6;7) states that:

“Conceptual differences characterizing terms like “colonialism” and “imperialism” may not be overly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in so far as the theoretical project of postcolonialism is concerned” (…) “Postcolonial theory and criticism is explicitly committed to developing a radical critique of colonialism/imperialism and neocolonialism. In doing this, postcolonialism is not attempting something entirely new; quite to the contrary, the critique of colonialism offered by postcolonialism forms a part of that long and impressive history of oppositional criticism of, and resistance to, Western colonialism”.

Therefore, “there is not one postcolonial, nor one school of theory, rather an interpretive sensibility conditioned by different thinkers and positions” (Jack, Westwood, Srinivas, & Sardar, 2011) such as those mentioned above. From this “sensitivity”, international management is now seen as a continuation of direct Western colonialism “without the traditional mechanism of expanding frontiers and territory control, but with elements of political, economic and cultural control” (Banerjee & Prasad, 2008: 91). So, one of the main concerns in the postcolonial agenda is to understand critically knowledge/technology transfer processes and, in so doing, challenge the resulting power structures.

Much of postcolonial theory tries to avoid over-simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed categories framed in oppositional terms such as West/The “Rest” (Philip, Irani, & Dourish, 2012) or Occident/Orient, West/East, Here/There, Core/Periphery, North/South, First/Third World, Developed/Developing world. Bhabha (1994a), a prominent postcolonial scholar, states that much of cultural diversity is shaped in the complexities and hybridity of the local-transnational, cross-cultural encounter. For this author, this encounter becomes a ‘third space’ that reshapes the world-view (of both the ruler and the ruled). But postcolonial approaches assert that the encounter is often based on unequal, uneven and ultimately asymmetric relations. Frenkel (2008) has suggested that multinational corporations (MNCs) can be conceptualized as that prominent ‘third space’ where actors (executives, line-managers, employees, etc.) deal from different angles of cultural exposure, tactics — and resistance.

Following this logic, the postcolonial critique claims that international management and cross-cultural academic disciplines tend to present themselves as benign, uncritical, apolitical and pluralistic multicultural <sup>fi</sup>elds while, in fact, they usually incorporate an over-simplistic representation of ‘the Other’. So, when analyzing knowledge and technology transfer processes taking place between MNCs and their subsidiaries, postcolonial theory condemns most of the scholarly literature on the grounds that it fails to recognize the importance of the broad social, political, economic and cultural arena in which transfer and change occurs in transnational organizations (Mir, Banerjee, & Mir, 2008; Prasad, 2003).

The postcolonial viewpoint strongly criticizes the narrow conceptions of technology that appear in mainstream research, where technology is usually considered to be a simple utilitarian application of a neutral technological device, a mere tool, devoid of the social and political burden that its context of use signi<sup>fi</sup>es — a <sup>fi</sup>nal solution of a problem or demand which is basically of the economic type (Shen & Williams, 2005). In contrast, postcolonial theorists have shown that practices of knowledge and technology transfer are often associated with imperial subjectivities (Mir et al., 2008), and efforts of the colonized to mimic and act like the colonizer. From a postcolonial view, MNC purposes go far beyond implementing mere ef<sup>fi</sup>cient initiatives in search of productivity, and have more to do with turning the foreign af<sup>fi</sup>liate into a reformed, recognizable, and easily dominated ‘Other’ — MNC headquarters' practices are usually aimed not only at changing the colonized's conduct but also reconstituting its very identity (Frenkel, 2008).

The growing importance and value of postcolonial theory in informing a critical agenda on information systems research has been argued in various places, such as in Kvasny and Richardson (2006), McGrath (2005) and Mitev (2006). There is now a stimulating body of research focusing particularly on the analysis of ICT and its transfer to “developing” countries. Scholars such as Agre and Rotenberg (1998) have produced insightful arguments aligned with the postcolonial thesis on privacy issues and information technology in order to explore the Local/Global divide. Turk and Trees (1999) used a postcolonial perspective to describe the participative development of an indigenous innovative system to preserve cultural heritage information in Australia. Lim (2001) has relied on postcolonial theory to deconstruct Singapore's “intelligent island discourse” supposedly based on the dissemination of ICTs and the departure from a rhetoric of crisis to one of utopianism. Gopal, Beaubien, and Marcon (2002) have dealt incisively with the issue of IT and the legacy of colonialism in India, under the metaphor of “old wolf, new wool suit”. Also in India, Chakravartty (2004) has used a postcolonial framework to criticize the intricacies of telecom policies and the unful<sup>fi</sup>lled promises of solving development problems. Mayasandra, Pan, and Myers (2006) have defended the usefulness of using a postcolonial lens to approach the analysis of information technology outsourcing organizations, drawing attention to issues of power, ownership, control and identity. Muranga (2009) has written on the topic of “ICT for development”, reconsidering from a critical-realist and postcolonial approach Kenya's transition to e-governance. Stahl (2008a,b) has used a critical discourse analysis aligned with postcolonial theory to contrast ICT policies and their “emancipation for all” claims. Bhuiyan (2008) has looked into the information society to dismantle the discourse of social progress, linking it to an imperial project designed to create markets for multinational corporations that own and operate ICTs.

Overall, much of this work provides “anti-market utopias” confronting traditional celebratory information technology discourses, and aiming to provide accounts more meaningful for those in “developing” countries.

## 2.3. Examining the field of eHR software through a critical-postcolonial lens

Despite these scholarly efforts, the <sup>fi</sup>eld of Human Resources and its software technologies continue to remain untouched by the critical research on information systems and the postcolonial turmoil. However, there is an emerging <sup>fi</sup>eld within critical management studies providing alternative critical accounts of HR and international HR management (e.g. Townley, 1993), which have portrayed them not as mere “rational <sup>fi</sup>elds” in the pursuit of higher corporate productivity, but Western-originated disciplines that need to be understood in their capitalistic context. HR is seen as a discipline that ultimately aims to regulate and offer individuals an identity and a sense of themselves subjugated to managerial objectives. The HR <sup>fi</sup>eld has been described as an attempt to control not only what employees do, but also how they should ‘be’ (Du Gay, 1994; McKenna et al., 2010). HR practices have been, thus, conceived as ‘technologies of the self’ (Foucault, 1988) that serve to order and regulate the workplace, to overcome local practices, to institutionalize auto-regulation and set values and regimes of truth, allowing private areas of the person to be open to surveillance, for expert judgment and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Rose, 1990).

When analyzing critically the export of HR discourses and practices from “developed” to “developing” worlds, Keenoy (2009) and McKenna et al. (2010) have also argued that, despite their supposedly ‘universal claims’, HR ‘best practices’ usually implemented in MNCs contain and export to their subsidiaries a North-American <sup>fl</sup>avored “common sense” focused on control, efficiency and performativity. Similarly, Frenkel (2006), using a Bhabhanian approach to explore the <sup>fi</sup>eld of International Human Resources Management (IHRM) from a postcolonial perspective, has argued that its assumptions on cultural and institutional distance are part of the developed world's control mechanisms over developing countries, that help to reinforce the superiority of the former over the latter: “camou<sup>fl</sup>aged as pluralistic and multi-cultural, the IHRM discourse reproduces the hierarchical world-view of the neo-colonial era, the era of business empires” (Frenkel, 2006:2). To offer alternative views, scholars such Cheung (2008) have started to analyze the export of HRM techniques to countries such as China, exploring the problems of these export– import exercises from the local employees' perspectives and the associated (and usually neglected) tensions.

Although, in our view, these are scholarly developments to be welcomed, to our knowledge no critical approach has informed the analysis of eHR software and (even less) its transfer to emerging countries. To date, mainstream ICT and international management literature debates have centered on purely functional preoccupations within a positivist theoretical framework. Research has revolved around the implementation dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of the systems, the duration of the processes of installation, training necessities (Tansley & Newell, 2007; Tansley, Newell, & Williams, 2001), the decision processes that in<sup>fl</sup>uence purchasing policies in different countries (Florkowski & Olivas-Lujan, 2006), the effects of (English) ‘language standardization’ on the acceptance and use of e-HRM systems in foreign subsidiaries (Heikkilä & Smale, 2011), etc.

We believe that the neglect of eHR software and its transfer is an important omission. Due to the global success of HR discourse and practices (Keenoy, 2009) and associated eHR software, a critical approach to these systems, informed with a postcolonial sensitivity, can bring new insights into their nature and transferability. ‘Artifacts have politics’ (Mir et al., 2008; Winner, 1986), and as such, it becomes pertinent to analyze the micro-practices, effects and “achievements” of information in the global exportation of eHR systems. Many questions remain unanswered, and it is the purpose of this paper to throw some light on the following: What might be an alternative analytical account of eHR software, as informed by a critical/postcolonial view on information systems? What are the new ordering possibilities (e.g. of control, inspection, surveillance, standardization) that Human Resources software may be generating — and transferring overseas? What are some of eHR software's informating capabilities? How does this software affect the import–export and integration of new organizational practices and discourses – e.g. on performance management – in MNCs? How are eHR systems perceived in China by local MNC HR managers? What are some of the new organizational possibilities that the software brings to HR management and MNCs operating in China? To what extent does eHR software facilitate colonization processes?

Exploring these issues, our research aims to contribute to the analysis of the ways in which technology (and, particularly eHR as an information-based integration mechanism) is implicated in “the postcolonial provincializing of ‘universal reason’” (Anderson, 2002:643).

## 3. Empirical work and data collection

This research relies on several qualitative in-depth interviews with renowned Chinese Human Resources experts in Shanghai, and the examination of diverse eHR software-related documentation and functionalities.

We carried out interviews in 12 organizations (MNCs) with the most senior HR leader (and on two occasions, also with their direct subordinates). Out of these 14 interviews, 11 were allowed to be audio taped and transcribed, and they became the most fruitful analytical resource of our study.

Along with the interviews we also examined various documentary materials (“texts”) related to the eHR systems in place in these MNCs, including internal software user manuals, corporate HR documents such as implementation guidelines, several system-produced reports — and various screens as they appeared in the computer graphical interface customized in these organizations. We complemented these sources with external publicly available material from various providers of eHR systems: brochures, webpages, user manuals and sectorial documents.

Ours is as a multiple exemplars methodological approach (Denzin, 1989; Miles & Huberman, 1994) in which we do not aim to treat organizations as cases, group them into families or compare them. Rather, our empirical sources are selected, inspected and treated as multiple instances that illustrate key issues on eHR and eHR transfer. This allowed us to deconstruct common views and reassemble those elements in a new light, into a reordered “whole”.

Thus, companies and candidates for the interviews were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed after one year during which we tried to <sup>fi</sup>nd some of the most active members and organizations of Shanghai's HR scene. For this, with the help of a translator, we reviewed local HR journals in Mandarin (such as, in its translation, “Training Magazine”) and also attended several events that took place in that city, also in that language (“China

HR Expo” in 2006 and “2nd Asia Human Resource Summit” in 2007). The MNC companies were chosen so as to include diverse sizes and sectors, as well as diverse levels of HR and eHR software implementation:

We also interviewed HR managers from three top organizations which preferred anonymity (two of them belong to the automotive sector, one American and the other German; and the third from the food sector, a direct competitor of Coca Cola). Some of these companies operate as fully-owned subsidiaries while others operate in partnership with local Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rms. These organizations, together, employ thousands of employees in inner China and the “special provinces” (Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan).

Several of these MNCs have a presence in more than one hundred countries, and publicize themselves as industry-recognized leaders in their commitment to product innovation. Among them. Unilever established of <sup>fi</sup>ces in China as early as 1923 – its entry linked to the soap industry in colonial times (Zurndofer, 2006) – while Bose, the latest, established itself in China in 2002. At the time of the interviews several of these organizations had recently moved their Asian central of<sup>fi</sup>ces to Shanghai from countries such as Japan and Australia, which we believe shows the growing importance of China to their operations. According to public corporate docu mentation and scholarly literature (Fernández & Underwood, 2006), several of these companies announced at that time their intention to make operations in China (and India) the key factor in their global success.

The face-to-face semi-structured interviews with these high-level HR executives all took place in the subsidiary of<sup>fi</sup>ces of these multinationals (all located in Shanghai city except one) during the year 2007, and all lasted between one and two hours. All fourteen individuals interviewed, except two, were Chinese nationals. Of these two, one had received training from a well-recognized university in the area of Human Resources (Michigan Ross School of Business), and the other was a Chinese returned home after several years of being an HR consultant in the US. Four of the interviewees were male and the rest female. Due to the fast pace of the Chinese job market, several of these executives have changed positions and roles since then.

The interview guiding themes were the following: 1. What is the overall value of HR management? 2. What are some of the key HR processes and eHR systems currently in place in the organization? 3. What is the role or mission of the HR department in this company? 4. What are the main challenges of managing people in China? 5. What would you suggest to a foreigner brought to China as an expatriate who has to manage Chinese subordinates? 6. What is the value of eHR software? Are there differences and commonalities between the HR practices you are now in charge of and previous ones, if any? 7. To what extent can Western HR practices and software be applied or used in China? 8. To what extent do the prac tices shaped by the MNC headquarters collide with others at the subsidiary level?

The interviews were conducted in English by one of the authors (although we had the help of a Chinese translator in case clari<sup>fi</sup>cations were needed) and transcriptions were later offered to the interviewees for their “validation”. (As a limitation of choosing that language, we are aware that the richness of their accounts would have been greater if interviews had been conducted entirely in Mandarin). One of the things that surprised us somewhat – as compared with our previous interviewing experience with other busy managers in Europe – was the openness and eagerness of most of these managers to be interviewed. Our supposition (which was supported in various ways in several of the encounters) was that their willingness to engage in a dialog resulted from their keenness to learn from supposed “Western experts” (in our case, a Spanish assistant professor).

Along with the eHR-related documentary sources mentioned previously, the graphical software interfaces, functionalities and modules in place in these organizations analyzed for this research included those such as “employee identi<sup>fi</sup>cation”, “job description”, “recruitment”, “training”, “performance”, “compensation” and “career management” from eHR systems such as Oracle, PeopleSoft and SAP.

Ours is a critical approach to research in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by Critical Management Studies (Fournier & Grey, 2000; Grey, 2005), and as such it is rooted in the following tenets:

. a de-naturalizing ontology: nothing is “natural” or inevitable, so things could be ordered in different alternative ways — it is necessary to problematize any knowledge/practice that claims to be “objec tive”, “given”, “true”, “inevitable”, “immutable”, “universal”, “classless”, “timeless” or “necessary”.

. an epistemological reflexivity: the acknowledgement that accounts of organization and management are mediated by those who give that account — so the active role of the analyst in the construction of knowledge needs to be made explicit.

. an anti-performative stance, in which the research objective is not to produce more “ef<sup>fi</sup>cient solutions” to reinforce current corporate orders but to open up possibilities for new (hopefully more emancipatory) accounts, particularly for those whose voices may be less powerful.

Therefore, here we share Cecez-Kecmanovic (2005:23) that not only is it legitimate but that “it is indeed an obligation for a researcher to actively engage in the transformation of IS practices that will contribute to a more democratic workplace with greater degree of autonomy and human agency, and ultimately lead to less repressive and more equitable solutions”.

Our data analysis is based on a critical discourse analysis approach (Wodack & Meyer, 2009) since it assumes that the narratives coming from the managers, the corporate documents, and the graphical interfaces of the eHR software, can all be seen as “texts”. This approach, often used in other postcolonialinspired research, assumes (Fairclough & Wodak, 1997) that power relations are partly discursive, that discourse is a form of social action and that dominance may be enacted and (re)produced by subtle, routine, everyday forms of texts (Van Dijk, 1997). Critical discourse analysis allowed us to explore how these texts may be drawing and constructing (favored) versions of reality and, in the process, positioning or favoring certain groups and interests, in<sup>fl</sup>uencing control, and supporting emerging orders.

Following this approach, the verbal interactions, the data, claims and images from the documentation and brochures, the instructions from the user manuals, the <sup>fi</sup>elds in the computer graphical interfaces, etc., were all treated and analyzed as structures of text. Since the point of critical discourse analysts is to take a position (Van Dijk, 1993), of the many possible properties of these texts, we focused on those that most clearly exhibited the discursive properties of the exercise of dominance. In our analysis, the <sup>fi</sup>rst author was in charge of reading all materials and generating themes from our data sources, recognizing regularities, consistency and commonalities. Then all three authors looked at discourse structures such as:

• semantic and topic choices, local meanings, argumentation forms, presuppositions, predispositions, analogies and metaphors used by our interviewees or available in the corporate documentation.

• graphs, stories, rhetorical/persuasion forms, <sup>fi</sup>gures and quotes (from the “credible experts” or users) appearing in the eHR documentation.

• eHR software screens, <sup>fi</sup>elds, labels and usability options.

This allowed us to identify discursive elements of control, power, constriction and tension. In the following section we present our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 4. Findings

## 4.1. HR practices and eHR software in China

Western in<sup>fl</sup>uence is truly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in all spheres of China's life, particularly in the business landscape of all the biggest cities. “Human resource” practices and discourse (e.g., conceiving people as “talent”) are already pervasive in China — as illustrated by one of our HR executives interviewed, working for a German automotive company with a presence in China:

“We see human resources as the most important asset of the company because it does not matter what the instruments are or the technology that is used, you always need people to collaborate, the talent [our italics] that creative input offers.”

In this context, most of the executives we interviewed in Shanghai acknowledged the United States' in-<sup>fl</sup>uence on their management practices, and the frequent imposition of HR practices and eHR software packages chosen by the MNC headquarters. They often expressed themselves along the following lines: “many of the HR practices come in cascade fashion from the central of<sup>fi</sup>ces” (Katrina Chun, Unilever China), and “people speak of a global environment but, in fact, it is an American environment” (an HR manager from a top American automobile company).

The accounts of our managers suggested that HR practices are part of the importation of corporate cultures. This is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the words of Jennifer Wood, Director of HR for the Asia-Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region at IBM:

“I believe that the culture of IBM is so strong that the national culture is converted into a secondary one. It is one of the things in which we strongly lead, we have a global culture. We respect the local culture but we do not let the national culture run over the culture of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The culture of IBM dominates, without a doubt. You cannot survive in IBM if your national culture does not allow you to adopt the norms of IBM. The culture of the <sup>fi</sup>rm dominates and we have to be sensitive, and we should <sup>fi</sup>ght against, well, not <sup>fi</sup>ght but rather train our managers.”

The “good news” in relation to HR practices and eHR software was repeatedly expressed by the managers we interviewed, in statements such as the following by Stella Jiang, HR Manager for Braun China: “there is just the need to do very small adaptations to the local culture”.

Moreover, the accounts of our executives suggested that the Western manager is often seen, subtly, as a “hero”. The Chinese employee wants to learn from the Western manager while the reverse process seems to be hardly necessary. John Giong, of Dow Corning (China), commented on this in the following way:

“The Chinese are more willing to be directed by foreigners than by Chinese because they want to learn from them and they want to be directed by foreigners, which means that the managers should also dedicate greater efforts in understanding the Chinese and showing them that it is a reciprocal learning process (I can also learn from you; you are my mentor of the Chinese culture”). Because, sometimes Chinese employees think: “You are the manager, I must learn from you”. If this process of dialog is developed successfully, the Chinese subordinates will feel more comfortable and independent inside the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Sometimes they have the abilities but not the con<sup>fi</sup>dence.”

So, eHR information systems arrive in China in this context of apparent pro-Western euphoria and openness to foreign management practices. Of course, our interviewees also mentioned the necessity of carrying out adaptations for both HR practices and eHR software systems to the Chinese context. But these were considered to be slight adaptations. Concerning the eHR systems, those “localizations” appeared to be mostly related to language use, as illustrated by Hilary Johnson from Honeywell China:

“De<sup>fi</sup>nitely, adaptations to local necessities must be carried out. Language will be one of the keys, it will be English but at a very, very basic level, to make it easier. These systems are designed to be global, they will have thought about that. Many employees, above all at lower levels such as technicians, do not speak English very well. We will see changes, but not so many. Perhaps there will be linguistic and location obstacles, but not that big. The way in which the system will be implemented will oblige managers to use it”.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that a few language adaptations may suf<sup>fi</sup>ce. The software has been “designed to be global”. This view, like many others from our interviewees, conceives the software programs as mere technical tools. However, if we approach the functionalities they bring in more detail, a different scene emerges. Let's explore it.

## 4.2. eHR software and the new possibilities to create (and export) visibility/inspection

When asked about the value of HR systems, Jennifer Wood (of IBM, China) argued:

“We have meetings with executives each trimester and I show them these data of the command structure. It is the only way to show where we are progressing. Voluntary leavings are a problem.

Salaries, according to the job, according to the level, if it is man or woman, what the increase has been, what is happening in the market. The job of any HR is to delve into these data and <sup>fi</sup>nd where the errors are, tell the managers of the business that they should pay attention to this factor and do something. Either, also, “we can make a new…” or propose that if this group of persons can be substituted by a service offered by third parties, we can save “x” amount of money. Our work consists of this: To understand all of the data and costs associated with persons, how much money we are spending on training, which persons leave in their <sup>fi</sup>rst year of service, in their <sup>fi</sup>fth year (…) And all of that is in the data, and the HR person that does not have data at his <sup>fi</sup>ngertips should not be in HR.”

Hence, data (produced by these systems) appear as inextricably linked to the HR profession. eHR systems amplify and expand the possibilities of HR analysis and, ultimately, action. Stella Jiang from Braun (China) noted that:

“The main bene<sup>fi</sup>t of these systems is to calculate the payroll; the second is to make managers use the system to inform their business judgment: e.g. if they create a new team, re-structure the division… they can access the data and know how many people they have, their age, section, etc., and then move people from one place to another…”

The line-manager can look at the screen of his employees and obtain percentages. Similarly, the employee can also consult and report his/her data on-line. Accordingly, the manager obtains scores, averages and differences, is able to follow the projects in which the employee has been involved, infer employees' strengths and weaknesses, “see” actual or desired behavior, workforce trends — by department, by unit, by region…

The accounts of our managers often suggested – as emphasized by Kimberly Lieu, from Bose – that “one of the main bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of these systems is to see the skills and competences of our employees”.

Employees, ultimately people, are here conceived of as “collections of competences”. The organization can initially “identify” the existent generic competences in the current staff and also those “desired or required” for the different positions – such as teamwork, emotional intelligence, autonomy, empathy – with different levels of “mastery” (for example, unsatisfactory, satisfactory, excellent). The person–position comparison becomes, thus, a supposedly “natural” operation. The dif<sup>fi</sup>culties and barriers in carrying out individual comparisons are eliminated at the global level. As we read in a brochure of the provider Oracle (2006: 4) that we were given at the food company that participated in this study:

“The centralized nature of Oracle Applications allows for the creation and deployment of a single, enterprise-wide competency framework. This lets organizations develop a standard description for all the core competences and quali<sup>fi</sup>cations necessary for the ideal ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of every role within the company. The central competency framework can become fundamental to the future success of an organization because it helps bring together disparate systems, removes international barriers to workforce comparisons, and creates a single view of the skills available within the workforce. Oracle HCM applications provide a complete set of integrated tools to ensure that leading-edge strategies for attracting, recruiting, developing, and retaining the best employees are consistently applied throughout all levels of the organization, while reducing administrative costs.”

eHR systems are (supposedly) of help in the global management of employees (now conceived of as “talent”), as long as there is the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and imposition of a single de<sup>fi</sup>nition for all. Competencies, profiles and comparisons are reinforced in the screens. That is. eHR systems contribute to the definition of people based on the options (of modeling, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, measurement, etc.) that are consolidated in computer screens, graphs, and functionalities available in the system. eHR software mediates – and brings new analytical, detailed, novel management possibilities to – salary decisions and other aspects of people (or “combinations of competences”) management. And these new options “travel” with the implementation of this software to “developing” countries, such as China.

## 4.3. HR and eHR software: amplifying the logics of HR-based “performance management” in Chinese organizations

eHR systems seem to facilitate an exercise of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant global homogenization. This is, of course, one of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts claimed by most eHR software providers, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in this brochure:

“Tyler also explains that prior to mySAP HR, assessing employee performance was done differently in Europe than in the United States. This was a real problem for us because no one really knew the common denominator,” says Tyler. “By having a common global appraisal process, you begin to get consistency, and it's easier to understand the development needs on an individual and collective basis.” SAP (2003:3)

Thus, Western HR practices and software bring their concepts, techniques and “common denominators” to the “developing world” and places such as China. The accounts of our interviewees suggested that, in particular, “performance management” HR techniques and software are now inscribed at the heart of people management. As we were told in the companies participating in this study, to compensate, promote, dismiss, and recognize are currently operations that are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly based on measured perfor mance (in part by means of eHR systems). As argued by Anne Ping, of GE:

“Performance is <sup>fi</sup>rst, very important, it is the basis of everything in GE…The managers must use the data that they have on performance, combined with the data on education, history and if he/she (the employee) grows or not with the company. And another process, after doing this, is that of classifying me for the job position. Am I a “top talent”? Am I a “high value employee”? Or a “less effective employee”? In the past we had seven… now we have these three categories of performance classi<sup>fi</sup>cation.”

## Katrina Chun used somewhat similar words:

“In Unilever there is a planning process based on annual activities: You <sup>fi</sup>nd the people, evaluate them, put them in “boxes” (your potential, what you are doing, how you are contributing…). You put them in a box-ranking and you begin to plan their career, how they are going to improve and how they are going to reach success”.

But these changes don't always come without tensions between the new forms of measurement, classi<sup>fi</sup>cation and compensation that come with the HR practices and the eHR systems, and the previous ones, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by this manager's comments:

“… China has its own culture, its perception of, for example… different classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. For instance, “putting people in a box”. Perhaps it is very logical. Your potential. True? For example, you are a “high <sup>fl</sup>yer”. But from the feedback that I receive from my surroundings… not only mine, in the Chinese context we are not accustomed to telling the people this type of af<sup>fi</sup>rmation in black or white— you are a “high <sup>fl</sup>yer” talent—because it usually lends itself to confusion. Many people with talent, if they receive this feedback, can think that they have arrived, and they themselves change their mentality, suddenly they become a bit pretentious with their co-workers, hierarchical, which does not necessarily put them in the correct environment of development. We believe that this is a question of cultural difference. And also when you move an employee up, it is easy, but when the change is down, this person feels strongly de-motivated and some even leave the company (…). “I, for example, worked in a company where it was little formal, indirect and you worked much better. You can give feedback, comment to a person where his/her strengths are, what the expectations of the company are of him/her, what ambitions exist regarding the worker, but you do not necessarily put the worker in a black box: “you have great potential… with a label or class of employee…”

The path of MNCs in China, therefore, is not a straightforward one, but rather one with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant dif-<sup>fi</sup>culties. In the words of Johnson (of Honeywell China):

“We need to train them in the philosophy of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We are a <sup>fi</sup>rm that pays in function of performance. We pay the top performers. We do not pay the low performers. And here is the cultural problem: In China one should not stand out. Everyone thinks they should all be treated in the same way, the personal performance of each individual can be distinct but the administrators, due to the culture… The theme of compensation is not a topic of their culture. Aggressiveness or confrontation before their employees is not a theme of their culture. Those are the occasions in which we see a challenge between the line that the <sup>fi</sup>rm wants to take and that which their administrators take.”

And here is where eHR systems come to “crystallize” and amplify the logic of HR practices and discourse, helping to shape and digitize HR procedures and categories. Computer graphical interfaces, something as “simple” as a screen, help in this process: through the buttons, scroll bars, iconic images, through the operations that it allows (list, select, <sup>fi</sup>nd, copy-paste, drag and drop, consult, compare, add-up, etc.), and the re-presentations that it offers (for example, as we saw in the software implemented in one of the automotive organizations, the photo of the employee, his/her “code”, “personal information”, “current position”, “annual target compensation”, “role type”, “headcount requested”, “targeted need ratio”, “match”, “gap”, etc.) This leaves very little room for ambiguity or interpretation. The company is simulated in the system, mediated by it. The world's “simpli<sup>fi</sup>cation” and people construction through HR categories – “in the past we had seven… now we have three…” – becomes visible in the screens of the system. When we looked at the eHR interface, this process could be seen in the form of several screens with a “Type of Employee” <sup>fi</sup>eld, and the following top-down menu:

01: “Top Talent”

02: “Highly Valued Employee”

03: “Less Effective Employee”

The eHR system supports the pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned HR discourse, logic, categories and practices and has the ability to partly circumvent possible frictions with local approaches. These are the categories that can (should) now be informed, reported, and used to help make decisions related to salary, job allocation, career plan, etc. The world constructed in the HR policies of the <sup>fi</sup>rm appear, are rei<sup>fi</sup>ed, and take shape in the (apparently neutral and aseptic) windows, reports and hyperlinks of the eHR system.

eHR systems embody the priorities, (new) values and ways of seeing and operating in the world. The “machine” becomes the carrier of “common sense” that is taken from HR. People, job positions and tasks are torn out, “<sup>fl</sup>ayed” from their local contexts and “put” into software modules that can be used with ease. This is part of a formidable “standardization” process.

## 4.4. eHR systems and the “standardization” of the employee

eHR tools gather individual data and consolidate and interconnect them at a global level. Those that we saw in Shanghai, for instance, are now connected to those of Atlanta, where the headquarters of one of our companies is located, and ready to be connected with the data of Sydney, London, Anchorage, Cape Town, Berlin… The information on “years lived outside of the country of birth” (a <sup>fi</sup>eld in the eHR system) can go on to form part of a report where it is related to “salary categories”, “civil state” “places of work” or “headcount number”. My salary income data can be related to my personnel management data, to my data of development, to my data of evaluation of abilities, to my data in the possession of the government on income tax, or to my work data contained in the databases of the Work Ministry. Some modules are related to others. The data “<sup>fi</sup>nd” ways of interconnecting themselves. The incompatibilities become solvable and exchanges more frequent. The local contexts become less important and global integration becomes easier.

The relations, states, conditions, tendencies, causes, predictions, trends, metrics, ratios, etc. made possible by eHR systems can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to relate directly to concrete, external objects “out-there”. The information which eHR software contains offers the possibility of capturing or producing new relations, data and “entities” linked to people. For example, when examining several system screens (Oracle Workforce Planning software) in place in two of the companies of this study in Shanghai, we observed “things” such as charts (clustered bars) representing “headcount requested”, the “amount” of competences available per business unit, per <sup>fi</sup>scal year, per accounting period, and also diverse “scenarios” in which simulations were played and colors displayed to show “mismatches”.

The products of the informating capabilities of eHR systems are, therefore, structures of meaning independent of countries or cultures: rotation indexes, indicators of absenteeism, performance categories, “employee types”, a pie chart showing the population of personnel eligible to be given early retirement a comparison “person–position gap” graph. These “chunks” of IT-produced meaning cross borders, arrive and settle in. They eliminate “ambiguities”. Local processes lose their singularity. People, in part, also.

eHR software constructs a “standard” employee that is supposed to be captured in its data bases, screens, forms, reports, tables, registers, etc. It produces an entity called “employee” (or “manager” or “candidate”), perpetuated in the eHR systems. Independently of the provider (SAP, Oracle, PeopleSoft, Meta4, etc.) it is a skeletal version that wants to circumvent local contexts, particularities and practices. It is a kind of “normal employee” that can be captured, apprehended and managed through these generic systems.

## 5. Discussion and concluding remarks

From non-critical perspectives, human resources (and its eHR software, also called “eHR solutions”) are just part of the “logical”, benign way organizations and people should be managed (globally) in order to maximize ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and modernization (McKenna et al., 2010). The transfer of eHR software becomes the “inevitable” outcome of the unstoppable globalization process. In this sense, the view that minimal or no change is needed to adapt eHR systems to China, as expressed by most of our interviewed managers, coincides with other research carried out in that country. where MNC HR managers affirm that their HR policies need only minor localization work in order to meet the requirements of PRC labor laws or local governmental restrictions (Fernández & Underwood, 2006); or that despite some necessary adaptations to local conditions best practice management translates remarkably well across cultures (Gamble, 2006).

However in our critical account inspired by postcolonial sensitivity, we have shown that there is nothing neutral or apolitical in these “solutions” and their (unavoidable?) transfer processes from the “developed” to the “developing” world. The critical/postcolonial perspective helps us to move the dialog away from the functionalist and managerial debates, to expose the power issues inherent in the bodies of knowledge and the technologies, as “systems of representations” that are transferred (Frenkel, 2008).

For us the discomfort and tensions before the imported practices revealed, very timidly, by a few of our interviewees in Shanghai are certainly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and cannot be overlooked — e.g. comments such as those made by Hilary Johnson, referring to the cultural differences in “paying for performance”. They support the assertions of Xiao and Tsui (2007) that in contrast to the traditional customs and values of Chinese organizational contexts. such as group harmony, distributive fairness understood as egalitarianism. social adhesion, loyalty and, particularly, seniority, new values are brought into the managerial scene by HR practices. Where respect, wages and promotions were intimately tied to the age of the employee and years worked in the company, now Western HR-based performance measurement practices drive most people management practices (White & Drucker, 2009).

Most of the accounts expressed by our managers seem to support the notion that (ultimately) the imperative of intervention traverses and gives form to the way in which the person and the organization are de<sup>fi</sup>ned in HR (Townley, 1993) and eHR software. This is the case of the well-established trend in HR, Management by Competences (Fernández-Lopez, 2005) that is embedded in much eHR technology. It provides a new momentum in making employees dependent and disposable entities for scrutiny, analysis and “breaking down”. In line with the arguments of diverse authors (Cooper, 1993) this suggests the pertinence of conceiving information systems as “technologies of re-presentation”. As such, eHR systems bring (new) concrete ways to re-present, regulate, discipline, “essentialize”, examine and govern, as well as to ways of naming, registering, codifying, classifying and measuring. These are logics that make new forms of surveillance, visibility and inspection possible. These pirouettes are one of the most illustrative effects of the informating power of these systems.

The eHR screenshot brings the possibility of nimbly <sup>fi</sup>nding a way around “struggles over meaning” (Sturdy & Grey, 2003). eHR systems help to consolidate – or “black-box”, as Latour (2005) would argue – HR discourse and practices. It molds them to the “electronic text” (Zuboff, 1989). Graphical interfaces, such subtle “hammer blows”, can reinforce this process and help to unmask possible tensions or shut down dialog — such as when, in the Chinese context illustrated previously, performance management logic takes form in the graphical user interface. Once implemented in the organization, information vehicularizes people management, becoming part of the “solution”: “information increasingly imposes its own recalcitrant actuality” (Kallinikos, 2006:49). The system is a “site” for the fabrication of meaning. It provides multiple exercises of translation in Latour (1994:32) of “displacement, drift, invention, mediation, the creation of a link that did not exist before and that to some degree modi<sup>fi</sup>es two elements or agents”.

As Kallinikos (2004) has noted, ERP packages are predicated on an understanding of human agency as a procedural affair, and of organizations as an extended series of functional or cross-functional transactions — performed through tasks that are supposed to be based on global “best practices”. This logic is also embedded in the software and promoted by eHR systems. We are facing a reality of data produced on top of other data. Each piece of “social reality” that is computerized can become part of these “habitats”, and can be put in relation to, and combined and compared with, other pieces of information (Kallinikos, 2006, 2009; Zuboff, 1989). From the combination and juxtaposition of information related to other information is born the resultant novelty of correlation, and the generation of scenarios and planning for the future (Tirado, Alcaraz, & Domènech, 1999). The system makes possible new meaning and new possibilities of organization-action, and of complexity and risk. Taking up the proposition that Kallinikos (2006) argues in the most ample context of information systems, we hold that eHR software offers a new momentum in the computational rendition of reality.

In the last instance, each character in the system – for example, the letters that form our names – are supported by ASCII binary code, that is, combinations (in theory unlimited) of the numbers 0 and 1, with which the computations or operations that the machine performs (additions, subtractions, multiplications, etc.) are executed. This is essential to what can be conceived of as primary organizing micro-practices (Chia, 1996) or the micro-texture of organizational operations. This is the architecture of the invisible (Kallinikos, 1995). That which does not lend itself to this translation, and these operations, does not compute (it does not exist or cannot exist in the micro-world of the eHR system). Expanding upon an idea of Kallinikos (2009), we suggest that the great exercise/effort that helps to consolidate HR software is that of the reconstruction of the heterogeneous nature of people, the organization and their relationships, as information. Similar to the exercise carried out by CRM software in which customers are translated into “simulations”, “data doubles” or “mimetic models” that are used by customer service representatives to interact with, and exercise power over, those the “copies” seek to portray (Alferoff & Knights, 2008), eHR software information becomes the medium in which humans circulate and are rendered accountable as employees.

The journey of eHR systems reminds us, thus, about the role and relevancy of informating processes in software import–export processes. They also re<sup>fl</sup>ect what Kallinikos (2006) refers to as “hyper-mobility”. Information travels by means of systems, databases, headquarters, subsidiaries and organizations. It is standardized, assembled, “disemboweled”, re-assembled and transferred by means of environments. This journey is the result of a “generification" process (Pollock & Williams, 2008): the diverse strategies that suppliers use to produce software that embodies characteristics common across many users, so that local sites can be treated as all the same. Apparently, providers are capturing the different local, cultural and organizational realities, so that software travels through frontiers. As these authors have shown, powerful software providers mobilize communities of users for consultations that result in the shaping of these tools. Accordingly, the software developers construct a standardized (“skinny”) version of the prospective “user” (the functionalities the user may utilize, the screens the system may contain, etc.). Similarly, the standard nature of these systems shapes, promotes and mobilizes a “generi<sup>fi</sup>ed employee” with apparent agility throughout diverse regions. The “generi<sup>fi</sup>ed employee” produced by this software allows new possibilities of discipline and identity construction — and the transfer of these globally.

Signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, this illustrates the Bhabhanian process of mimicry (Bhabha, 1994; Frenkel, 2008) in which colonial practices aim not only at changing the colonized's conduct but also at reconstituting their very identity. It interferes with person–organization relations, and in the intimacy or privacy of people themselves, as when a Chinese line-manager re<sup>fl</sup>ects on his/her team before informing the Manager Self-Service web application: “Is my subordinate a “High Value Employee”? “Am I a “Top Talent?”… and then selects among the (three) available options from a scroll-down menu in the graphical interface.

Certainly, the integration exercises of management and HR discourses, techniques and software represent just a (very) tiny movement within the huge changes taking place in the corporate world reshaping China, and the many integration modes used by MNCs across borders (Kim et al., 2003). Smale (2008:147) has argued that global human resource management integration does not refer “to the complete standardization of HRM, but rather to attempts by MNCs to achieve consistency in the delivery of HRM in foreign af<sup>fi</sup>liates”. But, as this author has shown, there is a growing trend towards more technology-driven HRM integration via eHR systems (Heikkilä & Smale, 2011). Certainly, the software is an actor that needs to be examined if we aim to contribute to the unraveling of the common tendency of organizational discourse… to camou<sup>fl</sup>age con<sup>fl</sup>icts and power relations (Frenkel, 2008).

A postcolonial approach assumes that knowledge/technology transfers are not purely one-directional, but processes where both the colonizer and the colonized meet and are transformed in the MNC encounter, in the power-laden and always hybrid Bhabhanian ‘third space’. The MNC policies, the Chinese educated abroad or those returned home, the expatriate, the local employee happy to <sup>fi</sup>nally work in a multinational, and the mediating technology. these are all elements of the cross-cultural encounter, as is the “electronic text” produced by software, which becomes one actor in that “third”, in-between space. However, our work on eHR has offered little evidence of the mutual exchange that is supposed to take place within that ‘third space’, portraying instead a clear asymmetrical stance that coincides with other critical accounts of technology transfer (Mir et al., 2008). As we will argue in the last section of this paper, we believe that this implies the need for further empirical exploration, if a postcolonial approach is to nurture a fertile ground for an enhanced critical IS agenda.

eHR systems can be seen as the “backbone” that supports the practices and effects of HR (Hendrickson, 2003). In fact the greater part of HR department budgets in MNCs is usually taken over by these information systems. Our paper is, to our knowledge, the <sup>fi</sup>rst critical approach to this software — and its transfer to China. It is in emphasizing some of the movements made possible by the possibilities/constrictions of information to achieve HR “consistency” where we see our contribution. Problematizing them allows us to expand the critical dialog on neo-colonial control. In focusing on the micro-mechanisms, unquestioned intricacies, “unobservable” moves, and possibilities brought by information, it has been our intention to provide an account that distances itself from the deterministic-celebratory, universalist mainstream discourses that populate the professional and scholarly literature on information systems. We believe this complements other postcolonial approaches on technology-organizational knowledge transfer that stress hegemonic processes (Mir et al., 2008), and also provide new insights for a critical understanding of Human Resources Management.

## 6. Limitations of the study and further research

Our research is certainly based on a limited source data and a relatively small number of MNCs, so it needs to be seen as a <sup>fi</sup>rst exploratory work; as such, it contains limitations that can point to future research. Along with the eHR software, other MNC integration mechanisms could be investigated in order to clarify in more detail the actual impact of eHR. This could explore additional MNCs from very different sectors and industries, and of different sizes and pro<sup>fi</sup>les (e.g. different degrees of “orientation” towards technology and innovation), and the views of HR directors with different levels of international exposure. Further research could also explore in more detail issues on adaptation, negotiation, destabilization and resistance within different HR functions and time frames — as well as possible reverse effects from the subsidiary to the headquarters.

Particularly, since postcolonial perspectives call for people and nations emerging from colonialism to “decolonize their minds” (Thiong'o, 1986) further research should focus on the actual interpretation at the receivers' end, beyond the views of HR managers. That is, it should allow the voices of ‘the Other’ to be heard (Cheung, 2008). The views of other employees, line-managers and other collectives should be examined, as well people's abilities to move between cultures, discourses and technologies surrounding HR. Certainly, Bhabha's notion of ‘third space’ in the context of ICT and eHR transfer, as we mentioned, requires further exploration.

We also anticipate stimulating research questions on identity at the Chinese employee level, as well as on possible tensions between forms of work organization, employee–boss relations and communication processes (face to face versus eHR-based), and other value conflicts related to performance management in China. (In this sense, it is worthwhile to remark that most HR techniques tend to individualize people's behavior in an “eat what you kill” fashion (Hanlon, 2007), which may collide with previous, more “traditional-collectivistic” (Fernández & Underwood, 2006) values until recently prevalent in Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rms (so well illustrated by the saying “together we share happiness, together we bear pain”).

To conclude, we would like to refer to the lines that appear in the brochure of an eHR vendor, which read: “mySAP ERP HCM enables a singular view of available talent worldwide while at the same time allowing all talent related practices to speak a common language” (SAP, 2006, p.6). As Anderson (2002) observed, a postcolonial view requires a continuing exploration of how techno-science becomes a site for the fabrication and linkage of local and global identities. We believe that analytical efforts such as ours may help to de-construct the kind of fantasy re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the SAP text — and open the <sup>fl</sup>oor for more meaningful options for those in the “developing” world.
