Ab strac t: Two types of information technology (IT) outsourcing governance— contractual and relational—are commonly employed to address different goals in IT service management in outsourcing arrangements. Contractual governance helps improve efficiency in an outsourcing relationship, whereas relational governance facilitates satisfying changing business needs. Past literature argues that both forms of governance are important and that an appropriate balance between them is necessary. This study finds that these two forms of governance often conflict with one another. We contribute to the research on IT outsourcing governance by opening the black box of the evolutionary process of achieving ambidexterity in this context. Organizations shift their focus between contractual and relational forms of governance in an attempt to develop practices that address conflicts between the two forms. We present the findings from a qualitative study of an organization that outsourced its IT services. Our findings reveal how a balance between contractual and relational governance can be achieved through a process we call the ambidexterity pendulum.

Key words and phrases: IT outsourcing, IT outsourcing governance, organizational ambidexterity, outsourcing, outsourcing governance, pendulum process.

Inform ation tec hnol ogy (IT ) governanc e m ec hanism s seek to establish decision rights and accountability frameworks to ensure that IT investments result in business value [39]. Different IT outsourcing governance mechanisms help organizations achieve different goals in managing their IT services. Prior research classifies these mechanisms into two major categories: contractual and relational. Two key competing positions have emerged in the literature that examines the roles of the two categories of governance practices in shaping outcomes. While one position argues that relational and contractual governance mechanisms are complements, the other argues that the two are substitutes. Those that subscribe to the notion of complementarity argue that well-defined contracts support relational governance [29]. In contrast, those who subscribe to the notion of substitutive effects refer to the role of one type of governance in attenuating the impact of the other on business outcomes. For example, Rai et al. [30] conclude that in situations wherein it is difficult to specify goals up front in a contract, exchanging information over the course of the relationship leads to positive outcomes. This view suggests that information exchange (a relational governance construct) has a substitutive effect on goal expectations (a contractual governance construct). Both streams of literature—complements and substitutes—consider a balance between relational and contractual governance as critical and that organizations cannot completely rely on only one type [12]. Although prior research in this area has reached such a consensus, there is a limited understanding of how such a balance could be reached. Also, contractual and relational governance mechanisms typically conflict with one another as suggested by the substitution argument in that the use of contractual mechanisms might undermine the use of relational ones. For example, reliance on a contract may generate distrust.

In summary, while a balance between the two forms of governance is desired, they may conflict one another, thereby presenting a challenge in reaching a balance. For contexts that require an understanding of conflicting demands and how to reach a balance between such demands, the theory of organizational ambidexterity serves as an appropriate theoretical lens. The debate in the literature that contrasts complementary and substitutive roles of contractual and relational governance leaves an interesting gap that is of theoretical and practical relevance—opening the black box of the evolutionary process of achieving ambidexterity. This leads us to the following research question:

RQ: How do organizations balance relational and contractual governance to achieve ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance?

We answer this question through a case study of an IT organization that was engaged in an outsourcing arrangement. Our findings highlight that opening the black box involves an understanding of not only the specific practices that bring together contractual and relational governance but also how the process of reaching balance unfolds through multiple stages.

The paper is organized as follows. We present relevant literature on IT outsourcing governance, specifically focusing on the conversation between the streams of literature that present the complementary and substitution arguments. In the next section, we review the literature on organizational ambidexterity and frame it as an appropriate theoretical lens in examining the conflicts between contractual and relational governance. In the following section, we present our research methodology including data collection and analysis. We then present our findings and discuss how our findings relate to and extend the extant research.

## Theoretical Background

## Contractual and Relational IT Governance in Outsourced Environments

IT governanc e aim s at ensuring that the expec tations of IT investm ents are met and IT risks are mitigated [2]. IT governance is defined as “specifying the decision rights and accountability framework to encourage desirable behavior in the use of IT” [40, p. 8]. It ensures that IT sustains and extends the organization’s strategies and objectives. IT governance establishes the distribution of responsibilities across the different parties involved. IT governance encompasses how decision rights are dispersed across the IT organization and the business units [7, 36]. Governance becomes significantly more important as IT organizations evolve from IT infrastructure management to IT services management, especially when IT services are provided by external entities. Governance frameworks for outsourcing engagements evolve based on the social interaction among the parties involved [24]. Governance is also considered to play a role in affecting efficiency and effectiveness in interorganizational coordination hubs [21]. Formal control (which focuses on contracts) and relational governance (which focuses on unwritten, practice-based mechanisms) have been identified as two broad governance modes used in this context [12, 30].

The extant literature that examines the two forms of IT outsourcing governance (contractual and relational) presents two key competing positions. On the one hand, contractual and relational governance are considered as complements in their roles in shaping the success of an outsourcing relationship [29]. For example, Lacity et al. [18] emphasize the complementary role played by contractual and relational governance in shaping the success of outsourcing engagements. On the other hand, some scholars argue that these two modes are substitutes [30] because contractual and relational forms of governance may be incompatible as increased reliance on contracts might result in mistrust [30]. The notion that the two forms are complements suggests that not only are the two forms compatible with one another but they also reinforce each other [29]. This is further justified by the notion that formal governance is typically insufficient to manage a relationship because of its inability to specify up front various contingencies in detail. Relational governance is considered to address this deficiency by functioning as a complement [29]. Tiwana [37] reports complementary effects between informal control mechanisms and formal behavior control, and substitutive effects between informal control and formal outcome control. The substitution argument is also explained by two mechanisms: (1) the use of a relational mechanism obviates the need for contractual agreement [14] and (2) the use of contractual mechanisms undermines the use of relational mechanisms [10]. This view implies a conflict between contractual and relational governance in that implementing one form adversely affects the ability to implement the other.

Prior research explains the need for the two types of governance and the factors that shape their use. For example, use of control mechanisms such as outcome and social controls have been examined in outsourcing relationships that involved the development of broad contracts with emergent standards [19]. While formal contracts are useful in early stages to clarify initial expectations, changes that are initiated during execution often necessitate a trust-based or relational approach to facilitate cooperation [24]. Relational mechanisms are considered to provide a way to reduce transaction costs imposed by contractual governance. Key social processes such as flexibility, solidarity, and information exchange enable the mitigation of the adverse effect of exchange hazards posed by contractual governance [29]. Although complementing contractual governance with relational forms may be considered as conducive, prior research also cautions against the use of relational mechanisms in situations that do not face or are not adversely affected by hazards that are typical in environments that are governed contractually [29].

Relational governance has been increasingly used in contexts that exhibit rapidly changing technologies or an uncertain environment. Extending Poppo and Zenger’s work [29], which supports the complementary view, Rai et al. [30] identify more granular elements of contractual and relational governance and argue that it is important to understand the interaction between the two at a more discrete level and define three factors for each governance mode. While contractual governance factors include goal expectations, activity expectations, and contractual flexibility, relational governance includes information exchange, trust, and conflict resolution. Presenting interactions among these finer-grained constructs, Rai et al. [30] call for further research on understanding how the roles of the two governance evolve over the course of an outsourcing relationship.

Both streams of literature (substitutive and complementary) agree that a balance between relational and contractual governance is essential. Goo et al. draw from Fitzgerald and Willcocks [8] and Sabherwal [32] to suggest that “excessive focus and reliance on only one of them can hurt performance” [12, p. 137]. We argue that the substitutive argument implies that contractual and relational mechanisms can be considered as conflicting with one another in that implementing one form of governance adversely affects the ability to implement the other. It is therefore important to understand how an organization can balance such conflicting demands on IT outsourcing governance.

To define “balance,” we draw from the work of Cardinal et al. [5]. Balance is defined as a state where an organization exhibits a harmonious use of multiple forms of governance (in our case, contractual and relational). We also subscribe to the notion that balance is not simply about equality or trade-off, and that it refers to the “need to actively consider both types” of governance [5, p. 412]. Also, what is considered as an appropriate balance depends on situational conditions. “Balance” is different from “complementarity,” which specifies that the two forms of governance are not only compatible with one another but they also reinforce one another, resulting in synergistic outcomes. Managers’ decision to focus on one form of governance more than the other may lead to lack of such synergistic outcomes, despite the complementarity between them.

In summary, prior research on the two forms of governance—contractual and relational—has given rise to an interesting debate that provides mixed evidence supporting and refuting complementary and substitutive effects of the two on outcomes. This debate has called for further research on understanding how a balance between them can be reached. Our study contributes to this conversation by examining the conflicts between contractual and relational governance and how a balance between the two can be achieved. It should be noted that prior research on IT outsourcing governance has highlighted the distinction between contract specification and contract enactment [33]. Our focus is completely on the latter.

## Organizational Ambidexterity

In our study, the literature on organizational ambidexterity that theorizes about how organizations address conflicting demands or trade-off situations is of particular relevance. Organizational ambidexterity refers to the ability to simultaneously pursue goals that conflict with one another [11]. Some examples of conflicts include alignment versus adaptability [11], exploitation versus exploration [20], and flexibility versus efficiency [1]. This definition has also been extended to include “the ability to simultaneously balance different activities in a trade-off situation” [31, p. 773]. The literature on organizational ambidexterity provides insights into different types of conflicts faced by organizations, their antecedents, strategies for addressing them, and their consequences [11].

The past literature on organizational ambidexterity has identified several critical antecedents of ambidexterity and the contextual variables that shape ambidexterity. Organizations need to manage the balance between disciplined performance management and a social context that fosters support and trust [9]. Past research has also examined different approaches to achieving ambidexterity. Two prominent recommendations are (1) to partition the explorative and exploitative parts of the organization into separate organizational units and (2) to enable individuals to make decisions about how they balance their time between exploitative and explorative tasks. While the former is referred to as structural ambidexterity, the latter is defined as contextual ambidexterity. In our context, balancing disciplined performance management with social context requires achieving a balance between contractual and relational IT outsourcing governance. We recognize contextual ambidexterity as the most appropriate approach in our setting because the client organization needs to balance contractual and relational governance within the same organizational units. Contextual ambidexterity requires the development of systems and processes that will provide an environment that is conducive for individuals to make decisions that balance the competing demands. Gibson and Birkinshaw [11] argue for simultaneity of the two competing components. This is suitable for our context because the objective is not to substitute one form of governance with the other, but to reach a balance.

Ambidexterity has also been recognized as a critical capability for alliance partners in balancing bridging ties and strong ties [35]. Tiwana [35] argues that while exploring innovations requires bridging ties to span boundaries between heterogeneous entities involved in an alliance, exploiting such capabilities requires strong ties. He adapts Gibson and Birkinshaw’s [11] view to define alliance ambidexterity as the simultaneous pursuit of “alignment with alliance objectives and adaptiveness to changes in the environment” [35, p. 253]. The relevance of ambidexterity in the context of governance for interorganizational relationships is emphasized by Konsynski and Tiwana [17]. In examining the need to balance improvisation and efficiency in interorganizational networks, Konsynski and Tiwana present relational governance and decision rights apportionment as two critical elements of governance that need to be studied for their alignment with IT architecture.

In summary, much of the past literature on organizational ambidexterity emphasizes the need for achieving a balance between conflicting demands faced by organizations and identifies mechanisms that can help organizations address these conflicts. Drawing from the above discussion of the prior literature, we identify three critical elements—conflicts, ambidextrous IT outsourcing governance practices that address these conflicts, and the results of ambidextrous IT outsourcing governance—and use them as seed concepts in analyzing the data collected in our case study. ## Research Methodology

We use a c ase study design [41] to examine how a balance between contractual and relational IT governance is achieved in an IT outsourcing arrangement. Although there is extensive literature on IT governance, conceptualizing the transformation of IT governance and how it evolves when organizations transition to outsourcing is still largely unexplored. Therefore, our research is exploratory rather than confirmatory. A case study approach is suitable in this context because of the lack of frameworks that examine the processes that lead to a balance between the two modes of governance. Also, how these two modes are used has to be examined in real-life settings to help us understand the rich interplay between them. A case study centered on this phenomenon will provide insights into how a balance between two modes of IT outsourcing governance is achieved and will help further research in developing causal questions.

## Site Selection

Following theoretical sampling [27], we selected a site that has transitioned from housing its IT function internally to being outsourced. Our unit of analysis is an organization that comprises business units and the IT unit. Particularly, we focus on the client organization (hereafter referred to as BCo) and how this organization achieved ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance. Although BCo is a subsidiary of a global organization, it is largely independently managed. Therefore, this organization was a suitable candidate as a case study site. BCo was among the largest financial services companies in the country with an annual IT budget of about \$25 million. The IT unit of BCo employed over 450 professionals who were distributed across the areas of IT application services, infrastructure management, and operations. It supported about 10,000 employees of BCo. The vendor was among the top ten largest IT service providers in the world. The transition from in-house to outsourced environment provides us with a fertile ground to gain insights into how the two modes of governance were balanced to address the needs of the client organization.

## Data Collection

Semistructured interviews were conducted to collect data. We also reviewed documentation (including e-mail communication, presentations, meeting minutes, governance documents, and excerpts of internal communication) that described the transition to the outsourced model. Driven by our research objective, we choose as our informants both executives and delivery managers who were involved in the transition process. The profile of the respondents is presented in Table 2. While most of our informants were responsible for IT services from the client side, we also included respondents from the vendor side. All of the informants were involved in all aspects of the outsourcing engagement, including vendor selection, negotiation, contract initiation and structuring, contract operationalization, outcomes review and evaluation, and contract change management.

Our interviews centered on understanding how contractual and relational governance practices were balanced. We investigated the various governance processes, the rationale, and the outcomes. Each interview lasted one to two hours. All of the interviews were audio recorded and transcribed. In addition, detailed notes were taken during the interviews. We also conducted follow-up interviews and engaged in e-mail correspondence to seek clarifications. Data collection was tightly interwoven with data analysis. As new data were collected, they were used to refine and/or modify concepts that were identified early on and to guide further data collection. Data collection and analysis were undertaken until “theoretical saturation” [6] was reached, wherein further data did not provide any new insights.

## Data Analysis

We started our data analysis with the seed concepts drawn from the literature on organizational ambidexterity (conflicts, ambidextrous IT outsourcing governance practices, and the results of ambidextrous IT outsourcing governance). New concepts emerging from our analysis were then related to the seed concepts. Our primary focus was on identifying how the governance modes evolved during the outsourcing engagement. Text fragments ranging from phrases to sentences from the interview transcripts were tagged with codes along with justifications for the selection of codes. Finally, key themes were identified based on the codes that emerged [27], which were then synthesized into a framework presented in the next section. Data from the vendor side as well as a review of the documents helped triangulate the findings, which are primarily based on data from the client organization. Minor adjustments were made to the framework based on feedback from the study participants. All the authors analyzed the qualitative data separately, and then the results were debated and modified until agreement was reached.

The data analysis process described above helped us establish the plausibility and consistency of the concepts, subcategories, and relationships that emerged. Furthermore, it ensured that our conceptual framework satisfies the established criteria for the credibility and validity of qualitative research [23, 28]. Theoretical relevance of the concepts was established by their repeated presence or notable absence [34] without any attempt to statistically evaluate the strength of these concepts [34]. While the findings from such analyses may be detailed and particularistic, they can be used to develop a general explanation [6, 26] through the use of analytic generalization [41].

## Findings

Contrac tual governanc e that foc uses on effic ientl y m anaging an outsourcing engagement conflicted with relational governance that is focused on responding quickly to business needs. BCo continually adapted its governance practices to address these conflicting demands to achieve ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance. In this section, we first describe the conflicts and then we describe in detail the four stages that the organization went through in achieving ambidexterity through practices that address these conflicts.

## Conflicts Between Contractual and Relational Governance

In this section, we present various conflicts between contractual and relational governance faced by BCo after it outsourced its IT service. We discuss several examples of the conflicts that affected the IT unit’s responsiveness to business requests, flexibility in satisfying changing or unique business needs, and the decision rights held by the vendor and the client.

After outsourcing, BCo paid for the services provided by the vendor as per a service catalog. In order to ensure that payments were made only for the services provided, each service was well defined and tracked. This transition caused some tensions between the client IT organization and the vendor because the former was concerned about meeting business needs as quickly as possible, whereas the latter required careful documentation, tracking, and evaluation of the services provided so that the client could be billed for these services. For example, when a client business unit needed some application servers for a project within two weeks, the vendor took more than a month to respond because they had to follow a process defined in the contract for adding new infrastructure components. A client IT service delivery manager explained how this new process slowed down the responsiveness to user needs:

Here the business is ready to pay for the infrastructure; however, they [the vendor] were informing us that first they will have to analyze existing infrastructure, which has the capacity to share the requirement for a new application. [If available], they will host the application in the shared infrastructure. If not, then they will process a request for new servers. (Client IT service delivery manager) (The text in the parentheses after each quotation identifies the role played by a respondent, rather than a particular respondent.)

Strictly meeting the contractual terms frequently conflicted with responding to the dynamically changing needs of the client. Initially, governance processes were designed to ensure that the metrics used to assess the performance of the vendor (specified in the contract as service-level agreements [SLAs]) were clearly defined and tracked so that the client could ascertain that the vendor satisfied the SLAs. While the vendor focused on providing services that were specified in the SLAs and on meeting SLA targets, BCo sometimes had to make critical requests for services that were not specified in the SLAs. For example, when a regulatory body ordered all the institutions to immediately roll out some control features, BCo was unable to comply in a timely fashion because of the delays involved in getting the vendor to provide these services. In essence, even though the vendor was meeting all the targets specified in the SLAs, critical requirements of BCo were not being met.

Moreover, the contract was defined based on generic templates of governance practices that the vendor had developed in other outsourcing engagements. Consequently, these practices were not tailored to suit the unique needs of BCo. Since it was impossible to specify all of the detailed activity expectations up front in the contract, the vendor found it difficult to ascertain whether some of the requests made by BCo were within the scope of the contract. When the vendor was not sure whether the requests were indeed within the scope of the contract, the vendor decided not to address them. Although there were clauses in the governance document that acknowledged that the governance processes and structures defined in the contract may need to be periodically reviewed and revised, it was often impractical to amend the contract.

There will be a sort of a resistance when it comes to amendment of the contract because, if you try to insert or delete a clause, the number of people who need to get involved is too high. It actually creates a nuisance. It will be a weighted decision. (Vendor country project executive)

A significant change that resulted from outsourcing was the dispersion of decision rights. Prior to outsourcing, all critical decisions regarding IT investments were made by the client IT organization. However, a majority of these decisions rights were transferred to the vendor after outsourcing. The role of the IT organization at BCo had changed from delivering IT services to managing the relationship between the vendor and the business units. Relationship managers, who were in fact IT delivery managers before outsourcing, were still tasked to ensure that the needs of the business units were met. However, they had little control or authority over the technical decisions that were made by the vendor. As a result, these relationship managers felt that they were held responsible for decisions over which they had little control.

The business was impacted. Business used to hit the delivery managers. But nothing was in our control at that point of time. (Client IT service delivery manager)

The conflict also relates to problems in knowledge transfer. After outsourcing, the vendor often did not possess the business knowledge that was essential for satisfying customer requests. Transfer of knowledge from BCo to the vendor did not occur effectively because the vendor initially relied on institutionalized mechanisms such as documentation of business processes and records of IT operations for knowledge transfer. They quickly realized that these mechanisms were inadequate. For example, the inventory of IT assets given to the vendor initially was not accurate. The vendor could not fulfill some service requests because they did not know that some of the infrastructure for which services were requested even existed.

If we go to the vendor, they say they do not have any clarity on any issues, where the infrastructure is lying, how many data centers are in the region, how many servers we have in the region, what operating systems are running, what builds are running, what type configurations are in the data centers? We do not have any specific inventory. (Client IT service delivery manager)

BCo started implementing practices to address these conflicts, thereby achieving ambidexterity.

## Process of Achieving Ambidexterity: Ambidexterity Pendulum

The goal of achieving ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance by addressing the conflicts was not reached in a single step. In fact, it was achieved finally through a process of adjustment, swinging back and forth between contractual and relational governance (as illustrated in Figure 1).

The four stages in this process are as follows: (Stage 0) preoutsourcing: focus on quick response to customer requests and adapting to changing business needs; postoutsourcing: (Stage 1) provide IT services as specified in the contract, (Stage 2) focus on relational governance practices that help address business needs, and (Stage 3) finally strike an appropriate balance between contractual and relational governance and achieve ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance through practices that address the conflicts.

## Stage 0: Preoutsourcing

Before outsourcing, IT governance was geared toward meeting the needs of the business units as quickly as possible. The strategic goal was to ensure that the technology infrastructure and IT services supported the mission of the business units. Procedures were established based on a clear understanding of business priorities and goals.

While satisfying customer needs was the primary focus and the strategic goal, the performance of the IT organization was not subjected to detailed assessment against any well-defined performance metrics. IT personnel had a thorough understanding of the impact of the services requested by the business units on business performance. The IT organization was less concerned about developing standardized processes for delivering services. Different procedures were enacted to handle different types of requests. As a result, the IT organization relied heavily on the mutual understanding developed between IT personnel who possessed specialized knowledge about various aspects of the business and the technology that supported them, and the customers in business units who were familiar with the specific procedures followed to satisfy the various types of requests for services.

We knew whom to go to if we had a problem. For example, if I could not log in or something was acting weird in my application, I knew whom to call and solve my issue. I would call [a specific IT personnel] if there was a login problem to the domain controller; he was the expert. (Client IT relationship manager)

In essence, BCo was tilted more toward relational governance in Stage 0, preoutsourcing.

## Stage 1: Standard Framework Defined in the Contract

After outsourcing, a standardized governance framework was implemented to bring structure to the IT services delivery process. Focusing on the contractual governance caused issues as manifested by the conflicts described in the previous section. For example, the vendor’s service engineers followed approval processes that were bureaucratic and hierarchical:

From the user’s perspective, something which was able to deliver in a couple of weeks [before outsourcing] now took months. (Client IT relationship manager)

Also, strict adherence to performing only those activities that were specified in the contract conflicted with the need to satisfy evolving business needs of the customer. For example, when some of the business processes used by BCo had to be changed because of changing business needs, it was necessary to request the vendor to make corresponding changes. The IT head of a core business unit explained this with an example on a change control process that was owned by the vendor and had to be modified due to a problem identified in an audit:

We had to bring in stricter control to the change control process, such as patch installation process on the application servers was not the vendor’s responsibility. But the vendor security team was the custodian of super user IDs [of the application servers too] and the super user IDs were needed to install a patch on those servers. We were stuck. (Client IT head of core business unit)

Moreover, the contract is defined at a very broad level that failed to address the context specific needs of BCo. For example, while SLAs for the performance of servers were specified in the contract, the fact that this performance was significantly affected by the power supply and air conditioning that was provided by BCo was not taken into account. More generally, the contract did not take into account the dependencies between BCo’s unique setting and its impact on metrics specified in the contract.

When you do the measurements on the SLA, you would probably define in your contract what kind of measurements you are going to have and what kind of metrics in which it will be measured. But those are not sufficient to bridge the gaps between the many different dependencies that crop up. (Vendor country project executive)

As a consequence of the dispersion of decision rights after outsourcing, the client IT staff often had to perform some activities to quickly meet the critical needs of the business units even though in the contract assigned the responsibility for performing these activities to the vendor. Losing decision rights while still being held accountable prompted the client IT organization to hold onto critical knowledge with the notion that this would provide them the ability to respond to business needs. As a result, a significant amount of knowledge that was necessary for effective service delivery was tacitly held by the client IT organization without being shared formally with the vendor.

Those [knowledge] were not migrated [to vendor]. The vendor had no clue where to pick up information from. We observed that the SAs [system administrators] or DBAs [database administrators] they deployed on site were incompetent to take care of these kinds of challenges. Previous to the migration model, the team [that] used to work in support, they went back to their own company. The proper handover mechanism was not in place, which would help them to deal with such kind of challenges. (Client IT service delivery manager)

Therefore, the outsourcing arrangement faced enormous challenges at this stage. The two parties had significant disagreements on meeting business expectations. The vendor believed that they had fulfilled the requirements of the contract as specified in the SLAs, while the client felt that some critical business needs were not met. These disagreements almost led to a legal action.

The first few months at the start of the contract were horrible. . . . The vendor used to flaunt SLAs which they had met [as per the contract] whereas we, as client delivery managers, felt nothing was delivered to meet end-user expectations. Our business was not satisfied. And we used to get the brunt of the anger from our business critical people. There was a frustration in the air as the name of the previous serving vendor also got a mention. In fact, a few times meetings were abandoned without any outcomes, with both sides escalating to their own management. I also heard that it was on the verge of being sent to legal for termination. (Client IT service delivery manager)

In summary, Stage 1 highlights the imbalance in that BCo had swung more toward contractual governance while relational aspects were largely neglected.

## Stage 2: Flexible Process Facilitated by the Relationship

Recognizing the inability to depend entirely on contractual governance to address the conflicts and problems in Stage 1, the vendor and the client IT organization attempted to design a flexible process based on relational governance. However, some practices that were implemented to establish and strengthen the relationship between the two parties sometimes overly emphasized relational governance. In this stage, the goal was to provide services with the same level of flexibility and speed as was done by internal IT prior to outsourcing.

We tried to put a new process in place, tried to change our own processes so that we get the deliveries which were expected by us and could have been delivered by the outsourced vendors. (Client IT head of application services)

This stage involved negotiations between the parties, resulting in a set of governance mechanisms that focused more on a collaborative, trust-based, relational approach. With the revised focus on building relationships between the client and the vendor and quickly responding to customer requests, both the organizations agreed to relax some of the governance processes initially identified in the engagement. For example, certain processes such as monitoring and tracking were skipped because they hindered the speed of service delivery. The SLAs defined in the contract were also relaxed. But in some instances, the parties went overboard in establishing relational forms of governance. For example, to satisfy the need to continue the relationship, processes that were required to handle issues faced by the client were bypassed and a solution that was not acceptable to the audit team was used.

Being a global account, our directive from our senior management was to make the customer happy. We were pulled up in our internal review meetings regularly. We were always in red. Hence we went and started agreeing to many things informally to maintain good relationships with the customer. In this process we went overboard few times [and] got pulled up by our internal audit again. And we were given strict warning from top—to deliver as per the contract only. (Vendor delivery lead)

The vendor delivery lead provided an example of the flexible process followed in this stage:

In one case, on a Monday early morning, one desktop on the trading floor was not booting with the user’s ID and it was time for the trading to open in a few minutes. Our engineer being pressurized by the user, who was a senior person in the [BCo] logged in with an admin ID. And there was so much escalation after that. (Vendor delivery lead)

Such a swing back to a relational focus from a contractual focus caused major issues. For example, the lack of contractual governance mechanisms for monitoring and control in certain processes led to problems in the quality of IT services delivered.

The services of locally deployed application are not started properly after due maintenance downtime, or space is not monitored, housekeeping is not done, . . . There are many such examples which you can also think of which are BAU [business as usual] in nature and hit us suddenly as we are not doing monitoring or check at our level and solely dependent on the respective sign-off from the various groups. (Client IT relationship manager)

Furthermore, overemphasis on relational governance led to some unexpected issues such as the neglect of some critical activities. For example, an IT team that was responsible for “clearing financial transactions” had to come early in the morning to upload data files before the commencement of business operations for the day. However, on a few occasions, the team that was charged with providing this service was busy handling other “urgent” requests from the client and therefore did not perform this activity. As a result, entire operations of the business unit came to a screeching halt.

Hence, suddenly business faces issues and the entire operational activity goes haywire on a given day, nothing much the management or site manager can do in such cases other than doing back and forth [with trial and error]. (Client IT relationship manager)

In essence, while both parties gradually recognized the need for a balance between formal governance structures and collaborative relationship management, they realized that they now had a lopsided focus on relational governance (in Stage 2).

## Stage 3: Balanced Contractual and Relational Governance

BCo realized that ambidexterity could not be achieved by overemphasizing either contractual or relational mechanisms and took actions to bring a balance between the two. The problems that resulted from overemphasis on relational governance in Stage 2 were mitigated by reinforcing some contractual governance practices. The practices employed in Stage 2 were revisited to make sure critical processes were followed and high-impact issues were properly tracked and resolved. For example, governance mechanisms such as adding vendor representatives as site mangers and instituting regular meetings between the site managers and the client were put in place. Formal procedures to provide clear definitions of decision rights and accountability and frequent updates on service requests on critical processes were implemented.

We speak to them on weekly basis again and asking for formal MOM [minutes of meeting] and action/tracking list every week for critical items. (Client IT relationship manager)

Another action taken was to reinstitute a hierarchical approval process for most of the tasks. For example, in Stage 2, the client managers often skipped the defined communication hierarchy and began to communicate directly with the vendor’s support engineers. Although such direct communication was effective in getting a quick response, some members of the management team who had decision rights and accountability for these services were not adequately informed or consulted about critical decisions. To overcome this problem, a process for reporting and requesting approvals was redefined in this stage.

This [the redefined approval process] helps us to ensure that we are not missing anyone [in the loop] and they [members of the management team] are equally responsible for delivery of critical change or fixes deployment and understand the impact of failure, beside they are made accountable. (Client IT relationship manager)

Finally, in Stage 3, BCo had reached equilibrium in that the organization had instituted practices that achieved a balance between contractual and relational governance. Below, we describe these practices in detail.

## Ambidextrous IT Outsourcing Governance Practices

## Planned Processes with Collaboration

Additional governance procedures that facilitated collaboration between the vendor and the client were instituted with the objective of increasing the vendor’s understanding of business processes. Examples of such actions include the institution of daily early morning telephone calls between the vendor and client IT organization, the use of checklists (e.g., to ensure that the activities required to be completed at the beginning of day and end of day), and the assignment of a vendor representative at each major client site as the site manager. A vendor project service manager elaborated on the impact of these changes in governance practices on the IT services provided to support an online trading system. This system needed some critical file updates before the trading floor opened each day. Governance mechanisms such as checklists and daily morning meetings that were not specified in the contract helped ensure that the vendor understood the impact of these updates on business outcomes.

These [morning calls] were not there [before]. But they were more like local controls, put for delivery organization to ensure that they do the due diligence. They do their check points. . . . We also had these intermediary checks. We had specific engineers stationed locally who would be attending to issues. We had specific people stationed only to support specific businesses. (Client IT relationship manager)

In addition, the vendor and the client agreed to redesign some governance procedures (such as approval processes) that occasionally delayed service delivery. Priorities for varies types of incidents were defined clearly. For critical incidents, service was delivered even before the necessary written approvals were fully obtained, but approvals were subsequently obtained and documented. Redesigned governance procedures facilitated collaboration between the vendor and the client, increased mutual trust, and increased the flexibility with which defined processes were enacted.

## Goal Expectation with Relationship Building

The overall goal expectations of both parties were reviewed and communicated across the client and the vendor organizations. Additional mechanisms such as review meetings were set up at different levels of the respective organizational hierarchies to address issues that were not specified in the contract. For example, incident managers and delivery engineers of the vendor periodically met with delivery managers of the client. The frequency of these meetings ranged from daily, to weekly, to monthly, to quarterly as their focus varied from operational to tactical to strategic levels. Performance metrics were reviewed in monthly meetings, whereas operational issues were addressed on a daily basis. All the parties involved in IT outsourcing governance participated in monthly review meetings. During these meetings, expectations and issues related to service delivery were also discussed and agreed upon.

[We] work out the service improvement plan that was monitored by the service management team on aggregation—whether we improved for the period of time, what we improved, and what was the acceptable level. (Client IT head—core business unit)

Since the meetings helped communicate goal expectations at different levels of the hierarchy of both the vendor and BCo, they helped develop a shared understanding between the parties and strengthen their relationship.

Slowly, both of us [client and vendor] tried to understand each other: I will try to implement what their framework is and they [vendor] tried to become flexible and understanding the business needs and how they can support. (Client IT relationship manager)

As a result of the strengthened relationship, the SLAs defined in the contract were followed in a more pragmatic fashion by allowing deviations when necessary. For example, when BCo requested essential services that were not defined in the contract, they were fast-tracked without strictly following the requirements defined in the SLAs. Furthermore, engineers with deep technical and domain knowledge in their assigned business units were identified. They served as first responders to troubleshoot or escalate business issues in a timely fashion.

We had an entire organization focused on and trying to communicate this perspective to get them [the vendor] beyond the SLA mind-set and move to a delivery perspective. We had people identified of all the critical businesses. For [a business unit] I know there was somebody who was specifically identified. He would not wait for call to be logged and a call to be coming through. We kind of structured it business-wise. (Client IT relationship manager)

## Contextualized Control with Mutual Understanding

BCo continuously tailored the standardized processes so that they could be better understood in its unique business context. The development of these mechanisms required that both parties understand and agree on contextualized needs. For example, the two parties agreed on a set of critical business requests that will be satisfied even though the contract itself was kept unchanged.

Can we have governance around it and control the required result because at the end of the day it’s a relationship? Certain soft items which come out through the governance and which are more of good to have, those are typically not included into the existing contracts. (Vendor country project executive)

New processes were introduced so that accountability and decision rights were defined more clearly than in the contract. For example, some processes were put in place that made people more accountable and issues easily traceable. These processes had a relational flavor in that they relied on conflict resolution through negotiation and collaboration between the parties.

In the contract reading session, and each and every decision, the biggest key factor is people and the ability of both sides to go beyond the contract and resolve issues. (Vendor country project executive)

## Standardized Operations with Mixed Control

Prior to outsourcing, BCo had primarily used behavior control mechanisms such as procedures for handling business requests. During the initial stages of the contract, the focus shifted to predominantly outcome controls. The vendor’s service performance was monitored and measured using a comprehensive set of metrics. With further adaptations, a mix of both behavior and outcome controls were used. Service performance was measured and analyzed, and new processes were implemented to enhance controls. Recognizing that the outsourcing of IT services may lead to the loss of control, BCo standardized significant parts of some IT operations. For example, the operational process for transferring files from a server in a remote location was evaluated, and an exhaustive checklist of all the activities that had to be performed was developed. These activities were carefully examined for exigency conditions and were standardized with the objective of reducing the options available to the vendor, and thereby minimizing the potential for “mistakes.” As a result,

there are not a lot of avenues for vendor to make mistakes on an operational basis. (Client country head of IT services)

However, BCo recognized that at the tactical and operational level, the vendor retained control over routine and day-to-day activities. Governance processes such as the use of a “gatekeeper” to channel requests and monitor services were set up to maintain control that was needed at these levels.

We have set up that everything has to be routed through delivery manager, who will monitor the requirements, understand the specifications and hand it over to the vendor to take it forward. And similarly, when it comes back, he is the one who will verify the solution by the vendors. (Client IT head—core business unit)

To address the conflict in knowledge transfer, contractual and relational mechanisms were used in a balanced manner. First, much of the technical knowledge was specified and documented in a structured way. Second, relational governance practices, such as workshops, were used to help both vendor and client personnel clearly understand their changed roles and responsibilities. These practices helped develop a closer relationship between the vendor and the client teams. They also helped set up appropriate mechanisms for knowledge transfer from the client to the vendor organization.

[Workshops were held] to make sure of their commitment on the knowledge transfer during the transition on what the internal organization needs to do, as well as from vendor side there is a requirement to be completely supportive during this process. There would be areas where the knowledge would be less than adequate and the vendor has to somehow gather resources from other accounts. (Client country head of IT services)

## Summary

To address the conflicts, BCo’s senior executives engaged the senior executives of the vendor to initiate adaptations of the governance processes. Based on these engagements, governance mechanisms were refined, balancing contractual and relational aspects thereby leading to ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance. Our framework presented in Figure 2 represents this process.

## Results of Ambidextrous IT Outsourcing Governance

By balancing contractual and relational governance, BCo achieved ambidexterity in its IT outsourcing governance, which led to the success of the outsourcing arrangement. Organizational stakeholders considered the outsourcing arrangement to be successful. Senior management had two objectives for the outsourcing arrangement: improving operational efficiencies and reducing the cost of IT operations, and meeting the needs of the users. Both of these goals were indeed achieved.

The balanced governance processes enhanced efficiency, helped develop standardized processes, reduced operational costs, and increased visibility. The adapted versions of industry best practices and governance frameworks helped improve efficiency.

If we look five years back and today, there is a huge difference in the way we are working between 2005 and 2011. We are much more process oriented [now] and much more SLA focused. . . . That is the only way which will give us benefits in the way we serve the customers. (Client country head of IT services)

The objective of achieving significant cost savings was also achieved.

The pricing that was agreed for the outsourcing deal was minus 10 percent of the base rate [cost prior to outsourcing], so necessarily the basic objective was to get a cost benefit of 10 percent. That objective was 100 percent achieved. (Vendor country project executive)

The vendor’s assessment on cost savings was confirmed by the client.

By and large, the benefits were that costs were rigidly kept under control. So it was cost effective. (Client chief operating officer)

Flexibility and responsiveness to customer requests improved considerably. The balanced governance processes helped the IT organization respond to changing business needs quickly. Changing business requirements were handled in a flexible manner with the governance framework that was customized to fit these needs.

Slowly, both of us tried to understand each other: I will try to implement what their framework is and they tried to become flexible and understanding whatever the business needs are and how they can support. So many things which had not been documented, not been provisioned for [in the adapted] framework, which was very high level, has been customized for the context. [The adapted governance framework was able to handle various types of business changes such as] scheduled change or planned change or unscheduled change or something that is emergency, or ad hoc. (Client country head of IT services)

Overall, the goals of the outsourcing arrangement were achieved by the ambidextrous IT outsourcing governance practices developed by the focal organization.

## Discussion

Two k ey im pl ic ations from our findings that help open the black box of the evolutionary process of achieving ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance warrant further discussion: (1) the nature of the balance between contractual and relational governance, contributing to the conversation on complementary versus substitutive roles of these two forms in shaping outcomes, and (2) the evolutionary process of governance mechanisms, with shifting foci between contractual and relational forms.

## Complements, Substitutes, and Balance

While much of the past research on IT outsourcing governance has shifted between substitutive and complementary roles of contractual and relational governance [12, 29, 30], it has also recognized the need for a balance between the two [18]. However, to our knowledge, there are no studies that examine the process used for reaching such a balance. While our objective is not to explicitly seek support for or refute either notion (complementary or substitute), our study draws from the substitutive argument by observing that the substitutive view implies that the two forms of governance conflict with one another. We reinforce the need for both forms of governance and highlight the complexity involved in the process of striking a balance between the two. Some forms of relational governance are used as temporary substitutes, all the while recognizing that eventually they may have to be supported by related contractual mechanisms. For example, to satisfy rapidly changing business needs, the client had to resort to relational forms of governance to engage the vendor. While the vendor adapted the process to incorporate flexibility, the vendor also reverted back to contractual forms of governance post facto so that services were not unaccounted for and that its performance was tracked and monitored. This illustrates the roles played by the two governance mechanisms in contrast to how they have been discussed in prior research. This contribution is particularly interesting when the complement/ substitute notion is considered using the literature on organizational ambidexterity as a theoretical lens [11].

While the prior literature on organizational ambidexterity [11] has argued for a balance between conflicting demands faced by an organization, our study extends this notion in IT outsourcing governance by examining how the need to balance conflicting governance mechanisms is shaped and how the entities involved adjusted their expectations and practices to arrive at a stable set of governance practices. Our findings present interesting extensions to Gupta et al.’s [15] analysis of certain key issues surrounding organizational ambidexterity. Our findings identify caveats to the argument of orthogonality of exploration and exploitation [15]. Gupta et al. concluded that exploration and exploitation are inherently incompatible. While our findings do reinforce this by suggesting that implementing contractual and relational governance requires different mind-sets, our findings also highlight that the two forms of governance need not necessarily be self-reinforcing. BCo went through different stages in adjusting the mind-set to finally reach equilibrium. Our findings also add to Konsynski and Tiwana’s [17] analysis of the improvisation paradox. For example, the nature of coupling between the parties involved and the way in which knowledge is organized are reinforced as key elements that play a role in a balanced governance approach. The process of oscillating across the contractual and relational governance to eventually reach a balance is an interesting contribution of our study to the literature on organizational ambidexterity. While our findings strike some similarity to the notion of disrupted balance examined by Cardinal et al. [5], it is different in that our focus is on the process of reaching a balance in a context that initially exhibited imbalance in governance. One interpretation of the process to achieve a balance presented in our findings is consistent with Cardinal et al.’s view that balance could be dynamic in that it could change over time based on various factors.

Our findings reinforce Gibson and Birkinshaw’s [11] observation of performance management and social context as key antecedents of ambidexterity. The governance process that emerged at BCo demonstrated the need to balance practices that focused on efficiency, cost reduction, and the ability to measure and control IT services with those that focused on mutual understanding, collaboration, and trust. Our study is unique in that it reveals how BCo’s governance processes evolved to finally achieve both performance-oriented and relational elements of governance. This adds a new dimension to the discussion on complementary versus substitutive roles of contractual and relational governance.

## Process of Governance Evolution

The pendulum of shift in governance is similar to the general notion of back and forth oscillation described by Grover and Kettinger [13] in the context of business process change and by Mihm et al. [22] in the context of complex engineering projects. While Grover and Kettinger [13] describe the need to satisfy process- and people-oriented factors in the broader context of organizational change, our findings exemplify this in the context of outsourced IT services. Our observations on the pendulum swings to reach ambidexterity bear some resemblance to Brown’s [4] examination of “The Emergence of Hybrid IS Governance Solutions,” but there are critical differences. Brown argues that hybrid IS governance solutions emerge based on certain characteristics of the business unit context (the autonomy of business units, mechanistic versus organic decision making, etc.). While Brown refers to a “pendulum swing” in IS governance solutions that was observed in a case study, the focus was on understanding decentralization leading to a federal design. Our findings refer to the “pendulum swing” to explain the back and forth changes in IT outsourcing governance that shifted between contractual and relational focus.

Also, our observations of the “ambidexterity pendulum” share some similarities with the notion of vacillation explained by Nickerson and Zenger [25] despite some important differences. While they focus on vacillations between organizational modes as managers attempt to capture efficiencies, they also argue that this is done even under static environmental conditions. Our findings suggest that BCo vacillated between contractual and relational forms of governance under changing conditions (outsourcing, changing business needs, etc.). Also, BCo’s intent was different from what Nickerson and Zenger had described in that the intent was on achieving balance and not on vacillation. This difference is attributed to the assumption by Nickerson and Zenger that choices for organizational modes are discrete in that “desired functionality lies in-between the steady state functionality delivered by two discrete choices” [25, p. 547]. Here, BCo focused on reaching a balance between the two forms of governance, indicating that the governance choices were amenable to blending. Closely related work on vacillation by Boumgarden et al. [3] presents vacillation as an alternative to organizational ambidexterity in an attempt to provide higher levels of exploration and exploitation that are not achievable by the stable balance sought by ambidexterity. Our findings highlight that such a vacillation is impractical in our context and the vacillation observed (ambidexterity pendulum) is the process followed to reach a stable balance between contractual and relational governance.

Our findings present an important connection to the Jensen–Meckling theory [16], which advocates collocation of decision rights with knowledge relevant to exercising these rights effectively. Our findings highlight the extent of knowledge fragmentation across the parties involved in the outsourcing arrangement (client business units, client IT organization, and the vendor) and the misalignment between the distribution of knowledge, both technical and domain, and the locus of decision rights. We extend this notion by presenting mechanisms used to mitigate this misalignment through a process of governance evolution that changed the nature of alignment between decision rights and knowledge location. Outsourcing IT services resulted in fragmenting the decision rights and knowledge location across the client and vendor organizations (Stage 1). This is similar in flavor to the distinction between attempted and realized control highlighted in prior literature on control [38] in that this stage involved some of the steps that the internal IT unit took in attempting to regain lost control. Stage 2 attempts to bridge this gap by swinging in favor of relational governance wherein informal exchange of knowledge helped mitigate the problems. This is then scaled back in Stage 3 to arrive at a balance that enables decision rights to be used appropriately by the vendor as contractual governance is used in conjunction with relational governance.

Our study has significant contributions to practice, primarily to senior executives in organizations who are responsible for designing and implementing IT outsourcing governance structures. Following are some practical recommendations for senior executive teams:

•	 How do we balance formal governance structures with relationship management? Our findings suggest that while outsourcing engagements begin with formal governance processes, they need to be balanced on an ongoing basis with a more relational approach to governance. Senior management teams that are entrenched in traditional IT outsourcing governance when IT operations are internally housed will need to recognize the need to make the transition from an IT delivery–based model to a relationship-based model. While a move from an internally housed IT function to an outsourced function might trigger protective and formal governance structures, senior executives will eventually face the need to develop an appropriate mix of relational and formal elements to governance. Furthermore, they need to recognize the challenges faced in reaching such a balance, acknowledging the need for continued refinement of governance practices.

•	 How do we address challenges in adapting governance structures? While specifying and establishing detailed governance processes up front is necessary, senior executives should also recognize that parts of the governance processes specified up front might turn out to be unrealistic and that experimental customization may be warranted. Senior executives from the vendor organization should also recognize that while formal governance presents them with a clear approach to outcome assessment, such formal elements may need to be customized to address evolving client needs.

Our study presents interesting opportunities for future research. While our findings present various patterns in how governance practices are adapted to reach a balance between contractual and relational modes, it will be useful to understand the contextual conditions that dictate the use of different approaches to reaching a balance. What are the factors that drive organizations to continuously tweak as opposed to having the ability to do one-time, ad hoc adaptations to address specific concerns? Do these processes vary depending on particular characteristics of outsourcing engagements? Are there conditions in which a substitutive or complementary notion leads to better outcomes as opposed to a balance? These are some of the issues that require careful examination in future research.

## Conclusions

Our findings desc rib e the c onfl ic ts between contractual and relational forms of IT outsourcing governance and how these conflicts are mitigated by balancing the two. We present a framework developed based on a case study that describes how this balance is achieved through a process we call the “ambidexterity pendulum.” Our findings reveal that ambidexterity in IT outsourcing governance is achieved through a process of swinging back and forth between focusing on contractual and relational modes of governance. Our study also identifies the practices adopted by the focal organization that address the conflicts and lead to the achievement of the ambidexterity. Overall, our study represents an initial step in understanding how balance between contractual and relational governance is gradually achieved through an evolutionary process. We contribute to the research on IT outsourcing governance by explaining how ambidextrous IT governance can be achieved in this context.
