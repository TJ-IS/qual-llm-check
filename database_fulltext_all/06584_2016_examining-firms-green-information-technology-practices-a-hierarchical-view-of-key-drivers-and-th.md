# Examining Firms’ Green Information Technology Practices: A Hierarchical View of Key Drivers and Their Effects

PAUL JEN-HWA HU, HAN-FEN HU, CHIH-PING WEI, AND PEI-FANG HSU

ABSTRACT: This study examines key drivers of firms’ green information technology (IT) practices. A hierarchical view, premised in institutional theory and competitive dynamics, leads to a model that explains firms’ practices. This model includes factors that pertain to the environment (environmental awareness and government regulations), industry (industry norms and competitors’ green practices), and firm (customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes and internal readiness) levels. Survey data collected from 304 major firms in Taiwan are used to test the model and hypotheses. In particular, attitudes of a firm’s customers and equity holders, as well as its internal readiness, directly influence its green IT practices, while also channeling the effects of important contextual factors. Among the contextual factors considered herein, environmental awareness and industry norms influence firms’ practices both directly and indirectly. The overall results highlight the significance of contextual factors and underscore the mediating roles of firm-specific considerations. According to our findings, firms should implement strategic goals and make resource allocations toward green IT practices that are aligned with the industry-wide atmosphere and general public’s awareness of environmental protection.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: competitive dynamics, environmental actions, firms’ green practices, green information technology, green IT, institutional theory, nomological network, sustainability.

—B. Chamberlin, principal client analyst at HorizonWatching and IBM [14]

Green information technology (IT) practices are increasingly integral to sustainability movements that seek to meet the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability to satisfy the demands of future generations [43, 54, 55, 57, 74]. In general, green IT practices refer to a firm’s systematic efforts to incorporate ecological principles and energy-efficient operations into its technology life cycle, which consists of the design, production, purchase, utilization, and disposal stages [26, 40, 63]. Furthermore, green IT constitutes a crucial, strategic technology [10] that “strives to achieve economic viability and improved system performance and use, while abiding by . . . social and ethical responsibilities” [65, p. 26]. In information systems (IS) research, a relatively new stream has emerged to examine essential issues surrounding green IT, including its practice by firms [54, 81, 88].

As the global business landscape continues to shift toward sustainability, firms need to adapt and respond to growing environmental demands from the general public, tightened government regulations, increased industry self-regulations, and escalated competitive actions in the market [27, 61, 74]. Worldwide, people have become more conscientious about environmental issues and express increasing preferences for green over traditional practices [26, 61]. Many governments are also establishing laws and regulations to foster environmentally friendly technology, manufacturing, imports, operations, and uses [48]. Various industries promote standards and norms that technology manufacturers and user organizations should follow, such that each actor is accountable for its own safe, environmentally friendly operations, disposal, and recycling [75]. These external forces converge to make green practices increasingly significant in firms’ technology decisions and operations [25]. Finally, green IT practices also could stem from firms’ internal strategic considerations and efforts to increase their competitive advantages, such as cost efficiency [25, 40, 60], risk avoidance [22, 83], social cost mitigation [13], positive image [1, 4], or favorable branding [75].

Although expectations of green technology are mounting and the economic benefits of pursuing green practices seem substantial [48, 61, 75], not all firms embrace green IT practices enthusiastically. For example, worldwide investments in renewable energy and energy-smart technologies totaled \$70 billion in the third quarter of 2015, 1 percent less than the same quarter a year earlier [7]. According to a Green Technologies and Practices survey by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 57 percent of U.S. businesses use green technologies and practices to improve their energy efficiency [85]. Such statistics indicate continual developments in green technology, yet ample opportunities for further development of green IT practices by firms remain. Olson [67] attributes firms’ lack of green strategies and practices to the relatively long time required to yield returns on these investments. The continued disparity between the recognized significance of green IT and continuous investments in green IT by firms is intriguing and deserves research attention, especially with studies that consider important factors beyond just financial concerns.

Both external pulls and internal pushes can influence a firm’s green IT practices; thus, key internal and external factors should not be considered independently. As Chen et al. [16] explain, firms usually make organizational decisions on the basis of their overall evaluation of the external competitive environment and internal strategic conditions. However, firms operating in the same environment could react to prevailing external pressures differently. For example, firms’ strategies and actions depend on how they perceive changes in the environment, in accordance with their strategy choice and resource (capability) availability [19]. Therefore, firm-specific factors directly influence firm decisions and behaviors, and could funnel the effects of external (contextual) forces as well.

Accordingly, we propose a hierarchical view of the effects of key drivers on firms’ green IT practices: from contextual factors, to firm-specific considerations, to firm practices. Building on this hierarchical view, premised in organization theories, we identify important drivers at different levels and analyze their influence paths to develop a model of firms’ green IT practices. Survey data from 304 Taiwanese firms support the model and most of the associated hypotheses. In the next section, we review relevant previous research to highlight the gaps that motivate our study.

## Literature Review

Among emerging information technologies, green IS and green IT are gaining momentum and have attracted growing attention from both researchers and practitioners [43]. In general, green IS refers to the development and use of information systems that contribute to sustainable business processes; green IT instead focuses on energy efficiency and technology equipment utilization [43, 86]. Despite their parallel relevance and close association, a distinction exists between green IS and green IT. Green IS offers a broader scope, spanning an integrated, cooperating set of people, processes, software, and technology [86]. The value of green IT instead can be recognized, evaluated, and realized more easily in practice by firms. We focus on green IT herein, because it represents an essential step toward environmentally friendly firm practices. In particular, we examine how firms decide whether to practice green IT, as conducted by Chen et al. [16] in a similar setting, using finegrained analyses and empirical testing at the organizational level.

Previous research analyzes the essential motivations that lead firms to practice green IT and presents key factors driving their adoptions of green practices. Many studies are exploratory in nature and feature conceptual analyses from an economic or moral perspective [4, 43, 57, 79]. Economic motives are a common focus [10]. For example, Ambec and Lanoie [1] examine key economic benefits of green IT and suggest that enhanced environmental performance can improve firms’ financial performance, beyond the costs of “going green.” Siegel [75] argues that a firm should implement green practices only if they complement its business strategy and contribute to its profitability. Chou and Chou [22] highlight the importance of the business value of green IT, in terms of monetary benefits or economic growth. Moral considerations represent another common emphasis. According to Marcus and Fremeth [55], firms pursue green practices to meet their social or moral obligations for sustainable development. Similarly, Babiak and Trendafilova [2] advocate that corporate social responsibilities constitute an essential motive for firms to adopt green practices.

As we summarize in Table 1, our review of the frequently cited studies on this topic suggests that extant research targets different issues related to green IT practices, often embracing a broad agenda [33, 43, 57, 86] or aiming to develop a high-level conceptual framework [8, 60]. For example, Butler [11] and Sarkar and Young [72] conduct case studies to explore essential drivers of green IT implementation and propose pertinent research models. Survey methods also have been used to assess firm readiness for green IT [63] or examine motivations for green IT implementation [59, 62, 73]. By and large, previous studies focus on internal motives or business outcomes, rather than providing a comprehensive depiction of firms’ practices according to an appropriate theoretical lens [10].

In another relevant research approach that emphasizes external (contextual) forces, several studies apply institutional theory to analyze green IT adoptions or green initiative implementations by firms [2]. For example, Daly and Butler [25] and Butler [11] develop models that suggest firms should introduce green IT if it is congruent with an institutional rationale, though they do not test their models empirically. Chen et al. [15] explore the effects of institutional pressures on firms’ adoption of green IT by viewing institutional forces as broad concepts rather than distinguishing different forces, specifying the respective sources of institutional pressures, or scrutinizing their patterns of influence on firms. Although these studies underline the importance of institutional forces and the resulting (external) pressures, they lack detailed, theory-based analyses of how different institutional forces jointly shape a firm’s green IT practices, and provide limited (if any) empirical evidence of the different sources of external pressures and their influence patterns. Despite recognition of contextual factors for firms’ practices, their direct and indirect effects warrant further scrutiny and empirical testing, especially with a theory-based holistic approach that simultaneously considers external pressures stemming from society, government, industry, and competitors in the market [8].

Essential forces that steer a firm’s green IT practices also could emanate from internal sources. According to Elliot [33], after recognizing sustainability issues in the environment, firms should evaluate their strategic choices and capabilities to develop green initiatives, then assess opportunities by considering their customers and (future) business. This view underscores the significance of a firm’s internal readiness, or the level of resources and capabilities a firm has available for a new technology or practice [42].<sup>1</sup> Furthermore, attitudes of the customers and equity holders may also affect a firm’s green IT practices, because they can shape the firm’s strategic directions [36].

Scant research considers firm-specific factors, in combination with important external forces. This indicates the need for finer-grained analyses that connect contextual factors and firm-specific considerations to explain firms’ green IT practices [8, 33, 43]. Although previous research has considered different external factors, how these drivers at different levels collectively determine a firm’s green IT practices remains unclear. As Jenkin et al. [43] note, to find ways to overcome the challenges of green IT implementation, we need appropriate theoretical premises that incorporate environmental drivers at different levels. Furthermore, even though contextual factors that constitute fundamental sources of institutional forces often exist at different levels [30], relatively little research addresses those external (contextual) factors at different levels from a theoretical lens or empirically examines their respective effects on firms’ green IT practices. In particular, the relationships of contextual factors at different levels and firm-specific considerations have been overlooked, especially those with hierarchical delineations. By addressing these gaps, we seek a fuller explanation of firms’ green IT practices that includes both contextual and firm-specific factors. We propose a hierarchical view, premised on institutional theory and competitive dynamics, to examine the effects of key drivers, together with their respective relationships, on firms’ practices.

## Theoretical Foundation

A firm’s decision to practice green IT can be understood according to the dynamics between the firm and its environment. As Chen et al. [18] explain, to maintain competitive advantages, firms must identify important changes in the environment (market), evaluate their own capabilities and future development opportunities, and then execute proper responses. This view describes how firms receive and interpret key changes in the environment. At the outset, contextual forces create and convey external pressures that require appropriate decisions and behaviors. The effects of the contextual factors can be explained by institutional theory, which holds that the environment provides information, resources, and opportunities for firms’ businesses and operations while simultaneously exerting external pressures on them [29]. To maintain their legitimacy, firms seek to respond effectively to such pressures, such that they homogenize their practices [79].

The external pressures that prevail in the environment often emanate from several fundamental institutional forces: coercive, normative, and mimetic [29]. In general, coercive pressures are created by regulations or associated with the essential entities on which a firm depends, often conveyed through laws, standards, or compliance requirements. Normative pressures usually relate to social obligations or professional values; firms might choose to comply with salient norms to fulfill social responsibilities or achieve professionalization at a high level. Mimetic pressures arise when firms seek to model others’ actions to reduce uncertainty in a competitive (changing) environment, gain legitimacy or resources, and ensure their survival. Together, these institutional forces nourish and bolster external pressures that can influence firms decisions and behaviors, including firms’ green IT practices in an industry [29].

In essence, institutional theory explains how environmental, regulatory, and market competitive factors create external pressures for firms [2, 44]. Facing coercive pressures to practice green IT, firms might comply with the relevant government regulations or with the norms and standards established in the industry, whether for expediency or to avoid the consequences of noncompliance [41]. Normative pressures for practicing green IT could also be transmitted through the general public’s growing awareness of sustainability, the salient norms and principles recognized in an industry, or specific practices recommended by highly regarded professional societies [44]. Furthermore, because green practices are relatively new, the associated uncertainty creates mimetic pressures, such that firms may consider their competitors’ decisions and actions [3, 8, 44].

Facing prevalent external pressures in the environment, firms respond with decisions and actions that manifest their reactions to contextual forces [42]. A firm’s response to external pressures might be explained by competitive dynamics, as reflected in the specific, observable moves the firm makes to defend or improve its competitive position [18, 28]. In general, competitive dynamics emphasizes several fundamental behavioral drivers: awareness, motivation, and capability [17, 19, 76]. A firm’s response to a changing condition in the environment begins with its awareness of a shift in the environment. The firm then reacts with a motive to create territorial market interests or maintain a competitive position valued by stakeholders [19] that include customers and equity holders. Finally, the firm configures the necessary resources and mobilizes its capabilities to achieve its motive and defined objectives. The availability of such resources and capabilities then determines the firm’s internal readiness, signifying whether the firm is ready to respond to a change [23]. The awareness–motivation– capability chain reveals how firms develop competitive strategies and choose business actions in response to institutional pressures in a changing environment [76]. The essence of competitive dynamics coincides with the premises of dynamic capabilities [32, 80]—that is, the firm’s ability to obtain competitive advantages by leveraging its technological, organizational, and managerial resources to achieve desirable congruence with changes in the environment [16]. Typically, dynamic capabilities emphasize a firm’s capabilities in reconfiguring its resources and strengths with flexibility, in response to dynamic changes in the environment. Both competitive dynamics and dynamic capabilities highlight the importance of firm-specific considerations (resource configurations and strategic objectives) for achieving competitive advantages, which affect firms’ behaviors while also channeling the effects of contextual forces.

Overall, institutional theory indicates the significance of contextual forces that lead to the external pressures firms face; competitive dynamics instead reveals the importance of firm-specific considerations by explaining how firms respond to external pressures according to their own considerations and internal conditions. Thus key factors internal to a firm can explain the differential decisions and behaviors exhibited by firms that compete in the same business environment. Furthermore, important contextual factors often pertain to different levels, such that factors at a broader level (e.g., competitive environment) can affect factors at a more defined level (e.g., industry) [30], and together they influence firm-specific considerations. The theoretical foundation described herein suggests a hierarchical view that delineates the respective effects of key factors on firms’ green IT practices. Building on this hierarchical view, we can develop a model and hypotheses to explain firms’ practices by identifying and connecting the key driving forces.

## Model and Hypotheses

In Figure 1, we propose that a firm’s green IT practices are influenced by both contextual factors and firm-specific considerations, as perceived and recognized by firms. We distinguish contextual factors at the environment and industry levels, which can affect a firm’s green IT practices both directly and indirectly, through firm-specific considerations. The environment-level factors represent pressures at a broad level, such as public awareness of environmental protection and government regulations. The industry-level factors apply to all firms in the same industry, such as industry norms and competitors’ green practices. According to institutional theory, a firm’s green IT practices can reflect coercive pressures from government regulations and industry norms, normative pressures from growing trends toward green practices and industry norms, and mimetic pressures due to competitors’ actions.

Important firm-specific considerations include the firm’s internal readiness for, as well as its customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes toward, green IT practices, in line with the notion of organizational readiness as a capability [23] and a stakeholder view of the firm [36], respectively. The former signifies whether a firm is ready for change by mobilizing the needed resources and capabilities toward green IT practices; the latter highlights the influences of essential entities in a firm’s technology choices and practices. We consider customers and equity holders, because they represent essential stakeholders of a firm and usually have significant influences on its business and operations. Firms must place customers in the center of their decisions and operations [70]; equity holders “own” the firm and have direct and vested interests in its offerings, image, and performance [1, 75].

Although our research model primarily adopts a hierarchical view, we also consider the probable relationships of factors at the same level (i.e., industry norms influencing competitors’ green IT practices, and customers’ and equity holders attitudes affecting internal readiness for green practices), as suggested by our theoretical foundation.

The attitudes of customers and equity holders represent a crucial determinant of firms’ reactions to changes in the environment [1]. To ensure market competitiveness and performance, firms need to manage the expectations of customers and equity holders, and comply with their preferences or requirements [36, 37, 75]. The cohesion with their attitudes enables firms to establish legitimacy, because it helps them make more conceptually appropriate and culturally supported decisions [73]. For example, positive customer assessments of and preferences for a firm’s operations, products, or services enhance firm performance [6, 84]. Equity holders usually demand sustainable performance and orchestrated efforts to reduce uncertainty or downside risks [1]. Empirical evidence shows that the preferences of stakeholders, including those of the customers and equity holders, can prompt firms to adopt environmental practices [73].

A firm’s internal readiness for change is a precursor of implementing an initiative, new practice, new technology, or innovation [63]. A firm’s readiness for green practices reveals an overall manifestation of its internal condition and ability to practice green IT [60]. That is, internal readiness describes a firm’s awareness of, commitment to, and resources for green IT practices, in light of competitive dynamics [23]. Firms that allocate more of their resources toward a new technology make better progress in terms of responding to the new technology [45]. Because green IT is relatively new to many firms, its practice requires them to possess the necessary resources, processes, guidelines, understanding, consensus, and knowledge to make targeted changes in their technology choices and operations [82]. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1: A firm’s green IT practices positively relate to (a) attitudes of the customers and equity holders toward green IT practices, and (b) internal readiness for green IT practices, as perceived by the firm.

Industry norms, an essential contextual factor at the industry level, signify the values shared by firms and professional peers in an industry and therefore can create normative pressures for firms [11]. To maintain industrial homogeneity and ensure the total profit pool, firms tend to conform to industry norms to some degree. To establish legitimacy, firms observe others’ actions and seek to follow the practices recommended by leading professional organizations [4]. Such conformity propagates salient norms in an industry [58]. As Jennings and Zandbergen [44] note, a normative agreement to engage in green IT practices might be conveyed by professional networks, which also determine firms legitimacy.

Competitors’ decisions and behaviors represent another important contextual factor within the industry. In a dynamic environment marked by rapid changes and tremendous uncertainty, firms often monitor competitors’ actions and respond accordingly [19, 29]. In light of institutional theory, competitors’ actions exert mimetic pressures on firms [11, 13], prompting them to model themselves on others in the organizational field to reduce uncertainty [3, 29, 56]. That is, competitors’ actions induce mimicry and strategic changes by firms [27], such as adopting environmentally friendly practices or green initiatives [44, 71]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: A firm’s green IT practices positively relate to (a) industry norms regarding environmental sustainability, and (b) competitors’ green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Because of their vested interests, customers and equity holders constantly make comparisons among firms; their expectations of a firm are a function of what they perceive as the norms for an appropriate referent group [49], such as firms in the same industry or its direct competitors. Customers are crucial to a firm and usually expect it to meet industry standards and norms. They choose among products and services offered by competing firms; if they observe green practices by a firm’s competitors, they likely expect the firm to practice in a similar way [71]. Equity holders are also essential to a firm and often expect that their invested firm does not deviate from a competitive market position but instead conforms to salient industry norms, to maintain a positive corporate image and brand [36]. These equity holders have a significant, direct interest in the firm and care deeply about its performance and competitiveness in relation to competitors; thus, their attitudes toward green practices might be influenced by competitors’ actions.

The normative pressures resulting from industry norms encourage firms to invest more resources in promising areas for legitimacy [68, 79]. To remain legitimate members of an industry, firms should observe and conform to prevailing industry norms; thus, firms are more inclined toward practicing green IT if salient norms in the industry recommend such practices [4]. When a new practice becomes the norm in the industry, it may lead to network effects that encourage individual firms to follow that norm [68]. Furthermore, the mimetic pressures from competitors’ actions can trigger a firm to allocate resources and capabilities for green practices, which improves its readiness for greening its own technology practices [4, 8, 60]. By observing competitors’ green IT practices, a firm likely becomes more serious about imitating such practices, then gauges and improves its readiness accordingly. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a: A firm’s perceptions of customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes toward green IT practices positively relate to (1) industry norms regarding environmental sustainability, and (2) competitors’ green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Hypothesis 3b: A firm’s internal readiness for green practices positively relates to (1) industry norms regarding environmental sustainability, and (2) competitors’ green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Rapidly spreading trends exert impacts and steer firms’ behaviors toward technology strategies that incorporate environmentally friendly practices [67]. According to institutional theory, the perception that the general public increasingly prefers green practices can create pressures on firms [15], demanding that they fulfill corporate social responsibilities and moral obligations. Competitive advantages can also accrue from the practice of green IT that addresses the general public’s increasing environmental awareness and expectations [30, 31]. Thus, environmental awareness among the general public should encourage firms to explore green practices that could conserve natural resources while potentially improving their operational efficiency and costs.

In addition, firms consider government regulations in their strategic decision making to avoid a loss of legitimacy or viability [27]. Legislative imperatives might foster green IT practices by firms, even in the absence of substantial, tangible economic benefits [60]. Firms seek to reduce their liability risks and avoid costly litigation, which encourages them to practice green IT [1]. Moreover, governments can establish regulatory frameworks that provide incentives for compliance with green standards and practices; the associated laws and regulations could fuel a green economy by granting nontrivial incentives to firms that engage in green practices. Therefore, we test the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4: A firm’s green IT practices positively relate to (a) environmental awareness among the general public, and (b) government regulations related to green practices, as perceived by the firm.

The environmental awareness among the general public, as perceived by a firm, likely affects its perceptions of the customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes toward green IT practices too [37]. A firm attains optimal benefits when its customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes are in sync with the shift toward sustainability. For example, increasing media coverage of environmental issues attracts the attention of customers and equity holders, who come to value the impacts of firms’ operations on the environment; their (expressed) preferences and expectations should influence firms’ strategies and operational choices [37]. Government regulations regarding environmental sustainability similarly could drive customers and equity holders to be more mindful of firms’ decisions and operations [11, 36] and patronize those that practice green IT. Many customers explicitly prefer products and services from firms that conform to regulations; similarly, equity holders prefer satisfactory and sustainable performance, positive corporate images, and strong branding by the firms in which they invest.

When firms sense changes at broader levels of the competition space, they allocate resources to meet the emerging trends [87], following the awareness–motivation–capability chain suggested by competitive dynamics. According to competitive dynamics [18], a firm’s internal readiness for adapting to green IT practices can be initiated by environmental awareness that motivates the firm to allocate resources and capabilities to such practices. As Bose and Luo [8] explain, firms allocate their finite resources across various operations and activities to react appropriately to external forces. Thus firms are more likely to allocate resources, whether tangible or intangible, to green IT practices in the presence of government regulations regarding green practices. Previous research confirms that increased regulations by governments enhance organizational readiness for green practices [82]. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5a: A firm’s perceptions of customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes toward green IT practices positively relate to (1) environmental awareness among the general public, and (2) government regulations regarding green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Hypothesis 5b: A firm’s internal readiness for green practices positively relates to (1) environmental awareness among the general public, and (2) government regulations regarding green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Growing concerns about environmental sustainability, disseminated throughout global networks of production, supply chains, logistics, and marketing, can shape salient norms in an industry [11]. Because all the firms in an industry are exposed to the same concerns and trends, prevailing norms can develop at the industry level. In addition, government regulations guide firms’ goal setting and schema selection, so they can affect industry norms and fuel their dissemination [60, 75]. All else being equal, norms are more likely to emerge in an industry when they align with government regulations [13]. A fundamental rationale for government intervention is to alleviate market failures and address the potential social costs or negative network externalities [75]. In this vein, government regulations should steer an industry toward environmental sustainability [60].

All firms seek competitive edges in the market [28] and closely monitor changes in the environment. According to competitive dynamics [18], both environmental awareness and government regulations create external changes that invoke reactions by firms that compete with one another. In response to global trends toward environmental sustainability and regulations created by governments around the world, a firm and its competitors likely practice green IT to defend or improve their competitive positions. Environmental awareness implies a contextual change that affects all actors in the market; thus, a firm can easily explain competitors’ green practices according to the global trend of environmental protection. In the same vein, a firm perceives its competitors’ green practices as a way to comply with laws, maintain legitimacy, mitigate legal risks, and avoid costly litigation. As a result, the green IT practices of a firm’s competitors can be rationalized, on the basis of the pressures put forth by environmental awareness and government regulations. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6a: Industry norms regarding environmental sustainability positively relate to (1) environmental awareness among the general public, and (2) government regulations regarding green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Hypothesis 6b: Competitors’ green practices positively relate to (1) environmental awareness among the general public, and (2) government regulations regarding green practices, as perceived by the firm.

Firms in direct competition often operate in the same business field, are exposed to highly similar external pressures, and share similar business goals and values. According to institutional theory, firms facing the same market field become more homogeneous over time because they are confined by the same intuitional pressures, including industry norms [29] that encourage environmental sustainability, create network effects [68], and help a firm and its competitors recognize the value of green IT practices. Such shared values and recommended practices, as conveyed by prevalent industry norms, likely encourage firms to compete in terms of their green practices [60]. A firm would thus observe both industry norms and its competitors green practices, then make connections between the two. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 7: Industry norms regarding environmental sustainability, as perceived by a firm, positively relate to its competitors’ green practices.

Finally, stakeholders, including customers and investors, usually have profound influences on a firm’s decisions and behaviors [5]. To establish legitimacy, firms tend to allocate resources to operations and activities that align with their customers’ and equity holders’ expectations and preferences. The support of customers and equity holders thus provides a critical justification for allocating resources to green practices, namely, as a means to create a positive image among these stakeholders [51]. That is, a firm increases its internal readiness for green IT to maintain its legitimacy if it perceives that customers and equity holders have expectations about its green IT practices. Thus we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 8: A firm’s perceptions of customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes toward green IT practices positively relate to its internal readiness for green practices.

## Study Design and Data

To test our model and hypotheses, we conducted a survey study of major manufacturing and service firms in Taiwan, noting their significance in the global economy and important cascading effects on firms’ operations elsewhere. Taiwan is ranked among the top 10 countries worldwide in terms of its green technology capabilities [53]. Its government also takes on a facilitating and supporting role in promoting green practices among firms in various industries, launching different initiatives and programs. For example, the Ministry of Economic Affairs has implemented a Green Trade Promotion Project to help companies respond to rising global trends toward green business [12]; several related programs have been initiated by other government agencies to encourage the development of green technology practices, such as the National Science and Technology Program for Energy and the Dawning Green Energy Industry Program. At the time of our study, the Taiwanese government had passed few laws and regulations that mandate green IT practices by firms though.

We used a directory of all companies in Taiwan (which includes company profiles, main business lines, and contact information) and targeted the largest 2,000 firms. We took a key informant approach, targeting each firm’s chief information or technology officer, because this position is usually central to the firm’s technology decisions and operations and has a sound understanding of its business strategies and IT planning. These senior executives thus represent logical and appropriate sources to indicate their firm’s considerations of, assessments about, and decisions regarding IT practices.

We used previously validated scales to measure each investigated construct. Specifically, we operationalized industry norms with items adapted from Babiak and Trendafilova [2] and Stern et al. [77]. For government regulations, we adopted items from Chien and Shih [20]. The question items developed by Mostafa [64] were used to measure customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes. We used items from Zhu and Sarkis [89] to operationalize competitors’ green practices, and we employed items developed by Hedwig et al. [40] and Molla et al. [63] to measure a firm’s green IT practices, our dependent variable. Environmental awareness was measured with items derived from the conceptual analyses by Babiak and Trendafilova [2], Cohen [24], and Franzen [35]. We adapted items from Lee and Shim [47] and Molla et al. [63] to measure a firm’s internal readiness for green practices. We made minor wording changes to the measurement items to better fit our context and informants. All the question items employed a seven-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 7 = “strongly agree”). In Appendix A, we list the items used in the study, together with their respective sources.

The survey was conducted in Chinese, the official language in Taiwan. Because all the question items were originally developed in English, we employed a translation and back-translation method [9]. Two professional translators first translated the items into Chinese; to ensure consistent semantics of each measurement item, three seasoned researchers, fluent in both English and Chinese and not involved in this study, then reviewed the translated items separately. Next, the items (in Chinese) were translated back to English by another two professional translators and reviewed again by the same panel of researchers, who indicated the satisfactory semantic consistency of each item.

We hired a prestigious, professional survey company for data collection. The survey company e-mailed the 2,000 firms about the study and its objective; 1,261 firms responded and were contacted by the survey company by phone. A team of well-trained telephone surveyors called each firm to solicit its voluntary participation. After obtaining an explicit agreement, the surveyor conducted the survey with the firm’s chief information or technology officer, by phone at a mutually agreed time. Before administering the survey, the surveyor used a prepared script to convey the study’s purpose, stated that the data analyses would take place at an aggregate level, answered questions related to privacy or confidentiality, and obtained the respondent’s explicit agreement to participate. To reduce the potential for common method bias, we mindfully administered the study procedure [45] and adopted Podsakoff et al.’s [69] recommendations. For example, we reduced the potential for variance due to social desirability or respondent acquiescence by preserving respondents’ anonymity and mitigating evaluation apprehension. We also assured each respondent that his or her participation was voluntary and anonymous and that any provided responses would be aggregated, solely for the purpose of understanding the status quo of green IT practices in Taiwan.

The survey questions were presented verbally, one at a time; the surveyor allowed sufficient time for responses. During the survey, respondents could request clarifications or elect not to continue. With explicit consent from each respondent, the interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. Among the 1,261 firms responding to the initial e-mail invitation, a total of 304 firms agreed to participate and completed the survey voluntarily, for an effective response rate of 24.11 percent.<sup>2</sup> On average, the survey took 20 minutes to complete.

Among the contextual factors that we consider, both environmental awareness and industry norms influence a firm’s green IT practices in a significant, direct manner. In addition to their direct effects, environmental awareness and industry norms have important indirect impacts on a firm’s practices, through customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes and internal readiness. For environmental awareness and industry norms, the magnitude of the indirect effects on a firm’s green IT practices is comparable to that of the direct effects.

Although government regulations and competitors’ green practices do not influence a firm’s green IT practices directly, they still affect the firm’s practices indirectly, through relationships with other contextual factors and firm-specific considerations. The observed indirect effects of government regulations and competitors’ green IT practices, which are more prominent and significant than the direct effects, indicate essential coercive and mimetic pressures on firm-specific considerations. Judged by the total effects, our results suggest the criticality of customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes and internal readiness, as well as industry norms and environmental awareness.

In addition to testing the proposed model, we also examined an ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression model that considers the direct effect of each predictor. As we detail in Appendix C, the results of the OLS regression models are similar to those of the proposed model; however, these models consider only direct effects and therefore the effects of government regulations and competitors green practices are overlooked. Relatively, the proposed model can better explain firms’ behaviors by providing more insights into their green IT practices.

## Discussion

Our study makes several contributions to research. First, we augment extant literature by proposing a hierarchical view of the key drivers that influence firms’ green IT practices, drawing on institutional theory and competitive dynamics as our theoretical foundation, then empirically testing the model that we derived and validating the patterns of effects. Most previous research has analyzed general benefits or key motivators of green practices at a conceptual level, using partial depictions that lack any theoretical underpinning or empirical testing. By offering these empirical results, we help explain the widely noted disparity between firms green awareness and their green practices.

Second, the study results highlight the importance of firm-specific channels of the influences of contextual factors on a firm’s green IT practices, in addition to the direct effects. This finding helps explain the gap between a firm’s green awareness and action; it sheds light on why firms in the same business environment may adopt distinct strategies and actions toward green IT practices. Our model also reveals how a firm’s current and future resource investments influence its green IT practices: Its internal readiness denotes its current investment, and stakeholders’ attitudes affect its future green investment priorities. In particular, the effects of competitors’ green practices could be discounted, if firm-specific considerations were overlooked. Government regulations can also affect a firm’s green IT practices, through influences on firms’ internal readiness for green practices. Thus, a firm may be motivated by government regulatory forces when green practices have prevailed in the industry and become a norm and also are widely adopted by competitors, especially if that firm is better prepared for green practices that are preferred and valued by its stakeholders.

Third, we contribute to green IT research by identifying critical sources of institutional pressures and revealing probable effect patterns of different contextual factors. The influence paths of contextual factors at different levels indicate the need to examine firms’ green practices by considering different institutional forces according to a hierarchical view. In particular, we single out a cascading influence pattern—from broader, contextual factors at the environment level (environmental awareness and government regulations) to external factors at the industry level (industry norms and competitors’ green practices) and then to firm-specific considerations (internal readiness and customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes)—for firms green IT practices. This influence pattern is in line with a hierarchical view of the effects of key factors on firms’ green IT practices. The proposed model suggests the need to analyze contextual factors according to the scope of their effects, to understand the influence paths of contextual factors at different levels. Toward that end, industry norms and competitors’ actions are essential factors that link broader environmental contextual factors and firm-specific considerations; their significance cannot be overlooked.

Fourth, we investigate relationships between contextual factors at the environmental and industry levels, as well as those between contextual factors and firm-specific considerations. The results offer a more complete explanation of how the drivers together influence a firm’s green IT practices. Our model (or nomological network), derived from a theory-based hierarchical view, can explain key phenomena and provide insights into their underlying effect processes. Specifically, institutional forces and competitive dynamics provide a viable way to study firms’ green IT practices. Further research in this direction could analyze and test specific contextual factors at different levels.

Fifth, our findings indicate the need for further scrutiny of influence patterns across different institutional pressures. Previous research examines firms’ strategic actions by considering the aggregate effects of various institutional forces [15, 25]; we observe distinct, perhaps unequal influences. Coercive and normative forces seemingly exhibit more prominent, significant effects on individual firms’ green IT practices than do mimetic forces. Although competitors constitute an important source of mimetic pressures, their direct impacts appear insignificant. Mimetic pressures arise when a firm models competitors’ taken-for-granted practices; in this vein, perhaps green IT practices simply are not prevalent enough to be imitated. Only when other factors are present, such as the firm’s internal readiness and stakeholders’ attitudes, can mimetic pressures take effect. By examining these respective impacts empirically, we provide a more complete understanding of firms’ reactions to different institutional pressures.

The effects of institutional pressures seem intertwined, thereby highlighting the need to examine distinct pressures and further analyze their paths of influence. For example, coercive government regulations might affect a firm’s green IT practices through normative industry standards; normative global trends instead influence green IT practices through normative and coercive industry norms. Previous research has not explicitly distinguished different institutional pressures but instead assumes implicitly that coercive and normative pressures are indistinct. In this study, we separate out the influence patterns of different institutional pressures to provide a clearer explanation of the mixed results that appear in previous studies.

We also find dissimilar effects of different institutional forces, suggesting the need for more detailed analyses of sources of institutional pressures, to determine their effects on firms’ decisions, both independently and jointly. Researchers cannot establish the effects of an institutional force without analyzing its root causes. For example, both industry norms and government regulations usually represent important sources of coercive pressures; however, regulatory pressure appears less prominent and significant than the pressure created by industry norms in this study. At the time of our study, the Taiwanese government did not enforce severe penalties on firms for creating social costs and negative externalities, for various reasons. Instead, government agencies functioned as facilitators, providing incentives and launching programs that created mostly weak institutional pressures on firms. This facilitating role might help explain the relatively limited direct effect of government regulations on firms’ green IT practices.

In addition, both sources of normative pressures that we consider—environmental awareness and industry norms—exhibit significant impacts on green IT practices, yet their total effects and influence patterns differ. Several previous studies focus on general effects of particular types of institutional pressure [15, 25], but the root causes of the respective pressures remain insufficiently analyzed. This study scrutinizes different sources of institutional pressures, generates empirical evidence about their respective effects, and sheds light on firms’ reactions to important contextual factors that produce the external pressures they face.

Our findings also have implications for practice and policy. For example, firms should revise their strategic goals and resource allocations toward green IT practices, in sync with the industry-wide atmosphere and the general public’s awareness of environmental protection. When they observe their industry (including value-chain collaborators and competitors) migrating toward green practices, firms must respond accordingly. In general, firms aware of emerging trends would be more serious about and ready for green IT practices. Environmental preservation groups’ efforts can also increase awareness of and preferences for green IT practices, thereby creating pressures that could invoke industry norms as well. From a policymaking perspective, governments should help firms recognize trends toward green practices and work closely with industry associations and professional societies to pollinate “green values.” As our results show, laws and government regulations, at a minimum, can influence firms’ green IT practices through the effects of industry norms and firmspecific considerations. Thus, the importance of government regulations regarding green IT practices should not be underestimated, even if firms might not be motivated directly by government regulations to practice green IT.

The significant direct effects of customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes also imply that customers and equity holders exert substantial pressures on firms’ decisions to practice green IT. Nevertheless, policymakers and environmental activists may not be able to exert direct influences on customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes; they need to encourage industry norms that can then change stakeholders’ attitudes. Similarly, firms’ readiness is central to green IT practices; firms need to allocate sufficient resources and obtain the necessary capabilities to respond to external pressures in the environment. Competitors’ green practices might motivate firms resource allocations to ensure their own green readiness and attract customers’ and equity holders’ attention to green IT practices.

Finally, several limitations of this study point to promising research directions. First, we analyze key environmental drivers of green IT practices by examining different sources of pressure in the institutional environment. However, we do not measure each particular pressure at a fine-grained level. To confirm the relationship associated with key institutional forces, further studies should apply reliable measures of institutional pressure and examine the relationships between environmental drivers and the pressures that firms perceive empirically. Second, our findings stem from a single study with a sample of voluntarily participating firms, so we cannot rule out potential self-selection biases. Our study results should be interpreted with caution, because we target the 2,000 largest firms in Taiwan. The 304 participating firms report relatively high levels of green IT practices on average (mean = 5.50, standard deviation = 1.02). To a large extent, the results thus suggest factors that influence firms’ green IT practices at various levels, in line with our goal to understand the key factors that contribute to disparities in green IT practices among firms. This study might be more representative for firms with a high level of green IT practices but not necessarily so for firms with a low level of green IT practices. The results instead can provide insights into the factors contributing to firms’ continuous investment into green IT practices. Additional studies might achieve more robust empirical results by employing comparative analyses and examining firms across different industries or countries. Cultural differences and industry characteristics might offer rich insights related to firms’ decisions and behaviors. Third, our study is cross-sectional; research using longitudinal designs might investigate the causal relationships of key determinants from a temporal aspect, which is essential as firms, industries, and governments advance and adopt increasingly mature green IT practices. Fourth, our model explained a significant portion of the variance in firms’ green IT practices, but additional organizational factors also could be taken into consideration. For example, the resource-based perspective asserts that firms are differently equipped with useful resources that likely affect their green IT practices. Senior managers and organizational decision makers also determine the priority assigned to green IT in firms. Additional research thus should test antecedents, such as firms’ current resource portfolios, senior managers’ attitudes, and organiza tion-level assessments of green IT practices. Fifth, our study does not investigate the probable differences among industries; we use industry as a control variable instead. Industries might vary in the dynamics of their norms or the government laws and regulations to which they are subject, so further research should compare particular industries to provide finer-grained insights into firms’ green IT practices.

## Conclusion

To study the effects of contextual factors and firm-specific considerations on firms’ green IT practices, we have analyzed and empirically examined how firms react to different institutional pressures, in accordance with firm-specific considerations. With a foundation in institutional theory and competitive dynamics, we propose a hierarchical view and develop a model to explain firms’ green IT practices. We test the model and associated hypotheses in a survey of 304 large manufacturing and service firms in Taiwan. The results show that environmental awareness, industry norms, internal readiness, and customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes influence a firm’s green IT practices directly. The effects of government regulations are funneled through the relationships of industry norms and competitors’ practices with firm-specific considerations (i.e., internal readiness and customers’ and equity holders’ attitudes). Competitors have limited direct influences but nontrivial indirect effects. Overall, environment-level contextual factors (environmental awareness and government regulations) and industry-level contextual factors (industry norms and competitors’ practices) significantly influence a firm’s green IT practices, directly and indirectly. In addition to their important direct effects, firm-specific considerations bridge external institutional forces and firms’ green IT practices.
