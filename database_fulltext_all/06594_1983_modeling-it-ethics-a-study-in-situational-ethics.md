## Abstract

Misuse of computer information systems has caused significant losses to business and society, even though computing has benefited both businesses and professionals. To this end, several measures have been suggested that both prevent and deter losses. One deterrent measure is to identify individual and situational characteristics of people who act ethically/unethically. This study identifies specific characteristics that are associated with and may influence the ethical behavior intention of information systems employees when faced with ethical dilemmas. The results of the study show that individual and situational characteristics do influence ethical behavior intention.

Keywords: Ethics, ethical behavior

ISRL Categories: AA0102, AA03, AA0701, BB0103, BD0104.01, DA0101

## Introduction

Computers have become an integral part of society. Consequently, both use and misuse of computers have increased dramatically. Whereas proper use has proven to be beneficial to businesses and professionals, inappropriate use has caused significant losses to businesses and society. Previous studies (Research Institute of America 1983; ABA 1984) have found that monetary losses suffered from computer misuse in the United States were estimated at billions of dollars per year. The more recent third annual InformationWeek/Ernst & Young Information Security Survey reported that nearly half of the more than 1,290 respondents representing information systems chiefs and information security managers suffered security-related financial losses in the past two years (Panettieri 1995). Most companies surveyed indicated that they did not have adequate tools in place to defend against information losses, but that security issues are becoming better understood. It has been posited that, even though organizations continue to develop and implement more protective security measures, computer misuse and computer crimes would continue to be a problem through and beyond the 1990s (Straub and Nance 1990).

The area of improper computer use and computer ethics has not remained unattended. Several authors (Aiken 1988; Conner and Rumelt 1991, Couger 1989; Heide and Hightower 1988; Oz 1990; Paradice 1990;

Saari 1987; Straub and Nance 1990; Zalud 1984) have addressed the problem of computer misuse from various perspectives. The enormity of the problem was discussed by Saari, who noted that: (1) of the cases (of computer fraud) that occur, only one in every 100 is detected, (2) of the cases detected, one out of every eight is reported, and (3) of the cases prosecuted, three out of every 100 result in a conviction. Recent articles have cited monetary losses to businesses in billions of dollars per year as a result of misuse and fraud (Flanagan and McMenamin 1992). Behar (1997) reported how easily the security of an information system was breached. The sizable number of firms, dollars involved, and the rising trend of fraud losses warrants investigation into the problem.

Accordingly, direct/preventive measures (e.g., enhanced, active security countermeasures) and indirect/deterrent measures (e.g., establishing and implementing codes of conduct for Information Systems (IS) professionals, identifying ethical issues in using computers, and including ethical issues in the curriculum for IS majors) have been supported in the literature (Couger 1984; Parker 1980, 1988; Straub 1986). An overlooked but potentially effective deterrent is the identification of individual and situational characteristics of IS personnel who act ethically/unethically. Identification of unique characteristics could lead to the formulation of more effective ways of solving the problem of unethical use or inappropriate use of computers. For example, studies indicate that, to some degree, moral reasoning may be enhanced through education (Davis 1987; Rest 1979; Thoma and Davison 1983). In addition, Kahn (1990) suggested that unethical conduct may be affected by organizational environment, in this case members working in fragmented subgroups who were discouraged from connecting their points of view with other subgroups. Such findings suggest that employee ethical judgments, along with corporate ethical climate, could be altered to deter the intention to behave ethically/unethically.

While several conceptual models for explaining ethical behavior have been proposed in the literature, few have been empirically validated.

This study improved upon previous research efforts by incorporating theories from disparate fields of ethics research to develop a model of ethical behavior intention and by using IS employees to test the proposed model. The purposes of this study were (1) to link the diverse literature in order to identify specific characteristics that are associated with and may influence the ethical behavior intention of IS personnel when these employees are faced with ethical dilemmas involving different aspects of computing and (2) to examine the relationship, if any, between these characteristics and ethical behavior intention of IS personnel. Identification of variables associated with ethical behavior intention of IS personnel could increase the understanding of the behavioral aspect of inappropriate or unethical use of computers. It could also help in designing an agenda to reverse the present trend of increasing computer misuse.

## Ethical Behavior: A Research Framework

Ethics, ethical development, and ethical behavior are the subjects of many studies. To develop a model that can explain the ethical/unethical behavior of IS personnel, diverse literature on related subjects was considered. Ethical or unethical behavior takes place in the context of an ethical dilemma (Blasi 1983; Bommer et al. 1987; Paradice 1990; Rosen 1980; Trevino 1986). Rosen explained that the choices among alternatives are more complex because, a priori, the decision maker does not know if a choice is right or wrong. Thus, it is only appropriate to conclude that ethical/unethical behavior is something special within the realm of human behavior. Rest (1986) suggested that moral judgement is linked to behavior only to the extent that moral judgement represents different ways of construing a situation. It is appropriate that ethical behavior is dependent on one's own posture with respect to the ethical issue and can be explained by the person's moral development stage scores. Others (e.g., Bommer et al. 1987; Burkett and Jensen 1975; Grasmick and Green 1980; Trevino 1986)

argued that, given an ethical dilemma, how a person will act depends on certain personality characteristics of the person facing the dilemma. In addition, several authors (e.g., Bommer et al. 1987; Hegarty and Sims 1979; Rest 1986; Staff 1979; Trevino 1986) suggested that a person's decision to act ethically or unethically is determined by environmental characteristics.

Several models deal specifically with the issue of ethical behavior. Rest's (1979) model is comprised of four functional components that interact to explain the psychology of morality. Rest's model is also consistent with the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975). Ferrell and Gresham (1985) proposed a model that considers individual factors (i.e., knowledge, values, attitudes, and intentions), significant others (i.e., differential association and role set configuration), and opportunity (i.e., professional codes, corporate policy, and rewards/punishment). However, there is no empirical validation of their model.

Trevino suggested an interactionist model for explaining general ethical behavior in organizations. It is based on Kohlberg's (1969) moral development stages. Trevino used individual moderators (i.e., ego strength, field dependence, and locus of control) and situational moderators (i.e., immediate job context, organizational culture, and characteristics of the work). However, her model is conceptual only; it has not been empirically validated.

Bommer et al. (1987) proposed a conceptual model that consists of perception and degree of influence of individual attributes, personal environment, professional environment, work environment, government/legal environment, and social environment with the decision process to determine ethical or unethical behavior. This model also has not been empirically validated.

Finally, Eining and Christensen (1991) developed and tested a psycho-social model of software piracy that includes computer attitudes, material consequences, norms, socio-legal attitudes, and affective factors as independent variables that affect software piracy.

An attitude-behavior model that can be applied nicely to ethical decision making is found in Fishbein and Ajzen's TRA, which implies that attitude toward the act and subjective norm explain behavior and intentions. However, TRA and TPB are not geared specifically to problems involving ethical issues or ethical behavior. Since this theory is not sufficient for IT ethical behavior, variables from models in moral development research also were added to enhance the predictive power of the research model. Moreover, Schwartz and Tessler (1972) indicated that numerous causal variables may be used to explain behavior. According to these authors, it is important that the model be parsimonious and have explanatory power. Thus, from these diverse fields of research—attitude, ethical behavior, and moral development—a model to explain ethical/unethical behavior was developed.

In addition, ego strength, locus of control, and organizational ethical climate represent moderator variables. Intention to behave in a particular manner is the antecedent to the actual behavior. TRA by Ajzen (1985, 1989, 1991), is perceived behavioral control. This factor refers to the perceived ease or difficulty of performing the behavior and is assumed to reflect past experience as well as impediments and obstacles.

Intentions are assumed to capture the motivational factors that affect a behavior. They are indicators of how hard a person is willing to try and of how much of an effort he/she is planning to exert to perform a behavior. Ajzen (1991) suggested that behavior can be predicted from intentions with considerable accuracy. Intention is viewed as one antecedent of actual behavior (Ajzen 1989).

Moral Judgement (MJ): Moral judgement is the way a person reasons when faced with an ethical problem. This reasoning depends on the current stage of moral development (Kohlberg 1969, 1971, 1976, 1980, 1984, 1985). Kohlberg's development stages are the basic foundation of numerous studies of ethics. He defined and identified the existence of three levels of moral development of human beings. Each level consists of two stages. Level one, the preconventional level, consists of stage one (obedience and punishment orientation) and stage two (instrumental purpose and exchange). Level two, termed conventional, comprises stage three (interpersonal accord, conformity, and mutual expectations) and stage four (social accord and system maintenance). Finally, level three, the postconventional level, comprises stage five (social contract and individual rights) and stage six (universal ethical principles). $^{3}$

Rather than relative values, Kohlberg's findings show culturally universal stages of moral development that are experienced in an invariant, irreversible sequence. Each person passes through one or more of these stages in the process of transformation from early childhood to adolescence to adulthood. Although everyone begins at stage one and moves to subsequent stages depending on his/her moral development, not everyone reaches the highest stage. According to Kohlberg, the way a person reasons when faced with an ethical problem depends on the current stage of moral development. This model has attracted scholarly attention internationally and has numerous critics and supporters. The theory has been widely studied, criticized, and empirically validated (across cultures, across countries, longitudinally/cross-sectionally, etc.). Some criticisms (e.g., Rich and DeVitis 1985; Simpson 1974; Williams and Williams 1970) have been directed toward the moral development stages and the logical order in the stages themselves. Movement of individuals through the stages in an invariant sequence has also been challenged. Stages have been added and split; different instruments for measuring absolute and relative moral stage scores have been devised and tested. Yet, Kohlberg's original developmental stages remain the basis for numerous studies of ethics.

According to Rest (1980), two extremes should be rejected. Moral judgement has no substantial or interesting relation to behavior and knowing a subject's moral judgement score affords us a complete picture of the inner world of the subject and how the subject is likely to organize his or her life.

Attitude (A): A vast amount of attitude research literature (TRA and TPB) has established that attitude is a reliable predictor of intention. The logic of a causal chain from beliefs to behavior, together with the principle of compatibility, is embedded in TRA (Ajzen and Fishbein 1969, 1980; Fishbein and Ajzen 1975) and, more recently, in the extension of the theory to TPB (Ajzen 1985, 1989, 1991;

Ajzen and Madden 1986). Fishbein and Ajzen indicated that attitude toward an act is dependent on the beliefs held and the evaluation of those beliefs. They defined attitude toward an act as the degree to which a person has a favorable or unfavorable evaluation of the behavior in question. Attitude is dependent on the beliefs held and the evaluation of those beliefs.

Personal Normative Beliefs (PNB): Personal normative beliefs is the moral obligation to performing an act (Schwartz and Tessler 1972). Schwartz and Tessler stated (p. 34):

Both here and in Ajzen and Fishbein (1969) personal normative beliefs and attitude toward the act made consistent, independent, and substantial contributions to the explanation of variance in intentions. The independent contribution of social normative beliefs, though usually significant, was less substantial. Thus, personal normative beliefs and attitude toward the act appears to be necessary predictors of intentions. Social normative beliefs may also be necessary, but there remains some doubt regarding its importance.

From TRA and TPB, perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform the behavior influences intention (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975). Perceived social pressure is an individual's perception of whether most people important to the person think that the behavior in question should or should not be performed.

Ajzen (1991) indicated that in certain contexts not only perceived social pressure needs to be considered, but also personal feelings, moral obligation, or responsibility to perform, or refuse to perform, a certain behavior. Thus, such moral obligations would be expected to influence intentions along with other variables. Ajzen (1991, p. 189) stated: "findings suggest that, for the behaviors considered, personal considerations tended to overshadow the influence of perceived social pressure." Based on these reasons, the proposed model utilized personal normative beliefs, as suggested by Schwartz and Tessler (1972), rather than subjective norm as advocated in TRA and TPB.

The research considered including perceived behavioral control in the model. Perceived behavioral control (PBC) is the perceived ease or difficulty of performing the behavior. It entails past experience as well as anticipated impediments and obstacles (Ajzen 1991). Ajzen (1991) indicated that the relative importance of attitude, subjective norm, and PBC in predicting intention is expected to vary across behaviors and situations. Further, the research literature showed mixed results in the case of ethical issues and PBC. Randall and Gibson (1991) found that PBC was not significant in predicting intentions. Another study reported that PBC was not significant in the prediction of behavior, although it was significant in predicting intentions (Ajzen 1991). Since deterrents (i.e., difficulty or obstacles) were not a part of the ethical dilemmas faced by the IS personnel in the current study, PBC was not included in the model.

Ego Strength (ES): Ego strength is suggested as one of the individual variables that interacts with other variables moderating the relationship among moral development stages, other situational variables, and ethical behavior. Trevino suggested the use of ego strength as a moderator variable. Others have also cited ego strength as a moderator variable for explaining ethical behavior (Krebs 1967; Mischel 1974; Rest 1980). Ego strength appears to be defined differently by each researcher studying the construct. Nevertheless, because of its relevance to ethical behavior, one definition, that of Trevino, is used in the present study. Trevino defined ego strength as a construct related to strength of conviction or self-regulating skills. Individuals with high ego strength are expected to resist impulses and follow their convictions more than individuals with low ego strength.

Locus of Control (LOC): Locus of control is the degree to which an individual perceives that a reward follows from personal attributes or behavior as opposed to forces outside (Rotter 1966). Trevino identified locus of control as an additional individual variable that affects a person's ethical behavior and, by interacting with other variables, moderates the relationship among moral development stages, other situational variables, and ethical behavior. Rotter distinguished between two different locus of control orientations. Internally oriented individuals believe events in their lives are determined by their own behavior and effort, while externally oriented individuals believe events in their lives are determined by fate, chance, and other forces over which they have no control.

Organizational Ethical Climate (OEC): Organizational ethical climate represents the ethical culture of an organization as perceived by an individual. Current approaches to understanding ethical decision-making behavior in organizations recognize that ethical/unethical behavior occurs in a social context and is heavily influenced by characteristics of the organization's ethical environment (Bommer et al. 1987; Ferrell and Gresham 1985; Trevino 1986). Trevino proposed the use of organizational culture as a variable affecting ethical behavior of persons in an organization. Although her model does not contain explicitly a variable called organizational ethical climate, the variable is implied. Beginning with theoretical considerations of nine possible types of organizational ethical climates, Victor and Cullen identified five types of ethical climates prevailing in organizations. They corroborated the work of Schneider and Snyder (1975) and Schneider and Reichers (1983) and posited that ethical climate is one of the many organizational work climate types. According to Victor and Cullen, these are: caring, law and code, rules, instrumental, and independence. In addition, they said that ethical climates vary for different industries, different firms within industries, and different organizational levels within firms.

Ferrell and Gresham, and Trevino suggested one or more moderating variables that relate to the characteristics of the organization. It is presumed that these characteristics influence an individual's ethical decision making in an organization. Although researchers have suggested several organizational moderating variables, no one incorporates organizational ethical climate. The argument presented here is that organizational ethical climate is the most appropriate moderator. Instead of using surrogates, the present model uses organizational ethical climate.

Victor and Cullen also offered this additional variable to capture the ethical climate of groups. Their construct of ethical organizational climate embraced aspects of personal as well as work-group ethical values. These work-groups extend from small work-groups within departments to larger aggregations thereof. This variable was included because (1) it captures aspects of social normative pressures represented by subjective norm in the TPB and (2) Trevino suggested a variable very similar to OEC in her model for ethical behavior.

## Method of Measurement

Consistent with prior research on attitude, ethical behavior, and moral development, a questionnaire was designed to measure the independent variables previously discussed and to capture each respondent's intention to behave ethically/unethically for two computing scenarios. This questionnaire utilized a number of previously validated instruments and measures.

Two sources (Parker 1980; Weiss 1991) were used to identify representative scenarios faced by computer professionals. The scenarios included previously identified ethical issues such as privacy, accuracy, property, and accessibility (Mason 1986). These dimensions were validated by Conger et al. (1995). After close scrutiny of Weiss' 15 scenarios, eight were selected for review by an 11 member panel. The panel was made up of three MIS professors and eight senior managers/executives in IS departments of companies. The panel members were asked to evaluate the scenarios for possible inclusion in this study based on two criteria. The first criterion was relevancy and importance to the participating organizations. The scenario also needed to involve an ethical issue with the action choice of the subject in the scenario clearly ethical or unethical. Scenarios were excluded from use in the study when there was a significant split of the panel's opinions regarding whether the subjects acted ethically or unethically. Based on their evaluations, seven of the scenarios were used in this study. These scenarios, described in Appendix A, represented Mason's aforementioned ethical issues of privacy, accuracy, property, and accessibility. The two scenarios that were ranked the highest by a panel member of a participating organization were included in that organization's questionnaire. $^{4}$

Ethical Behavior Intention: Ethical behavior intention was measured with a single seven-point semantic differential item with probable/improbable as anchors. For example, the Corrective Action scenario item was "I intend to allow the employee to continue to work on the integrated system."⁵ Relatively high reliabilities for single seven-point bipolar semantic differential scales, such as those employed here, have been reported (Osgood et al. 1957). Fishbein and Ajzen also indicated that responses to scales of the semantic differential type tend to yield highly reliable measures of the strength of beliefs of intentions.

Moral Judgement: The Defining Issues Test (DIT) is an objective measure of moral reasoning development (Rest et al. 1974) and is an instrument used extensively in ethics research. The validity and reliability of the DIT $^{6}$ have been well established. Moreover, it is easy to administer, score, and interpret. A well-maintained database of results has accrued from the use of this instrument. The DIT was used in the present study and is available from the Center for the Study of Ethical Development, University of Minnesota (Rest 1986).

Several indices reflecting moral judgement can be computed from the DIT. They are a P-score, a percent P-score, a D-score, and a U-score that have direct correspondence and equivalence to Kohlberg's stages of moral development (except that the DIT splits stage 5 into 5A and 5B). These scores reflect the amount of reasoning displayed by the subject at the respective stage of Kohlberg's theory. The scores for this study's respondents were well within the acceptable range (Rest 1986, p. 7-2). The P-score reflects preference for postconventional reasoning over conventional and preconventional reasoning. It is an absolute score of a person's stand with respect to principled morality. The percent P-score is a relative, standardized measure, expressed as a percentage ranging from 0 to 95. The D-score captures a person's ratings of specific questions regarding their importance in defining the situation in the context of a particular ethical dilemma. Rest (1986) cited numerous studies using DIT and the P-score and D-score to determine a correlation between a measure of the moral judgement of a person and his/her ethical behavior under different conditions. He reported a positive correlation approximately equal to 0.3.

Thoma proposed the U-score (utilization score) to measure the degree to which the two sources of information in deriving the P-score and the D-score overlap. He contended that the U-score measures the degree to which moral judgements operate in determining a decision on a particular ethical dilemma. Thoma used data from several studies and tested the result of including the U-score and an interaction of the U-score with the P-score. His premise that the correlation between the DIT score and ethical behavior increases when the U-score is used in conjunction with the P-score or the D-score was supported. Correlations increased about 10% (to around 0.4) when the P-score, U-score, and an interaction between P-score and U-score were used in predictive models.

Attitude: Attitude measurement instruments typically contain semantic differential scale items for measurement of both beliefs and evaluation of consequences. Accordingly, as in Schwartz and Tessler, attitude was measured with three items that utilize a seven-point evaluative semantic differential scale with antonyms as anchors. Three sets of antonyms were used: good/bad, right/wrong, and punishing/rewarding. For example, the Corrective Action scenario item was "How would you evaluate your behavior as the supervisor."7 Rest (1986) reported DIT scores to be unrelated to attitudes in controlled experimental situations, yet he provided an elaborate discussion on the correlation of attitudes with moral judgement scores as measured by the DIT. He argued that DIT scores are able to discriminate between people with different ideologies or attitudes (liberals, moderates, and conservatives).

Personal Normative Beliefs: Personal normative beliefs was measured with a single seven-point item with anchors "strong obligation not to . . .," "no obligation either way," and "strong obligation to . . ." For example, the Corrective Action scenario item was "How morally obligated are you to take the necessary correction action in this case." According to Schwartz and Tessler, personal normative beliefs measured in this manner is a good predictor of intention under scenarios dealing with moral/ethical dilemmas.

Ego Strength: The fourth subscale of Barron's ego strength scale deals with moral issues and was used to measure ego strength since it conforms to Trevino's definition. Robinson and Shaver (1969) reported odd-even reliability of 0.76 and test-retest reliability of 0.72 for ego strength.

Locus of Control: Rotter's (1966) instrument for measuring locus of control was used in this study. Robinson and Shaver (1969) reported a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.72. They also stated (p. 143):

Several factor analyses reported by Rotter support the assumption of unidimensionality of the I-E scale, and numerous laboratory and survey studies find evidence for its construct validity.

Organizational Ethical Climate: Victor and Cullen's (1988) ethical climate questionnaire was used in this study. Respondents were classified according to a dominant climate type: caring, law and code, rules, instrumental, or independence. Classification resulted from calculating each respondent's mean scores for the five climate types and then assigning that respondent to the climate type with the highest mean score, as in Victor and Cullen. Thus, the study captured the individual perception of each respondent's view of the organization's climate.

In summary, the questionnaire for this study contained Rest's DIT, Barron's ego strength scale, Rotter's locus of control instrument, Victor and Cullen's organizational ethical climate, $^{9}$ and an instrument designed to measure attitude, personal normative beliefs, and ethical behavior intention for the scenarios.

## Results

## Sample

A summary presentation of the research project design was made to 21 IS department managers/executives. Following the presentation, these individuals were asked to participate in the study and to have their employees form the sample for the study. Eight companies located in the central and south-central United States initially agreed to participate. In addition, the computing services department of a major state university consented to participate. These companies (software manufacturing and support, and petroleum-based product manufacturing and/or distribution) are fairly representative of the IS departments in business firms.

A forwarding letter and the questionnaire designed for this study were mailed to the manager/executive of each of these nine organizations. After a review of the questionnaire, three individuals indicated that their organizations were unwilling or unable to participate. As a result, five companies and the state university constituted the sample (Table 2).

The managers/executives of the six participating organizations distributed copies of the questionnaire to their IS employees with a cover memorandum requesting that they complete the questionnaire. The memorandum specified that completed questionnaires were to be returned either to the manager/executive or directly to the researchers. Accordingly, some completed questionnaires were returned to the researchers, and some were routed through the respective managers. In all instances, respondents were anonymous. Respondents who failed to complete the entire questionnaire were not included in the analyses.

Table 2 gives a summary by organization of the total response, percent response rate, and usable response rate. Organizations A, B, and D engaged in software manufacturing and support; organizations C, E, and F dealt in petroleum-based product manufacturing and/or distribution. Of the 256 questionnaires distributed, 139 were returned, for an overall response rate of 54%. The usable response rates for the organizations varied from 24% to 100% with the median being 73%. Overall, the usable response rate was 51%. Since each respondent's questionnaire contained two ethical scenarios, a total of 261 observations were available for analysis. Table 3 presents the mean ethical behavior intention of the scenarios (scale of 1 to 7) selected for use by each organization. Smaller values of the mean indicated greater intent to behave ethically; larger values implied greater intent to behave unethically.

As reported in Table 3, ethical behavior intention varied widely by scenario. The overall scenario means ranged from 1.25 for Overdrawn Account to 4.79 for Voting Machine. Interestingly, the ethical behavior intention for a specific scenario was similar when evaluated in different organizations. For example, the Friendly Gesture scenario had almost identical mean organization ethical behavior intention in organizations B and C, 4.37 and 4.38, respectively. For each of the five scenarios used in more than one organization, there was no statistically significant pairwise difference between organization means for that specific scenario.

When considering a specific organization, it was apparent, as anticipated, that ethical behavior intention was not the same for different scenarios. In organization A, for example, the mean ethical behavior intention for the Marketing Data scenario and for the Overdrawn Account scenario was 2.63 and 1.13, respectively. Although practical differences existed in the magnitudes of the mean ethical behavior intention within an organization, except for organization B, the only statistically significant pairwise difference occurred in organization A. $^{10}$ This might be explained due to the relatively small sample sizes in the other organizations.

One reason could be the presence or absence of ethical codes of conduct in the organizations. $^{[12]}$ Another reason may be the stringency of implementation of the codes of conduct in place. On the other hand, the results may simply mean that situational ethics were in play.

## Test of model

A first-order multiple regression model with ethical behavior intention as the dependent variable was analyzed to determine the effect of the set of nine independent variables (see equation (B1), Appendix B). Table 1 described these variables. One variable, organization-scenario, was incorporated into the regression model as a control variable. This reduced the experimental error variance since, as reported earlier, ethical behavior intention varied by organization and by scenario. $^{13}$ The relative importance of each independent variable was measured by the statistical significance of the F-statistic for its partial regression coefficient. Considering the investigative nature of this research, and in order to increase the power of the statistical tests, the 10% significance level ( $\alpha = .10$ ) was used.

The full multiple regression model was statistically significant (p-value = .0001) and explained 41% of the variation of ethical behavior intention. The marginal contribution of each independent variable showed that organization-scenario (p-value = .0001), personal normative beliefs (p-value = .0372), and organizational ethical climate (p-value = .0573) were statistically significant (see Table B1, Appendix B). $^{14}$ This suggested that these three variables were associated with a person's intention to behave ethically/unethically and that his/her moral judgement, attitude toward ethical behavior, ego strength, and locus of control were not significant primary effects. These results, and the results for the moderator analyses discussed later, are summarized in Table 4. If it is assumed that the relationship being studied exhibits a medium effect size $(R^{2} = .13 \text{ in the population}), \text{then post hoc determination of the power showed it to exceed } .97 \text{ (Cohen 1988). Thus with } R^{2} = .41 \text{ in this study, the analysis provided a very high probability of detecting an effect if, in fact, it exists and therefore enhanced the conclusions emanating from this research (Baroudi and Orlikowski 1989).}$

## Moderator analysis

Analysis of the full or unrestricted multiple regression model determined which variables had a primary influence on the intention to behave ethically/unethically. As depicted by the proposed model for ethical behavior (Figure 1), the overall strength of the relationship among ethical behavior intention and the three variables—moral judgement, attitude toward ethical behavior, and personal normative beliefs—may be moderated by the particular degree (or level) of ego strength, locus of control, or organizational ethical climate. Consequently, these moderator variables were not expected to be statistically significant in the full multiple regression model.

The presence of a moderator variable could modify the strength and the relationship systematically among ethical behavior intention and the other variables. In this case, some subgroups of the data (defined by levels of the moderator variable) have higher predictive validity than the total sample and others have lower predictive validity. Thus, the moderator analysis required that various levels of each moderator variable be defined. Accordingly, respondents were classified according to the magnitude of their ego strength scores as strong ego or weak ego. The strong ego group corresponded to approximately the top one-third of the scores; the weak ego group to the bottom one-third. Similarly, two locus of control levels were defined: external and internal. For organizational ethical climate, three levels were defined: independent, law and code, and rules. $^{15}$

Investigation of each moderator's influence, if any, involved fitting separate regressions to the data for each level of the moderator variable. Hence, various reduced or restricted multiple regression models that incorporated only the first six independent variables (see Table 1) were developed for two ego strength levels (weak and strong), for two locus of control levels (external and internal), and for three organizational ethical climate levels (independent, law and order, and rules) (see equation (B2), Appendix B).

The presence of a moderator variable was determined by testing the equality of regression models across the various levels of this variable (i.e., Chow test) (Neter et al. 1990; Sharma et al. 1981). A statistically significant test indicated that a different regression model existed for each level. In other words, the form and strength of the relationship between ethical behavior intention and the primary effects (moral judgement, attitude, and personal normative beliefs) was modified by the levels of the moderator variable.

The results pertaining to the full multiple regression model were both somewhat surprising and yet not entirely unexpected. According to the theoretical model (Figure 1), ego strength, locus of control, and organizational ethical climate were specified as moderator variables. Their presence at different levels could affect the relationship between ethical behavior intention and the primary effects (moral judgement, personal normative beliefs, and attitude). Thus, the statistical significance in the full multiple regression model of organizational ethical climate was surprising since, if it was a moderator variable, it was not expected to be significant in the full regression model. The statistical non-significance in the full multiple regression model of ego strength and of locus of control were anticipated a priori.

Ego strength also did not appear to be a moderating variable. The reduced multiple regression models for weak ego strength and for strong ego strength were statistically significant (p-value = .0051, .0001) and explained 43% and 51% of the variation in ethical behavior intention, respectively (Table B2). The organization-scenario control variable was significant (p-value = .0014, .0187) for both models, while personal normative beliefs was also significant for strong ego strength. However, the Chow test for equality of the regression models for the two levels of ego strength was not significant (p-value > .10) (equation (B3), Table B2). This indicated that the levels of ego strength did not systematically modify ethical behavior intention. Thus, given the statistical contributions of other variables, a person's ego strength may be inconsequential in affecting whether he/she intended to behave ethically or unethically.

Locus of control also did not appear to be a moderator. The external and internal locus of control reduced multiple regression models were statistically significant (p-value = .0029, .0002) and explained 43% and 50% of the variation in ethical behavior intention, respectively (Table B3). In both models, the organization-scenario control variable was significant (p-value = .0495, .0010), as was at least one moral judgement variable. For the external model, the D-score and the U-score were significant (p-value = .0395, .0968); for the internal model, the P-score was significant (p-value = .0626). Testing the equality of these regression models for locus of control (i.e., Chow test) did not show statistical significance (p-value > .10) (Table B3). This indicated that neither external nor internal locus of control levels had higher predictive validity than the total sample. Thus, given the statistical contributions of other variables, locus of control may not be important in determining whether a person intended to behave ethically or unethically.

Finally, organizational ethical climate did not appear to be a moderator variable; however, it was significant in the full regression model. The reduced multiple regression model for independent climate was not statistically significant (p-value = .1884) (Table B4). Although this model had a large predictive validity ( $R^{2} = .80$ ), the number of observations was rather small (n = 22). The models for the other two levels of climate —law and code, and rules—were statistically significant (p-value = .0001, .0001) and explained 39% and 50% of the variation in ethical behavior intention (Table B4). In both of these latter models, but not in the independent climate model, organization-scenario was significant (p-value = .0001, .0001). In addition, a moral judgement variable—P-score—was significant (p-value = .0465) in the rules model. Once again, testing the equality of the regression models across the organizational ethical climate levels did not show statistical significance (p-value > .10) (Table B4).

## Discussion and Conclusions

Foremost among the problems that are inherent to investigations of ethical behavior and ethical behavior intention is the difficulty in identifying a plausible set of variables while limiting the selection to a relatively few in order to create a parsimonious model. Rest (1979) noted the challenge that underlies the measurement of behavior under a quandary of ethical dilemmas and that this effort may not be error-free. Although a substantial volume of ethics research espoused ethical behavior as a sound business goal, the difficulty and complexity of directly assessing actual ethical behavior has proven troublesome for researchers. This is at least partly attributable to measuring a construct that has not yet been acceptably defined (Gatewood and Carroll 1991). Therefore, the surrogate—intention to behave ethically/unethically—instead of the actual behavior was measured.

Based on prior research, it was theorized that several factors affected the ethical behavior of IS employees. The proposed theoretical model (Figure 1) suggested that ethical behavior intention was an antecedent to ethical/unethical behavior. It was hypothesized that the primary effects on an IS employee's ethical behavior intention was his/her moral judgement, attitude toward ethical behavior, and personal normative beliefs. Ego strength, locus of control, and organizational ethical climate were also considered as moderator variables. Thus, a goal of this research was to determine whether a linear statistical relationship existed among ethical behavior intention of IS personnel and the identified individual and situational variables.

This research showed that the most important variable in explaining ethical behavior intention was the organization-scenario variable—a variable that was added to control the analysis—rather than the proposed, theoretical, independent primary or moderator variables. In addition, an individual's personal normative beliefs and organizational ethical climate were statistically significant variables. Moral judgement and attitude were not statistically significant. These results indicate that an IS employee's intention to behave ethically/unethically was strongly related to the specific computing context of the individual's perceived organizational environment and was influenced by the person's moral obligation toward performing an act (i.e., personal normative belief). This was consistent with the recognition that ethical decision-making behavior in organizations is heavily influenced by characteristics of the organization's ethical environment (Bommer et al. 1987; Ferrell and Gresham 1985; Trevino 1986) and, as reported by Schwartz and Tessler, that personal normative beliefs offers a good explanation for ethical behavior intention. Barnett and Karson (1989) also report the setting to be important, and therefore support the ethical relativists' argument that ethics are situationally specific.

However, the way an employee reasons when faced with an ethical dilemma (i.e., moral judgement) and the degree to which the employee had a favorable/unfavorable evaluation of the behavior (i.e., attitude) are not factors that dominate intention to behave ethically/unethically. This conclusion is contrary to Kohlberg's (1969, 1971, 1976, 1980, 1984, 1985) theory and Fishbein and Ajzen's theory. Moral judgement and attitude may not have been important because of the powerful role that situational ethics seem to have on ethical behavior intention. Moreover, while reasoning depends on the current stage of moral development and attitude is dependent on beliefs held and the evaluation of those beliefs, the environment (e.g., corporate goals/policy and codes of conduct) (Bommer et al. 1987) may be influencing personal as well as work-group ethical values and is captured, according to the results, by the organizational ethical climate variable.

Although there were no statistically significant moderating variables, insight was obtained from the moderator analysis. Interestingly, personal normative beliefs had an impact on the ethical behavior intention for strong ego persons, but not for weak ego persons. This implied that, for an employee who expected to resist impulses and follow his/her convictions, moral obligation toward performing an act affected the intention to behave ethically/unethically. Given this, companies may be able to institute training programs for employees identified as having weak ego strength, which could influence actions when they are faced with ethical dilemmas. In addition, company policy and codes of ethics may play an important role for weak ego individuals. For strong ego persons, both preventive and deterrent measures, such as training programs, may need to be established.

Furthermore, different aspects of moral judgement influenced ethical behavior intention for an IS employee via internal, as opposed to an external, locus of control. For employees who believed that events in their lives were determined by fate, chance, and other forces over which they have no control, ethical behavior intention was influenced by their own perceptions of the importance in defining the situation in the context of an ethical dilemma (D-score) as well as the degree to which moral judgement influenced the decision in the dilemma (U-score). The ethical behavior intention of IS employees who believed events were determined by their own behavior was influenced by preferences for postconventional reasoning (P-score). That is, they followed self-chosen ethical principles. Once again, company policy and codes of ethics may help to influence behavior. These conclusions were consistent with Trevino.

In the rules climate setting, ethical behavior intention also was affected by an employee's moral judgement (P-score). Relating this to Kohlberg's moral development stages, it is possible that organizations with a rules climate may help employees develop their ethical principles. Upon further investigation, it was observed that the mean ethical behavior intentions (Table 3) for employees within organizations that had a rules climate were less likely to behave unethically. These results were consistent with Victor and Cullen's theory relating to ethical climate.

Clearly, an individual's perception of organizational ethical climate for IS departments was identifiable and differed within an organization and from organization to organization. This suggests that IS managers can identify the various climates that occur in their respective IS departments and therefore could make efforts to change climate perceptions so that their employees have more ethical dispositions regarding computing dilemmas.

These results also lend support for a plan of action by management to formulate, implement, and enforce codes of conduct for IS personnel, thereby enforcing ethical standards. Indeed, Couger (1984) and Parker (1988) suggested the need for providing ethical norms and codes of conduct for entering IS personnel. Harrington (1996) concluded that codes of ethics have some effect on certain forms of computer abuses and recommended efforts to strengthen their effect. Professional societies such as the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), the Association for Information Technology Professionals (AITP) (formerly the Data Processing Management Association, DPMA), the Institute for Certification of Computer Professionals, the

Canadian Information Processing Society, and the British Computer Society already have published established codes of conduct for computing professionals. $^{16}$ IS managers should publicize these documents in their endeavors to educate their employees and to formulate codes of conduct within their organizations. Couger (1989), among others, mentioned the need to teach or train IS students in ethical issues. Both the ACM and AITP curriculum committees included ethical issues in the recommended curriculum for IS majors.

A survey by the Center for Business Ethics (1992) of the Fortune 1000 industrial and service corporations showed that, although some progress in building ethics into corporations seems to be occurring, much more work needs to be done. The article indicated that having a code of ethics, followed by ethics training, is the most common approach for implementing ethics initiatives. On the other hand, Barnett and Karson noted that executives had not considered ethical implications: ethics did not play a role in decisions because ethical values had not yet been formed. This presents obvious challenges and opportunities for education.

Through training programs, management examples, the formulation of codes of conduct, and the enforcement of company policies and rules, management may be able to reverse the trend in computer misuse. Rest (1986) discussed the effectiveness of training programs to help raise the moral development of individuals. Hence, training programs could be used to influence an individual's moral development—the goal being the reduction in computer misuse. However, as Nielsen (1988) pointed out, there are limitations of ethical reasoning that, in many cases, render it ineffective as an action strategy for influencing or changing ethical/unethical organization behaviors. It is imperative that recognition and understanding of these limitations be considered when attempting to utilize ethical reasoning as an action strategy.

Even with preventive measures (e.g., enhanced, active security countermeasures) as well as deterrents (e.g., articulating and disseminating codes of conduct, identifying computing ethical issues), improper computer practices continue to be a problem. An understanding of the ethical behavior of employees and the specific characteristics that affect ethical behavior are necessary in order to develop guidelines to provide management with approaches to curb this problem. $^{17}$

Clearly, the results of this preliminary investigation of ethical behavior intention, in light of results from other studies, suggest further research is warranted.

Behavior Issues: Consistent with other studies, respondents were presented with ethical dilemmas and asked to indicate how they felt about or might react to alternative courses of action. A major limitation is that the data represent hypothesized actions, rather than actual actions or behaviors. There is a need for research to go beyond behavior intention, to behavior and beyond behavior to look at the consequences of actions. Gatewood and Carroll indicated that major conceptual distinctions between an employee's behavior versus results and the interactions with stakeholder groups are required to explain ethical behavior. These distinctions raise the question of whether it is the employee's actions that are assessed for ethical performance or the results that are achieved. These variables are fundamental to an explanation of how everyday events alter ethical performance in organizations and with what consequences. Conger et al. identified the importance of distinguishing between the computer user (the person who experiences the ethical dilemma) and the stakeholder (those who deal with the consequences of the dilemma). Barnett and Karson also suggested that factors such as the nature of the affected party (e.g., customer, competitor, coworker, company's shareholders) and methods and results have important influences on ethical decisions.

Demographics: An a priori decision was made during the initial phase of developing the research model not to consider selected demographic variables. With the exception of the Bommer et al. model, which has not been empirically validated, demographics are not among the multitude of variables appearing in other researchers' models on ethical behavior. On the other hand, Rest (1979) reported age to be a significant demographic variable related to ethical development. Other demographics may, in fact, be more critical. Wong (1985) noted that the dollar amount involved in computer frauds perpetrated by managers is higher than that by staff members and outsiders. Wong also reported differences between males and females in perpetration of computer crime. In addition, Barnett and Karson suggested that factors such as gender, age as defined by career stage, and organizational level may influence ethical decisions.

Organization Environment: How does the specific organizational setting affect ethical outcomes? According to Nielsen, managers say they understand what is ethical, but choose not to practice it. Gatewood and Carroll explained how middle-level managers have power to block or facilitate organizational change. This is crucial, although it was found that each management level has a distinct effect on an organization's overall ethical performance. Nicholson (1994) argued that behavior research has ignored the ethical aspect of employees' and managers' experience. Such findings suggest that more work needs to be done in this area.

Longitudinal Studies and Replications: Longitudinal studies are needed to investigate (1) changes in ethical behavior as a result of implementation and enforcement of rules and codes and of conduct and (2) the results of ethics training programs to change the moral judgement of employees. Nicholson called for more observational cases that focus on dilemmas and problems experienced in organizations and other longitudinal methodologies to reconstruct the flows of ethical influence through organizations. The focus, once again, is to explain the consequences of how everyday events and disclosure alter ethical awareness. The image of ethics as history emphasizes that ethics evolve over time; thus, it is only possible to understand them by using a longitudinal perspective (Gatewood and Carroll 1991).

Replications with different samples are necessary for validation of the results reported here. Implicit in this recommendation is that not only are employees in the field of computing different from those in fields related to social sciences, but they also differ among themselves depending on their social cultures (Couger et al. 1994; Couger and Zawacki 1978). Rest (1979) noted that professional dilemmas are often a mix of both technical and moral values, suggesting that, even though the moral aspect may remain constant in ethical problems, the technical aspect differs in various professions. This makes ethics in computing different from those in, for example, management or medicine or nursing. Both the issues and the nature of dilemmas are different. It has been argued that cross-cultural value/philosophy differences affect ethical reasoning (Nielsen 1988; Kumar and Bjørn-Andersen 1990). The value orientation of IS personnel differs from culture to culture: inter-country and intra-country differences exist.

Possible Ways to Change Behavior: Gatewood and Carroll indicated that a key issue is categorizing organizational members into individual or group units for accountability. Researchers need to consider individual versus group ethical actions. The ethical performance for which an individual is held accountable must be distinguished from that for which the group is held accountable. In addition, since the stages of moral development are increasingly complex in the reasoning used to resolve ethical dilemmas, a valuable way to provide a robust environment for upward movement in moral growth is to create a mixture of the stages. Nelson and Obremski (1990) indicated that peer-led moral discussions may be an effective way to promote moral development in education programs.

Finally, it is conceivable that ethical behavior intention and ethical behavior are associated with and influenced by other variables not included in the research model. Certain situations call for specific ethical responses, especially since some individuals are already disposed to act morally. The problem occurs when there is disagreement or ambiguity concerning proper behavior. In such cases, companies may be able to influence the behavior of employees by making clear the policy of the company (the company's implicit ethical position) and ensuring that strong deterrents are in place. In situations where employees have weak ego strength, strong deterrents must be in place. With regard to individuals with strong egos, deterrents should be used, as should preventive measures (Straub et al. 1993). Moreover, it is important that detective measures not be neglected. Part of the solution is based on the assumption that companies have specific policies and user awareness programs. Evidence indicates that this is often not enough. Kearns (1994) noted that fewer than half of organizations surveyed include security awareness as a part of new employee orientation. Similarly, Roy and Park (1994) pointed out that, despite repeated warnings about control and security, the lack of policies or the noncompliance with security policies are pervasive in many business and governmental organizations.

The question remains: how does a company make its implicit ethical position known, reinforcing those already disposed to act ethically and further influencing those who may not be disposed to act ethically or in concert with the company's ethical position? Perhaps companies could have new employee training sessions and training sessions for continuing employees to influence judgement. Companies also could make employees aware of deterrents and penalties and efforts to detect abuse. The most promising and effective approach for the long term may be to create an ethical organizational environment as opposed to one-shot training sessions or mandatory attendance at ethical training.

This raises the final question for future research. What is not clear from prior research, including the present study, is the form ethical training should take in the organizational setting. Further research is needed to determine how extensive and thorough ethical reasoning training should be. Are one-shot sessions effective or only long-term programs of education (Rest 1986)? This is a critical issue for pragmatic understanding of the investment required to achieve efficacious ethical reasoning training.

Although more research is needed to help explain situational ethics and behavior in the IT context, the results of this study provide a theoretical basis for understanding ethical behavior. Further research is needed to address the following issues:

\- Do companies have specific policies, and do they enforce these policies?

\- Are policies, other deterrents, and detective measures effective?

\- Is there a difference between long-term programs designed to influence the organizational ethical environment as compared to short-run programs? Does significant education in morals make a difference?

\- Are deterrents or detective measures effective for certain individuals who are predisposed to act unethically?

\- Does the education of managers make any difference?

\- What do employees think is ethical behavior (where are the ethical boundaries)?

We are in an era that is coming to recognize that the ethical reasoning of individuals and groups operating in organizations is a critical issue. Individual and group ethics, moreover, are embedded in an ethical climate or culture that is distinct for each organization. Further, these ethical frameworks affect reasoning processes that then lead to major decisions about the use and protection of the assets of the firm, including information and information systems. Over time, research into the theoretical and practical implications of ethical behavior will help us to envision how organizations can be both ethical and effective.

# Appendix A

# Ethical Scenarios Used

## Corrective Action

A software professional was assigned the task of developing software to control a particular unit of a large system. Preliminary analysis indicated that the work was well within the state of the art, and no difficulties were anticipated with the immediate task. To function properly, or to function at all, however, the software to be developed required inputs from other units in the system. Someone gave the professional an article by an eminent software specialist that convinced him that inputs from other units could not be trusted. Thus, neither the software he was designing nor the unit his company was providing could correctly accomplish their task. The professional showed the article to the supervisor and explained its significance. The supervisor's response was "That's not our problem; let's just be sure that our part of the system functions properly." The software professional continued to work for the project.

## Usage Fee

A commercial time-sharing service offered use of a program at a premium charge, the program to be used only in the service company's computer. A user obtained a copy of the program accidentally, when the service company inadvertently revealed it to him in discussions through the system (terminal to terminal) concerning a possible program bug. All copies of the program outside of the computer system were marked as a trade secret, proprietary to the service, but the copy the customer obtained from the computer was not. He used the copy of the program after he obtained it, without paying the usage fee to the service.

## Voting Machine

Company XYZ has developed the software for a computerized voting machine. Company ABC, which manufactured the machine, has persuaded several cities and states to purchase it. On the strength of these orders, ABC is planning a major purchase from XYZ. XYZ software engineer Smith is visiting ABC one day and learns that problems in the construction of the machine means that one out of every ten machines is likely to miscount soon after installation. Smith reports it to her superior, who informs her that that is ABC's problem. Smith does nothing further.

## Friendly Gesture

A microcomputer user purchased and legitimately used numerous commercial software packages protected by a typical license agreement. He wrote his own programs that called for the use of the commercial packages. Because his friends wanted copies of his programs, the programmer copied them and inadvertently, without noticing, also copied the commercial programs onto diskettes. His friends proceeded to use his programs and, without knowing, also used the commercial programs.

## Electronic Mail

The information security manager in a large company was also the Access Control Administrator of a large electronic mail system operated for company business among its employees. The security manager routinely monitored the contents of electronic correspondence among employees. He discovered that a number of employees were using the system for personal purposes; the correspondence included love letters, disagreement between married partners, plans for homosexual relations, and a football betting pool. The security manager routinely informed the Human Resources Department director and the corporate Security Officer about the communications and gave them printed listings of them. In some cases, managers punished employees on the basis of the contents of these electronic mail messages. Employees objected to the monitoring of their electronic mail, claiming that they had the same right of privacy as they had using the company's telephone system or internal paper interoffice mail system.

## Overdrawn Account

A computer programmer in a bank was in deep financial difficulty. He kept this secret from his employer. A significant personal tragedy, in addition to his financial difficulties, caused him to require a modest amount of cash for a short time. He concluded that violating his trust at the bank to solve his problem would cause the least amount of harm to the least number of people.

He made a change in the checking account computer program that caused his own checking account number to be ignored by the part of the program that produced the daily overdraft report on the basis of which the bank stopped further debiting of overdrawn accounts. He was aware that during the periods between monthly statements the bank relied entirely on the overdraft report to take action against customers and prevent further check cashing.

He was able to cash checks exceeding the balance in his account, which quickly went negative. All controls and totals within the system balanced and only the reports to people who could have taken action against him or stopped him were changed temporarily. His monthly statement showed a temporary overdraft and the assessment against his account as an overdraft penalty. He was able to restore to his account the money covering the overdraft before the month-end accounting took place, and he removed the change in the program.

He reasoned that he had done nothing particularly wrong, since no person suffered losses, and his act was against an inanimate object, the computer. He rationalized that the bank had given him a short-term small, unauthorized loan. He even paid a high interest rate in the form of the overdraft penalty. The only way he could have been wrong was if he could not have paid back the “loan” in time and had been caught.

## Marketing Data

A marketing company's employee was doing piece work production data runs on company computers after hours under contract for a state government. Her moonlighting activity was performed with the knowledge and approval of her employer. The data were questionnaire answers of 14,000 public school children. The questionnaire contained highly specific questions on domestic life of the children and their parents. The government's purpose was to develop statistics for behavioral profiles for use in public assistance programs. The data included the respondents' names, addresses, and so forth.

The employee's contract contained no divulgement restrictions, except a provision that statistical compilations and analyses were the property of the government. The employer discovered the exact nature of the information in the tapes and its value in business services his company supplied. He requested that the data be copied for subsequent use in the business. The employee decided the request did not violate the terms of the contract, and she complied.
