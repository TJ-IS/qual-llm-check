# Case studies and generalization in information systems research: A critical realist perspective

Eric W.K. Tsang ⇑

Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, 800 West Campbell Rd., SM 43, Richardson, TX 75080, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 6 December 2012 Accepted 24 September 2013 Available online 18 October 2013

Keywords: Case study Generalization Theory testing Critical realism Positivism Interpretivism

## a b s t r a c t

The status of case study research as a legitimate scientific method in IS research is often challenged by the view that case findings are not readily generalizable. Positivist and interpretivist perspectives have typically dominated discussions of this important methodological issue. I provide an alternative perspective by presenting a critical realist view of generalizing from case findings. I show that critical realism represents a very different view than either positivism or interpretivism. Critical realism recognizes the role of case study research in empirical generalization, theoretical generalization, and theory testing. In contrast, the role of case study research in empirical generalization and theory testing is either ignored or neglected by interpretivism and positivism. Embracing critical realism would therefore enable researchers to more fully explore the potential for case finding generalization.

## 1. Introduction

Even though this case study was conducted longitudinally and six major IS decisions made over a 17-year period were examined, it is very difficult to generalize this study’s results to other organizations (Newman and Sabherwal, 1996, p. 47).

The above comment reflects a typical concern regarding generalizing from case findings. As Bryman (1989, p. 172) well argued over 20 years ago, ‘‘[t]he problem of generalization is often perceived as the chief drawback of case study research’’. Case studies are frequently challenged on the grounds that their findings are not readily generalizable to other settings due to the small-N problem (Dubé and Paré, 2003; Steinmetz, 2004). This is a very serious criticism because a key objective of the social sciences is to elucidate and articulate the generalities of social life (Danermark et al., 2002; Hägg and Hedlund, 1979). The status of case study research as a legitimate scientific method is therefore threatened.

IS researchers voice different and varied opinions concerning the generalizability of case study results. For instance, using the analogy between a case study and a natural science experiment (and adopting a positivist perspective), Lee (1989a, p. 41) concludes that, ‘‘generalizability poses no more, and no less, of a problem for MIS case research than it does for the studies conducted in natural sciences’’. Case study research is a legitimate scientific method of investigation, on par with natura science experiments. Discussing IS case study research from an interpretivist perspective, Walsham (1995) proposes four types of generalization that can be drawn from the results of a case study. Nevertheless, his view is in contrast with that of some interpretivists such as Denzin (1983), Lincoln and Guba (1985), and Taylor (discussed in Martin, 1994) who simply deny the possibility of generalization. To summarize, the literature presents an incoherent picture; yet this methodological issue—generalizing from case findings—is indeed important as there is ‘‘a strong case-study tradition in the academic field of management information systems’’ (Lee, 1989a, p. 33).

Orlilowski and Baroudi’s (1991) review of IS research articles published from 1983 to 1988 found that positivism was the dominant philosophical perspective, followed by interpretivism. This situation has not changed during the following decades (Myers and Klein, 2011; Wynn and Williams, 2012). Discussions of case studies and the generalizability of case findings by IS researchers are mostly based on these two perspectives alone (e.g., Cavaye, 1996; Dubé and Paré, 2003; Klein and Myers, 1999). This article discusses how the results of a case study can be generalized from a critical realist perspective, showing that critical realism provides an enriched view of this methodological issue relative to positivism and interpretivism. It also represents a response to Wynn and Williams’ (2012, p. 18) comment that ‘‘generalizability has a particular significance in critical realism and requires further elaboration relative to case study research’’.

I begin by defining the terms ‘‘case study research’’ and ‘‘generalization’’. I adopt Easton’s (2010a, p. 119) definition of case study research as a method that ‘‘involves investigating one or a small number of social entities or situations about which data are collected using multiple sources of data’’. The subject being investigated is usually a contemporary phenomenon in its real-life context (Yin, 2009). A case study is therefore an intensive investigation of a phenomenon in its natural setting, and often makes use of a variety of data sources (Benbasat et al., 1987). I follow Schwandt (1997, p. 57) and define generalization as a ‘‘general statement or proposition made by drawing an inference from observation of the particular’’. This definition is consistent with that put forward by logicians such as Copi and Cohen (1990) and Hurley (2003).

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a brief comparison of three philosophical perspectives, including critical realism and the two current dominant perspectives in IS research—positivism and interpretivism Section 3 provides a more in-depth discussion of critical realism. Section 4 discusses empirical generalization, theoretical generalization, and theory testing with a contrast of the three perspectives. Section 5 draws some implications for strategic information systems (SIS) research, and Section 6 offers some conclusions.

## 2. A brief comparison of key philosophical perspectives

Positivism and interpretivism still dominate IS research in spite of Weber’s (2004, p. xi) comment that ‘‘it is time to assign the rhetoric of positivism versus interpretivism to the scrap heap’’. This section briefly compares the ontological, epistemological, and methodological assumptions between positivism, interpretivism, and critical realism before introducing a more in-depth overview of the relatively new perspective of critical realism. The comparison is summarized in Table 1 with some key references suggested for each perspective.

Positivism assumes an objective reality, adopting the Humean conception of causality which treats the constant conjunction of events as an indicator of a causal relationship. Following Hempel’s (1965) covering-law model of explanation, positivists adopt a hypothetico-deductive approach and aim at discovering law-like relationships among a set of empirically measurable constructs that have predictive power. Using statistical methods in data analysis is a major way of establishing a nomothetic body of knowledge. Since positivism relies on correlations between variables to identify empirical regularities and infer causation, reliability of results increases with sample size. Thus, positivist researchers tend to use quantitative methods such as questionnaire surveys, experiments, and analysis of archival data.

Unlike positivism, interpretivism considers the methods of natural science inadequate for conducting social science research. While the natural sciences seek to explain non-intentional phenomena, the job of the social sciences is to understand intentional phenomena by interpreting the meanings attached to the phenomena by their actors (Schutz, 1970). Viewing reality as socially constructed (Berger and Luckmann, 1967), interpretivists adopt a relativist stance ‘‘such that diverse meanings are assumed to exist and to influence how people understand and respond to the objective world’’ (Gephart, 2004, p. 457). They aim to ‘‘interpret the meanings and actions of actors according to their own subjective frame of reference’’ (Williams, 2000, p. 210). Intentional phenomena are therefore meaningful in the sense that they are what they are according to the interpretation of those who are involved. Intensive interviews and observations are required in order to understand such phenomena, and qualitative methods such as ethnographies and case studies are preferred.

As discussed in the next section, similar to positivism, critical realism assumes an objective reality but that reality is stratified, consisting of structures and mechanisms that generate events observed in our daily lives (Bhaskar, 1978). Employing the inference method of retroduction, researchers probe into ever-deepening layers of reality and attempt to work out theories that describe the structures and mechanisms responsible for giving rise to the observable events. Unlike the positivist stress on discovering law-like relationships with predictive power, critical realists are content with adequate explanations of past events (Sayer, 1992). While positivists and interpretivists tend to favor quantitative and qualitative research methods respectively, critical realists do not have any specific preference. In fact, adopting a critical realist approach, Mingers (2001) advocates a pluralist methodology in IS research.

## 3. Critical realism

Critical realism is a growing intellectual movement in various social science disciplines. There is a diverse array of philosophical positions called ‘‘realist’’ (Putnam, 1987). The version of realism advocated by Bhaskar (1978) and Harré (1970) has two basic philosophical theses. First, a reality independent of human perception and cognition exists. Second, this reality has its own inherent order (Fay, 1996). Bhaskar (1978) draws a sharp distinction between scientific concepts, laws, and theories that he calls the transitive objects of knowledge, and describes the intransitive objects of knowledge as the structures and mechanisms of the world to which our theories aim to refer. Intransitivity here means that these structures and mechanisms exist independently of our knowledge of them, Structures are defined as sets of internally related obiects, and mechanisms refer to ways of acting (Sayer, 1992). The latter are inherent to the former, enabling or limiting what can happen within a given context (Wynn and Williams, 2012). Objects are internally linked within a structure in the sense that their identities depend on their being in a relationship with the other structural components. For instance, the software vendor–buyer relation presupposes the existence of a purchase agreement governing the terms and conditions of usage, monetary payment, the software itself, customer support, and so forth; together they form a structure. Models used in IS theories in fact display structures of interrelated constructs

## 3.1. Domains of reality

The combined effects of structures and mechanisms may generate events that in turn may be observed. However, events take place whether or not they are observed or detected by people. Structures and mechanisms are real and distinct from the patterns of events they generate; they remain the same even when they counteract each other in such a way that no events are generated. The absence of an event does not necessarily mean that the underlying mechanism does not exist. The fact that the book on my desk does not fall to the ground does not imply that the gravitational force no longer exists, but instead that the force is counteracted by the presence of the desk.

Bhaskar (1978) distinguishes between three domains of reality: the real, actual, and empirical. The empirical domain is made up of events experienced through either direct or indirect observation, and is the only domain positivism and interpretivism focus on. This domain should be distinguished from the actual domain, consisting of events whether or not they are observed. Finally, the real domain is one in which generative mechanisms capable of producing patterns of events reside. These domains are distinct and nested in that the empirical domain is a subset of the actual domain, which in turn is a subset of the real domain (Bhaskar, 1978). If one considers a live volcano the real domain and considers eruptions the events in question, then the actual domain consists of eruptions regardless of whether or not they are observed while the empirical domain consists of only those eruptions that are observed. The moves from the real to the actual and from the actual to the empirical depend on the ambient contingent conditions (Outhwaite, 1987). Hence, structures and their associated mechanisms are ontologically decoupled from the events they produce (Smith, 2006).

## 3.2. Causality

Perhaps the distinguishing feature of realism is its view of causality (Pawson, 1989). Contrary to the Humean conception of causality adopted by positivism, realism argues that a constant conjunction of events observed in the empirical domain is neither a sufficient nor a necessary condition for a causal law. There is an ontological distinction between causal laws and their associated patterns of events. Realism holds that there is a real connection between causes and their effects in the form of some ‘‘natural necessity’’ that links the two together. It posits that there is a more basic level of reality than events, namely the underlying structures and mechanisms residing in the real domain that generate patterns of events and subsequently lead to the establishment of causal laws. Harré (1970, p. 125) points out that ‘‘scientific explanation consists in finding or imagining plausible generative mechanisms for the patterns amongst events’’.

From a realist viewpoint, causality concerns the causal powers of objects or their relations rather than a relationship between discrete events which we identify as cause and effect. To ascribe a power to an object is to say something about what it will or can do in the appropriate conditions in virtue of its intrinsic nature (Harré and Madden, 1975). For example, airplanes have the power to fly; people have the power to learn, walk, speak, work, and so on. Whether or not a person will perform a certain action and thus exercise his power has to be explained by acknowledging the conditions surrounding the action. Often causal powers inhere not simply in individual objects but in the social relations and structures they form. Hence, a CEO derives his or her powers from interdependent relations with stakeholders such as shareholders, the board of directors, subordinates, customers, suppliers, and creditors.

Whether or not a causal power is actually activated depends on conditions whose presence and configuration are contingent. When causal powers are exercised, the actual effects of the mechanisms activated will again depend on the conditions in which they work: ‘‘[t]he relationship between causal powers or mechanisms and their effects is therefore not fixed, but contingent’’ (Sayer, 1992, p. 107). There are two types of contingent conditions, intrinsic and extrinsic. Intrinsic conditions refer to the nature of an object enabling consistent mechanism operations. An example of violating an intrinsic condition is that an airplane with a broken wing cannot fly properly. Extrinsic conditions are external to the object and yet affect the functioning of mechanisms. An extrinsic condition for an airplane to fly is not satisfied if the outside temperature is so low that the gas in its tank is frozen. The relationships between structures, mechanisms, events, and contingent conditions in a complex system are represented in Fig. 1. Mechanisms generate events in conjunctures when activated. For instance, $M _ { 1 }$ in the figure generates events $E _ { 1 }$ and $E _ { 2 } .$ The same mechanism may sometimes produce different events depending on the contingent conditions. One structure can be associated with more than one mechanism.

Bhaskar (1978, p. 70) defines a closed system as ‘‘one in which a constant conjunction of events obtains; i.e. in which an event of type a is invariably accompanied by an event of type b’’. When both intrinsic and extrinsic conditions are satisfied, a closed system is achieved and regularities of events are generated. Scientific experiments are conducted precisely because of the open character of the world where events are subiect to diverse causal variations. The idea of experiments in the natural sciences is to create closed systems by producing the appropriate intrinsic and extrinsic conditions so that regular sequences of events occur in the empirical domain. Positivists focus on the regular sequences of events generated under controlled experimental conditions based on the Humean conception of causality. On the other hand, realists regard experiments as providing ideal conditions for the study of mechanisms (Pawson and Tilley, 1997).

## 3.3. Critical realist view of case studies

Perry (1998) argues that critical realism should be the preferred philosophical perspective for guiding case study research, although positivism and interpretivism are currently the dominant perspectives. The case study method accords well with the critical realist view that closure conditions are rarely achievable in the social sciences. This is attested by the artificiality of laboratory experiments performed by social psychologists (Harré and Second, 1972), and that investigation of social phe nomena often must be conducted in an open-system environment. There are two main reasons for the openness of social systems. First, social structures are sometimes modified by human actions and thereby violate the extrinsic conditions. Second, our capacity for learning, self reflection, and change violates the intrinsic conditions. The impossibility of constructing closed systems in the social sciences implies that the theories so generated mainly serve the purpose of explanation rather than prediction (Sayer, 1992). The critical realist does not deem the discovery of quantitative social laws that can accurately predict social events as feasible (Tsang and Kwan, 1999).

Case researchers collect detailed data via various sources of events that occur naturally. Interviews conducted in case studies are usually less formal, less standardized, and more interactive than questionnaire surveys, enabling the researcher to learn how the different circumstances that the respondents face would affect their behavior (Sayer, 1992). Although data are collected in the empirical domain, case studies shed light on the specific contingent conditions under which the postulated mechanisms operate and interact in the real domain by observing social phenomena in their natural contexts (Tsoukas, 1989). Using retroduction (detailed in Section 4.3 below), researchers are able to tease out ever-deepening layers of reality and peer into the box of causality in order to locate the factors lying between some critical cause and its purported effect (Gerring, 2007).

## 4. Generalizing from the results of a case study

As shown in Section 1, IS researchers hold different and varied opinions concerning how far the results of a case study can be generalized. These opinions are based on different philosophical perspectives. In this section I first present the general views of positivism, interpretivism, and critical realism with respect to generalizing from case findings. I then provide a more in-depth comparison between these perspectives by classifying generalization into two main types: empirical and theoretical (Gomm et al., 2000; Sharp, 1998; Tsang and Williams, 2012). I also discuss theory testing, which is closely related to theoretical generalization

## 4.1. Different views of generalization

From a positivist perspective, a case study is based on a very small sample and its contribution to establishing law-like relationships is therefore marginal. Yin (2009, p. 38) strengthens the methodological legitimacy of case studies by arguing that a ‘‘fatal flaw in doing case studies is to conceive of statistical generalization as the method of generalizing the results of the case study’’ because cases are not sampling units and should be treated as experiments. Lee (1989b) attempts to justify the generalizability of case findings by making arguments parallel to Yin’s (2009) analogy and referring to laboratory experiments in the natural sciences: ‘‘a single organizational case study, conducted as a natural experiment, can assure no more, but no less generalizability than any single laboratory experiment conducted in the natural sciences’’ (p. 135).

Interpretivists hold different views of generalization which can be roughly divided into three categories. The first and most extreme category argues that since multiple ‘‘realities’’ are created by different parties related to a phenomenon, generalization is simply impossible. For instance, Lincoln and Guba (1985) coin the term ‘‘transferability’’ instead of using any variant of ‘‘generalizability’’ because they argue that generalization is impossible to achieve in qualitative research: ‘‘[t]he aim of inquiry is to develop an idiographic body of knowledge. This knowledge is best encapsulated in a series of ‘working hypotheses’ that describe the individual case. Generalizations are impossible since phenomena are neither time- nor context-free’’ (p. 238). Using a different rationale, Denzin (1983, p. 133) also claims that ‘‘the interpretivist rejects generalization as a goal and never aims to draw randomly selected samples of human experience’’. His core argument is that every instance of human interaction represents ‘‘a slice from the life world’’ (p. 134) carrying layers of meaning. Such layers come in mul tiples and are often contradictory. Investigation of each instance of human interaction has to be done hermeneutically and be seen as carrying ‘‘its own logic, sense of order, structure, and meaning’’ (p. 134). In short, there is an ‘‘inherent indeterminateness in the everyday life world’’ (p. 133) resulting in too much variability to allow for generalizing from one specific in stance to others

The second category emphasizes that case study research involves a kind of generalization that is very different from the positivist conception of generalization. For example, Stake and Trumbull (1982) create the term ‘‘naturalistic generalization’’ arguing that cases are a powerful means for building naturalistic generalization. Cases provide thick descriptions and vicarious experiential accounts that are more epistemologically in harmony with the reader’s experience than quantitative studies. As such, the reader of a case is able to determine if and how far the case can be used to understand a new setting (Stake, 1994). Naturalistic generalization, or what Chenail (2010) calls case-to-case generalization, is therefore a kind of tacit recognition by the reader of the similarities and differences between a case and a new setting. A problem here is that ‘‘naturalistic generalization" is a misnomer (so is “case-to-case generalization") because the term “generalization" is misused. The core idea of making inference from a case to a new setting falls into the domain of inductive analogy since it concerns the inference from particular premises to a particular conclusion. Inductive analogy is one kind of induction and does not involve generalization (Tsang and Williams, 2012). In brief, naturalistic generalization is inconsistent with the meaning of generalization.

For the third category Walsham (1995) puts forward four types of generalization: development of concepts, generation of theory, drawing of specific implications, and contribution of rich insight. Only two—generation of theory and drawing of specific implications—are consistent with the meaning of generalization. Development of concepts is abstraction and different from generalization because there is no conclusion that must be ascertained in order to be true or false (Tsang and Williams, 2012). Contribution of rich insight is a miscellaneous category referring to capturing ‘‘insights from the reading of reports and results from case studies that are not easily categorised as concepts, theories or specific implications" (Walsham 1995, p. 80). However, not all such insights are concerned with generalization (i.e., inferring from the observation of the particular to a general statement or proposition), although some of them might

Critical realism deems that conditions of closure are rarely attainable in the social sciences, but it does not disparage generalization. Danermark et al. (2002, p. 73) argue that ‘‘[m]ethods for acquiring knowledge of the general and for examining the validity of generalizations are fundamental for all social science research’’. The case study method is an excellent way to explore the interaction of structures, events, human actions, and contexts for identifying and explicating generative mechanisms (Wynn and Williams, 2012). When compared with quantitative methods, such as questionnaire surveys and analysis of archival data, case study research is more likely to discover relations that are ‘‘causal, structural, and substantial, i.e., relations of connections’’ (Sayer, 1992, p. 246). Such discoveries often have generalization implications as detailed in the following discussion.

## 4.2. Empirical generalization

Empirical generalization concerns whether the findings of a case study are typical of the population from which the cases are drawn or of another population. Thus, it is confined to the empirical domain and excludes the domain of deep reality structures (Danermark et al., 2002). Empirical generalization sometimes serves as the first stage of the process that aims at generating theoretical generalization. However, this implies neither that theoretical generalization is necessarily preceded by empirical generalization nor that empirical generalization is necessarily followed by theoretical generalization. Either kind of generalization can exist on its own. Statistical generalization is a common type of empirical generalization (Tsang and Williams, 2012). A key objective of empirical generalization is to establish whether or not there is some kind of empirical regularity in the population the researcher is interested in rather than providing an explanation of the regularity

Positivist discussions of case study research have focused on theoretical generalization—how to build theory from case studies—at the expense of empirical generalization (Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007; Yin, 2009). For instance, Yin’s (2009) work is based on the premise that ‘‘the investigator is striving to generalize a particular set of results to some broader theory’’ (p. 43). This may be a knee-jerk reaction to the popular view that case studies are weak in generalizability, which is usually taken to mean statistical generalization.

The idea of empirical regularity does not fit well with interpretivists’ emphasis on interpreting meanings and actions according to the actor’s own and probably unique frame of reference because ‘‘interpretations of reality may shift over time as circumstances, obiectives, and constituencies change" (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991, p. 14). Thus, empirical generalization is not generally recognized by interpretivists. Naturalistic generalization is somewhat related to empirical generalization in the sense that it concerns comparing one setting with another in the empirical domain. Nevertheless, as mentioned it is inductive analogy rather than generalization. In sum, as Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991, p. 5) comment on interpretivist studies, ‘‘Generalization from the setting (usually only one or a handful of field sites) to a population is not sought’’.

Although the ultimate goal of scientific research is to develop theories that can explain the empirical phenomenon in question (i.e., theoretical generalization), critical realism does recognize the function of empirical generalization. There have been incidents in natural science that the discovery of empirical regularities alone contributed to the well-being of mankind. An excellent example is the discovery of penicillin by Alexander Fleming in 1928. The discovery helped to save many lives during the Second World War, although the molecular structure of penicillin was determined by Dorothy Hodgkin only in 1945 and the exact generative mechanism that accounted for its healing effect was gradually understood even later (Lax, 2004). <sup>1</sup>

Admittedly, the extent of regularities observed in the social world is much smaller than that in the natural world because social phenomena are historically conditioned and culturally determined (Nagel, 1979). Yet social phenomena are not completely chaotic. Observations in the empirical domain indicate the existence of some invariance in the social world. Lawson (1997), a critical realist, describes this situation by creating the term ‘‘demi-regularity’’, which ‘‘is precisely a partial event regularity which prima facie indicates the occasional, but less than universal, actualization of a mechanism or tendency, over a definite region of time–space’’ (p. 204). Where demi-regularities are observed empirically there is evidence of relatively enduring structures and mechanisms at play in the real domain. Owing to the existence of demi-regularities in the social world, it is meaningful for IS researchers to make claims based on empirical generalization. Danermark et al. (2002, p. 166) maintain that ‘‘social scientific research is about identifying demi-regularities in the empirical domain and from them trying to find explanation’’. Researchers may consider identifying demi-regularities as the first step of an inquiry into possible mechanisms that reside in the real domain (Manicas, 2006), and this step can inform subsequent data-gathering and analysis of qualitative data collected from case studies (Zachariadis et al., 2013).

Critical realists deem that although it is not possible to statistically generalize case findings to the population from which the cases were drawn (Cavaye, 1996), case studies do have something to contribute if we consider the broader domain of empirical generalization. A representative case can provide useful information regarding certain characteristics of the population to which the case belongs. Consider for instance Malhotra et al.’s (2001) study of what they call a Virtual Cross-valuechain Collaborative Creative team (or VC<sup>3</sup> team) at Boeing–Rocketdyne. The team’s mission was to develop a radically new product. The team consisted of eight engineers from three different companies with expertise in different disciplines. They had never worked together on any projects before and thus did not share a common set of coordination norms. Their primary obligation was to their own company and no team member allocated more than 15% of his or her time to this project Throughout the project life of 10 months, all interactions were through a virtual collaborative technology. At the end, the team successfully achieved their mission.

The authors were able to closely trace the team’s life cycle for the entire project life using multiple data sources. They identified three management practices adopted by the team: (1) establishing an umbrella agreement in advance of team formation, (2) using collaborative technology not only to collaborate but also to manage knowledge, and (3) restructuring work processes without changing the core creative needs of the team. Owing to the complexity of the phenomenon, it is virtually impossible to collect such rich longitudinal data using a large sample quantitative research method, such as a questionnaire survey. In fact, identifying a sufficient number of $\mathsf { V C } ^ { 3 }$ teams to form a sample is itself a daunting task. Although quantitative methods are generally superior to case study research in terms of statistical generalization, unlike case study research they may not be able to discover potential demi-regularities that involve complex relationships among a substantial number of variables. This is a key relative merit of case study research that has been neglected by the literature.

Malhotra et al.’s (2001) study is more descriptive than explanatory and admits that ‘‘the generalizability of the results can only be assessed by observing future similar cases and by applying theory to understand the behavior patterns’’ (p. 245). Since the study was based on only one case, it is premature to conclude that the three management practices will be commonly found among similar $\mathsf { V C } ^ { 3 }$ teams that succeed in achieving their objectives. There is a chance that the results of this study are partly the outcome of certain case idiosyncrasies. Multiple studies of the same phenomenon are required in order to determine whether or not these results constitute a demi-regularity (Helfat, 2007). However, the study is still an important initial step.

An often neglected association between case studies and empirical generalization is that they provide useful information for assessing the generalizability of their results. For instance, in addition to describing the phenomenon, Malhotra et al. (2001) discuss some contingent conditions that are likely to affect the generalizability of their results. One contingent condition concerns the nature of the communication technology used by the team. Reviewing prior studies of virtual organizations, DeSanctis and Monge (1999, p. 697) state that ‘‘the only consistent finding in the empirical literature with regard to task and media is that [the tasks of] thinking convergently, resolving conflict, or reaching consensus [are] better done faceto-face than electronically’’. On the other hand, Malhotra et al.’s (2001, p. 246) study found that ‘‘the eight engineers were performing tasks that involved convergent thinking, conflict resolution, and consensus development—and managed to do so without the use of face-to-face’’. They attribute the difference between their findings and those of prior studies to the nature of the team’s communication technology. Many of the results described in the literature are based on studies of email and computer conferencing systems, whereas the virtual team in Malhotra et al.’s study used one kind of knowledge portal tech nology. It may be difficult to combine context and content when email is used; however, ‘‘combining portal technologies with voice allows for making authorship, documents, document histories, and team comments as well as context accessible to all participants simultaneously’’ (Malhotra et al., 2001, p. 246) and accordingly facilitates the process of resolving conflicts and reaching consensus. This speculative explanation points to an interesting research direction when future attempts of theoretical generalization are made to build a theory of $\mathsf { V C } ^ { 3 }$ teams.

## 4.3. Theoretical generalization

Theoretical generalization refers to generalizing from the findings of a case study to theory, which under critical realism consists of postulated structures and mechanisms that operate in the real domain. In contrast to empirical generalization, researchers make theoretical generalizations by developing explanations of the relationships between variables observed in their case studies (Sharp, 1998). Hopefully such explanations are applicable to either the population from which the cases were drawn or to other populations (Firestone, 1993). The belief is that the generative mechanism or mechanisms that caused the observable events in a case setting will also cause similar outcomes in other settings (Zachariadis et al., 2013).

As mentioned, positivists underscore theoretical generalization. Their aim is to discover law-like relationships embedded in a theory, which is supposed to explain certain general empirical phenomena. Other than those who simply deny the possibility of generalization, interpretivists are more receptive of theoretical than empirical generalization: ‘‘[i]nterpretivists often maintain that rather than making empirical generalizations, they are making theoretical inferences, that is they draw conclusions from their data about the necessary relationships that exist amongst categories of phenomena’’ (Williams, 2000, p. 218). Similarly, among the principles that Klein and Myers (1999) propose for conducting and evaluating interpretive field studies, the principle of abstraction and generalization requires relating the idiographic details revealed by data interpretation to ‘‘theoretical, general concepts that describe the nature of human understanding and social action’’ (p. 72). As discussed above, only two of Walsham’s (1995) four types of generalization—generation of theory and drawing of specific implications—are consistent with the meaning of generalization. Both concern theoretical rather than empirical generalization. The meaning of ‘‘generation of theory’’ is self-evident. Walsham (1995, p. 80) draws on the concept of generative mechanism to explain drawing specific implications by stating that ‘‘[t]he implication provided a good description of a ‘generative mechanism’ in the case study which was investigated, and it may prove a useful insight for related work in other organizations and contexts’’. However, interpretivists’ emphasis on theoretical generalization while downplaying or even ignoring empirical generalization is problematic because a major function of theory is to explain empirical phenomena that usually display a certain degree of regularity across time and space

From a critical realist perspective, scientific theories offer what Bunge (1997) calls ‘‘mechanismic explanations’’ of empirical phenomena that unveil the phenomena’s actual or possible mechanisms. As mentioned, case study researchers not only observe events in the empirical domain, but also aim to understand the generative mechanisms in the real domain leading to the events. Unlike studies based on quantitative methods, case studies seek to investigate phenomena within their contexts rather than independent of context (Gibbert et al., 2008). Case studies provide useful information regarding how the postulated mechanisms operate under a set of contingent conditions by striving to understand empirical events in their rich context (Tsoukas, 1989).

Critical realists generate mechanismic explanations by employing a mode of inference called retroduction.<sup>2</sup> In retroduction, ‘‘we take some unexplained phenomenon and propose hypothetical mechanisms that, if they existed, would generate or cause that which is to be explained’’ (Mingers, 2004a, pp. 94–95). Thus, retroduction presents a logical argument explaining what properties must exist in order for the phenomenon of interest to exist and be what it is (Danermark et al., 2002). Unlike deduction but similar to induction, the validity of a retroductive conclusion entailing the addition of new knowledge beyond what the premises contain cannot be assessed with certainty. Unlike induction, which is chiefly concerned with whether or not unobserved entities or relationships are similar to those that have been observed, the process of retroduction is less structured and more creative. The process is likely ‘‘to operate under a logic of analogy or metaphor and to draw heavily on the inves tigator’s perspective, beliefs and experience’’ (Lawson, 1997, p. 212).

Researchers are likely to engage in retroductive inference iteratively during the stages of data collection, coding, and analysis. Since retroduction is largely a creative and intuitive process, Wynn and Williams (2012) argue that specific guidance for retroducing mechanisms is problematic. That said, Eco (1983) proposes and Bertilsson (2004) elaborates four types of retroduction—overcoded, undercoded, creative, and meta—that may be useful for researchers’ reference. For the purpose of my discussion overcoded retroduction refers to the case where the mechanism that explains a phenomenon is given either automatically or semi-automatically. The explanation is obvious based on existing knowledge. For undercoded retroduction the current body of knowledge suggests a number of potential mechanisms and the researcher has to select the most plausible one given the specific context in which their cases are located. When engaging in creative retroduction the researcher has to invent the mechanism because no suitable ones exist.³ Finally, meta-retroduction occurs when observations do not fit our cur: rent conceptual apparatus and require us to think ‘‘anew’’ (Bertilsson, 2004). This may result in some kind of paradigm change

For example, consider Bygstad’s (2010) case study of Norwegian Corp. which operated in the airline, banking, and telecommunication industries. His research question is: ‘‘how can an information infrastructure provide generative mechanisms for innovation of ICT-based services?’’ (p. 156). The case data indicate two generative mechanisms. The first is the service reinforcement mechanism. An information infrastructure offers complimentary products, making it more attractive to users and generating greater use. This in turn increases the size of the infrastructure; this growth generates a profit that may be invested in innovations. Since this mechanism, which was originally proposed by Grindley (1995), emerged rather naturally from the case data, it illustrates overcoded retroduction. The second mechanism is called innovation reinforcement mechanism. An information infrastructure offers what Bygstad (2010) calls ‘‘a space of possibilities’’, which creates new service ideas. Together with external partners, new components are then assembled into new services that in turn increase the space of possibilities. Bygstad (2010) undertook creative retroduction and invented this mechanism. Together these two mechanisms build on each other and lead to the growth of the information infrastructure and innovation of new services. Bygstad (2010) also points out that several potential mechanisms, such as the market mechanism and entrepreneurial drive, might have been derived from his case data. He reviewed and ruled out these alternative mechanisms by engaging in undercoded retroduction

Another example is Volkoff et al.’s (2007) longitudinal case study of an enterprise system implementation in a multinational manufacturer of precision industrial products. The authors identify a major deficiency of existing theories that explain how technology leads to organizational change. These theories ‘‘have focused either on the technology and ignored the influence of human agency, or on social interaction and ignored the technology’’ (Volkoff et al., 2007, p. 832). They develop a theory of technology-mediated organizational change by drawing on Feldman and Pentland’s (2003) conceptualization of organizational routines and Archer’s (1995) morphogenetic approach to organizational change. Their data indicate that the overall mechanism of change is the embedding of organizational elements including organizational routines, roles, and data into the software. The result of embedding differs for different organizational elements. For instance, a consequence of the routine embedding is that the variety in the ways a routine can be performed is reduced, whereas role embedding often changes the job content of a role. Although Volkoff et al. hesitate to generalize their theory to technologies other than enterprise systems, they deem that the theory is likely to apply to enterprise systems in general. In addition to creative retroduction, this study shows some signs of meta-retroduction since the authors reflected on existing theories that explain how technology affects organizational change and found that these theories were not satisfactory. Their new theory may represent a revolution in this area of research.

All else constant, a multiple-case design provides a stronger basis for theoretical generalization than a single-case design. However, if a rigorous and defensible mechanismic explanation has been produced in a single case as exemplified by Bygstad’s (2010) and Volkoff et al.’s (2007) case studies discussed above, then the elements that constitute the explanation provide a basis for developing theory beyond the case (Easton, 2010b). Moreover, studies of single ‘‘deviant’’ or ‘‘extreme’’ cases can be useful for identifying new theoretical constructs or postulating new mechanisms for theory development (George and Bennett, 2005).

## 4.4. Theory testing

While a great deal of attention has been paid to building theory from case studies, much less has been given to using case studies for the purpose of theory testing. Theory building in fact goes hand in hand with theory testing. Critical realism admits that our knowledge is fallible (Sayer, 1992). Given the intransitivity of real structures and mechanisms, we can never know for certain that the mechanisms proposed by a theory actually do exist (Mingers, 2004a). A methodological implication is that we should attempt to eliminate alternative explanations by testing the potential effects of these mechanisms, particularly in contexts other than the one where the theory was created. For example, Volkoff et al.’s (2007) theory of technologymediated organizational change was constructed based on one case of enterprise system implementation. Further research has to test the theory in other settings in order to establish its domain of applicability. The results of such tests may lead to revision of the theory, which is part of the theory building process. As indicated by his research question stated above, Bygstad (2010) began his study as a theory building endeavor. The fact that his results confirmed Grindley’s (1995) service reinforcement mechanism has the unintended effect of theory testing.

Positivist discussions of case studies often downplay their role in theory testing since positivism relies on correlations between variables in order to infer causation and tends to test theory using statistical methods. The number of cases included in a single study is usually too small to allow such tests. Interpretivism is premised on the belief that ‘‘social process is not captured in hypothetical deductions, covariances, and degrees of freedom’’ (Rosen, 1991, p. 8). Interpretivists generally do not recognize theory testing as a function of case study research partly due to the natural association of theory testing with the hypothetico-deductive approach to positivism’s scientific inquiry. Walsham (1995), for example, develops four types of generalization from case findings but is silent regarding theory testing. Likewise, interpretivist discussions of case studies rarely touch on the issue of theory testing.

From a critical realist perspective theory testing refers to ‘‘showing that the generative mechanism that the theory describes produces the actual events that constitute the research domain to which the theory applies’’ (Johnston and Smith, 2010, p. 32). Tsang (2006) reviewed the history of empirical research on transaction cost economics and found that it had been dominated by quantitative studies. The methods used in such studies did not directly investigate generative mechanisms and thus failed to rule out alternative mechanismic explanations of the phenomenon under investigation. Case studies are well suited for theory testing because they adopt an in-depth investigation approach to revealing generative mechanisms. The mechanisms revealed can be compared with those proposed by the theory concerned, and confirming results throw light on the generalizability of the theory to new contexts. On the other hand, disconfirming results contribute to establishing the boundary conditions of the theory.

For example, Silva and Hirschheim’s (2007) study of the implementation of SIS in the two largest hospitals of Guatemala, both of which were under the control of the Ministry of Health. Their study was conducted in a unique context in the sense that virtually all prior studies on this topic had focused on for-profit organizations in developed countries. Although there is no clear theory explaining the implementation of SIS, Silva and Hirschheim’s results as shown in their Table 4 have either disconfirmed or supplemented a number of theoretical propositions generated from previous studies. For instance, these studies propose three main reasons explaining failure of SIS to be institutionalized: (1) inadequate planning, (2) lack of leadership, and (3) organizations not having the necessary skills for designing and implementing SIS. Interestingly, Silva and Hirschheim’s results suggest that ‘‘an SIS can fail to be institutionalized despite having adequate planning, strong leadership, and very knowledgeable IS professionals’’ (p. 347). Instead they found that the most relevant factors influencing the outcome were related to the organizational structure and political changes. Their results indicate that a different set of generative mechanisms were at work, establishing a boundary condition for the theoretical propositions generated by prior studies— these propositions are not likely to be applicable to non-profit organizations in developing countries.

Also consider Smith’s (2011) comparative case study of the implementation of two information and communication technologies (ICTs) in the Chilean public sector. The author first derives from the literature three general propositions regarding institutional trustworthiness related specifically to institutional performance, aligned interests, and the values of fairness and distributive justice. For each ICT he presents a within-case analysis by examining how its implementation interacts with the trustworthiness of the public sector with respect to each proposition. Then he conducts a comparative case analysis by comparing and contrasting the similarities and differences between the findings of the two cases, again with reference to the three propositions. The results indicate some fundamental limitations for existing theories concerning the building of insti tutional trust through implementing ICTs by the government

Theory testing concerns how a theory performs within a range of ‘‘institutional settings, cultural contexts, time periods, geographic settings, and situational contexts’’ (George and Bennett, 2005, p. 119). Conducting replicated studies under various contexts is a means of testing theories. Tsang and Kwan (1999) create a classification of six types of replication from a critical realist perspective, and recommend that for the sake of theory testing researchers should begin with exact replication where a previous study is repeated on the same population by following basically the same procedures. Thus, the contingent conditions are close to those of the original study. If different results are obtained it would be easier to identify the reasons for the difference. Unfortunately, owing to journal editors’ emphasis on novel contribution such replicated studies are rarely published.

## 4.5. Summary

Table 2 summarizes the above discussion by comparing between the positivist, interpretivist, and critical realist views of how the results of a case study can be generalized. Positivists embrace theoretical generalization at the expense of empirical generalization and theory testing as exemplified by the single focus of Eisenhardt and Graebner (2007) and Yin (2009) on how to build theory from case studies.

Interpretivists uphold case study research as a legitimate or even the preferred research method. Yet they have varied views of both the definition of generalization and whether and how the results of a case study can be generalized. Overall, interpretivists perceive a limited role for case studies in producing generalizable findings. Some interpretivists simply replace the term ‘‘generalizability’’ by such terms as ‘‘transferability’’ (Lincoln and Guba, 1985), ‘‘comparability’’, and ‘‘translatability’’ (LeCompte and Preissle, 1993).

Finally, critical realism recognizes the role of case study research in empirical generalization, theoretical generalization, and theory testing. Moreover, the role is properly justified within the ontological and epistemological framework of critical realism. The aim of empirical generalization is to study the demi-regularities observed in the empirical domain while that of theoretical generalization is to understand the generative mechanisms in the real domain that produce the regularities. Fallibility of knowledge implies that once a theory is created we need to subject it to further empirical tests, and case studies are a suitable way of conducting such tests.

## 5. Implications for research in strategic information systems

Case studies represent a significant method for SIS research. For example, among the 316 research papers published in the Journal of Strategic Information Systems during 1991–2009, 15 have the term ‘‘case study’’ in their titles (see Gable, 2010). The significance of case study research is likely to increase rather than decrease in the future, Merali et al 's (2012) review of the SIS research domain for the last three decades or so indicates that IT capabilities are employed in an increasingly uncertain, complex, and dynamic competitive landscape. SIS researchers must often conduct their studies in a natural setting where control or manipulation of subjects is not feasible in order to obtain an in-depth understanding of the phenomenon. Case study research is a particularly suitable option under these circumstances (Benbasat et al., 1987), as illustrated by Nolan’s (2012) recent longitudinal study of the development of the Boeing 787 aircraft. Buhl et al. (2012) also conducted a review of SIS research concluding that SIS researchers should become boundary spanners who actively engage in industry collaboration. Yet one of the challenges faced by these researchers is that their small case bases may ‘‘invite questions about re search rigor, generalizability, and validity’’ (Buhl et al., 2012, p. 174)—an issue that triggered this article

The above discussion clarifies and compares the positivist, interpretivist, and critical realist views of generalizing from case findings, offering two main contributions toward improving SIS case study research. First, it helps researchers properly structure their generalization claims regardless of which perspective they subscribe to. For instance, consider Abraham and Junglas’s (2011) longitudinal study of the IS implementation process at Sentara Healthcare. This study contributed to a more descriptive understanding of a mechanism by which successful organizational transformation in a healthcare organization can occur—the IS implementation process as an opportunity for changing coordination, culture, and learning. . . .We suggest that the insights that emerged from this study would apply to any networked organization comprised of autonomous professionals that perform long-linked tasks in a somewhat unpredictable, transformational process facilitated via technology (Abraham and Junglas, 2011, p. 190).

The first statement refers to a mechanism and concerns theoretical generalization, whereas the second statement discusses inference to other networked organizations and concerns empirical generalization. Their generalization claim would be more forceful if they could explicitly state the nature of the generalization that they want to make. Moreover, Table 2 indicates that positivism downplays empirical generalization. If they intend to adopt a positivist view they may prefer to drop their claim of applying their study’s insights to other networked organizations, particularly because theirs is a single-case study. As discussed in Section 4.2, a multiple-case design is often required for determining whether or not a result represents a demi-regularity.

Another example is Deodhar et al.’s (2012) study of hybrid business models that integrate the open source approach with the traditional proprietary software development approach. The authors adopt an interpretivist single-case design and make the following comment concerning the generalizability of their results:

In using the single case, the idea was to provide insights into hybrid business models as a configured system of organizational practices and emergent strategies, which had not been previously examined. The authors did not aspire to make grand claims or generalizations about a set of practices that would be part of all forms of hybrid business models; rather, we found it useful to conduct an in-depth analysis of a single case study to identify specific practices and strategies (Deodhar et al., 2012, p. 279)

The first statement seems to imply a theoretical generalization whereas near the end of the second statement the authors seem to say that they are only interested in studying the specific practices and strategies of their single case without any intention of generalizing their results. Deodhar et al.’s (2012) ambiguous position may be due to the fact that interpretivists are somewhat divided in their views regarding how far case findings are generalizable, as shown in Table 2. It would still be more appropriate for them to take a clear stance at the beginning such as Walsham’s (1995) view that interpretivist case studies allow for theoretical generalization. They then incorporate it into their research design, data analysis, and presentation of results.

Moreover, Deodhar et al. (2012) claim to follow the ‘‘modified grounded theory’’ method:

Thus a researcher goes into the field with his mental models in place and guides the data collection and analysis procedures; this is modified grounded theory. The challenge, however, is to avoid enforcing this model on the data but, instead, allow the data to confirm the model. If the data does not fit the model, then the model has to change (Deodhar et al., 2012, p. 279).

If the researcher already has a theoretical model before collecting data and attempts to examine whether or not the data confirm the model then this activity would fall into the realm of theory testing discussed in Section 4.4, and Deodhar et al. (2012) may prefer to explicitly relate this to their modified grounded theory.

The above examples of Abraham and Junglas’s (2011) and Deodhar et al.’s (2012) studies show that it advisable for SIS researchers to spell out the philosophical perspective that they intend to adopt, consistently follow the tenets of that perspective, and make their generalization claims explicit. A clear and consistent generalization claim helps to strengthen a case study’s contribution to the SIS literature, regardless of the philosophical perspective on which the study is based.

The second contribution of this article is that it brings in the insights of critical realism so that the generalization potential of case studies can be more fully explored. Since interpretivism and positivism have been dominant in IS research, it is not surprising that empirical generalization and theory testing have been neglected by case researchers, who mostly claim theoretical generalization as their contribution. Yet focusing only on generalizing to theory may be counter-productive. Sometimes the case findings do not provide sufficient information concerning the underlying structures and mechanisms; however, these findings may indicate some sort of empirical regularity. If that is the case then researchers should be satisfied with describing the regularity and defer the task of theoretical generalization until further data are collected.

Similarly, the role played by case studies in theory testing should be better appreciated. It is a misconception to equate theory testing with statistical tests based on large samples. Rich data collected from cases, particularly deviant cases (such as the above-mentioned Silva and Hirschheim’s (2007) study of the implementation of SIS in Guatemala), may be useful for confirming or disconfirming the structures and mechanisms proposed by existing theories. These tests provide insights that cannot be drawn from the usual correlation-based statistical tests. This article aims at rectifying the bias in favor of theoretical generalization as the major contribution of case studies by explicating a critical realist view of generalizing from case findings.

## 6. Conclusion

In spite of its significance in SIS research, the status of case study research as a legitimate scientific method is threatened by the usual criticism that case findings are not readily generalizable. Discussions of this important methodological issue have been constrained by the two dominant philosophical perspectives adopted by IS researchers, namely positivism and interpretivism. I present a critical realist view of generalizing from case findings as an alternative perspective. I support Min gers’ (2004b) argument that critical realism can be a significant way forward for ending the epistemological battle between positivism and interpretivism. Wynn and Williams (2012) recently proposed a set of methodological principles for conducting critical realist case study research. I supplement their discussion by showing that critical realism offers a more comprehensive view than positivism and interpretivism concerning how case studies may contribute to theory development through empirical generalization, theoretical generalization, and theory testing. It is high time SIS scholars consider adopting critical realism as another viable philosophical perspective for guiding their case study research.
