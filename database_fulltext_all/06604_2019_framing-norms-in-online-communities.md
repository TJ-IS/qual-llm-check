# Framing norms in online communities

Koteswara Ivaturi<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Cecil Chua<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Swinburne Business School, Swinburne University of Technology, PO box 218, Hawthorn, Victoria, 3122, Australia

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Systems & Operations Management, University of Auckland, 12 Grafton Road, Auckland, 1010, New Zealand

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online communities Norms Beliefs Framing analysis

## A B S T R A C T

One problem many organizations deal with is how to e<sup>f</sup>ectively govern their online communities. Unlike em plovees, online community members often respond poorly to formal control mechanisms. This study develops theory on how moderators can build norms in online communities using frame analysis theory from the social movements literature. Through a case analysis of two communities focused on discussing recreational drugs, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that norm formation is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by two phenomena: (1) norm acceptance, and (2) norm transience. Norm acceptance requires that moderators be consistent in their expression of norms. To a lesser extent, moderators must be credible in their expression of norms. If these things occur, the community will accept the norms. Norm transience, on the other hand, occurs when moderators are not sensitive to the community identity. If accepted norms are not aligned with community identity, they will be dropped by the community within one or two years.

## 1. Introduction

Increasingly, online communities are playing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant roles in the world economy. Globally, the economic value of adopting online communities is estimated at USD 1 trillion annually [12]. Many commercial and non-commercial organizations have already adopted online communities [41,56,15]. For example, Dell runs IdeaStorm, an online community that allows customers to initiate and vote on product ideas. Noncommercial organizations such as PatientsLikeMe.com provide a platform for patients su<sup>f</sup>ering from similar conditions to connect and share information. To be successful, online communities need to have active user engagement, i.e., members need to engage in online discussion that furthers the community's agenda. As such, leaders of online communities face an interesting conundrum. On the one hand, it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult for community leaders to directly exert formal control on their community members [22]; [62]. If community leaders <sup>fi</sup>rmly control the online discussion between members, they face the risk of member disengagement or even attrition [45]. On the other hand, if the online community is left ungoverned, undesirable member behavior can adversely impact the public perception of the online community and the community will not gain new users [7]. To deal with this conundrum, community leaders must carefully cultivate desirable norms to achieve desired member behavior in online communities.

Norms are shared cognitions on the basis of which a group of people manifest routinized behavior [8]. An e<sup>f</sup>ective online community leader is one who in<sup>fl</sup>uences speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors of group members to collectively achieve something by creating norms bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the online community [52]. However, how online community leaders cultivate norms remains an unsolved problem. This study attempts to develop theory on norm formation in online communities through a crosscase analysis of two online communities focused on the consumption of recreational drugs. The study of such non-traditional online communities better allows the study of norm formation, as many of their norms (e.g., positive attitudes toward recreational drug use) are unfamiliar. Because norms are unfamiliar, they become highly visible to researchers. In contrast, when norms are familiar, they become invisible.

We examine norm formation in these communities using the lens of frame analysis [5]. Frame analysis is an ideal lens, as it explicitly explores how individuals are convinced to perform speci<sup>fi</sup>c actions, in this case, adopt new norms. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that norm formation is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by two phenomena: (1) norm acceptance, where the norms proposed by moderators are accepted and (2) norm transience, where norms don’t sustain over a period of time. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that norm formation requires moderators to be consistent in their expression of norms. To a lesser extent, moderators must be credible in their ex pression of norms. Norm transience on the other hand, re<sup>fl</sup>ects moderators being insensitive to the community identity. Only norms aligned with community identity will be complied with.

Our paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews the literature on online community governance and norm formation. It is highlighted that while much of the literature emphasizes the importance of norms on online community governance, how such norms can be developed remains unclear. We then discuss in detail the frame analysis lens, and how it can be used in studying norm formation. We follow with our research methodology detailing how we collected data and analyzed our two case sites. We then present our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and end with a brief discussion and conclusion.

## 2. Online communities and norms

Online communities are generally formed as open collectives of dispersed and often unacquainted individuals who share a common interest [19]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of online communities such as listservs, newsgroups, blogs, and wikis have emerged as spaces for social inter action and as such represent new forms of organizing Fulk & DeSanctis, 1995; Zammuto et al., 2007. These social interactions provide value to organizations who are constantly trying to understand customer opi nions and intentions. As such, it becomes important to understand and leverage the dynamics of these social interactions. Given that online communities carry a huge value potential, it becomes important to manage these communities e<sup>fi</sup>ciently while at the same time sustaining the motivations and aspirations of the contributors.

Many activities that take place in online communities such as knowledge sharing [9,64], knowledge construction through collaboration [38,42], and knowledge consumption are social processes. Studies have found that generating a strong sense of social identity among members of an online community has a positive impact on their con tribution to the community [36]. Such social processes are usually selfgoverned with the help of norms that emerge from within the com munity [33,48]. However, there is evidence to suggest that leaders or managers of such communities also have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence in shaping norms. Leaders can facilitate group-based control mechanisms or norms by encouraging collegial relations and enhancing the shared understanding of tasks and goals [34,43]. Accordingly, online community leaders can help achieve organizational objectives by reinforcing norms and motivating people to comply with them. Norms can facilitate achieving organizational goals, especially for IT & software develop ment projects [10,49]. However, how norms in online communities are formed is unclear as most research on norm formation has been conducted in the context of real-world management hierarchies [20,50] rather than on online communities.

Norms are shared cognitions on the basis of which a group of people manifest routinized behavior [8]. Establishing routinized behavior often increases the group’s performance e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness [25]. As a result, norms have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on overall group performance [44]. They are a low visibility and yet immensely powerful form of control over human behavior. Norms are informal as they are not written down and lack visible mechanisms to encourage compliance [2].

## 3. Norm formation

Prior research on norms identi<sup>fi</sup>es two main in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the norm formation process. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is group members’ interpretive capacity, or how group members understand what is expected of them through events that happen in their environment or through their interaction with other group members [8,47]. Based on the extent to which a group member’s own interpretation of a task matches with others in the group, the group member either implicitly re<sup>fi</sup>nes his interpretation to form a consensus or pulls the group toward his interpretation of the task. The second in<sup>fl</sup>uence is the expectations set by senior members or leaders of the group. Norms that are explicitly set or projected through the expectations of the leader of the group tend to be complied with [60]. Also, norms set by leaders that espouse the central values of the group have a higher chance of being adopted [20]. However, it is unclear how leaders or managers of online communities actually construct norms that appeal to group members. In this study, we use frame analysis [57] as a lens to explain how leaders structure information in ways that appeal to the collective interpretation of the group so that

norms can form.

## 4. Frame analysis

Frame analysis has been extensively used to analyze how people are convinced to take action based on the strength of speci<sup>fi</sup>c perspectives or frames Benford et al., 2000. Framing is an active and dynamic phenomenon of constructing or packaging a message to bring about a particular interpretation by its recipients [5]. Framing has been used in social movement research to study a diverse array of advocacy programs such as anti-smoking [32] and nuclear disarmament campaigns [57]. It has also been used in communication and media research to demonstrate how media frames public opinion through sponsored journalism & advertising [24,53,55]. Also, the management literature has demonstrated that frames are useful in bringing about strategic changes that are typically riddled with uncertainties [30]. In IS, framing has been used to explore motivating stakeholders in IS project [31].

This paper uses two main ideas proposed by the framing literature: (1) The construction of frames by the proponents of the frame and (2) the interpretation of frames by group members. The literature argues that successful framing, which in our context is the forming of norms, is linked to whether frames constructed by the proponents of the frame appeal to the target audience. A frame resonates with its targeted au dience when it aligns with the social, cultural, and personal experiences that are held close by them [58].

## 4.1. Frame construction

Most framing research conceptualizes frame construction as comprising three core framing tasks – Diagnostic, Prognostic, and Motivational [5]. Diagnostic frames describe the core of the problem, often by identifying a source of causality and appropriating blame. For example, the diagnostic frame of the Occupy Wall Street (OWS) movement, which was centered on economic inequality, blamed the banks for the global economic meltdown of 2008. Through a prognostic frame, the proponent articulates a solution or a strategy to overcome the problem presented in the diagnostic frame. For example, the prognostic frame for OWS was to apply higher tax rates for the wealthy as a means to neutralize economic disparities [54]. The literature groups these two tasks as “consensus mobilization” as they provide a platform to allow the frame proponents and their target audience to develop a shared understanding of the issue [35]. Motivational framing is then an “action mobilization” task where the constructed frame is intended to mobilize the target audience to employ the ideas proposed in the prognostic frame. For example, OWS activists appealed to the public to share their personal stories of how they were a<sup>f</sup>ected by the global economic meltdown through the social networks of the web and thereby force bankers to respond to their claims.

## 4.2. Frame interpretation

The e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of a frame is based on whether the frame is interpreted by its target audience in ways intended by the frame’s proponents (i.e., the “resonance” of the frame) [26]. There are two general in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the frame’s resonance: frame credibility and frame salience [57].

## 4.2.1. Frame credibility

Frame credibility refers to the extent recipients truly believe in the content pro<sup>f</sup>ered in the frame. Frame credibility in turn is dependent on two factors: frame consistency and empirical credibility [5].

Frame consistency is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the congruency of the frame’s articulation with the proponent’s beliefs, claims, and actions. The degree to which there is consistency between what an organization claims its beliefs are, and the representation of those beliefs in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations by the organization, will lead to a higher chance of the frame resonating with its recipients. For example, the consistency between student activists’ claims for reforms through non-violence and their behavior at Tiananmen Square resulted in the mass mobilization of Chinese citizens during the Chinese democracy movement [67].

Empirical credibility is how well the contents of the frame <sup>fi</sup>t with events happening in the real world. For example, some frames that supported nuclear disarmament failed, in part, because the nuclear winter these frames portrayed was hypothetical; no one had ever experienced one [57]. The idea here is not whether the pro<sup>f</sup>ered frames are factually valid, but whether the events relevant to the frames are believed by recipients to be real [23].

## 4.2.2. Frame salience

The frame analysis literature suggests the importance of a second dimension – frame salience in building e<sup>f</sup>ective frames. Frame salience is a measure of whether the proposed frames are relevant and identi <sup>fi</sup>able with the members of a group [59]. Frames will fail to mobilize or in<sup>fl</sup>uence group behavior if they do not align well with the group’s social identity [61]. For example, frames constructed to mobilize people to take part in revolution can elicit the use of peaceful or violent methods. Frames that subscribe to violent methods will not be salient or be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with by people who believe in non-violence. This is because people who believe in non-violent methods categorize themselves in a particular group distinct from the group that believes in violent methods. The level of distinctness from other identities and level of awareness of the existence of other identities are the primary antecedents of salience [3]. The forming of such identities, in turn, leads to increased commitment to the group, alignment with beliefs, and adherence to norms and homogeneity in behavior [1].

In addition to predicting group behavior, salience plays a crucial role in sustaining the behavior. For example, in the change management literature, Kotter mentions that for the target change to sustain over a period of time, it is important that targets of change continue to <sup>fi</sup>nd the salience in the change [37]. Similarly, Lewin’s force <sup>fi</sup>eld analysis states the need for congruence between the goals of the group and the goal of the change, i.e., the group needs to <sup>fi</sup>nd the change idea to be salient with their own goals.

The constructs of frame analysis discussed above suggest a model of norm formation in online communities as seen in Fig. 1. We consider the leaders or managers of online communities to be the proponents of a frame and hence initiate the frame construction process. They are involved in developing the frame through the diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational frame construction tasks. Whether a constructed frame successfully becomes a norm is dependent on the collective interpretation of the frame by the community members and on its relative salience to them. Salience, in turn, is associated with how well the frame is aligned with the online community’s identity. Higher frame credibility and salience increase the chance of the proposed frame’s acceptance as a group’s norm. The model guides our case analysis of formation of norms in online communities that discuss issues related to the consumption of recreational drug use.

## 5. Methodology

To study norm formation in online communities, we performed a cross-case analysis on two online communities that focused on recreational drug use. We used framing analysis as our interpretive lens.

## 5.1. Case selection

Recreational drug-based online communities were selected as case sites, because cultural practices, beliefs and norms in these sites would be foreign to the researchers. Researchers in the IS community are familiar with more traditional online communities such as open source software [13,63], computer games [11], or IS speci<sup>fi</sup>c mailing lists [38]. As researchers are comfortable with the norms in these traditional communities, norms become invisible to researchers. This invisibility becomes a barrier to understanding the norm formation process. Although it is possible to identify norms in traditional online communities, it becomes much harder to do so when compared to unorthodox communities. Because the objective of this study is to not only identify norms of the online community but also to analyze the norm formation process, we chose recreational drug-based communities as case sites.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the selection criteria used for this study are as follows. First, the case sites had to employ English as the medium of communication. Second, the case sites had to be in geographically separate locations to minimize bias that a single country or region might have on the online community. The region-based bias could constrain or enable discussions on topics such as availability of speci<sup>fi</sup>c drugs or their variants for recreational use. Third, the site had to be active for at least <sup>fi</sup>ve years, which provided us with some evidence that the online community was “permanent” or at least would not disappear in the near future.

Table 1 presents a summary of the characteristics of our two case sites. It should be noted that while both sites are physically hosted in their respective countries, site membership is not restricted by nationality. Also, although the New Zealand case site was a smaller community in terms of number of subscribed members and the number of years in operation, it had more than twice the number of threads than the UK case site. However, this number does not represent a higher density of interaction between the members of the New Zealand case site. This is because in a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of threads, members just posted links to recent news articles without initiating a discussion. Such threads did not prompt any response from community members and accordingly just in<sup>fl</sup>ated the number of threads on the online community.

## 5.2. Data collection and analysis

Data collection and data analysis were done iteratively. We <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the data collection process that employed two separate criteria – (1) Moderator contribution and (2) Rich interaction.

## 5.2.1. Moderator contribution

The focus for this study is on how leaders of online communities, i.e., moderators, in<sup>fl</sup>uence the formation of norms. As such, we restricted our data search to all threads that were either started or con tributed to by the moderators of both the case sites. We could do this using the inbuilt search functionality provided by both online communities that allowed us to use conditions such as “moderator name” to retrieve relevant threads. We believe that moderators can introduce norms by both starting a conversation (in a thread) or by contributing to existing conversations (i.e., contribute to an existing thread). This is the reason we include threads that were both started by moderators and contributed to by moderators in our data collection.

## 5.2.2. Rich interaction

Once we collected all data that matched abovementioned search criteria, we applied our second criterion to further narrow down ou search. In this step, we looked for posts that had rich interaction. For norms to take e<sup>f</sup>ect, there needs to be a consensus. To observe consensus, we needed to analyze the interactions between moderators and online community members related to the proposed norms. We believe that threads that have “rich interaction” help identify consensus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c moderator proposed norms. We considered threads to be “rich” in interaction through a combination of subjective and objective assessments. For example, when a conversation between members in a thread was focused on a particular topic or concern, it was considered to be subjectively rich, i.e., there was a lot of content to analyze. Similarly, if there were multiple members of the community con tributing to the thread, it was considered to be objectively rich, i.e., one could quantitatively determine that the thread possessed more information. Accordingly, as the number of posts registered on a thread about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c topic increased, the interaction between the members of the online community also increased, leading to a higher chance of observing the consensus on moderator proposed norms.

## 5.2.3. Data analysis

Once we obtained the list of all threads with moderator participation, we sorted the list by number of posts per thread. We then analyzed each thread, starting with the one having the greatest number of posts. We iteratively worked our way to smaller threads until we obtained theoretical saturation [17]. Within each thread, we analyzed the posts that belonged to that thread in a chronological order to understand the evolution of the norm formation process. We could do this by looking at the timestamp that was available for each post in the thread. The outcome of our analysis resulted in three categories of norms – (1) Moderator proposed norms, (2) Accepted norms, and (3) Transient norms. Below, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the methodology used to discover the three norm categories.

## 5.2.4. Moderator proposed norms

From the data collected above, we <sup>fi</sup>rst looked speci<sup>fi</sup>cally at moderator posts across all the threads for both case sites. The aim of this step is to analyze all moderator posts using the three dimensions of frame construction [16].

Diagnostic frame. For each moderator contributed post, we attempted to identify what the moderator considered to be the problem. For example, in some posts, moderators blamed governments for the prohibition on drugs while being biased toward alcohol and cigarettes which were equally dangerous.

Prognostic frame. We attempted to identify the existence of a prognostic frame in each post. Here, we looked for solutions proposed by the moderator to the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed diagnostic frame. For example, one post suggested the establishment of medical centers to provide free sterilized needles for people addicted to intravenous drug injection to reduce the number of people infected with HIV (AIDS).

Motivational frame. Motivational frames were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed where it seemed that the moderators were suggesting a “call for action” to members of the group in favor of the advocated position. For example, the use of the slogan “legalize, regulate & educate” was used to spread awareness of di<sup>f</sup>erent pro-legalization protest events.

## 5.2.5. Frame construction to moderator proposed norms

We next reviewed all identi<sup>fi</sup>ed diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational frames to identify common topics between them. We grouped common frames into conceptually distinct topics which were used to identify the norms for this study (i.e., axial coding). We performed this grouping under the premise that a topic common to diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational frames proposed by moderators can form the basis of identifying moderator proposed norms. We acknowledge that there may already be some pre-existing norms. However, our research question is focused on moderators in<sup>fl</sup>uence on norm formation and hence we looked speci<sup>fi</sup>cally at moderator proposed frames as the <sup>fi</sup>rst step. This exercise resulted in the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of 9 moderator proposed norms as seen in Table 2 in the results section below.

## 5.2.6. Accepted norms

It has to be noted that even though moderators propose norms (as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the step above), it is not necessary that every moderator proposed norm is always accepted. Once we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the 9 moderator proposed norms, we then looked for evidence for how community members interpreted these proposed norms. This step was for us to understand why some moderator proposed norms are accepted while others are not. According to the framing analysis theory, we used two speci<sup>fi</sup>c criteria − (1) Frame credibility and (2) Frame salience. We analyzed whether frame credibility and frame salience led to accepted norms.

Frame credibility. We evaluated each thread that belonged to each of the 9 moderator proposed norms to evaluate its frame credibility. Consistent with theory, frame credibility was assessed along two dimensions, frame consistency and empirical credibility. Frame consistency was measured by the alignment between beliefs and actions of the moderators across di<sup>f</sup>erent threads. For example, there is frame inconsistency if the moderators’ frames advocate joining pro-legalization protest events in one thread but moderators indicate their inability to participate in such events in another thread. Empirical credibility was viewed as some form of external validity and was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by whether the thread was backed by evidence including referenced news or academic articles.

Frame salience. In addition to frame credibility, frame interpretation is a<sup>f</sup>ected by frame salience, i.e., the relative salience of the proffered frames to the target population. Although frames may prove to be credible, if the target population’s central belief system does not align with what is being proposed, there is little chance the frames will be successful [5]. Frame salience is a cognitive trait that cannot be readily observed; therefore, we used group identity as a proxy to understand frame salience. Group identity is the perceived sense of self derived from the sense of belongingness to a group [61]. Members who feel they are part of a group identify and adopt behavior and stereotypes of the group and constantly compare themselves with other groups. Recent research has shown that alignment of beliefs is a major factor for members identifying themselves with groups [4]. In our study, we identify group identity by analyzing the articulation of beliefs, either explicitly or implicitly, of belonging to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c group.

Finally, we attempted to evaluate whether the norm was actually promulgated through the community (i.e., norm acceptance). To assess norm acceptance, we re-evaluated all threads, including those omitted from our earlier analysis. This was done by analyzing all threads that did not have moderator participation but were related to a specific moderator proposed norm. The set of all such threads was retrieved using keywords related to the moderator proposed norms. For example, for the norm “harm minimization,” keywords such as a “safe use” and “harm reduction” were used to retrieve threads related to the norm. Then; the content of the thread was analyzed to identify the acceptance of any of the 9 norms identi<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier. Across frame consistency; empirical credibility; and frame salience; we analyzed a total of 9 scenarios looking for the individual impact. Where there was a strong evidence for the presence of a construct; we labeled it as high (H); and where there was weak evidence; we labeled it as low (L) (i.e.; HHH; LLL; HHL; HLL; LLL; LHH; LLH; LHL; and HLH). We considered evidence to be strong if there were more than 2 instances of evidence for each construct. The outcome of this analysis showed that there was no impact of frame salience on any of the moderator proposed norms. i.e.; there was no evidence of frame salience found for the norm ac ceptance process. As such; the number of scenarios that we present reduces to four. i.e.; (1) High frame consistency – High empirical credibility (2) High frame consistency – Low empirical credibility (3) Low frame consistency – High empirical credibility; and (4) Low frame consistency – Low empirical credibility. For each of these; we determined whether norms were accepted.

## 5.2.7. Transient norms

We then looked for any qualitative di<sup>f</sup>erences between the accepted norms. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that while some accepted norms sustained, some ceased to exist. In our sample it took an average of 1.5 years for an accepted norm to cease. We call norms that ceased as transient norms; de<sup>fi</sup>ned as norms that are active for a short period of time or norms that are impermanent. We wanted to understand why some accepted norms become transient norms.

There is normative precedence to this behavior in the change management literature. For example, Kotter’s 8 step change model talks about the need to reinforce the change with mechanisms to ensure that the change introduced sustains over time [37]. Similarly, Lewin’s force <sup>fi</sup>eld analysis talks about the need for “refreezing” on achieving a desired state of change [40]. Lewin’s model further states that it is important for the new change to be congruent with the rest of the group’s behavior i.e., the group’s identity or that it sets o<sup>f</sup> new rounds of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation that may lead to a change reversal.

We reanalyzed all the accepted norms obtained from the above step using frame consistency, empirical credibility, and frame salience, and this time viewed it from a norm evolution perspective. Because we arranged all our data in a chronological order based on the timestamp from each thread, it was easy to take this evolutionary approach. ## 7. Discussion

Our research attempts to develop theory on how moderators can form norms in online communities. Norms act as informal controls that enable online community members to behave in a uniform manner that leads to overall better group performance [20]. Using the lens of frame analysis Benford et al., 2000, we demonstrate that moderators of online communities can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the formation of such norms. Furthermore, we unpack how norms can be successfully in<sup>fl</sup>uenced both in the short term and long term.

In the short term, norm acceptance is directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by two moderator behaviors, frame consistency and empirical credibility. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that while frame consistency has an impact, norm acceptance empirical credibility does not. Frame consistency by itself is both a necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition, i.e., norms with low empirical credibility are accepted as long as there is strong frame consistency. That frame consistency even with low empirical credibility can result in norm acceptance is likely associated with the level of trust and faith members place in the moderators of the community. Online communities are driven by a small set of core contributors and a very large periphery of irregular or un-interactive members [65]. As such, the high level of activity by core contributors, that includes moderators, is seen as a mark of expertise that leads to trust and faith.

Norm transience represents the lack of resilience of the norm to endure over time. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that norm acceptance does not preclude norm transience. While norm acceptance is an outcome of moderator strategies (frame consistency & empirical credibility) used to establish a certain behavior as a norm, transience represents a norm’s inability to sustain over a period of time. Norm formation should not be equated to “e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness” or “success.” This is because some powerful norms exist and are popularized, but do not persist over a long period of time.

Thus, norm acceptance is a necessary, but not su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition to establish long lasting norms. Norm formation thus requires that norms espoused by moderators align and are found salient (i.e., frame salience) with community members’ shared sense of identity (i.e., group identity). The longer norms are found salient with the group’s identity, the longer the group members will be committed to. In general, group identities are shaped by categorizing people as belonging to a certain social group or category. Also, the presence of other dissimilar groups leads to strong in-group homogeneity [51]. However, group identities do change over a period of time [28] and as such norms that were previously salient can become non-salient and hence fail to continue.

Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we propose the process model in Fig. 2 as a way for online community leaders to develop norms in their online communities.

## 7.1. Theoretical contributions

Our development of a process model for establishing norms in online communities is our principal theoretical contribution. It implies the following three extensions to frame analysis when studied in the context of online communities.

First, Benford et al [5] explain the dynamics of social movements through three processes – frame construction, frame credibility, and frame salience. We contribute to the theory by not only con<sup>fi</sup>rming these constructs as predictors of norm acceptance, but also extend them by showing that they realize framing outcomes in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c temporal ordering in online communities. As seen in Fig. 2, frame construction that includes the three framing perspectives is usually the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, followed by frame credibility and frame salience. These two stages are individually responsible for norm acceptance and norm transience respectively. A failure at either the credibility or salience step results in two qualitatively distinct outcomes i.e., lack of norm acceptance or creation of transient norms.

Second, the social movements’ literature unpacks each of these processes into sub-dimensions. For example, frame credibility is unpacked as frame consistency and empirical credibility. Our research highlights that some of these conditions are more applicable in the online community context than others. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, frame consistency is more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant than empirical credibility, as frame consistency is a su<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for norm acceptance, while empirical credibility is not. Online communities generally operate in a state of pre-trust, and moderators’ actions when perceived to be consistent contribute to and enhance this trust [29].

Also, moderators are expected to provide community leadership by giving advice to those who ask, and share knowledge on past experi ences [39]. Consequently, any action that violates this trust, such as inconsistencies in moderators’ actions, will lead to decay in trust and hence negatively impact any moderator proposed norms.

Third, in online communities, frame salience was not seen as a necessary condition for norm acceptance, although it impacts norm transience. There are two possible reasons for this <sup>fi</sup>nding. One, as moderators are generally considered subject matter experts and experienced [21], as long as they adhere to the frame consistency condition, their intentions are not doubted when a norm is proposed. However, in due course when the proposed norms con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the group’s shared identity and hence are not found to be salient, moderator-inspired norms lose subscription and become transient. Another explanation is that when the norm is proposed there may not yet be a group whose identity is in con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the norm. As group identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation takes shape, the norm is found non-salient and hence will not endure.

In e<sup>f</sup>ect, moderators’ frames are granted a “trust bonus” by default. Individuals who lack the knowledge to assess moderators’ credibility give moderator arguments more weight than arguments from non moderator naysayers. Naysayers must present more convincing argu ments when refuting a moderator argument than if the argument were articulated by a non-moderator. Furthermore, ambiguous statements by moderators (e.g., synthetic drugs are bad) are less likely to be challenged, because naysayers interpret such ambiguous statements as nonchallenges. However, the “trust bonus” does not allow a moderator to arbitrarily enforce their will. A su<sup>fi</sup>ciently large group of naysayers from distinct online community/sub-communities can kill a moderator’s norm.

## 7.2. Practical contributions

From a pragmatic perspective, the study makes several contribu tions potentially of bene<sup>fi</sup>t for organizations spending signi<sup>fi</sup>cant monies in building and maintaining online communities. Prior work argues that online community success factors such as knowledge sharing and creation depend on inter-member interaction [42] or member attributes [64]. Our study builds on this literature by highlighting the role of the moderator by looking at moderator-member interaction, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally how moderators can design and cultivate successful norms that are positive to the organization. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings add a new dimension to the role of an online community moderator. The traditional depiction is that a moderator is a “referee” for the com munity whose primary job is to ensure smooth <sup>fl</sup>ow of communication between members [39]. However, our study shows that moderators can also be “in<sup>fl</sup>uencers” by establishing norms favorable to the commu nity’s goals and vision, in the following two ways.

First, our research highlights the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of moderators’ need to be consistent in their interactions. Moderators must always exhibit exemplary behavior in an online community. A perception that moderators’ actions do not match their articulated beliefs can cause sig ni<sup>fi</sup>cant changes in community members’ norms.

Second, our research highlights one e<sup>f</sup>ective strategy for creating frame salience- moderators can frame their arguments as being about the social identity of the group by comparing the group against an outgroup. For example, in our study, moderators referred to society at large as misinformed and judgmental about drug use. By creating this out-group, moderators were able to give an identity and a purpose that de<sup>fi</sup>ned their group. Accordingly, moderators could bene<sup>fi</sup>t by identifying and emphasizing the presence of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant “out-group” while proposing norms. The creation of an out-group shapes and de<sup>fi</sup>nes the in-group. Once the in-group is de<sup>fi</sup>ned, norms that align with the basic tenets of the in-group will be salient and hence sustain. Although it is not always necessary to create an in/out group environment for norms to sustain, it is nevertheless a useful and successful strategy for moderators to use.

## 8. Conclusion

This study sets out to explain how moderators can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the formation of norms in online communities. Online communities are open spaces with few formal control mechanisms. Thus, norms play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in keeping the community together and functioning. We use frame analysis as our theoretical lens to build theory that explains how moderators exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence on community members. We conduct empirical analyses of two case sites where moderators construct and propose favorable norms and community members interpret them. We show that when moderators who are consistent in their beliefs and actions create norms aligned with the community’s identity, those norms form and sustain over time. Our study contributes to both theory and practice. It contributes to frame analysis theory by both suggesting a temporal order of the constructs and identifying some constructs as more critical than others. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the study highlights that in virtual communities, sending a consistent message is more important than the credibility of the message. Similarly, the study also suggests that proposed norms aligned with the community’s identity will sustain longer than ones that are not aligned to the community’s identity. The study contributes to practice by providing strategic insights for building a cohesive community that works toward common goals.

There are some future research directions that this study points to. For example, frame salience was found to be critical to norm transience. Group identity that we used as proxy for frame salience determines which norms the community will accept, however, norms over a period of time simultaneously shape a group’s identity. So, in practice, a norm desired by the moderator may not suit the online community at a particular point in time because it does not align with the online community’s group identity. Instead, the moderator could slowly introduce other norms that gradually shift the group’s identity of the online community to one that is more willing to accept the target norm, before actually introducing the target norm. However, this idea needs to be empirically tested.

We acknowledge some limitations to our study that can be addressed by future research. First, our study uses recreational drug use communities as case sites. We chose these case sites as they enable researchers to carry fewer assumptions of the community’s norms when compared to traditional online communities familiar to IS researchers. While we believe our norm acceptance model is applicable to all online community contexts, we acknowledge that the nature and dynamics of drug use communities may be di<sup>f</sup>erent from other communities. Future work on other communities is necessary to demonstrate that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are generalizable to the online community population at large.

We also acknowledge that the study predominantly takes a moderator’s perspective. Moderators are the central gatekeepers of most online communities. They ensure the smooth functioning of the community. However, we acknowledge that senior contributors and other community leaders together complete the sphere of in<sup>fl</sup>uence for the community. Hence, it would be interesting to understand whether the norms that the moderators propose are in anyway in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other senior contributors. Finally, we acknowledge that the data we worked with and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings depend on the level of participation of the members in the community. While it is well known that typical online communities are made of a small core set of active contributors and a large set of silent observers, there is a chance that this behavior might skew <sup>fi</sup>ndings.
