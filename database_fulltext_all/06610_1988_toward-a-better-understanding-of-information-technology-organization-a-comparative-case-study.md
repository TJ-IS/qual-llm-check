## Abstract

The role of information technology (IT) has changed from being merely a tool for processing transactions to a weapon that can affect an organization's competitive position. Because of this change, previous organizational structures for IT groups may no longer be adequate. Organizational theorists have shown that the appropriate structure is influenced by the organization's external environment and strategy. This study contributes toward a better understanding of IT organization by exploring the relationship between the organizational structure of IT groups and the effectiveness of IT support in two companies with similar IT environments. Specifically, the study uses: (1) qualitative analysis to examine IT organizational structure in two very similar companies; (2) quantitative analysis to determine which company has more effective IT support; and (3) an expert panel to identify those differences in IT organizational structure that appear to facilitate effective IT support. Several propositions from these findings are presented and discussed.

Keywords: Organizational structure, differentiation, integration, information technology, management of information systems

## Introduction

Managing information systems includes organizing the activities associated with identifying information technology (IT) applications, allocating IT resources, and providing IT support. In the past, companies could organize IT activities autonomously because early applications were limited to transaction-oriented functions requiring only limited integration with the activities of other organizational groups. Today, however, IT is not simply a tool to record transactions and process data. It has been suggested that IT can be a competitive weapon that can change an industry's structure, alter key competitive forces, and affect a company's choice of strategy (Parsons, 1983). In order to meet the challenges of these new roles of IT, changes must occur in how IT activities are organized (Zmud, 1984). Organizational theorists argue that proper structures for organizing activities are related to environmental characteristics and chosen strategy, suggesting that a contingency approach to organizing IT activities is appropriate.

The purpose of this study is to contribute toward a contingency theory of organizing IT activities into responsible IT groups (IT organization) by exploring the relationships between certain characteristics of the organizational structure of IT groups (IT organizational structure) and the effectiveness of support provided by IT groups (IT support). Specifically, the study: (1) identifies two companies with similar organizational profiles and environmental states; (2) examines IT organizational structure in those companies; (3)

determines which company has more effective IT support; and (4) identifies those differences in IT organizational structure that appear to facilitate more effective IT support. Because of potential confounding effects, the study was limited to examining companies with comparable environments characterized by a high degree of complexity, change, and uncertainty. Based upon the findings of the study, several propositions are presented for future examination.

## Research Framework

The research framework for this study (see Figure 1) has its roots in the extensive body of interdisciplinary literature that examines the linkage of organizational environment, structure, and strategy to organizational performance (Lenz, 1981; Miller, 1988). Early studies (Dill, 1958; Mason, 1939) attempted to link a firm's performance directly to its environment, i.e., the market or industry within which the organization competes. Organizational environments are not homogeneous but have differing degrees of complexity, uncertainty, and change (Schoderbek, et al., 1990). Complexity is related to the number of environmental components with which an organization must interact (Duncan, 1972). Uncertainty is related to the information about environmental components held by an organization (Jurkovich, 1974). Change is related to the dynamics between an organization and its components (Zey-Ferrell, 1979). Duncan (1972) suggests that environments that have increasing degrees of complexity or change also become more uncertain. As environmental uncertainty increases, the information processing requirements of organizational structures also increase (Tushman and Nadler, 1978). Technological sophistication, i.e., the degree of technological advances and complexity in an industry's core products and processes, has also been identified as an environmental characteristic with important implications for the management of organizations (Covin, et al., 1990).

The lack of explanatory power of the early bivariate models gave rise to efforts to include the intervening variables of organizational structure and/or strategy. Serious efforts to develop a structural-contingency model of the relationships between environment and organizational structure began with Burns and Stalker's (1961) study of 20 organizations, some operating in relatively stable environments and the rest in dynamically changing environments. Two distinctly different types of management systems were identified in these organizations—mechanistic and organic. The mechanistic systems, which emphasize production, were found to be more effective in the organizations operating in relatively stable environments. Organic systems, which emphasize adaptiveness, were found to be more effective in the organizations operating in dynamically changing environments.

Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) conducted one of the more extensive examinations of the structural-contingency model. Using a combination of qualitative and quantitative techniques, they examined the organizational structure and economic performance of six companies in the chemical processing industry. The researchers defined organizational structure as two processes:

Differentiation—the process of dividing the organization into groups that deal with different components of the organization's external environment.

Integration—the process of coordinating the interrelated activities of these groups in order to obtain unity of effort.

At the time of the study, the chemical processing industry was undergoing rapid technological change and product innovation. The researchers found that, in this dynamic environment, the higher-performing companies were those that developed organizational structures with higher states of differentiation and integration. Based upon their findings, they concluded that the increased information processing demands created by the dynamic environment were better satisfied by organizational structures with high states of differentiation and integration, thus leading to better organizational performance.

Organizations use portfolios of integrating mechanisms to coordinate groups with interdependent activities. The selection of appropriate integrating mechanisms is important to the successful integration of interrelated groups (McCann and Ferry, 1979). These mechanisms can be grouped by their ability to integrate groups working with various external environments, ranging from formal, static, and unidirectional mechanisms to informal, dynamic, and multidirectional mechanisms (see Figure 2) (Zmud, 1984).

Other research efforts have identified contingency relationships between environment and organizational structure (Emery and Trist, 1965; Khandwalla, 1973; Perrow, 1970; Thompson, 1967). However, several research efforts failed to support the structural-contingency model (Pennings, 1975; Pfeffer and Leblebici, 1973; Tosi, et al., 1973) and suggest that refinements to the model are necessary. Child (1972) notes that the model does not consider the ability of power-holders to choose between alternative organization structures to accomplish corporate strategy. He argues that failure to consider this “strategic choice” could help explain why studies inconsistently support the structural-contingency model.

Expanding on Child's “strategic choice,” Miller (1979) and Miller and Friesen (1978) developed a typology of adaptive orientations, i.e., strategies, which are available to organizations when adjusting to their environments. Using this typology, they confirm the importance of identifying organizational strategies in order to explain the bivariate relationships between environment, structure and strategy variables, and performance variables. Miller (1988) concludes that performance is a product not only of the match between environment and strategy, but also the use of the appropriate organizational structure.

In a study of small companies, Covin, et al. (1990) found a positive relationship between a company's performance and the match between the industry's technological sophistication, strategy, and organizational structure. Specifically, they found that in high technological sophistication industries, the higher performing companies tend to have more entrepreneurial strategies and organic organizational structures. In another study of small companies, Miller (1992) found that those companies with an external match between environmental and structural variables tend to have an internal mismatch between structural and process variables.

Galbraith (1973) integrates the different contingency approaches by suggesting that organizations are information processing systems, and appropriate organizational structures are those that best match an organization's information requirements with its information processing capabilities. Expanding on this, Tushman and Nadler (1978) and Nadler and Tushman (1988) present an information processing model for

## IT environment

The IT environment is defined as those physical and cultural components that are taken into consideration in the IT groups' decision-making behavior. The IT environment is made up of two parts—an external environment and an internal environment. A taxonomy developed by Duncan (1972) is used to illustrate the components of the IT environment (see Table 1). The relevant physical and cultural components outside the boundaries of the organization make up the external environment, and those inside the boundaries of the organization make up the internal environment. In this study, the IT environment was held as constant as possible by examining companies with very similar IT environments.

## IT organizational structure

IT organizational structure is defined as the organizational groupings, coordination devices, and management control processes involving the IT groups. Issues concerning IT organizational structure have received significant attention in IT literature. Four studies are of particular importance to this study. Kroeber and Watson (1979) describe and discuss the appropriateness of three alternative IT function structures—central, distributed, and dispersed. ## IT strategy

IT strategy is defined as the portion of an organization's overall strategy that relates to the IT groups. Business policy researchers have identified two primary components of an organization's strategy: first, the definition of the business domain in which the organization will compete and second, the determination of how the organization will compete within that domain (Hofer and Schendel, 1978). The second component includes organizational structure decisions concerning power distribution, resource allocation, and monitoring and control of outcomes (Bourgeois, 1980).

## IT support effectiveness

IT support is defined as those services and products provided by IT groups. Evaluating effectiveness of IT support has been examined in many IT research efforts, sometimes as a dependent variable and other times as a topic in itself. In a comprehensive examination of IT support effectiveness, Hamilton and Chervany (1981) propose a conceptual framework for evaluating IT support effectiveness based upon the objectives of the system when implemented. Their framework identifies two fundamentally different types of approaches: efficiency-oriented and effectiveness-oriented. Efficiency-oriented approaches are concerned with issues of technical quality, resource consumption, production capability, and resource allocation. Effectiveness-oriented approaches are concerned with issues of information content quality, IT support quality, use of systems, and impact on user and organizational performance. Within each orientation category, they further classify approaches by means of measurement, either subjective or objective.

In this study, evaluating the overall effectiveness of IT support required measuring the effectiveness of many different types of IT support. Therefore, a subjective approach, consistent with Hamilton and Chervany's framework, was developed to evaluate IT support effectiveness from the users' perspective. To ensure comprehensiveness of the evaluation, three components of IT support effectiveness were employed: availability of IT support, utilization of IT support, and perceived effectiveness of IT support. Availability of IT support is a measure of how easy it is for a user to obtain IT support. This measure is included as an indicator of effectiveness because although support may be available in the organization, it may be difficult to obtain. Utilization of IT support is a measure of frequency of use of IT support by a user while performing job-related tasks. Perceived effectiveness of IT support is a measure of how well IT support meets the needs of the user as perceived by the user. Although these measures are not independent, together they provide a relative indication of the overall effectiveness of a company's IT support.

## Research Method

As stated earlier, the purpose of this study is to contribute to a contingency theory of IT organization by examining the relationships between IT organizational structure and IT support effectiveness. This study does not attempt to completely describe or confirm these relationships but uses exploratory data analysis (Tukey, 1977) to suggest certain attributes of the relationship.

Because of limited research on the issues explored, a comparative two-case field study was designed to examine them. The methodology used combines the richness of case analysis necessary to examine each company's IT organizational structure and the rigor of empirical analysis required to detect the differences in IT support effectiveness. Case-oriented methodologies are traditional in organization/environment research and have been used for examining similar IT issues (Olson, 1981; Robey, 1981; 1983; Schonberger, 1981).

## Sample selection

In order to properly explore the relationships between IT organizational structure and IT support effectiveness, it was necessary that the two companies selected for the sample have similar IT environments and organizational profiles. To ensure this, only companies in the same industry (banking) were considered. The banking environment has undergone radical changes since 1980 (Sippel, 1989), becoming highly uncertain, complex, and dynamic. The removal of interstate banking barriers spurred a wave of mergers as banks crossed state lines, changing intra-industry rivalries. In addition, extensive deregulation changed a heavily regulated industry with protected markets into one that allows non-banking institutions to compete for consumer and corporate dollars. Consequently, non-bank competitors have invaded the traditional banking markets, while banks still face restrictions on the types of products and services they can offer. These non-bank firms can often operate with more favorable leverage than banks, forcing banks to ask IT for help in improving their productivity (Bowen, 1990; Kessner, 1989; Morseman, 1991; Warren, 1990), thus increasing the technological sophistication of the banking industry. The IT function is considered essential for a bank's survival (Warren, 1990) and therefore provides a rich area for investigating the effects of IT organizational structure on IT support effectiveness.

In order to reduce the confounding effects of other environmental and organizational variables, the following criteria were also included in the selection process:

• Banks in the same geographical marketplace

\- Banks with similar product offerings and customer bases

\- Banks similar in size and overall organizational structure

The first step of the selection process was to interview the senior IT executive of several large banks. Based upon the information gained from these interviews, two banks that best fit the selection criteria were identified and agreed to participate in the study. In order to maintain anonymity, the banks are referred to as Bank A and Bank B.

## Data collection

Three components of IT organizational structure were examined: the organization of IT functions, the IT application planning and development process, and the IT management controls and operating procedures. These components illustrate the forms of differentiation and types of integrating mechanisms used for the IT groups and the degree of integration achieved in similar IT environments. Data for all three components were collected from company documents and structured interviews with senior managers of IT groups. The structured interview questionnaire is shown in Appendix A.

Company documents including annual reports, position descriptions, budgeting processes, committee charters, and others supplied by the banks provided data concerning such topics as: general organization profile (e.g., size, number of employees, computer facilities), organizational structure, responsibilities of IT managers, IT application planning and development processes, and IT management controls and operating procedures. Structured interviews of senior IT managers (eight in Bank A and six in Bank B) were used to verify, enhance, and fill in the data collected from the documents.

Effectiveness of IT support data were collected using a questionnaire (see Appendix B). Within each bank, the senior managers of four functional areas encompassing the majority of IT support users (from Finance, Trust, Banking, and Funds Management) received an IS Department memorandum describing the study and 15 questionnaires with return envelopes. The managers were instructed to distribute the questionnaires to a cross-section of IT support users based on sex, age, and job position. Using a seven-point scale, users evaluated a list of the different types of IT support. For each IT support type the users indicated its availability, utilization, and perceived effectiveness. As requested by one of the banks, data concerning the “importance” of each IT support type were also collected; however, the data were not used in the analysis of IT support effectiveness because of reliability concerns (Cronbach alpha = .55). Forty-two usable questionnaires were received from Bank A and 31 from Bank B, resulting in a return rate of 70 percent and 52 percent. Bank managers agreed that the survey respondents were a representative cross section of the functional area on the basis of position, age, and sex (see Table 2).

## Data analysis

The first step was to identify the major differences in IT organizational structure (i.e., forms of differentiation, integrating mechanisms, and degree of integration achieved) between the two banks. This was accomplished by an expert panel of four academicians (three from MIS and one from industrial psychology). Each panel member was given a package containing a description of the study, background material concerning differentiation, integration, and integrating mechanisms, and a complete case description of each bank (the identity of the banks was kept anonymous). After reviewing this material, the panel met as a group to discuss the processes of differentiation and integration in both banks and to identify the differences in IT organizational structures that they felt impacted IT support effectiveness. In addition, at the end of this meeting, each panel member independently classified each bank as having either: (1) high IT group integration, (2) moderately high IT group integration, (3) moderate IT group integration, (4) moderately low IT group integration, or (5) low IT group integration.

Next, the bank with the more effective IT support was identified in a two-step analysis procedure. First, using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), four different test procedures were used to determine if any differences existed between the responses to the user questionnaires of the two banks. Second, confidence intervals of the difference between the means of the responses were used to determine where significant differences existed. Differences were identified at an overall probability of 0.05 or less.

In the qualitative portion of the study, validity concerns were addressed using two techniques recommended by Yin (1984): multiple sources of evidence and reviews by key informants. Qualitative data were collected from company documents and structured interviews with senior IT managers. After all of the qualitative data were collected, case descriptions were written and reviewed by the respective senior IT managers. A few minor changes were made based upon their comments.

Reliability is improved by reducing the errors and biases that impact the ability to conduct the same study again. In this study, this objective was accomplished by developing a case study protocol (Yin, 1984) that documents as much as possible the sources of data, the interviews with user and IT managers, and the procedures for the expert panel and data analysis. In addition, having the case descriptions analyzed by the expert panel also improves reliability by reducing researcher bias.

In the quantitative portion of the study, validity of the IT support questionnaire was addressed during construction of the instrument. A list of types of IT support was developed during the interviews with the senior IT managers. This list was reviewed for completeness by the other IT managers and developed into the user questionnaire that was pretested using the office staff of each bank's senior IT executive. Reliability within all three components of the IT support questionnaire was tested using Cronbach's alpha.

## Findings

The findings of the study fall into two broad categories: descriptive case analysis and empirical data analysis. Based on an analysis of the case descriptions, the expert panel identified several substantial differences in the banks' IT organizational structures and concluded that Bank A has higher IT group integration than Bank B. The analysis of the empirical data resulted in the conclusion that Bank A has significantly more effective IT support than Bank B.

## Organization of IT Functions

Figure 4 shows the organization structure of the Operations Group at both banks. The Operations Group at Bank A has four departments, all of which contain IT groups. The Operations Planning and Development Department is responsible for the strategic planning of the Operations Group and the development of large business applications, and it has three IT groups: Operations Planning, Systems Services, and Operations Acceptance. The Operations Services Department also has three IT groups: Automated Operations, Technical Support, and Regional Support. The Corporate Operations Department has one IT group, Application Services, which provides maintenance and enhancement support on a day-to-day basis for approximately 140 business applications. The Management Services Department represents a broad range of services and has three IT groups: Corporate Support, Corporate Services, and Contingency Planning.

The Operations Group at Bank B is divided into three departments: Bank Operations, Information Systems and Services, and General Services. All of the IT groups are centralized in the Information Systems and Services Department (ISSD), which has four divisions. The Software Services Division is made up of two IT groups: Technical Services and Information Systems. The Administrative Services Division has four IT groups:

Quality Assurance, Planning, Data Security, and Training, which is responsible for scheduling and conducting computer-oriented training internal to ISSD. The Processing Services Division has three IT groups: Data Center, Voice Networks, and Data Networks. The Office Systems Division is responsible for managing the department's word processing center and the indirect supervision of the secretarial staff.

The panel identified three significant differences in the forms of differentiation and the types of integrating mechanisms used in the organization of the IT functions. First, Bank A is more highly differentiated than Bank B. At the third level of the Operations Group, Bank A has 10 groups with IT support responsibilities and Bank B has only four (shaded in Figure 4).

Second, the banks' distribution of IT groups (hierarchy of authority) is very different. All of the IT groups at Bank B are centralized into one department, ISSD. However, the IT groups at Bank A are distributed into all four departments of the Operations Group according to the type of support they provide. For example:

Corporate Support is responsible for managing the Information Center and also provides other operational and training assistance corporatewide.

## IT Application Planning and Development Process

Both banks use a hierarchical process for planning, developing, and implementing IT applications (Figure 5). At Bank A, small project requests (less than 500 hours) are reviewed during monthly meetings between representatives from the Application Services Division (software maintenance and enhancements), the Regional Support Division (user liaison), and user groups. Medium-sized project requests (between 500 and 3,000 hours) are reviewed by a steering committee called TEAM II. The Application Services Division develops and implements both small and medium applications. Large projects (over 3,000 hours) are reviewed by a steering committee called TEAM I. A separate division within the Operations Group—Operations Planning and Development—is dedicated to planning, developing, and implementing TEAM I applications.

Bank B uses a formal work request system for all projects. Small projects (less than 250 hours) are approved and priority-ranked by the systems owner, a senior functional manager. Medium-sized projects (between 250 and 2,500 hours) are reviewed by the Systems Management Committee. Large projects (over 2,500 hours) are forwarded through the Management Committee to the Systems Steering Committee for review. The Software Services Division is responsible for developing and implementing all applications.

The processes are similar from the aspects that steering committees review large and medium-sized project requests and small and medium-size projects tend to be enhancements to existing applications, while large projects tend to be new cross-functional applications. The panel did, however, identify a significant difference in the processes that could affect their effectiveness.

At Bank A, IT application planning and development is the responsibility of two independent review process groups. One process group is responsible for large applications and is supported by a dedicated development group. The other is responsible for small and medium applications and is supported by a second development group. At Bank B, all projects flow through a single process, which is supported by a single development group.

## IT Management Controls and Operating Procedures

Both banks use a mixture of committees, formal meetings, and informal meetings to establish and communicate management and operational controls for the IT groups. At Bank A, a two-tier committee is responsible for establishing and communicating management controls. The first tier consists of the Management Committee, which is primarily concerned with the overall policy decisions and long-term strategic directions of the Operations Group. For issues involving IT, this committee is supported by a second tier of four tactical committees. The Computing Committee is responsible for the overall computing environment including long-range plans, standards, capacity management, and cost allocation. The Communications Committee is responsible for long-range communications plans, standards, and monitoring the use and implementation of voice and data networks.

The other two tactical committees, the Delivery Committee and the Systems Committee, help improve the use and management of IT. The Delivery Committee primarily focuses on improving the distribution of information and IT support to users. The Systems Committee focuses on improving the productivity of systems resources and facilitating coordination among systems groups to ensure smooth transitions of software, common standards, defined procedures, and guidelines. The decisions of all four tactical committees are communicated to operating managers through committee reports and formal and informal meetings.

In contrast, Bank B primarily relies upon informal meetings to establish and communicate management controls and operating procedures for IT groups. Periodic informal meetings are held at the appropriate management level to review the status of operations and to make and disseminate new controls and procedures. Bank B has established a Standards Committee to review requests for new standards and to monitor compliance with published standards.

Expert panel analysis of IT group integration

The expert panel's classifications of the banks were as follows:

## Quantitative analysis of IT support effectiveness

The purpose of this analysis was to identify which bank has more effective IT support. This objective was accomplished using SAS in a two-step analysis procedure. In the first step, the responses for each of the three effectiveness measures of each bank were aggregated by category of IT support type: Data Processing and Telecommunications (DP&T), Software Services (SS), Office Services and User Liaison (OS&UL), and End-User Computing Services (EUCS). The descriptive statistics for the resulting 12 dependent variables are shown in Table 4. Then, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) test was used to determine that significant differences did exist somewhere between the two banks' 12 dependent variables (see Table 5).

In the second step of the analysis procedure, the dependent variables with significant differences were identified by computing confidence intervals of the difference between the means of each dependent variable (Table 5). The confidence intervals that do not include zero indicate the dependent variables that are significantly different. Since the testing involved 12 dependent variables, it was necessary to tighten the significance level, thereby mitigating the possibility of a Type 1 error (Bray and Maxwell, 1985; Huck, et al., 1974). Accordingly, the significance level was set at 99.6 percent, resulting in an overall “cascaded” significance level of p < .05.

As illustrated in Table 5, eight of the 12 dependent variables tested significantly different, with Bank A being higher in all eight. Significant differences were not found between the other four dependent variables, even though the means for Bank A were all higher than for Bank B. Based upon the above analysis, the researchers concluded that Bank A has more perceived effective IT support than Bank B.

## Discussion

Effective design of an organization involves differentiation (i.e., dividing the organization into separate groups with specific authority) and integration (i.e., coordinating the actions of the groups so that they complement and support each other). To ensure that groups work together to achieve organizational goals, appropriate integrating mechanisms must be in place to facilitate the required vertical and horizontal flow of information within the organization.

This study focused on two organizations in an industry (banking) that must contend with external environments characterized by a high degree of uncertainty, complexity, and change, brought about by increasing deregulation, competition, and mergers. These changes in banks' external environments result in increased information processing requirements. Effective banks, in turn, must evolve organizational structures with increased information processing capability.

The major finding of this study is that Bank A's IT organizational structure had higher degrees of differentiation and integration and more effective IT support (as perceived by the users). The major conclusion drawn from this is that Bank A's IT organizational structure provided more information processing capability than Bank B's, which better matched the information processing requirements of their environments. As noted earlier, three elements determine an organizational structure's information processing capability: grouping, structural linking, and management and operational process (Nadler and Tushman, 1988). Below are three propositions (relating to these three elements of organizational structure) supported by the findings of this and other studies. They are not, however, directly deducible just from the findings of the study alone. Future studies are necessary to either confirm or deny them.

Proposition 1 (Grouping): Locating IT groups that provide personalized IT support with other groups that provide other types of personalized support to office workers is more effective than centralizing them within the IT department.

and (4) grouping by multiple foci, which puts groups together by several dimensions simultaneously. Each of the organization form options focuses the groups in a different way and has unique strengths and weaknesses.

The findings of the study suggest that the “grouping by user/customer” organization form is more effective than the “grouping by activity” organization form for IT groups that provide personalized IT support to office workers. That is, it is more effective to locate these IT groups with other groups that provide other types of personalized support to office workers (as in Bank A), instead of centralizing them in the IT department (as in Bank B). For example, office automation and end-user computing support in Bank A is managed and provided by the Management Services Department, which also provides a broad range of office support such as facilities maintenance, purchasing, print shop, copy center, mail distribution, and transportation.

Duncan (1979) argues that the “grouping by user/customer” organization form better facilitates inter-group communication than the “grouping by activity” organization form, resulting in a better understanding of user/customer needs. The results of this study support this argument by showing that the company with higher degrees of differentiation and integration also had more effective IT support (as perceived by the users). Watson, et al.’s (1989) study concerning the location of DSS support groups supports using the “grouping by user/customer” organization form for IT groups that provide end-user computing support and suggests that they should not be located with IT groups that provide more traditional MIS-oriented support (grouping by activity).

In organizations with environments characterized by lower degrees of complexity, change, and uncertainty, the information processing requirements are also lower. In these organizations the “grouping by activity” organization form may provide sufficient information-processing capability between the IT groups that provide personalized support and users of that support. Therefore, based upon organizational theory and the findings of this study, a significant difference in the perceived effectiveness of IT support may not exist between organizations using the “grouping by activity” organization form and those using the “grouping by user/customer” organization form for IT groups that provide personalized support.

Proposition 2 (Structural Linking): IT management control processes using matrix structural linking mechanisms are more effective than IT management control processes using hierarchical structural linking mechanisms.

Organizations use management control processes to monitor activities associated with achieving the objectives of organizational groups. Based upon feedback information from these processes, management can choose to make changes in how the activities are accomplished. This proposition concerns the structural linking mechanisms used within management control processes implemented to monitor and change the activities of the IT groups.

Five types of structural linking mechanisms have been identified (Nadler and Tushman, 1988): hierarchy, liaison, cross-unit groups, integrator roles/departments, and matrix organization. These mechanisms are listed from low to high in terms of both their cost and their information processing capability. Decisions concerning the appropriateness of specific mechanisms must consider both their cost to the organization and the information processing requirements of the organization's environment. Matrix structural linking mechanisms have been shown to transfer information and adapt more quickly to changing environmental demands than other types of mechanisms (Davis and Lawrence, 1977; Galbraith, 1971; 1973) and are appropriate when information processing requirements are substantial (Nadler and Tushman, 1988).

The findings of this study suggest that a similar analogy applies to the structural linking mechanisms used in IT management control processes. As information processing requirements increase, the IT management control process must analyze feedback information and effect appropriate changes more quickly. At Bank A this is accomplished through a matrix mechanism consisting of four committees. Two of the committees, the Computing Committee and the Communications Committee, are specialized groups with functional-oriented responsibilities, whereas the other two, Delivery Committee and Systems Committee, are integrative groups with product-oriented responsibilities. At Bank B, the IT management control processes use hierarchical mechanisms that are limited in their ability to process information required to monitor and change activities associated with the IT groups.

Just as in Proposition 1, in organizations with environments characterized by lower degrees of complexity, change, and uncertainty (and therefore lower information processing requirements), structural linking mechanisms that provide less information processing capability may be sufficient. In these organizations, no significant differences in perceived effectiveness of IT support may exist between those that use structural linking mechanisms in their IT management control processes that provide more information processing capability.

Proposition 3 (Management Control): A dual IT application planning process supported by separate development groups is more effective than one multi-tiered process supported by a single development group.

Anthony (1965) developed a taxonomy for classifying management decisions into strategic planning, management control, and operational control and argued that each category requires the development of different types of information systems. Using his taxonomy, Gorry and Scott Morton (1971) developed a framework to illustrate the differences in decision structure between the three categories and argued that the organizational structure for developing information systems should also be different for the decision categories.

The findings of the study support Gorry and Scott Morton and suggest that an IT application planning process consisting of a hierarchy of review bodies supported by one development function is not as effective as a dual planning process supported by separate development groups. Multi-tier planning processes cause slower reaction to environmental changes because information must pass through several layers of review. In addition, they tend to be bottom-up and dependent upon groups low in the organization to identify and initiate information system requests.

The division of the IT application planning responsibility into separate processes allows each to specialize in responding to the needs and changes of its respective environment. The strategic-oriented process aids in planning systems that respond to changes in the marketplace, industry, product lines, etc., and can be supported by an independent group responsible for providing planning information and systems development and implementation. The operations-oriented process aids in planning systems resulting from changes in internal operations and enhancements and maintenance of existing systems, and can be supported by a second development group. Integration between the two can be achieved through a liaison function responsible for ensuring that overlapping issues are resolved appropriately.

As with the other two propositions, in organizations with environments characterized by lower degrees of complexity, change, and uncertainty, and, therefore, lower information processing requirements, single IT application planning processes may provide adequate information processing capability and be able to react quickly enough to competitive requirements. In these organizations, the additional costs associated with dual IT application planning processes supported by separate IT development groups may not lead to more effective IT support.

## Conclusion

In today's “information society,” companies must organize IT activities effectively and efficiently. Zmud (1984) argues for “research projects aimed at developing a contingency theory for organizing information systems activities.” By examining a particular IT environment and identifying those characteristics of IT organizational structure that best facilitate effective IT support, this study contributes to a contingency theory of IT organization. However, because of the exploratory nature of the study and the research methodology used, several limitations need to be considered. In addition, further research is required to adequately address the questions raised by these limitations.

## Contributions

The findings of this study contribute to a contingency theory of IT organization by examining the relationships between IT organizational structure and IT support effectiveness in several ways. First, they suggest that how companies organize IT activities may have a significant impact on the overall effectiveness of IT support.

Second, the findings suggest that in environments with a high degree of complexity, change, and uncertainty, organizations may improve the effectiveness of IT support by implementing IT organizational structures with higher states of differentiation and integration.

Third, as discussed previously, some integrating mechanisms may be more effective than others in achieving a higher state of integration. The findings suggest that more effective integrating mechanisms are those that promote feedback on IT performance, gain cross-functional participation in IT planning, and facilitate communication among IT groups and between IT groups and user groups through liaison functions.

Fourth, environments characterized by high degrees of complexity, change, and uncertainty place pressures on organizations to process information more quickly, requiring a proactive IT decision-making process rather than a reactive one. A proactive process can only be achieved by having appropriate integrating mechanisms in place before decision situations arise. Conversely, integrating mechanisms that result in a reactive decision-making process cannot adequately process information quickly enough to support IT decision making.

## Limitations

The major limitation of this study is its sample size of two companies with similar environmental states and organizational profiles. Specifically, three characteristics of the two companies and their environments could affect the extent to which the findings of this study can be generalized to other companies. First, the competitive environments of the companies are very complex and dynamic. The relationship between IT organizational structure and IT support effectiveness may not be as evident in companies with simpler and more stable competitive environments.

Second, banking is a very “information-intensive” industry (Sippel, 1989) in which IT is considered to have a high degree of strategic relevance (McFarlan, et al., 1983). Banks have very information-enriched product lines that focus considerable corporate attention on the effective management of IT. The findings of this study may not apply to companies in less information-intensive industries in which IT plays a more supportive role.

Third, accounting for all extraneous variables in an active corporate environment is not possible. Other variables—such as corporate culture, senior management vision regarding the significance of IT support, and leadership attributes of individual managers—also impact IT support effectiveness. At least in part, however, these variables manifest themselves in the organizational structure of IT groups and, therefore, are not totally deleterious to the findings of the study.

In addition, the overall effectiveness of IT support was evaluated solely by users' perceptual reports rather than objective measures. Such reports are vulnerable to certain measurement weaknesses such as recency effects (the user's most recent experience is most salient), halo effects (the user's experience in one area colors his or her perceptions of other areas), and social desirability effects (the user believes responses reflect upon his or her own credibility). As such, these perceptual measures of IT support effectiveness may not be reflective of measured variables exclusively.

## Future research

In order to properly determine the relationships between IT organizational structure and IT support effectiveness, the following research needs to be conducted (in addition to that which empirically examines the propositions stated previously):

1. Research using companies with other IT environments to determine if IT organizational structure has similar impacts on IT support effectiveness.

2. Additional measures of IT effectiveness need to be examined to see if agreement is obtained with the study described in this article. Recommended alternatives include attitudinal measures such as user satisfaction and performance measures such as improved decision making and increased revenues.

3. Research to determine which integrating mechanisms have the greatest impact on IT group integration. No attempt was made in the study to determine a relative ranking of integrating mechanisms by their impact on IT group integration.

These research suggestions are not exhaustive and certainly do not address all of the issues. In addition, given the revolutionary changes occurring in the structure of organizations today (Drucker, 1988) and the increased strategic relevance of IT, research on IT organizational structure issues may become even more important.

# Appendix A

# Structured Interview: IT Managers

The purpose of this interview is to examine how the decisions affecting information technology (IT) support are integrated between responsible organizational groups. IT support involves the services provided to users from data processing, telecommunications, and office automation.

The divisions and groups provided on a separate sheet have been identified as being responsible for decisions directly affecting the IT support provided to users. All questions should be answered as they relate to integration between these groups.

Please answer each question carefully and based on your own feelings. There are no “wrong” or “right” answers. It is very important that an accurate measure of the coordination between these groups is obtained.

THANK YOU VERY MUCH FOR YOUR COOPERATION

## I. Manager's Profile

1. What is the title of your position?

2. What are the major responsibilities of your position?

## II. Degree of Integration Between IT Groups

Answer the following questions as they relate to each of the other groups involved with IT.

1. Briefly describe the degree of integration that is required between your group and each of the other groups. (e.g., close, moderate, little, etc.)

2. Around which issues is integration required?

3. What sorts of contacts are required between your group and each of the other groups? Do you feel that there is any room for improvement in this area?

## III. IT Integrating Mechanisms

1. Committees (you are on)
Purpose? Who? When?

2. Committees (you know of)
Purpose? Who? When?

3. Meetings (formal - scheduled)
Purpose? Who? When?

4. Meetings (informal - ad hoc)
Purpose? Who? When?

5. Liaison personnel Responsibilities? Who?

## IV. IT Support Provided to Users

1. What are the major types of IT support that you provide to users? (Example: develop information systems, word processing, build databases, etc.)

2. Who are the major users of these support types?

3. Which support types require contact with other groups? In what context is this contact required (project approval, development, implementation, training, etc.)?

## V. IT Application Planning and Development Process

1. Budget process
Steps? Integration? Approval?

2. Application planning process (small vs. large) Steps? Integration? Approval?

3. Application development and implementation (small vs. large) Steps? Integration? Approval?

# Appendix C

# Expert Panel: Procedures and Results

## I. Training Phase

A. Meetings were held with each panel member to explain the responsibilities of the expert panel and the procedures to be followed.

B. Each panel member was provided with a package containing background and reference material concerning differentiation, integration, and integrating mechanisms.

## II. Evaluation Phase

A. The panel reviewed case descriptions of each bank. In a joint meeting, they identified and discussed differences in IT organizational design.

B. Each panel member independently classified each bank as having:

1. High IT group integration.

2. Moderately high IT group integration.

3. Moderate IT group integration.

4. Moderately low IT group integration.

5. Low IT group integration.

## III. Results of Expert Panel Analysis

A. The percentage of agreement was computed by dividing the number of consistent classifications (7) by the total number of classifications made by the panel (8).

B. This percentage of agreement was sufficient to conclude that:

1. Bank A has high IT group integration.

2. Bank B has moderately low IT group integration.
