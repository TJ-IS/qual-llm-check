# Overcoming theory-practice inconsistencies: Critical realism and information systems research

Matthew Longshore Smith

The London School of Economics and Political Science, Department of Information Systems, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, United Kingdom

Received 15 June 2005; received in revised form 25 October 2005; accepted 31 October 2005

## Abstract

This paper argues that information systems (IS) research conducted within the standard account of the paradigms of positivism and interpretivism sufers from persistent theory-practice inconsistencies. These inconsistencies are located between researchers’ stated (or implicit) ontological assumptions and research practice and results. Such a situation calls for a (re)consideration of the underlying ontological premises of information systems research and practice. This paper proposes that a critical realist ontology allows for one re-interpretation of the activity of science as implicitly predicated upon natural and social realism as well as the concepts of structures and generative mech anisms. This interpretation provides greater explanatory power vis-a\`-vis current research practices and resolves the theory-practice contradictions highlighted above. Consequently, it is a powerful logical argument for accepting this new conceptualization as an improvement upon the former. To illus trate, this paper recasts one such debate in light of critical realist assumptions; technologica determinism vs. social construction of technology. - 2006 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Critical realism; Positivism; Interpretivism; Causality; Generative mechanism; Determinism; Social construction of technology

## 1. Introduction

Eventually, when there is an incompleteness in a researcher’s or practitioner’s philosophy, theory-practice inconsistencies emerge (Bhaskar, 2002, pp.15–16). These inconsistencies manifest themselves in a variety of ways such as in performative contradictions or simply a mismatch between theory and empirical evidence. Of course, the emergence of theory-practice inconsistencies is inevitable in research given the constraints and limitations of human knowledge. Such inconsistencies constitute a point of diference that communicates valuable information encouraging further research and the modification, refinement, or rejection of concepts and theories. Ideally, through the conventional cycli cal process of scientific practice, there is some resolution. Some theory-practice inconsistencies, however, are more recalcitrant. They defy resolution due to deeper, more fundamental issues.

This article argues that much of information systems research sufers from long-standing theory-practice inconsistencies. These theory-practice inconsistencies are a disjuncture between stated or implicit philosophical assumptions of scientific practice and the scientific practice (or the results thereof) itself. The uncovering of persistent theory-practice inconsistencies should be a moment of critical importance. While living with inconsistencies is inevitable, they bring underlying and often taken-for-granted assumptions into question. Resolving these inconsistencies, if possible, could constitute a fundamental shift and potentially a significant step forward for the discipline as a whole.

One contribution of this article is to identify several of these inconsistencies, as found in each of the two dominant paradigms of IS research; positivism and interpretivism. What makes this argument slightly confusing is the nature of these inconsistencies. The inconsistencies identified here are disjunctures between researchers’ stated (or implicit) assumptions and the actual practice of science. The positivist reliance on a Humean notion of causality and the location of generalizable scientific laws at the level of the empirical conjunction of events is inconsistent with both the experience of information systems research and the goal of understanding. On the other hand, the interpretivist rejection of the causal power of the natural and social worlds belies their inclusion of this world in their research. If we reject any regulation of our knowledge by empirical data aforded us by the objective world, we have given up on the practice of science. This is neither an argument for the infallible nature of knowledge nor that we should not maintain a healthy epistemic skepticism. Instead it is an argument for an alternative conception of science that resolves this disjuncture through a new set of ontological premises.

The fundamentals of these inconsistencies are not new to the literature and they are often found as critiques of the other paradigm of research. This article takes a slightly different tack. This article does argue for an approach to information systems research based on the philosophy of science of critical realism (CR) as developed by Roy Bhaskar (Bhaskar, 1978, 1998b). However, the argument is not simply a critique of the other sides and a demonstration of the benefits of critical realism, as has been done (e.g. Carlsson, 2003; Dobson, 2002; Mingers, 2004c, 2004d). Instead, this article asserts that most research already implicitly assumes an ontology compatible with the critical realist natural and social realism as well as the concepts of structures and generative mechanisms. This is not a surprise as critical realism emerged in part through the study of scientific practice. CR allows for the resolution of the theory-practice inconsistencies discussed in this article through a reinterpretation of the activity of science. This is a compelling logical argument for the application of critical realist philosophy for information systems research for the betterment of information systems research and practice.

Beyond resolving inconsistencies, CR lays one potential foundation for future information systems research. As we will see, it ofers a notion of causality that is consistent with the quest for answering the underlying ‘‘why’’ questions posed in IS research. It also provides interpretivists with an ontology that strongly asserts the crucial role of meanings, interpretation, and context. In doing so, CR allows for the pursuit of an interpretivist agenda without denying the existence of the subject under study or its role in regulating research.<sup>1</sup>

This article proceeds in three parts. The first part lays out the current discourse behind the two dominant research paradigms of positivism and interpretivism and shows how research within each paradigm sufers from persistent theory-practice inconsistencies. The second part is an introductory description of some of the more salient aspects of crit ical realism as a philosophy of science, with a focus on the critical realist non-deterministic notion of causality as generative mechanisms. This concept is central to the critical realist ability to resolve identified theory-practice inconsistencies within the information systems field. This discussion enables the final part to illustrate how the critical realist approach can efectively dissolve some of the contentious debates in the IS literature. Ultimately this is done not through an introduction of a radically new methodology, but in the reinterpretation of current science. To illustrate, the article recasts one debate in the IS literature; technological determinism vs. the social construction of technology.

## 2. State of IS research

Information systems research has long been dominated by two paradigms of research labeled positivism and interpretivism (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1995a). Of course, any categorization necessarily draws distinctions that may be problematic at the edges. Philosophical and methodological adjustments to critiques have meant that the rigidly defined boundaries separating the two categories are becoming increasingly dubious (Weber, 2004). However, it is indisputable that these categories shape the current state of research, discourse, and pedagogy in the IS literature today, as evidenced by the myriad of papers discussing this divide and its implications (Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998; Fitzgerald, 1998; Kaplan & Duchon, 1988; Khazanchi & Munkvold, 2003; Lee, 1991; Mingers, 2004b; Mingers & Brocklesby, 1997; Weber, 2004).

## 2.1. Positivism

Positivism has been characterized in various ways in the literature, usually embracing the doctrines of a value-neutral empiricism, and a unity of the scientific method for the natural and social sciences (Hirschheim, 1985). However, arguably nobody takes such a strong positivist stance anymore, and post-positivist philosophies have come to terms with the value-laden nature of science, fallibility, etc (Weber, 2004). Despite these characterizations, there are two underlying aspects of a positivist methodology that still influence social science research today; the Popper-Hempel ‘hypothetico-deductive’ model of scientific explanation (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 10) and a reliance on the Humean notion of causality as a constant conjunction of events (Bhaskar, 1978).

Explanation and causality are intimately related. To understand this relationship we must consider the Humean notion of causality. For Hume, causality is nothing more than a constant conjunction of events. In other words, there is no necessity connecting cause and efect. Hume locates causality in the subjective illusion that there are necessary connections in nature, but this is nothing but an illusion (Searle, 2004, pp. 198–200). This is an important realization as it means the positivist notion of causality is rooted in a rejection of causality that is instead replaced by a tempo-spatial conjunction of events (Cartwright, 2004). The importance of these conjunctions of events is not that they represent causality, but instead that they can explain (ibid). Most commonly, the constant conjunctions of events form the basis of our knowledge taking the form of universal covering laws (Hempel, 1966). Perhaps the clearest example of these assumptions at work is the application of classical statistical inference tests that posit some relationship between variables (e.g. Baroudi & Orlikoswki, 1989).

The impact of positivist assumptions on theory and research is still prevalent (although diminishing) in IS literature and research (Avgerou, 2000, pp. 573–574; Walsham, 1995a), and is often associated with analyses of modern organizational rationality (e.g. Angell & Ilharco, 2004; Townley, 2002). The underlying assumptions can be found in many categorizations. For example, it is clearly manifest in the polarized ‘‘utopian techno-enthusiasm’’ and ‘‘paralysing anti-technology’’ (Avgerou & Walsham, 2000) literatures that are predicated on law-like relationships between technology and social outcomes. However, the implementation of technology has proven not to entail such a straightforward causal relationship as the process is fundamentally shaped by the practices of humans in every-day life such as drift, tinkering, and improvisation (Ciborra, 2002; Ciborra & associates, 2000). Obviously something deeper and more complex is transpiring than a direct empirical conjunction of events between technology and intended outcomes.

At the root of this problem is the notion of causality and scientific laws as a constant conjunction of events. Empirical relationships are phenomenological instead of theoretical (Cartwright, 1983). In other words, they do not capture the underlying unobservable entities (Maxwell, 1998) and causal processes (Cartwright, 1983) that bring them about. This realization has led to the distinction between causal description and causal explanation (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002). A causal description is a description of constant conjunction of events, mapping inputs to outcomes. Such a description, however, lacks the necessity that explains this connection. Recall that in Hume’s notion, there is no necessity in the connection of events. Causal explanation, on the other hand, locates this necessity through a clarification of the ‘‘mechanisms through which and the conditions under which that causal relationship holds’’ (ibid, p. 9). In other words, it opens up the black box linking the empirical conjunction (Hedstro¨m & Swedberg, 1998).

This diference can be best seen when contrasting statistical methods, such as a survey that asks ‘‘who’’, ‘‘what’’, and ‘‘how many’’ type questions, to the more in-depth ‘‘how’’ and ‘‘why’’ questions posed by a case study research strategy (Yin, 2003). A reliance on statistical methods and a notion of a covering law has meant that a majority of information systems research only captures the ‘‘what’’, but does not open the black box to understand the ‘‘how’’ and ‘‘why’’ (Lee, Barua, & Whinston, 1997). While statistical techniques depend upon suficient samples to establish the existence of constant conjunctions, explanatory theories can be gleaned from only a few, or even one, case study (Eisenhardt, 1989;

Lee, 1989; Tsoukas, 1989; Yin, 2003). The case study is analogous to the experimentalists in the natural sciences who do not perpetually run experiments to reveal regularities, but instead can draw causal conclusions from a properly done single case (Cartwright, 1989, p. 92). Of course, while social scientists cannot artificially generate closed systems in the same way, the ultimate goal of the social and natural scientist is the same – to move beyond events to uncover their causal explanation. This highlights a theory-practice inconsistency between the philosophical insistence on constant conjunctions of events as underpinning universal covering laws and the practical reliance on single cases that uncover underlying unobservable causal mechanisms (ibid). In other words, the quest for understanding leads scientists to implicitly accept a causality that transcends the empirical conjunction of events.

Another related theory-practice inconsistency emerges in the movement from explanation to generalization. Positivistic generalization is confronted with ‘‘Hume’s truism’’ (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). As Lee and Baskerville explain, generalization is a form of induction that, as Hume pointed out, always requires the assumption of the uniformity of nature. Simply stated, if we wish to generalize from one situation to another, we will always have the problem of not knowing whether the contexts of application will be the same. Since we must always assume that the new context is the same, we are making assumptions beyond what the evidence provides. Consequently, generalization is never fully justified logically. Thus, if researchers wish to generalize beyond the experimental sample, or case study, we must relax the uniformity of nature requirement. This, however, is not consistent with their stated philosophy;

‘‘An irony is that a scientific researcher’s departure from any scientific requirement can give the appearance that university-based scientific researchers, who appeal to the authority of science to legitimize their voice in the practitioner world, are not practicing what they preach’’ (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, p. 238 emphasis added).

In other words, it highlights a theory-practice inconsistency.

## 2.2. Interpretivism

This article has argued that the Humean notion of causality as associated with the constant conjunction of events is insuficient as a form of explanation. This inadequacy is especially poignant in the social sciences where human agency is determined in part by beliefs, desires, motives, and goals (Fay, 1994). These observations, coupled with philosophical attacks on positivism, or ‘‘scientism’’, led many researchers to argue for an interpretivistic approach to the study of information systems (KIein & Lyytinen, 1985; Walsham, 1993). Interpretivist studies are characterized by the prioritization of ‘‘subjective meanings and social-political as well as symbolic action in the process through which humans construct and reconstruct their reality’’ (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 13). For example, Schech (2002) rejects the current pro- and anti-technology arguments of the modernist and structuralist positions respectively. Instead, she argues for a post-modernist position that ICT needs to be examined in terms of a power/knowledge discourse. Avgerou and Walsham (2000), wish to overcome this dichotomy with ‘‘a broadly interpretive’’ approach that focuses on diverse cultures, socioeconomic conditions, and the ‘‘experiences and perceptions of the actors involved’’ rather than through an ‘‘objective ‘scientific’ framework of hypothesis testing’’ (ibid, pp. 2,7).

Interpretivist research is more than just a focus on human constructs and society as the prime (if not only) drivers of change. There is also a stated rejection of the positivist notion of causality and scientific method. This is constituted by either a rejection of causality completely (Guba & Lincoln, 1989), or the acceptance of the multidimensionality (Symons & Walsham, 1988, p. 119) or circularity (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991, p. 14) of cause and efect. The positivist goals of explanation and prediction are rejected in favor of subjective descriptions and understanding the diverse meanings of stakeholders (Nissen, 1985). Consequently, some interpretivists argue that it is ‘‘naı¨ve’’ to think that social science can harness the ‘‘potential impact’’ of technology through ‘‘informing correct choices of action’’ (Avgerou, Ciborra, & Land, 2004, p. 4). When it comes to the social world, the only alternative to universal laws appears to be local contingencies and descriptions. Hence the logical conclusion that interpretive research should focus on context (Avgerou & Walsham, 2000; Walsham, 1993).

One consequence of this position is the inability to discern contingent efects from necessary ones (or a rejection of the distinction between them). For example, from this perspective, it makes no sense to theorize the impacts of information technology apart from the context of implementation. As we will see in the final section, attempts to do so often result in accusations of technological determinism. However, if we cannot discern between contingent and necessary causal factors then how can there be any knowledge gained from research or any notion of generalization? What do we generalize and what is specific to the context?

Indeed this skepticism is inherent in the strong constructionist position that all knowledge and beliefs are equally valid and ‘‘there are no correct and incorrect theories but there are interesting and less interesting ways to view the world’’ (Walsham, 1993, p. 6). It is important to be clear what is being stated here; it is a rejection of any rational basis to decide between competing theories. Where does this relativism come from? In this case, it is a logical conclusion of an ontological position. As Walsham (1995b) plainly states, the ontological position of interpretivists is generally either ‘‘internal’’ or ‘‘subjective’’ realism that view reality as an intersubjective construction or a personal construction respectively. Both of these positions are placed in contrast to ‘‘external’’ realism that asserts the existence of reality independently separate from our constructions of it (ibid, p. 75). Given these ontological positions, it only seems logical that truth becomes a relative concept. However, the denial of reality independent of individuals is not necessary to reach Walsham’s epistemic position. Instead, his position renders impotent the crucial role of empirical data as a regulator of scientific research. At its core, it is a denial of the importance of ontology beyond subject-subject experiences.

This relativism has been decried by some as a ‘‘paralysing ‘anything goes’ attitude’’ (Avgerou, 2002, p. 65) and comes at a cost. First, there exists a contradictory relationship between this relativism and generalization. On the one hand, given stated interpretivist assumptions, these researchers are faced with a basic contradiction; if there is no rational basis to choose between competing theories then how can one justify any particular generalization? On the other hand, many researchers do accept the possibility of generalization from interpretivist research (Lee & Baskerville, 2003; Walsham, 1995b; Williams, 2000).

However, if interpretivists do wish to engage in the scientific enterprise, they are confronted with another inconsistency. While the interpretivist departure from the idea of covering laws is complete, their break with the Humean notion of causality has not been so clean (Bhaskar, 1998b, pp. 19–20). Interpretivists often rely on constant conjunctions of events when presenting findings and generalizing from them. The goal of much interpretivist research is not to uncover universal laws, but instead to supply descriptive narratives of the events under exploration (Latour, 2004; Nissen, 1985). However, implicit in any narrative is a glossing over of the diference between temporal succession and causality (Sayer, 2000, p. 142). In other words, while intepretivists state that causality is nonexistent, circular, multidimensional, or otherwise, research communication is inherently positivist in the sense of a constant conjunction of events through time (Fitzgerald & Howcroft 1998).

This implicit acceptance of a Humean notion of causality is also evidenced by Lee and Baskerville (2003) illuminating discussion of diferent forms of positivist and interpretivist generalization. It is important to understand the notion of generalization that Lee and Baskerville are discussing. They define generalization as follows, ‘‘Among other things, it [generalization] refers to the validity of a theory in a setting diferent from the one where it was empirically tested and confirmed’’ (ibid, p. 221). This is a very general account. However, more specifically, they are interested in the inductive style of generalization. This can be seen in the importance they place on Hume’s truism, as discussed earlier. They argue that, despite the diferences, both positivist and interpretivist generalization is sub ject to the assumption of the uniformity of nature;

‘‘Hume’s truism makes no distinction between positivism and interpretivism and, therefore, is no less problematic for an interpretive theory that faces real-world application in a new context (i.e., a setting where it was not developed or tested)’’ (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, p. 238).

The problem of induction, as discovered by Hume, is (not surprisingly) predicated on a Humean notion of causality. It is a question of whether the theories are valid in other contexts such that we can expect them to result in the same conjunction of events.

Inductive generalization, however, is not the only existing method of generalization. Lee and Baskerville limit their article to a consideration of positivist and interpretivist research and do not consider the possibility of scientific realist philosophies. If generalization is viewed, as critical realists view it, as the abstraction of a tendency (a notion of causality that will be introduced shortly) then the replication of events in other locales is no longer the goal of generalization. Induction is not the logical strategy. This eliminates the necessity to make the universality of nature assumption to secure generalization. Thus, if the uniformity of nature assumption holds for interpretivist research, as Lee and Basker ville suggest it does, then something akin to Humean causality must also hold.

At the end of the day, interpretivists appear caught between the proverbial rock and a hard place. The rejection of the ontology beyond subject-subject experiences comes with inherent tensions with respects to the regulation of research by reality and the desire to generalize. At the same time, the need to generalize and convey research results in the implicit acceptance of causality as the constant conjunction of events. This position sufers from the same inconsistencies related to causality as positivists described above. Either path results in theory-practice inconsistencies.

This discussion has focused on the role that causality plays in the emergence of theorypractice inconsistencies between interpretivist research and practice. This implies that one approach to solving these inconsistencies might be to reconceptualize causality. For example, Walsham (1995b) argues that interpretivist generalization is possible vis-a\`-vis the development of (among other things) concepts and theories. However, as constant conjunctions of events do not pertain in the social world, he comes to a logical conclusion that generalizations should be described as tendencies rather than predictions (ibid, pp. 78–79). To do so, Walsham explicitly cites the critical realist notion of causality as tendencies. However, as we will see, this notion is very diferent than the causality articulated by interpretivists.

That Walsham opted for a new notion of generalization based upon the critical realist notion of tendencies lends credence to a central theme of this paper; despite the stated philosophy, in practice researchers are compelled to think in realist terms. This type of argument has been advanced before by others advocating a scientific realist position (e.g. Hunt, 1990, p. 8). The reason for this is simple. Reality is constantly regulating our experiences, as necessarily must be so, ineluctably confronting us with the unpleasantness of any theory-practice inconsistencies.

At the end of the day, there is no alternative to having an ontology as all research tacitly presupposes it (Bhaskar, 2002, pp. 217–218). In other words, scientists are ‘‘‘background realists’, i.e., their practices take the independent existence of their domain of objects for granted’’ (Dreyfus, 1991, p. 9). In science, building a theory is ‘‘seeking to account for the state(s) of some thing (or things) or an event(s) that occurs to some thing (or things)’’ (Weber, 2003, p. iv). If concepts and theories do not ultimately refer some thing in the world (that thing could even be a concept or theory), then they refer to no-thing, thus losing their meaning and import for the social sciences (Archer, 2000, pp. 154–159).

Given this perspective, it would appear that interpretivists, while skeptical, are causal realists about the powers of the natural world and those powers that are commonly considered social, such as beliefs, intentions, and language. Without these minimal requirements, research itself is neither possible nor interesting. Social science research is predicated on the power of subjects to interpret and respond to questions. IS research is also predicated on the power of a particular ICT innovation to causally interact with the world. Without this, the innovation plays no role in the generation of the empirical diferences that constitutes the empirical variation essential to research, and is thus inconsequential and uninteresting from the perspective of a researcher.

## 2.3. Summary

This section has argued that there are some fundamental theory-practice inconsistencies in information systems research. The crux of these inconsistencies rest on the fact that in practice researchers arguably rely on a realist ontology with a non-empirical, non-deterministic notion of causality that conflicts with their philosophical stance. The stated positivist reliance on Humean causality does not adequately account for non-deterministic social science phenomena or deeper causal explanations that link empirical events. Conversely, the interpretivist denial of ontology beyond subject-subject relationships belies their reliance on even a basic set of causal powers that reside in their subjects. As we will see, both of these stated assumptions play a role in the technological determinism/social construction of technology debate.

The persistence of the theory-practice inconsistencies highlighted here are not unique to the information systems field. They have been persisting across the social sciences where similar ontological assumptions are made (e.g. Fleetwood, 2001; Ron, 2002). This persistence points to an inadequacy in these ontological assumptions. Of course, diferences in ontological positions are highly contentious, if not entirely irreconcilable. However, if a new account of ontology emerges that has greater explanatory power vis-a\`-vis current research practices and resolves these theory-practice inconsistencies then you have a pow erful argument for accepting this new conceptualization as an improvement upon the for mer. The following section argues that critical realism is one such account.

## 3. Critical realism

Many have argued for the usefulness of critical realism as a philosophical underpinning for the social sciences in general (Carter & New, 2004; Danermark, Ekstrom, Jokobsen, & Karlsson, 2003; George & Bennett, 2005; Miles & Huberman, 1994; Robson, 2002; Sayer, 2000; Stones, 1996) as well as economics (Lawson, 1998), organizational and management research (Fleetwood & Ackroyd, 2004; Mingers, 2000; Tsoukas, 1989), marketing (Zinkhan & Hirschheim, 1992), health and social work (Houston, 2001; Kazi, 2003), education (Maxwell, 2004), and social science evaluation (Pawson, 2004; Pawson & Tilley, 1997). Within the information systems literature, Mingers (2004b, 2004c, 2004d) has been the most active at promoting critical realism along with a few others (Carlsson, 2003; Dobson, 1999, 2002). This growing popularity stems in part from its ability to transcend some of the classic dualisms in the social sciences such as positivism vs. interpretivism, and structure vs. agency (Bhaskar, 2002, pp. 19–22). This transcendence occurs through a new conceptualization of ontology.

This article argues that CR assumptions efectively resolve the theory-practice inconsistencies discussed in the previous section. As we will see, this is done through the introduction of a stratified ‘‘naturalist’’ ontology for the natural and social sciences with a non-deterministic, non-Humean notion of causality. The following subsections set out the main tenants of critical realism with a focus on the ‘‘realism’’ component.

## 3.1. Scientific realism

Crotty (1998) in The Foundations of Social Research lays out what he views as the four main elements of social research; epistemology, theoretical perspective, methodology, and methods. He explicitly excludes an in-depth discussion of ontology as, for him, ‘‘ontological issues can be dealt with adequately without complicating our four-column schema further by expressly introducing ontology’’ (ibid, pp. 11–12). Bhaskar turns this enterprise on its head. The starting point for critical realism as a philosophy of science is ontological, not epistemological. When Bhaskar was developing his critical naturalism for the social sciences, he asked, ‘‘what properties do societies and people possess that make them possible objects of knowledge for us’’ (Bhaskar, 1998b, p. 13)? In other words, instead of asking how we can know something, a scientific realist asks what the world must be like to generate a particular phenomenon. It is only when we have a working hypothesis about what exists can we attempt to know it – or even understand how it may be possible to know it! This is, to put it simply, the ‘‘problem of the problem’’ (Weber, 2003, p. iv). The result of Bhaskar’s inquiry is critical realism.

Bhaksar’s critical realism is one of several versions of scientific realism (see: Hunt, 2005; Niiniluoto, 2002, chapter 1). However, as we will see, critical realism adds special ontological assumptions that diferentiate it from the others (Zinkhan & Hirschheim, 1992). At the core of scientific realism is a belief that successful theories are representative of what exists.

This is not a form of ‘‘naı¨ve’’ realism where truth is simply the direct product of experiences. Hunt (1990) has identified four fundamental tenants of scientific realism that diferentiate it from other philosophies: classical realism, fallibilistic realism, critical realism, and inductive realism. To understand the diference it is helpful to discuss critical realism in light of these tenants.

Bhaskar’s first conclusion is that for science to happen there must be a reality independent of our knowledge of it. This, in Hunt’s classification, is classical realism. Of this reality, Bhaskar makes a useful analytical distinction between the intransitive and transitive objects of knowledge. Intransitive objects are the world of things and structures that are independent of our knowledge of them. As Searle (1995) would describe it, these are the ‘‘brute facts’’, that are objective ‘‘in the sense that they are not a matter of your or my preferences, evaluations or moral attitudes’’ (ibid, p. 1). The transitive objects are the ‘‘raw materials of science – the artificial objects fashioned into items of knowledge by the science of the day’’ (Bhaskar, 1998a, p. 16). Thus, while our knowledge of the world (transitive) constitutes a part of the world (intransitive) the existence of the world is not dependent upon this knowledge.

This distinction allows for the combination of ontological realism with an epistemological relativism (Archer, Bhaskar, Collier, Lawson, & Norrie, 1998, pp. x–xi). The process of science and scientific knowledge is viewed as a historically emergent, political, and incomplete. These limitations imply that our theories are fallible (fallibilistic realism). No knowledge is ever 100% certain. For this reason CR is characterized as ontologically bold, but epistemologically cautious (Bhaskar, 1998b, p. 176).

The fallibility of knowledge, however, does not logically entail that all knowledge is equally fallible. Thus, while people subjectively hold multiple theories of the world, some of these theories may better approximate objective reality than others. The exact nature of truth, or how a realist theory may approximate reality, is still a matter of philosophical debate (Hunt, 1990, 2005; Psillos, 1999; Zinkhan & Hirschheim, 1992) even amongst critical realists (e.g. Grof, 2004; Willmott, 2002), and falls out of the scope of this paper. However, as a practical matter, one useful realist perspective is that the truth of a theory is an attribute of that theory (in the transitive domain) that corresponds to how accurately it represents and explains the phenomenon it refers to (in the intransitive domain) (Hunt, 2005).

Of course, choosing between theories is not straightforward given the socially constructed and value-laden nature of theories. Crucially, however, these features of knowledge do not make science incompatible with the goals of truth and objectivity (Meckler & Baillie, 2003) nor does it render science impossible (Kincaid, 1996, p. 45). For critical realists, despite the dificulties, a judgmental rationality is possible and central to science. The problem of realist science then, is to ‘‘use its method to improve our perceptual (measurement) processes . . . and thereby generate the most accurate possible description and understanding of the world’’ (Hunt, 1990, p. 9). This is the essence of the critical realism tenet of scientific realism. Theories must and can be accepted critically and tested according to an objective scientific rationality (Kincaid, 1996, chapter 3).

However, theories should be apprehended in an undogmatic way; they are fallible and changeable and there will always be a number of competing theories (Danermark et al., 2003, p. 117). However, critical realists do believe in inductive realism, that the long-term success of a scientific theory ‘‘gives us reason to believe that ‘something like’ the entities contained in the theories actually exist’’ (Hunt, 1990, p. 10). This is not simply a case of the theory not being falsified, but also that these theories have stood up to rigorous testing and the accumulation of evidence.

The transitive/intransitive distinction paved the way for the observation of the episte mic fallacy, that is, the conflation of statements about being in terms of statements about knowledge (Bhaskar, 1998a, p. 27). For example, logical positivists commit this fallacy when they claim that a proposition is meaningless unless it is empirically verifiable (ibid, p. 28). Another illustrative example can be found in Avgerou (2002) Information Systems and Global Diversity. Avgerou can be interpreted as committing this fallacy when she writes, ‘‘that diversity as a social condition cannot be ironed away with universalistic theories and practices implies a relativist stance’’ (ibid, p. 65). It does not logically follow that because universalistic theories cannot adequately explain social diversity that the world is such that there can be no rational basis for deciding between competing claims. These are two separate issues. Instead, Avgerou is confronted with the problem of the inability of universal laws to adequately explain the diversity to which she refers.

The crux of the issue lies in the type of social explanations or laws that researchers strive to uncover. As we will see, there are more alternatives to social explanation than universalistic notions. Indeed, much interpretivist work advances theories of local adaptation that in fact provide at least a partial explanation of processes at work that can account for such empirical diversity. So the question should not be framed as one of universalistic theories vs. relativism, but rather how we can explain phenomena in a non-Humean nonuniversalistic manner that is consistent with research such as Avgerou’s. As the following section outlines, the CR notion of generative mechanisms (or tendencies) provides the possibility for scientific explanation that allows for the causal impact of the artifact itself while stressing the importance of the local context and social diversity.

## 3.2. Transcendental realism and social naturalism

Besides the transitive/intransitive diferentiation, CR accepts a three-fold ontological stratification between the distinct domains of the real (what exists), actual (events), and empirical (observable events). While they are separated into three domains, the domain of the actual is a subset of the real, and the domain of the empirical is a subset of the actual. The deepest level of reality, the real, is populated by things (structured entities). What exists at this level are both social and physical phenomenon with their particular structures and capacities to behave in particular ways (Sayer, 2000, p. 11). These capacities to behave are termed generative mechanisms, that, simply stated, are ‘‘nothing other than the way of acting of a thing’’ (Bhaskar, 1998a, p. 38). It is these mechanisms that combine to generate the flux of phenomena that constitute the actual states and happenings of the world (ibid, p. 34). When a causal power in the domain of the real becomes activated (actualized) the result is referred to as the actual. Finally, the domain of the empirical consists of those events that are observed. The crucial insight to take away from this ontolog ical stratification is that structures and their generative mechanisms are ontologically decoupled from the events that they produce.

These structures are also stratified in another sense. Some structures emerge from a combination of structures and have qualitatively diferent causal powers that are irreducible to their constituent parts. For example, the combination of two hydrogen molecules with one oxygen molecule results in water whose emergent properties are new and very diferent than those of the parts taken separately (Sayer, 2000, pp. 12–13).

This emergence extends to the level of individuals and social structures. Individuals have emergent powers such as a continuous sense of self, personal identity, beliefs, interpretations, reflexive actions and the like<sup>2</sup> (Archer, 2000, 2002). Thus, these form part of the causal mechanisms operating in a setting (Sayer, 2000, p. 27). Social structures such as gangs, churches, governments, etc. are also ascribed ontological status. Like natural structured objects, these social structures emerge from relations, the relations between humanity and between these relations and nature (Bhaskar, 1998b). This relational conception of society is fundamentally diferent from methodologically individualist, holist, and structuralist conceptions<sup>3</sup> (Archer, 1995; Bunge, 2004) and can be traced back to Marx (Porpora, 1998).

The realist approach calls for a methodology of analytical dualism that traces the interaction of structure and agency through time (Archer, 1995). Social objects are both autonomous from individuals while dependent upon their activities. That social structures are autonomous is obvious when one considers that they pre-exist the individuals within them (imagine a university, culture, or language). Furthermore, and centrally important, independence is established through their causal impact on the world. In other words, the emergent social structures have social properties that causally impact on the individuals that constitute them as well as on other social structures (macro–micro and macro–macro) (Bhaskar, 1998b; Mayntz, 2004; Sawyer, 2002, 2003, 2004).

It is important to be clear how social structures can be considered causal. Social structure does not initiate activity and cause things to happen in the same way that people do (Lewis, 2000). This is not a standard ‘‘eficient’’ notion of causality (like billiard balls) as described by Aristotle (Grof, 2004; Lewis, 2000). This view replaces the overly deterministic notion of social structures as strings controlling a marionette with the metaphor of the rules of chess or the blueprint of a house (Grof, 2004, p. 110). Archer characterizes this mediation as an ‘‘objective influence which conditions action patterns and supplies agents with strategic directional guidance’’ (Archer, 1995, p. 196). In other words, an individual’s position in relation to structure supplies reasons and resources for action; socially emergent phenomenon supply material reasons and the culturally emergent phenomenon supply normative ones (ibid, p. 212). Individuals are enabled and constrained depending upon their position vis-a\`-vis society, but they still maintain a degree of voluntarism. For example, an individual is still free to act on false beliefs. However, acting in discord with objective reality will have objective consequences as the objective natural, practical, and social worlds constantly regulate our possibilities for action (Archer, 2002; Elster, 1989, p. 20).

## 3.3. Causal realism and tendencies

It is important to understand that the nature of real objects at a given time does not predetermine what will happen. Instead the structures of objects enable what can happen through the workings of their mechanisms within geo-historical contexts (Sayer, 2000, pp. 12, 115). In this sense, mechanisms can be better thought of as the tendencies of structures and not universal laws. This notion of tendencies is sustained through the very real distinction (‘‘ontological gap’’) between the structures, their causal powers (mechanisms), and the events they generate. This has important implications. For example, there are many interacting mechanisms whose actualization never reaches the realm of the empirical. Thus the ‘‘laws’’ (causal powers) that ground the natural mechanisms may endure and thus be ‘‘both applicable and true (that is, not falsified) though its efect (i.e. the consequent) [is] unrealised’’ (Bhaskar, 1998b, p. 11). In this sense reality runs much wider and deeper than that to which our senses allow access.

Importantly, this ontological gap allows for the possibility of simultaneously competing ‘‘laws’’. The flux of events is always co-determined by a myriad of interacting mechanisms. This co-determination renders accusations of determinism irrelevant. The actualization of any causal tendencies depends upon the co-presence of or mediation by a myriad of other structures and contingently related phenomena (Sayer, 2000, p. 95). While tendencies are the ‘way of acting’ of a thing, this way of acting always hap pens in an open, highly stratified context of mechanisms whose presence (or absence) codetermine what happens.

This ontological perspective further underscores the problem with the conception of a world governed by covering laws. In such a world, the simultaneous operation of conflicting laws would be impossible without a ‘‘nomological dangler’’ (Cartwright, 1989, p. 139). In other words, a world governed by covering laws that connect events does not allow for the possibility of two competing laws that are concurrently active. Indeed, as we will see in the following section, this inability to allow for the simultaneous operation of tendencies plays into the perpetuation of dichotomous discourses such as technological determinism vs. the social construction of technology that unnecessarily eliminate one side of the equation.

## 4. Dissolving dualities through tendencies

As we have seen, critical realism boldly asserts the existence an objective natural and social world. Central to this ontology are non-deterministic causal tendencies that reside at the level of the real and not the empirical. Interpreting the practice of science in light of these new assumptions is essential for resolving current theory-practice inconsistencies in information systems research. Once these concepts are introduced into the specifics of information systems theorization, they work to efectively resolve long standing theoretical debates that are predicated on positivist or interpretivist notions of ontology. That this comes about should be no surprise for a fundamental reason; social science explanations, currently and historically, are inherently mechanistic explanations (Bunge, 2004, p. 207; Hernes, 1998). In this sense, the focus on causal explanation rooted in a notion of generative mechanisms cuts across the various schools of thought (George & Bennett, 2005, p. 128). The critical realist ontology supplies a conceptual framework that consistently grounds these explanations. Theory more accurately matches practice. To demonstrate, this section considers one such debate; technological determinism vs. the interpretive flexibility of the social construction of technology (Bijker, 2001; Winner, 2001).

The notion that the physical materiality of technology plays a causal role in social change is sometimes dismissed as technological determinism (Kallinikos, 2004). Despite the rhetoric, such a conception is undoubtedly a straw man, as one would be hard-pressed to find a researcher who would argue that either the social or material world has no influence. The problem is to understand what kind of influence, and to have a theoretical vocabulary that is appropriate to describe that influence.

This article submits that this debate draws its sustenance from the limited philosophical choices researchers have drawn from to represent causality. Theoretically, technological determinism, with its simplistic one way arrow of causality, is an extension of the understanding of causality as the exercise of a universal law, in the Humean successionist account. The notion of interpretive flexibility applied to facts and artifacts shows that ‘‘nature does not ‘force the issue’ of the existence or non-existence of some purported phenomenon, one way or the other’’ (Pinch & Bijker, 1984, p. 420). Here Pinch and Bijker were concerned with the design of technological artifacts and not their eventual use after stabilization. Arguably Pinch and Bijker’s perspective is a restricted interpretivism that allows for some technological determinism after the point of closure and stabilization (Woolgar & Grint, 1991). More thorough-going interpretivism, however, asserts that technology’s character is essentially indeterminate (ibid). Inherent in both of these positions is an analytical focus on the beliefs of relevant social groups rather than an understanding of how the technology itself may influence interpretations. The result is that researchers take a ‘‘nominal’’ view of technology where technology is invoked ‘‘in name only’’ and remains an omitted variable (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001).

Examples abound, and are generally couched in terms of an either-or set of alternatives. For example, Avgerou and Madon (2004) contrast ‘‘universalistic’’ with ‘‘situated’’ research perspectives. The universalistic perspective, they argue, considers the potential and value of technology independently from the circumstances of implementation. This perspective, so stated, implicitly assumes that causality runs consistently and unfailingly from the values of the technology to people, regardless of the context. This conception of causality as purely a physical phenomenon leaves no room for the openness of human interpretation. The underlying argument is simple: if there were causality, there would be regularities, by definition. Certainly, there is no evidence to support such regularities between technology and social change, and thus any and all notion of causal impacts of technology must be rejected. They turn to the only other available option, the situated perspective that is concerned with the actors who ‘‘construct technology artifacts and information resources while they work out organizational changes’’ (ibid, p. 165).

At the end of the day, this approach opts to focus on one set of mechanisms over another – that is, those mechanisms associated with interpretation and agency such as beliefs, communication, and interpretation. There is no doubt that these mechanisms are central to most ICT innovations. The problem is, the extent of their influence cannot be determined a priori. This must be resolved empirically. In many cases, this focus might highlight the most influential mechanisms in the research context. However, if a researcher ignores a priori the contribution of the technical artifact they run the risk of overlooking important causal influences. For example, it becomes dificult, if not impossible, to address important questions and empirical phenomenon such as the appearance of stages of development (Lawson, 2004, p. 254), the objective political implications of technology (Introna & Nissenbaum, 2000; Winner, 1986), or all the ‘‘visions of run-away technology that prevail in society at large’’ (Winner, 2001).

The inability to adequately incorporate technology is also arguably due to another logical fallacy. Central to this is understanding that contingency does not logically imply underdetermination (Hacking, 1999, pp. 68–80). The argument of a social constructionist is that many interpretations are possible and the one arrived at is a contingent outcome. In other words, to assert the social construction of something means that the construction ‘‘could have been otherwise’’, that ‘‘it was not inevitable’’ (ibid, p. 6). From this they illogically conclude that the resultant interpretation must be indeterminate. Contingency and interpretive flexibility does not have to imply that possible outcomes or interpretations are infinite. Instead, interpretations are ‘‘constrained by the material characteristics of the technology’’ (Orlikowski, 1992, p. 409). The material elements of technology are structures with causal tendencies whose activations are dependent upon actions of social actors within a given geo-historical context.

In this scenario, the conception of technological determinism is softened into a less threatening conception of the actualizations of technologically based causal powers. Perhaps it is more accurate to say that for a majority of individuals the interpretive flexibility of technology is a bounded infinity. Just as there are an infinite number of points between any two points on a line, the nature of technology may be infinitely flexible within the objective constraints of technology. Fortunately, reality dictates that this is a theoretical infinity rather than a practical one. The individual’s history and position relative to society will to a large extent determine their individual interpretation of technology (Kallinikos, 2004). This aspect has been well researched by those in the social construction of technology school. What remains under-theorized, however, is the technological artifact itself (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) and what aspects of technology constrain and influence human behavior. That is, how technology ofers reasons and resources for particular human actions.

An example of this type of theorizing of the artifact has been done by Kallinikos (2002, 2004, 2005). Technological artifacts are better viewed as ‘‘standing possibilities’’ that ‘‘invite(s) specific courses of action’’ and excludes alternative paths of actions (Kallinikos, 2002, p. 3). In a very realist move, Kallinikos argues that one way to understand the human–computer situated interaction is to move beyond the human–technology interface to uncover the core properties of technology and how malleable they are. Kallinikos is attempting to uncover the ‘‘distinctive forms’’ (Kallinikos, 2005) through which technology constrains and enables human behavior at the moment of human-technology interaction and beyond. This is an analytical project uncovering the centrally relevant causal mechanisms inherent in the technology and its interaction with the structural mechanisms of the context within which the technology is embedded. It is decidedly not a descriptive account of interactions. Such an approach allows for an account of the persistence of diversity as a social condition without denying any role for technology or resorting to relativism.

This approach also allows for a new type of generalization that is not subject to the limitations of induction. Generalization does not come from a movement of empirical events in one context to empirical events in a novel context, but rather it results from the uncovering of the underlying essence of things, a movement from ‘‘surface to depth’’ (Danermark et al., 2003, pp. 77–78). This is the abstraction of the essential structures and relations with their inherent generative mechanisms (causal powers and liabilities).

Of course, these mechanisms are tendencies. Thus, placing a technology with particular inherent mechanisms into a new context represents a potential rather than a guarantee tha the mechanisms will be activated.

‘‘Generality . . . depends upon how common instances of the object are, and upon the circumstances or conditions in which objects exist, these determining whether the causal powers and liabilities of objects are activated, and with what efect’’ (Sayer, 2000, p. 136).

As Sayer points out, which mechanisms are fired is a function of the unique geohistorical conditions of context. As such, acontextual theories of technology only tell part of the story. The application of these generalized abstractions to new contexts also requires the knowledge of what and how diferent contextual mechanisms interact with the intrinsic mechanisms of the technology.

In this way, the critical realist notion of tendencies makes possible the reconceptualization of the technological determinism vs. the social construction of technology debate, efectively dissolving it. It is dissolved because the approach to knowledge accumulation it proposes asserts the importance of incorporating knowledge gained from each perspective. To understand the potentialities that diferent technologies bring, acontextual casual theories of technology are helpful. On the other hand, understanding the activities of humans to uncover how and why these mechanisms are fired (or not) requires a more qualitative understanding of how they socially construct technology. Together, they paint a more complete picture of the implementation of technology in context.

## 5. Conclusion

Over the last 20 years information systems researchers and theorists have, for the most part, been caught in a debate between competing philosophical paradigms for research (Mingers, 2004b). While there has undoubtedly been some reconciliation between the two sides (Weber, 2004) this has largely come about due to the acceptance of the epistemological validity of particular research methodologies for highlighting diferent aspects of the research situation through an integrative or pluralistic approach (Fitzgerald & Howcroft, 1998). However, before we can safely call a truce, a nagging question remains; whose ontology wins? For example, is there a notion of causality that will be accepted by both sides? Is it linear, circular, non-existent, or otherwise? The answer to that question will have a profound impact on one’s adopted epistemology. Ultimately, the debate is really about ontological, not epistemological, assumptions, as ontology is prior to epistemology.

One of the fundamental reasons the debate has persisted and will continue to exist is that among researchers there are fundamentally diferent ontologies. When two researchers hold diferent views on ontology, it is only logical that they will not see eye-to-eye on all matters of epistemology. In this light, it is notable that Weber’s (2004) discussion of the coming together of positivism and interpretivism does not mention the notion of causality. Any potential dissolution of the two paradigms must ultimately resolve this issue. To not do so would be to continue to perpetuate theory-practice inconsistencies. It follows that an epistemological resolution to the debate avoids the tough ontological issues that work to sustain it in the first place.

Unfortunately, the assumptions underpinning particular philosophical schools of thought are often uncritically embraced by researchers, if they are even aware of them (Lee, 2004, p. 6). This article argues that the emergence of persistent theory-practice inconsistencies within both paradigms of research brings their ontologies into question. This does not imply that all research under incomplete or false assumptions is for naught. Despite tensions and internal contradictions most research remains necessarily true to (and constrained by) the reality that the research is trying to explain. In other words, at the end of the day, we are all realists (Bhaskar, 1978; Latour, 1999). For example, Wal sham (2003) draws attention to what he considers to be essential issues when he argues for a combination of an interpretivist approach with a critical focus on issues such as social inequity and power relations, despite the internal contradictions discussed above. Indeed, the ineluctable regulation of reality on our experience implies that theory-practice contradictions will emerge when there is an incomplete philosophy. This regulation dictates that, despite their stated philosophical positions, researchers often diverge from their theoretical stance and engage in good science and produce good results (Bhaskar, 2002, pp. 27–28), as this article demonstrates. Consequently, this discussion highlights the continuing importance of increased critical examination of ontological assumptions in IS research.

This paper has argued that critical realism, in particular through its novel ontological position, has the potential to advance information systems theory and research. The major benefits of critical realism flow from the reinterpretation of the activity of science (Ron, 2002, p. 140) that then can better explain previous research (e.g. Befani, 2005; Fleetwood, 2001; Pratschke, 2003; Ron, 2002). Indeed, such a reinterpretation of the current practice of information systems research arguably resolves some long-standing theory-practice inconsistencies. In resolving these inconsistencies, CR provides a notion of causality that allows for the capturing of the underlying ‘‘why’’ questions posed in IS research. Furthermore, it gives a solid ontological grounding for interpretivist research reafirming the importance for a focus on meanings, interpretation, and context as causal influences without unnecessarily denying their existence.

This paper should be seen for what it is; an attempt to clarify research rather than argue for the dominance of one perspective. This is not an argument that critical realism is the solution to all research problems. Such a belief would be inconsistent given the stated fal lible nature of knowledge. There may be other interpretations of science that also resolve these inconsistencies. It is however, a logical argument for the adoption of critical realist assumptions. In due course, the application of research underpinned by critical realist assumptions, like all new ideas, means that its limitations will also eventually be reached. Unavoidably, reality will assert itself, theory-practice inconsistencies will emerge, and the cycle of philosophy and research will continue.
