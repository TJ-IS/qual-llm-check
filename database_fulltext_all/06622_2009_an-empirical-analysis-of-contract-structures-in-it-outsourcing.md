# An Empirical Analysis of Contract Structures in IT Outsourcing

Yuanyuan Chen

Department of Information Systems, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117543, Republic of Singapore, chenyy@comp.nus.edu.sg

Anandhi Bharadwaj

Goizueta Business School, Emory University, Atlanta, Georgia 30322, anandhi\_bharadwaj@bus.emory.edu

utsourcing of information technology (IT) services has received much attention in the information systems (IS) literature. However, considerably less attention has been paid to actual contract structures used in IT outsourcing (ITO). Examining contract structures yields important insights into how the contracting parties structure the governance provisions and the factors or transaction risks that influence them. Based on insights from prior literature, from practicing legal experts, and through in-depth content analysis of actual contracts, we develop a comprehensive coding scheme to capture contract provisions across four major dimensions: mon itoring, dispute resolution, property rights protection, and contingency provisions. We then develop an empirical data set describing the contract structures across these distinct dimensions, using a sample of 112 ITO contracts from the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) database from 1993 to 2003.

Drawing on transaction cost, agency, and relational exchange theories, we hypothesize the effects of transaction and relational characteristics on the specific contractual provisions, as well as on overall contract extensiveness. Furthermore, we examine how these associations vary under conditions of fixed price and time and materials pricing structures. The results provide good support for the main hypotheses of the study and yield interesting insights about contractual governance of ITO arrangements.

Key words: outsourcing; IT outsourcing contract; contract structure; fixed-price contracts; time and materials contracts

## 1. Introduction

It is essential to establish a solid legal contract with a supplier before entering into any outsourcing deal - - - the contract could be the key to success or failures - - - try to anticipate in the contract, anything that might happen - - - (McFarlan et al. 2003, p. 8).

Understanding outsourcing contract structures has been a focus of theoretical and empirical research in law and economics for more than 20 years. Transaction cost economics (TCE) has been widely used to explain how outsourcing governance structures have enabled exchanges by safeguarding against hazards arising from exchange characteristics, such as asset specificity and uncertainty (see, e.g., Ang and Straub 1998, Lacity and Willcocks 1995, Nam et al. 1996). Recent empirical research has examined discrete contract provisions such as pricing clauses (Crocker and Reynolds 1993, Kalnins and Mayer 2004), contract duration clauses (Crocker and Masten 1988, Joskow 1987), take-or-pay provisions (DeCanio and Frech 1993, Masten and Crocker 1985), and termination clauses (Arruñada et al. 2001), focusing on both the rationale for and the performance consequences of specific contract terms.

Despite the rapid growth in information technology outsourcing (ITO), information systems (IS) research has largely focused on issues such as the propensity of firms to outsource their information technology (IT) activities (Ang and Straub 1998, Aubert et al. 1996, Grover et al. 1996), the types of IT functions that are likely to be outsourced (Lacity et al. 1996, Quinn and Hilmer 1994), and variables influencing ITO performance (Grover et al. 1996, Lacity and Willcocks 1998, Lee et al. 2004). There has been much less emphasis on examining contract structures with a view to understanding the specific provisions that are emphasized in ITO and the transaction characteristics that affect the choice of contract structures. However, a few scholars have noted the importance of understanding contract structures in ITO (Gopal et al. 2003, Kalnins and Mayer 2004, Kern and Willcocks 2000, Saunder and Hu 1997). For example, Kern and Willcocks (2002) note that “a major finding in research has been that, if an organization outsources IT, the outsourcing contract is unarguably essential for outsourcing success” (Kern and Willcocks 2002, p. 16). Additionally, some empirical work examining discrete contracting provisions, such as incentive mechanisms (Gopal et al. 2003, Kalnins and Mayer 2004) and management control mechanisms in IT contracts (Anderson and Dekker 2005, Kern and Willcocks 2000), have begun to pave the way for a more holistic understanding of the entire contract structure.

Against this backdrop, the objectives of this study are (1) to conduct a detailed investigation of ITO contracts to refine our understanding of the essential dimensions or major contract terms typically found in ITO contracts and (2) to examine how transaction characteristics, such as the type of function outsourced, the nature of the outsourcing relationship, etc., affect contract design choices in ITO. We develop our understanding of ITO contracts through a careful examination of contractual provisions, using a sample of 112 ITO contracts culled from the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) database. Information in the contract clauses provides rich data about the manner in which firms structure the exchange and cope with problems that might arise.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, our paper is one of the early attempts to examine ITO contract structures in detail based on a comprehensive analysis of actual contracts. This allows us to go beyond the case study and game-theoretic approaches common in much of the literature examining ISO contract choices (Mahnke et al. 2005). The difficulty of obtaining detailed contract information has limited empirical investigation of contract design choices. Even some recent empirical work on ITO contract design is based on perceptual (i.e., survey) data about the contracts used (Anderson and Dekker 2005) and not on the actual contracts. In contrast, our ex post analysis of the ITO contracts spanning a 10-year time period allows us to develop a comprehensive scheme for classifying contract clauses and provides a more comprehensive understanding of the structure of the contracts.

Second, our detailed database of ITO contracts provides a unique opportunity to examine the transaction characteristics that affect the structure of the contracts. Using data culled from other external sources about the contracting parties, the type of activities outsourced, and the nature of the outsourcing relationship, we provide important insights into when contracting firms are likely to include specific provisions. For example, we find that when partners have contracted with each other in the past, they are likely to include more, not fewer, contractual provisions for monitoring, dispute resolution, and contingency planning. We also systematically examine the effects of other transaction characteristics, such as the use of customized technology and process interdependence, on specific contract provisions.

Finally, we recognize that, especially in ITO, many risks are managed through the use of discrete pricing structures; the two prevalent forms are fixed-price (FP) and time-and-materials (TM) contracts (Gopal et al. 2003). Use of other contractual provisions such as monitoring, dispute resolution, property rights, and contingency planning facilitates management of the residual risks not fully covered by the pricing structure.<sup>1</sup> We therefore examine the transaction characteristics associated with the use of discrete contract provisions within each of the two broad contract types, FP and TM, respectively. The subgroup analysis yields interesting results that are masked in the full sample. For example, although asset specificity is associated with more dispute resolution and property rights provisions in the case of FP contracts, dispute resolution and property rights do not increase with asset specificity in the case of TM contracts, presumably because the vendor’s risks are adequately covered by the pricing flexibility of TM contracts.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we explain the contract structure and the broad provisions in great detail. Next, we develop the research hypotheses, followed by the research methods, empirical analysis, and results. We conclude the paper with a discussion of implications for research and practice, as well as directions for future work.

## 2. Contract Structure in ITO

The theoretical background for much of the work on contract structure comes from the management control framework posited by Jensen and Meckling (1992), which describes management control systems as policies and practices that govern performance management, rewards and sanctions, and the assignment of rights and responsibilities. Although the structure of the contract has been noted in the IS literature as a key determinant of subsequent performance (Fitzgerald and Willcocks 1994, Lacity and Hirschheim 1993), only a few studies provide guidance about the structure of ITO contracts, and empirical evidence about the extent to which different structures are used in real-world settings is relatively rare.

The earliest work on the structure of ITO contracts analyzes five outsourcing contracts (Whang 1992). Although his work was based on a small sample, Whang (1992) concluded that while some similarities reflecting boilerplate templates exist, there was still considerable diversity across the five contracts in their use of various contractual provisions. The three components that he found in all contracts were product/task definition, intellectual property (IP) protection, and payment terms. Ryall and Sampson (2006), in their analysis of the structure of technology development<sup>2</sup> contracts, also note a similar diversity in contract specifications; they found three broad categories that account for a large portion of the variance across contracts: (a) the extent to which parties obligations are precisely specified, (b) the extent of monitoring that was explicitly provided for, and (c) the specification of penalties for noncompliant behavior. Specific subclauses across these three broad categories were identified, and the heterogeneity in the use of these clauses across the different technology development contracts was tracked and associated with the nature of interactions between the parties engaged in the transaction.

The most recent empirical study on the structure of IT contracts (Anderson and Dekker 2005) was based on survey data collected from a sample of IT buyers (clients) representing a broad array of IT products. Although this study is significantly different from ours because it focused on outright IT purchasing contracts and not on ITO contracts,<sup>3</sup> it nevertheless provides a useful starting point through its mapping of contract clauses into four dimensions of management control: assignment of rights, product and price terms, after-sales service terms,<sup>4</sup> and terms of legal recourse. Using exploratory statistical methods, the authors empirically detect the presence of these four dimensions of management control provisions and hypothesize that transaction costs are associated with more extensive (i.e., greater use of provisions) contract structures.

Using the aforementioned frameworks of contract structure as a starting point, we followed the approach described in Ryall and Sampson (2006) to develop a comprehensive understanding of contract structures used in the ITO context. We first chose three detailed and well-specified contracts from our sample and conducted a textual analysis of the contract provisions to understand the structure and provide the basis for a coding scheme to be used subsequently to analyze the broader sample of contracts. Using these three contracts we made an exhaustive list of all the terms and provisions. Next we eliminated redundancies by identifying the same or equivalent clauses that were repeated across these contracts, retaining only unique terms and clauses. Then we eliminated boilerplate-like terms (such as task/service description) that were relatively invariant across contracts. Clauses related to pricing and payment terms were also removed, because these were captured separately in our subsequent analysis under FP and TM contracts. These eliminations resulted in a set of 16 provisions that captured fairly exhaustively all of the remaining governance provisions used in them. Next, we grouped these 16 terms into the following four broad categories that seemed to represent distinct governance choices relating to (a) monitoring provisions, (b) dispute resolution, (c) property rights allocation/protection, and (d) contingency provisions. Finally, we ensured that the classifications adopted in the study corresponded to the frameworks identified in prior literature and were validated by an expert panel of three members, comprised of two law professors and a practicing lawyer. The experts verified that the textual analysis of the three contracts was comprehensive. A summary of the four dimensions and the underlying clauses in each dimension is provided in the appendix in Table A.

## 2.1. Monitoring Provisions

Monitoring is an integral part of the enforcement apparatus of contracts. It reduces contractual risks by specifying procedures designed to oversee the vendor’s operations on an ongoing basis. Monitoring provisions include, among others, clauses that describe periodic audit and review procedures to be followed, including timing and content of reviews, benchmarking of vendor’s performance against prespecified standards to ensure compliance, and adherence to guidelines such as disaster recovery plans in case of failures. In the control system framework of Jensen and Meckling (1992), monitoring provisions correspond to the performance measurement dimension, establishing the governance framework for observing and recording the vendor’s performance on an ongoing basis. Whereas some level of monitoring provisions is likely to be found in many ITO contracts, the extent of specification of different monitoring mechanisms has been found to vary across different contracts (Anderson and Dekker 2005).

## 2.2. Dispute Resolution

Although monitoring provisions record the governance framework for ongoing contract management and are helpful for detecting underperformance or noncompliance of the parties, they provide little guidance for managing disputes between the vendor and the client. The dispute resolution dimension of ITO contracts correlates with the rewards and sanctions dimension of the Jensen and Meckling (1992) framework. ITO contracts can vary in the extent to which specific clauses for resolving disputes are actually recorded in the contract. Whereas some contracting parties may prefer to specify in detail the arbitration processes for dispute resolution, others may prefer to manage such situations through relational mechanisms and therefore leave these clauses out of the contract (Ang and Beath 1993). To the extent to which they are specified, they help establish the processes to be adopted when disputes arise, including provisions for problem escalation, provisions specifying conditions for arbitration, and conditions that constitute grounds for a more formal lawsuit.

## 2.3. Property Rights

ITO often involves intensive information exchange and IP sharing between vendors and clients. In the Jensen-Meckling framework, this dimension corresponds to the assignment of rights and responsibilities and specifies the ownership and rights to control and use the IP, along with associated provisions for security and confidentiality. These clauses cover ownership or copyright, title and licensing rights, patent protections, data security and confidentiality terms, and rights to access the source code (in case of software development), including rights to modify or enhance the product. Significant heterogeneity of ITO contracts with respect to property rights protection has been suggested in prior literature, with the diversity related to the underlying transaction characteristics (Clemons and Hitt 2004, Walden 2005).

## 2.4. Contingency Provisions

In dynamic environments, outsourcing decisions may need to be continually reevaluated. Responding to unforeseen contingencies necessitates an adaptive capability or flexibility built into the contract. Contingency provisions refer to the built-in provisions for variation or modification, suggesting that they incorporate both proactive and responsive perspectives (Johnson et al. 2003). Although this dimension does not find a corresponding parallel in the framework of Jensen and Meckling or in the structures described by Whang (1992), Ryall and Sampson (2006), and Anderson and Dekker (2005), our content analysis of actual contracts suggests that this is an important dimension along which contracts seemed to vary in their specifications. Against a backdrop of evolving enterprise requirements, as well as industry and technological changes, client companies seem to want to ensure that the contract provides sufficient flexibility to allow for future changes. Equally, a vendor firm would want to ensure that its performance capabilities are not compromised by unforeseen environmental contingencies. The option to terminate the contract for convenience gives one or both parties the right to terminate the agreement (either in whole or in part) without cause at any time by simply giving written notice to the other party (Holland 2003). The IT marketplace is subject to periodic change, as are the requirements of outsourced IT functions. These changes could enhance or diminish the parties’ interest in the outsourcing deal. As such, the flexibility to be able to terminate or exit from the relationship could be an important strategic concern when the contractual arrangement ceases to meet the needs of the parties (Evans 1991, Minehart and Neeman 1999, Young-Ybarra and Wiersema 1999). Another contingency provision found in some ITO contracts allows the parties to make mutually agreed-upon changes to the contract in response to changes in the environment or because of evolving needs of the relationship. The right to modify contract mechanisms is likely to specify generic processes or procedures for proposing and accepting contract amendment for resolving change requirements, including scope and price adjustment provisions (Mulherin 1986). Finally, some ITO contracts also include contingency clauses to deal with more specific issues that might arise between the parties and are sometimes idiosyncratic to the transaction and are thus unlikely to be captured in generic contingency planning templates (Argyres et al. 2007). This provision allows the specification of parties’ obligations in case of a foreseeable event happens. For example, a specific contingency provision might allow for specifying which party will bear the costs of revising the application code if the operating system vendor were to release a new version during product development.

Taken together, the four dimensions of contractual provisions yield a measure of contract extensiveness (Anderson and Dekker 2005), defined as the total number of subprovisions written into the contract along each of the four major dimensions. The provisions not only cover the most important clauses in ITO contracts but also are likely to yield significant variance across contracts because these are not the standard boilerplate terms. Next we describe the broader empirical sample of ITO contracts used in this study. We then set out the hypotheses positing the relationship between the transaction characteristics and contract structures.

## 2.5. Contracts Sample: Coding and Description

We gathered a set of 112 ITO contracts, entered into between 1993 and 2003, from public SEC filings. Under SEC disclosure requirements, public firms in the United States must file “material contracts”<sup>5</sup> as exhibits of their 8-K, 10-K, 10-Q and S-1 filings, including substantial or important ITO contracts. We downloaded SEC filings from the Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval (EDGAR) database and performed textual searches to filter the ITO contracts<sup>6</sup> covering a broad array of IT functions from maintenance type contracts to multifunction ITO. Our final sample consists of firms drawn from nine industries: food, finance and banking, printing, chemicals, industrial machinery and equipment, petroleum and coal, electronic equipment, computer, and instruments and related products. The average client company in our sample had a sales volume of about \$17 million per year, whereas the vendor company average was about \$5 million per year. Contract value ranged from large contracts of more than \$1 billion to much smaller contracts of about \$50,000. Among the 56 contracts that released contract value, 26 were worth more than \$10 million. A sample list of contracts is included in the Appendix in Table B.

We use the contract structure described in the previous section to examine the 112 contracts in our broader sample. Using the dimensions identified above, we developed careful and precise coding criteria to measure the extent to which each contract contained provisions for monitoring, dispute resolution, rights protection, and contingency provisions. To keep the contract coding task simple and straightforward, we coded each target provision simply as present or absent. Thus, the expected values for monitoring provisions can range from zero to seven, indicating a minimum of zero monitoring provisions to a maximum of seven monitoring provisions in each contract. Likewise, the ranges for dispute resolution, property rights protection, and contingency provisioning are zero to three (please refer to Appendix Table A).

To ensure coding consistency and reliability, two coders (one of the authors and a doctoral student in engineering who had significant software development experience) independently coded all the contract documents. Examples of sample provisions from actual contracts were also provided to the coders. For each contract, the coders independently generated scores for the four major dimensions, representing the extent to which the contract contained the four provisions. A total score for contract extensiveness representing the sum of the four dimensional scores was also computed. We checked the interrater reliabilities for all categories and found them to be high (Cohen’s k <sub>=</sub> 0-80 or above). We further attempted to ensure test-retest reliability by recoding a small number of contracts after some time had elapsed since the original coding. Throughout the coding process, the results of the recoding were almost identical to the previous results, and the agreement rate ranged from 0.96 to 1.00. Additionally, two law professors coded a small subsample of the contracts and found good overall agreement with the coders. Overall, we believe that this process resulted in highly reliable coding of the contract data.

Table 1 provides the frequencies and crossfrequencies of the contract terms for all the contracts in the sample (Panel A) and separately for the FP (Panel B) and TM (Panel C) contracts. As expected, the contracts exhibited considerable variance in overall contract extensiveness as well as across the different provisions. For example, whereas 87% of the contracts incorporated a periodic review provision, only 27% of the sample contained specific clauses to ensure regular benchmarking of vendor performance. Overall, only about two thirds of the sample contained any arbitration clauses, with only about one fourth specifying additional litigation provisions. A good majority of the contracts included one or more clauses for property rights protections (between 75% and 90%). Only slightly more than one third of the contracts included any contingency provisions for modification or termination for convenience from the contractual stipulations, and less than 30% of the contracts included any specific contingency stipulations. Panels B and C also reveal some interesting patterns across the FP and TM contracts. In general we find greater reliance on monitoring provisions in the case of TM contracts when compared to FP contracts. Likewise, with the anticipation of greater likelihood of disputes, the dispute resolution provisions are also more extensive in the case of TM contracts. In contrast, FP clients may feel that risk of cost escalation is already mitigated by the pricing structure.

## 3. Determinants of Contract Structure

Outsourcing contract structures are best understood through an integration of perspectives derived from transaction costs theory (Williamson 1985), agency theory (Alchian and Demsetz 1972), and relational exchange theory (Macneil 1978, 1980). Table 2 provides an overview of the various risks associated with contracting that are identified by the different theories and the corresponding contractual provisions with which they are most likely to be associated. Agency theoretic perspectives in the literature have emphasized the role of factors such as task complexity, uncertainty of (vendor) behavior, and measurement problems in increasing the cost of writing and enforcing contracts. ## 3.1. Asset Specificity

Asset specificity defines the degree to which the assets in an exchange are more valuable in their current application than in their next best use (Leiblein and Miller 2003). In the IT context, asset specificity stems from highly customized solutions or technologies developed for clients, making their use in alternate contexts difficult and suboptimal (Lacity and Willcocks 1995). Customized technology may involve physical asset specificity, wherein a piece of equipment or a component is designed exclusively for a specific client. Developing customized solutions can also require human asset specific investments in training and learning. For example, developing a customized customer relationship management (CRM) system for a firm would require intimate understanding of the firms’ customer interactions and other customer-centric processes. The greater level of business process specificity also creates an increased requirement for integration and coordination of activities that incur additional relationship specific investments (Wang et al. 1997). Thus heightened asset specificity raises the potential value loss if the contractual relationship were to turn sour and increases switching costs for the clients. Companies will therefore find it beneficial to implement close monitoring processes (e.g., review and auditing procedures) to offset the shirking and holdup problems caused by such specific assets (Reuer and Ariño 2007, Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995). Hence, we offer the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A.</sup> Asset specificity will be positively associated with monitoring provisions.

In addition to the risks of shirking and holdup, misappropriation or theft of IP is another kind of strategic risk prevalent in outsourcing (Aron et al. 2005) because the transfer of intangible assets is irreversible and difficult to detect (Anand and Khanna 2000, Teece 2000).<sup>7</sup> The problems of IP control are exacerbated by weak IP protection for intangible properties such as software code, proprietary information, and knowhow (Clemons and Hitt 2004). Therefore, IP owners will try to use contractual provisions to clearly stipulate IP rights and institutionalize stricter IP protection mechanisms aimed at deterring misappropriation and theft. Empirical work demonstrates that asset specificity increases the level and scope of IP control provisions in technology agreements (Brousseau and Coeurderoy 2005). Hence we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B.</sup> Asset specificity will be positively associated with property rights provisions.

ITO contracts involving customized technology development are generally more idiosyncratic (Niederman et al. 1991) and require intensive sharing of proprietary information and knowledge. Both parties anticipate a greater likelihood of disputes under such situations and are therefore more likely to invest time and energy to specify contractual stipulations for dealing with disputes (Poppo and Zenger 2002). The asymmetric information about the quality of customized technologies developed and effort invested in the service delivery processes, typically observable only by the contracting parties and not verifiable by third parties such as courts and mediators, makes dispute resolution in ITO particularly challenging (Grossman and Hart 1986, Hart 1995). Resolving disputes through legal processes is therefore considered time-consuming and complicated (Thornburg 2000). The involved and often very long trial processes relative to the pace of technology may allow the IP abusers to develop next-generation products on the existing technology and, ultimately to overtake the IP holders as the industry leader (Costello 1999). In an attempt to control litigation costs, many companies prefer alternative dispute resolution mechanisms, such as mediation and arbitration processes, to avoid lawsuits. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1C.</sup> Asset specificity will be positively associated with dispute resolution provisions.

The association between asset specificity and contingency provisions that generally confer more flexibility in modification and termination of the contract is not very straightforward. Prior literature in marketing and alliance contracts (Heide and John 1992,

Parkhe 1993) suggests that firms become committed to making the existing relationship succeed to protect their idiosyncratic investments in the alliance. Hence, firms are more willing to be flexible in terms of modifying the agreement rather than causing it to fail by being unwilling to adjust. However, a more recent study by Young-Ybarra and Wiersema (1999) on IT alliance proposes an opposite association between these two constructs. They found that both intangible and tangible asset specificity have a detrimental impact on the strategic flexibility of the IT alliance relationship. The reason behind that rigidity is that the requirement to create specific technologies that cannot be put to productive use in other contexts tends to make the contracting parties less amenable to periodic changes in specifications and especially more resistant to provisions that guarantee termination of the contract at will. Contracting parties are therefore likely to seek protection of their asset-specific investments by disallowing such contractual guarantees for modification or exit flexibility. Given the contrasting viewpoints, we leave this open to empirical examination by specifying alternative forms of the hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1D.</sup> (i) Asset specificity will be positively associated with contingency provisions. (ii) Asset specificity will be negatively associated with contingency provisions.

Given the lack of specificity about the association between asset specificity and contingency provisions, the relationship to overall contract extensiveness is also unclear, and we therefore leave it open to empirical assessment.

## 3.2. Process Interdependence

Process interdependence in ITO occurs when clients need to integrate their business processes and systems with those of the vendor company. For example, if a vendor manages the billing systems for an online company in real time, then the software for bill presentment, verification, and payment has to be integrated with the client’s order management systems. This kind of interdependence is well exemplified in the outsourcing relationship between Independent Bank Corp. and Fidelity Information Services, Inc.; in this case, Fidelity’s proprietary HORIZON Banking system had to be integrated to work with Independent and its affiliates’ applications to support the full range of loan, deposit, and investment management products and services to businesses and individuals. In general, we expect process interdependencies between the client and the vendor to create more risks for the clients, such as risks stemming from switching costs and loss of internal control over critical aspects of its operations. Process interdependencies also create risks for the vendors because they are more likely to be associated with specialized investments that may be hard to deploy elsewhere without retraining (in the case of human assets) or reconfiguring (in the case of technological assets). Thus, our expectation is that process interdependence will result in more extensive contracts through increased use of all contractual dimensions to mitigate the risks. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2[A–D].</sup> Process interdependence will be positively associated with monitoring, property rights, dispute resolution, and contingency provisions.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2E.</sup> Process interdependence will be positively associated with overall contract extensiveness.

## 3.3. Prior Interaction

It is generally believed that prior interaction between the parties will lead to better mutual understanding of each others’ processes and norms, which may in turn encourage further contracting (Heide and Miner 1992). Generally, two elements of partner-specific interactions have been studied in the literature: trust and learning. Both are expected to be positively influenced by successful prior interactions between the vendor and the client. However, the effects of these two factors on the level of contract specifications have been posited to be the opposite of each other. It has been argued that prior interactions provide some degree of mutual trust and therefore reduce the need for devoting attention to crafting extensive or highly detailed contracts (Dyer and Singh 1998, Gulati 1995, Ring and Van de Ven 1994). However, more recent theoretical and empirical work in contract structures notes that prior interactions increase the opportunities for contracting parties to become more familiar with the other party’s business processes, decision-making styles, and corporate cultures and thus improve overall ability to draft more specific contracts (Mayer and Argyres 2004, Poppo and Zenger 2002, Ryall and

Sampson 2006). Parties are thus more likely to learn mutual needs and therefore be able to describe the tasks and associated monitoring requirements such as ongoing review, staffing, and benchmark provisions with greater precision. Therefore, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3A.</sup> Prior interactions will be positively associated with monitoring provisions.

A history of prior interactions is also likely to have enabled partners to learn more effective strategies for dispute resolution and therefore evolve built-in provisions for handling such disputes through a streamlined process specified in the contract rather than leave it unspecified or be resolved ex post through litigation. The partner-specific learning will help firms codify best practices for dispute resolution and arbitration mechanisms, and rather than allow these to remain unspecified, firms are more likely to encode these provisions in the contract arrangement. In a similar vein, contracting parties are also likely to learn more about issues that might arise because of IP protections or third-party obligations toward data protection and, therefore, to be able to write better contractual safeguards against such risks. Thus, we hypothesize as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3B.</sup> Prior interactions will be positively associated with property rights provisions.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3C.</sup> Prior interactions will be positively associated with dispute resolution provisions.

Contingency planning provisions rely heavily on the contracting parties’ abilities to foresee situations that might need adjustments and stipulate corresponding actions accordingly. Prior interactions might increase the ability of the parties to better anticipate the need for contingency provisions (Argyres and Liebeskind 1999). It is also plausible that with prior interactions, however, parties have evolved other informal (noncontractual) mechanisms to resolve unanticipated contingences, which may cause them to actually make fewer stipulations in the contract, given that it is costly to identify contingencies ex ante. We therefore leave this open to empirical examination by specifying alternative forms of the relationship between prior interactions and contingency planning.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3D.</sup> (i) Prior interactions will be positively associated with contingency provisions. (ii) Prior interactions will be negatively associated with contingency provisions.

Overall, given our preceding arguments, the effect of prior interactions on overall contract extensiveness is unclear, and we therefore leave it open to empirical assessment.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

We supplemented the data coded from the contracts (see §2) with additional data about the contracting activity and the contracting parties from numerous sources, including 10-K reports, industry reports, industry specific articles reporting outsourcing deals, the Factiva database, and the Dow Jones Interactive database. In their 10-K reports to the SEC, firms explained the context of their outsourcing decisions and their reasons for choosing a specific vendor. The 10-K reports also disclosed information about prior contractual relationships, if they existed, with the same vendor and about the client’s perception of the vendor’s ability to provide the outsourced functions. We also searched the Factiva and Dow Jones Interactive databases on the company names to collect news and press releases related to the outsourcing deal. These documents allowed us to code the explanatory (independent) variables for examining contract structures. These included the client’s prior interaction with the same vendor, the use of customized technology in the outsourcing deal, and the process level interdependence of the outsourced activities. Additionally, we collected data on several variables that were introduced as controls in our empirical model. These included firm size, of both the vendor and client, measured for each as the log of average sales over the three years before the contract date; contract duration, a measure based on deviation of earnings forecasts over the three years before signing the contract, as our proxy for environmental dynamism; and four task dummies for measuring complexity of the outsourced tasks, respectively. The sales and earnings data were obtained from the I/B/E/S and COMPUSTAT databases. Table 3 provides detailed descriptions for variables and measurements, and Table 4 provides summary statistics and a correlation matrix for the variables. About 41% of the contracts was between parties that had prior relationships, and 47% incorporated the customized technologies. The mean contract term (duration) was three years, and 77% of our sample represented contracts signed after 1997.

## 4.1. Model Estimation and Results

We ran separate models for the four dependent variables, reflecting the contract dimensions of monitoring, dispute resolution, property rights, and contingency provisions, as well as an overall model of contract extensiveness. To avoid potential problems stemming from heteroskedasticity, we first ensured that there were no repeating firms (both vendors and clients) in the sample. The explanatory variables in our model were proxies for asset specificity, process interdependence, and prior interactions, and the control variables included in the model were environmental dynamism, vendor size, client size, contract duration, and task dummies. Because our sample time frame extended from 1993 to 2003, we also added a time dummy to capture other unaccounted time-period effects by using 1997, the midpoint of our sampling time frame, as a cutoff point. We also conducted the analysis using the full sample, which included an additional dummy to control for pricing terms, FP or TM. We also conducted separate subsample analyses for these two types of contracts. The basic structure of the different models that test the factors associated with the use of the major contractual provisions is as follows:

The results of the ordinary least squares (OLS) analysis are captured in Table 5 (Panel A <sub>=</sub> full sample; Panel $\mathrm { B } = \mathrm { F P }$ contracts; and Panel C TM contracts).

Overall, the results obtained provide very good support for the hypotheses. Across all models for the combined sample, the R-squared values range from a high of 0.76 (monitoring) to a low of 0.34 (property rights) and 0.74 for overall contract extensiveness. Among the controls, contract duration; the task dummies representing software development, network/telecommunication management, and multifunction outsourcing; and the price dummy representing TM contracts were significant in explaining overall contract extensiveness, and client size and time dummy were significant for some discrete provisions.

As posited, we find asset specificity to be positively associated with property rights provisions and dispute resolution, and although it is negatively associated to contingency provisions as hypothesized, it is nonsignificant. Overall, asset specificity has no discernable relationship to contract extensiveness, because it increases the use of some provisions while decreasing others such as contingency planning. Process interdependence was predicted to positively impact all contractual specifications, and the data provide very good support for this hypothesis. Finally, in line with our expectations, prior interaction was positively associated with monitoring, dispute resolution, and contract extensiveness. However, we did not find it to be positively associated with property rights. We had made no predictions about its association to contingency provisions but found it to be positive and significant in the results.

The subsample analyses revealed some interesting differences between FP and TM contracts. Overall, our model was a better predictor of contract extensiveness in TM contracts $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 7 4 )$ compared to FP contracts $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 1 )$ . Although asset specificity showed no relationship to contract extensiveness in the full sample, the subsample analysis showed it to be positive and significant for TM but not for FP contracts.

## 4.2. Robustness Checks

Before we discuss the implications of the results obtained, we will describe a number of additional robustness checks carried out to confirm the validity of the results obtained in the OLS estimations. First, we note that we included several control variables in the model to address potential omitted-variable biases. We also conducted the OLS regressions in a hierarchical manner that included the full sample (which included a control for pricing terms), as well as the FP and TM subsamples, to account for outsourcing risks that are already captured by the pricing arrangements. Thus our models seek to explain the residual risks over and beyond those captured by the pricing arrangement.

Because our study is built on variables obtained through the coding of contracts, it is conceivable that the results are sensitive to coding standards. First, in our main empirical model, we had coded contract time as a binary measure. ITO contract structures may be influenced by events that dramatically changed the IT industry in the 10 years on which we focused, e.g., Y2K, the dot-com bubble and subsequent burst, etc. Therefore, we used three time dummies, dividing the total period into four periods of outsourcing activities: 1993–1995, 1996–1998, 1999–2001, and 2002–2003. However, none of the three time dummies was significant across the five models. Second, we tested for the sensitivity of the contract provisions because the assignment of clauses to provisions was done manually by the coders. We dropped two items (auditing and periodic review) that appear in more than 80% of the sample and ran the OLS regression. We find that the results still hold, thereby validating the robustness of the coding scheme. These results are shown in Appendix Tables C and D.<sup>8</sup>

Next we addressed the potential concern of using OLS when our dependent variables are all ordinal data. Although we treat the contract dimensions and contract extensiveness as continuous variables, we also tested the model using ordered probit, which is a more robust estimation technique for ordered variables (Crocker and Reynolds 1993). The corresponding results from the ordered probit estimations are provided in the Appendix in Table E and show remarkable consistency with the OLS results obtained earlier. In the case of constructs that were measured on a fewer than five-point scale (i.e., property rights, dispute resolution, and contingency), we also used multinomial logit model to estimate them. The estimation results were once again found to be consistent with the OLS and ordered probit results (Appendix, Table F).

Another potential concern with the results obtained stems from the possibility of endogeneity. One candidate for endogeneity would appear to be asset specificity because the decision to develop or use customized systems or technologies in the outsourcing arrangement represents a choice variable under the control of the parties. The decision to source a customized technology is made well in advance of the contract negotiations that determine the contract structure. Firms may choose to buy customized technologies based on unobserved characteristics associated with the firms or transaction. Failure to address this selection process leads to the possibility that variances of contract structure may be incorrectly attributed to outsourcing asset specificity when they in fact stem from unobserved factors associated with the asset specificity. To correct for these potential biases we used a two-stage econometric technique (suggested by Heckman 1979 and Shaver 1998). In the first step, we split the sample into two subsamples: contracts that specified development or use of customized technology and those that used generic or noncustomized technologies. Following Shaver (1998), we estimate a probit model using maximum likelihood to assess the effects of process interdependence, prior interactions, and task dummies, as well as other controls that might be expected to influence the decision of firms to use customized technologies. Endogeneity is corrected for by creating a control variable “lambda” using estimates obtained from the first-stage. Lambda $( \lambda _ { i } )$ , or the “inverse Mill’s ratio,” is calculated as $\hat { \lambda } _ { i } = \hat { \phi } ( \hat { \gamma } _ { i } \omega _ { i } ) / \Phi ( \hat { \gamma } _ { i } \omega _ { i } )$ if asset specificity 1 and as $\hat { \lambda } _ { i } = - \phi ( \hat { \gamma } _ { i } \omega _ { i } ) / ( 1 - \Phi ( \hat { \gamma } _ { i } \omega _ { i } ) )$ if asset specificity 0, where  is the standard normal density function, $\omega _ { i }$ and $\hat { \gamma } _ { i }$ are the vectors of independent variables and coefficients from the firststage probit model, and  is the standard normal distribution function, respectively (Greene 2003). In the second stage, the contractual provisions and contract extensiveness were estimated as a function of their appropriate determinants and via the inclusion of lambda as an additional predictor variable (Shaver 1998). Once again, the OLS results with the inclusion of the instrument variable were consistent with the results obtained earlier. Lambdas are not significant in all four models. The corresponding results for the first stage are provided in the first column in the Appendix (Table G). The rest of the columns in Appendix Table G present the second-stage estimation. In addition to the OLS estimation, we also used ordered probit analysis for the second-stage estimation. The results indicate that the directions of the main effects in our models do not change (Appendix Table H).

A final remaining concern is that the error terms of the estimated equations may be correlated across the equations because we used the same data to estimate multiple equations. To account for the correlated error terms, we use seemingly unrelated regression model (SUR, Greene 2003) to estimate the first four equations jointly. The SUR results were also consistent with the OLS results obtained earlier, as shown in Appendix Table I.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

The empirical results obtained here provide substantial theoretical and practical insights into the structure of ITO contracts and the characteristics of the contracting arrangements that influence that structure. Given the rapid growth of ITO, there is considerable interest in understanding contracting structures and their drivers in both the academic and practitioner communities. From a theoretical perspective, our study lays the groundwork for more rigorous analysis of ITO arrangements including both contractual and noncontractual provisions. From a practical standpoint, ITO managers would do well to compare the contracts they draft to the general provisions and frequencies of use described here as an average benchmark. In particular, they can compare the transaction risks they face to those generally covered in the broad sample of contracts described here to make appropriate decisions about design choices.

We show that ITO contracts are not monolithic documents containing largely boilerplate items. Rather, there is considerable diversity across contracts in the manner in which contractual provisions are used and described. The heterogeneity in structure likely arises because of differences in the goals, contracting environments, and other transaction-specific characteristics. We also examine structural differences between FP and TM contracts and show that TM contracts are likely to be more extensive overall and also across every contract dimension because these contracts exert greater monitoring pressures on the client. However, the results also show that it would be folly to assume that FPs automatically take away all the risks in outsourcing and, therefore, contract structures do not have to incorporate any details. We find that even in FP contracts, more than 80% incorporate audit and review procedures, although provisions such as benchmarking are much lower (10%) when compared to TM contracts (52%). In general, contingency planning provisions are relatively scarce, with only a third of the contracts incorporating them. Presumably, managers are constrained in their ability to foresee and contractually resolve future contingencies. As the ITO market matures and there is greater learning over time, we expect that this dimension will become more critical in future contracting.

Contract extensiveness was found to be increasing in contracts where parties had engaged in previous interactions. This is consistent with findings in other contracting contexts, in which prior experience with the partner has been found to consistently increase the comprehensiveness of contracts (Ryall and Sampson 2003). A critical implication of our finding is that although prior interactions may signal strong bilateral cooperation, it does not mean that partners are willing to draft less extensive contracts under those circumstances. On the contrary, prior experience suggests a better understanding of mutual requirements and capabilities, that paradoxically allows parties to draft more comprehensive contracts. The only contract dimension not influenced by prior interaction in the full sample was property rights provisions, and this finding was consistent across both the FP and TM subsamples. The major clauses in this dimension relate to rights to use, transfer of license rights and obligations, ownership of derivative assets, and other confidentiality and noncompete clauses. We might speculate that perhaps this dimension alone, more than others, is influenced more by the trust effect, as opposed to the learning effect, thereby allowing contracting parties to be less stringent about specifying property rights and ownership provisions in contracts. However, more research is needed to verify if this result is indeed generalizable to other samples.

Interdependence of processes also greatly influenced contract extensiveness and, not surprisingly, was a consistent finding across all subsamples. Process interdependence suggests that systems and work processes outsourced to the vendor are not fully modularized or decoupled from the client’s operating systems and therefore require additional contractual stipulations to ensure smooth operations. The consistency of this finding, even among the FP contracts, suggests that pricing terms alone do not fully remove the risks associated with outsourcing interdependent processes and that additional safeguards are warranted.

Our comprehensive investigation across contracting dimensions and pricing structures also paints a more interesting picture of the differential effects of asset specificity in contracting. The positive relationship of asset specificity to monitoring in the case of TM contracts but not in FP contracts suggests that under FP terms, contracting parties may feel more secure about their respective obligations without stringent monitoring. However, parties in FP contracts are more likely to protect their investments via additional provisions for dispute resolution and property rights allocation. Thus, focusing solely on overall contract extensiveness masks important distinctions about which contracting provisions are paramount under different pricing terms. Among the controls, environmental dynamism (captured as variability in earnings) was only significant in the dimension of contingency planning. The result is not surprising in the case of the IT industry, in which the market changes so dramatically that contracting parties are prone to prescribe the ways or processes to deal with unforeseeable contingencies. However, environmental dynamism is insignificant in other contracting dimensions suggests that such macrofactors may have little or no influence on the nonvolatile aspects of contracts. A more relevant proxy might have been technological uncertainty, but we were unable to collect any data about that.

## 5.1. Limitations and Extensions

The study has important limitations that suggest caution in generalizing the findings. First, we obtained detailed nonduplicate contract information for only 112 ITO contracts for the years 1993–2003. Although the sample size was adequate and reflected considerable diversity for our purposes, the results must be tested with larger samples and from more recent periods. Second, because public firms are required to file only “material contracts,” our sample is likely biased toward the larger ITO contracts that the firms voluntarily chose to disclose. Third, the study sample comprised only U.S. clients and vendor firms. It would be interesting to extend the study to a global sample that includes offshore vendors. For example, although we did not find client or vendor size to be significant, the results are likely to be different in offshore contracts in which clients presumably wield more power over the vendors. Fourth, we are restricted in our estimation by the availability of data from secondary data sources. Some potentially interesting variables, such as level of trust between the parties, might be gleaned through targeted primary data collection. Furthermore, richer measures of some of the variables, such as asset specificity, are likely to yield more insightful results about the differential effects of specificity emanating from the client and vendor sides of the partnership. Fifth, we chose to code the contract provisions as equally weighted binary codes, indicating the presence or absence of the provisions. Finally, the model does not account for interactions among the transaction characteristics to analyze the joint effects of some of these variables.

Future research can build interesting extensions by using richer coding mechanisms and differentially weighted schemes for contract provisions as well as by including interaction terms to obtain more nuanced results. Another interesting extension to our study would be examining the performance implications of choices of contract structure. One way to examine this question is to test the influence of contract structure on the success of the outsourcing initiative. Our study provides the foundation for more integrative research on ITO that examines the nomological relationships among the antecedent conditions (transaction risks), contract structural elements (monitoring, dispute resolution, property rights, and contingency provisions), and eventual performance. Such work may also offer a basis for more sound normative guidelines for practitioners.
