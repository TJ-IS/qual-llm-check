# From Elements to Structures: An Agenda for Organisational Gamification

Ali Khan <sup>a</sup>, Farzam Boroomand<sup>a</sup>, Jane Webster<sup>a</sup> and Xerxes Minocher<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Smith School of Business, Queen’s University, Kingston, ON, Canada; <sup>b</sup>School of Journalism and Mass Communication, University of Wisconsin Madison, Madison, WI, USA

## ABSTRACT

Gamification is gaining popularity in organisational settings, yet it is unclear if investments in organisational gamification will pay of, given that reports of mixed results are commonplace in the literature. It is important that potential factors behind any mixed results from the initial wave of gamification research be identified and addressed before organisational scholars and practitioners start investing valuable resources into large-scale gamification projects. In this Issues and Opinions paper, we identify and discuss several reasons that may be contributing to the problem of mixed results. We ground our arguments in an umbrella review of the gamification literature. In line with the theme of “Putting more than mere ‘Fun and Games’ into Systems” for this special issue, we propose a framework grounded in Adaptive Structuration Theory and present a set of research questions that can help guide future organisational gamification research. Further, based on the strengths and limitations of our work, we identify several additional avenues to stimulate future research and produce fresh practical insights.

## 1. Introduction

Over the past decade, interest in gamification has surged in all corners of society, from scholars with diverse academic backgrounds to practitioners working in various industries and even to popular media. On the academic side, thousands of articles have been published in peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings, and edited volumes. On the practitioner side, numerous commercial solutions have been developed by various firms, ranging from small businesses specialising in gamification to large enterprise software and service providers. Examples include SAP Cloud Platform Gamification,<sup>1</sup> Microsoft Dynamics 365 Gamification,<sup>2</sup> and Salesforce’s Trailhead<sup>3</sup> training system. Meanwhile, industry and market research firms such as Gartner have reported extensively on gamification trends and future directions. In the broader societal environment, gamification features prominently in popular and social media: in addition to several websites, blogs, and social media accounts dedicated to the topic, many reputed media and journalism outlets have covered gamification (e.g., Manjoo, 2014; Mason, 2018; Mauk, 2013). Although gamification may be applied in many contexts, the focus of this paper lies in the design and use of gamification in organisational settings – what we refer to as organisational gamification (e.g., Cardador et al., 2017; Holzer et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2017; Morschheuser & Hamari, 2018; Silic & Lowry, 2020; Suh et al., 2017; Vesa et al., 2017; Warmelink et al., 2020). In this context, gamification has been defined as “the incorporation of game design elements into a target system while retaining the target system’s instrumental functions” (Liu et al., 2017, p. 1013).

Despite the heightened interest and expectations for gamification, as well as some promising results (e.g., Hamari, 2017; Hamari & Koivisto, 2015; Johnson et al., 2016), the extant gamification literature sufers from a number of conceptual and empirical issues. We believe that organisational gamification research and practice can benefit greatly from examining and learning from these issues before committing sizeable resources to enterprise-level gamification projects. Therefore, in this Issues and Opinions paper, we examine one of these issues in depth, namely, a mix of positive, negative and inconclusive outcomes from gamification endeavours (Hamari et al., 2014; Koivisto & Hamari, 2019; Seaborn & Fels, 2015; Suh et al., 2017; Treiblmaier et al., 2018). We propound four possible reasons as to why this is the case: the dominance of a technological determinism paradigm, an incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS, an inadequate consideration for the context, and overlooking the role of designers. We identified these reasons as a result of our engagement in several gamification research projects (e.g., Liu et. al., 2017; Oppong-Tawiah et al., 2020) as well as from a high-level “umbrella” literature review, that is, a review of existing literature reviews (Paré et al., 2015), that we present. We then draw on Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST: DeSanctis &

Poole, 1994) and the closely related literature on the IT artefact (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018; Markus & Silver, 2008) to propose ways in which gamification research may address these issues moving forward: we do so through the presentation of a framework and related research questions.

Overall, the main contributions of this paper are to forewarn organisational gamification research to some common pitfalls based on the lessons learned from the initial wave of gamification research, and to propose a way to build on the strengths and avoid the weaknesses of this early work. By no means do we claim that ours is the first or the only way to do so. For example, some gamification pioneers have advocated for a focus on motivational afordances of gamified systems rather than on game elements (e.g., Deterding, 2011, 2014) and we welcome the fact that more recent gamification scholarship has begun to heed these calls (e.g., Suh et al., 2017; Van Roy et al., 2019). However, our framework proposes a more comprehensive way of addressing these issues, and it does so by drawing on a well-established theory of our field that scrutinises the impacts of IS in organisational settings. We hope that this opinion piece will inspire a constructive discussion among gamification scholars and contribute to progress of the field.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we provide an overview of the gamification literature that provides evidence for the issues we identify from prior research. We then lay out our proposal for addressing these issues in the form of a framework and a corresponding set of research questions to guide future research. In our last section, we summarise our work, discuss its implications, and highlight additional possibilities to extend this work.

## 2. Overview of gamification research

In this section, we present an overview of the gamification literature with a focus on the problem of mixed results and the four underlying reasons we believe contribute to this problem. This overview is based on an umbrella review (Paré et al., 2015). Umbrella reviews, also called overviews of reviews or metareviews, represent “a tertiary type of study that integrates relevant evidence from multiple systematic reviews (qualitative or quantitative)” (Paré et al., 2015, p. 188). They summarise the overall findings of other literature review papers (e.g., Francke et al., 2008) and allow the findings of separate review papers to be compared and contrasted (Smith et al., 2011). To conduct our review, we followed criteria set for conducting umbrella reviews $( \mathrm { e . g } ,$ Aromataris et al., 2015; Smith et al., 2011). The details of the methodology, findings, and our 55 included review papers are provided in online Appendix A.

The question of whether and how IS investments create value for businesses remains an important question for both researchers and practitioners (Kohli & Devaraj, 2003; Sabherwal & Jeyaraj, 2015). Therefore, it is expected that organisations will ask these same questions before making large-scale investments in gamified IS. Although some gamification researchers argue for the overall success of gamification endeavours in achieving their expected outcomes (e.g., Seaborn & Fels, 2015), our umbrella review reveals that the majority of review papers points to mixed results in empirical research (i.e., 43 of 55 review papers: see Table A.1, column 4). For example, after reviewing 273 empirical gamification articles, Koivisto and Hamari (2019) conclude that “the amount of mixed results is remarkable” (p. 192). By mixed results, they mean findings from studies in which “some efects of the featured gamification experiment were positive, while others showed inconclusive or negative efects” (p. 201). Treiblmaier et al. (2018) highlight the same problem: for example, they point out that, in some cases, gamified e-learning systems that were expected to increase learners’ intrinsic motivation and eventually their performance on exams either did not succeed in doing so or even resulted in converse outcomes. We argue that such mixed results can be attributed to at least four underlying reasons, namely, the dominance of an additive-deterministic paradigm, an incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS, an inadequate consideration for the context, and overlooking the role of designers. We explain each of these next.

## 2.1. Dominance of the additive-deterministic paradigm

Gamification research has been dominated thus far by what Deterding (2014, p. 316) aptly calls an “additivedeterministic paradigm”, that is, the assumption that adding a number of “game elements” (e.g., points, leaderboards and badges: PLBs) to instrumental information systems will inevitably lead to pre-determined desirable outcomes such as increased motivation or performance. While it is dificult to make an unequivocal inference about paradigms, given that it is rare for authors to explicitly identify the paradigm that guides their research, some characteristics of the extant literature lend support to this claim. That is, most of the papers included in our review (50 of 55 papers) demonstrate an overemphasis on game elements (see Table A.1, column 5), which we see as evidence of additive-deterministic thinking. On some occasions, the authors have explicitly highlighted this issue. For example, after reviewing 79 studies, Helmefalk (2019, p. 11) laments that “badges, leaderboards and points are frequently mentioned in the context of gamification, sometimes to the degree of misleading practitioners into believing that simply applying these will guarantee success”. Others have highlighted the prevalence of quantitative methods such as surveys (Alsawaier, 2018) which are more commonplace in typical deterministic studies. We see additive-determinism as a plausible and significant contributing factor to the problem of mixed results because such results have a long history under deterministic assumptions and are not unique to gamification (e.g., DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Robey et al., 2013).

Gamification work has begun to mature in recent years (Nacke & Deterding, 2017): both scholars and practitioners have recognised the shortcomings of the PLB-focused approach and have moved to address them by adopting more nuanced and less deterministic theoretical and design approaches (e.g., Deterding, 2014, 2015; Herger, 2014; Liu et al., 2017; Morschheuser et al., 2018; Suh et al., 2017). For example, recent theoretical and empirical work has moved from a focus on game elements towards afordances (e.g., Deterding, 2014; Suh et al., 2017) which are possibilities provided by technological objects (i.e. game elements) to specific actors within specific contexts (Markus & Silver, 2008; Suh et al., 2017; Volkof & Strong, 2013). We will develop this point further as we present an enhanced conceptualisation of gamified IS in the next section.

## 2.2. Incomplete conceptualisation of gamified iS

A related but distinct reason behind mixed results in gamification research might be an incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS. This is related to the first reason in that it is manifested in a narrow focus on game elements which are the most tangible aspects of a gamified IS, while overlooking the symbolic and abstract layers of such systems including symbolic expressions, afordances, and spirit (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Markus & Silver, 2008). Yet it is distinct from the first: whereas the first reason concerns the relationship between gamified IT artefacts and (organisational) change (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Markus & Robey, 1988; Robey et al., 2013), this second reason concerns the ontology of gamified IT artefacts (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018). As such, a narrow conceptualisation of gamified IS may appear in both deterministic and non-deterministic approaches to gamification. In deterministic studies, the incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS might represent a potential source of unexplained variance in the outcomes. Some preliminary empirical evidence suggests that the often-overlooked abstract layers beyond game elements can explain a significant amount of variance in IS-related outcomes (e.g., Grgecic et al., 2015; Van Roy et al., 2019). Yet our umbrella review revealed a very limited consideration of these layers of gamified IS, in contrast to a strong emphasis on game elements, perhaps best manifested in the prevalence of the Mechanics-Dynamics-Aesthetics (MDA) framework (Hunicke et al., 2004). For example, most of the review papers (50 of 55 papers) report on game elements (see Table A.1, column 6), while no review papers report on more abstract layers of gamified IS such as symbolic expressions or afordances which we see as evidence of incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS. While a few primary studies include afordances (e.g., Suh et al., 2017) and even symbolic expressions (albeit under a diferent name: see functionalisations in Van Roy et al., 2019), we did not identify any reviews focusing on symbolic expressions or spirit of a gamified IS. One literature review (i.e., Matallaoui, 2018) which reported on motivational afordances of gamified IS used the term afordances to refer to game elements such as PLBs. Finally, we note that the incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS is not necessarily limited to deterministic studies. Nondeterministic studies of gamification, such as those proposed in this paper, may overlook the abstract layers of gamified IS as well (e.g., only consider the new organisational structures that emerge from game elements).

## 2.3. Inadequate consideration of contextual factors

We believe that gamification research sufers from inadequate consideration of the context within which a gamified IS is designed and used (Morschheuser et al., 2018; Richards et al., 2014). Context can be defined in various ways; here we adopt Johns (2006, p. 386) definition: “situational opportunities and constraints that afect the occurrence and meaning of organizational behavior as well as functional relationships between variables.” Context may refer to task or social contexts (Addas, 2010). Task contexts include task characteristics, while social contexts cover a range of factors, from the physical conditions of the workplace to national culture and macroeconomic conditions (Bamberger, 2008). Our umbrella review also supports a lack of focus on contextual factors in gamification research: 23 reviews emphasised that gamification outcomes are context dependent (see Table A.1, column 7). For example, Kasurinen and Knutas (2018) argue that the success of a gamified system is “very contextdependent, because of the diferent intrinsic and extrinsic motivators associated to the diferent activities” (p. 39). In another example, Seaborn and Fels (2015) argue that gamification outcomes are very context-specific and therefore similar gamified IS do not necessarily produce the same results in diferent contexts. Koivisto and Hamari (2019) also conclude that the role of context has been largely overlooked in gamification and needs to be incorporated in future research.

Prior work in both IS and human-computer interaction (e.g., Addas, 2010), as well as organisation studies (e.g., Bamberger, 2008; Johns, 2006) has called for more engagement in context. Organisational researchers have argued that contextual variations are “likely responsible for one of the most vexing problems in the field: study-to-study variation in research findings” (Johns, 2006, p. 389). The efects of context gain more prominence as gamification research, thus far limited primarily to the education and health domains with an individual-level focus (see Table A.1, columns 9 & 10), enters organisational contexts (Colbert et al., 2016; Herger, 2014; Suh et al., 2017; Treiblmaier et al., 2018): it is not clear whether the findings from these former contexts can be generalised to the latter (Te’eni, 2016).

## 2.4. Overlooking the role of designers

Finally, we argue that the role designers can play in the eventual outcomes of gamified IS has been generally overlooked. This is particularly salient to gamified IS, because they are considered a subset of persuasive systems (Deterding, 2014), which are systems designed with the explicit goal of influencing attitudes, behaviours, and other outcomes (Lockton et al., 2010). For such systems, an important consideration is to identify the designers and the intentions behind their design choices, more formally referred to as their intents (Lockton et al., 2010; Oinas-Kukkonen & Harjumaa, 2009), as these intents guide designers choices in the components they incorporate into an IS and eventually influence the outcomes of the system. In addition, just as users, designers are embedded within a context, and this context shapes their intents and design choices (Leonardi, 2009).

Another consideration regarding designers is that they wield some influence in the eventual outcomes of gamification, through analysing users and their contexts, monitoring their use, and adjusting and improving the gamified IS (Deterding, 2015; Morschheuser et al., 2018). From this perspective, gamification involves “second-order design”, an inherently unpredictable and iterative process in which designers “cannot directly design player behavior, so they do it indirectly through the design of the rules of the game” (Van Bree, 2014, p. 89). This becomes increasingly more feasible and important with the rising popularity of iterative design methodologies such as agile (Cram & Newell, 2016; Morschheuser et al., 2018), as these methods allow for rapid and successive analysis, design, and deployment of prototypes in users’ contexts, monitoring use in real-time, collecting users’ feedback, and making adjustments when necessary (e.g., Oppong-Tawiah et al., 2020; Silic & Lowry, 2020).

Although designers are important in the gamification process, our umbrella review demonstrates little research focusing on the role of designers. Of the 55 reviewed articles, 48 do not explicitly address designers’ roles (see Table A.1, column 8). The remaining articles mainly view the role of designers as entities who execute a design framework such as MDA: for example, Bui et al. (2015) considered the role of designers as manipulating game elements to create challenges for users. One article highlighted the role of designers in understanding gamification contexts (Helmefalk, 2019); however, no review papers report on theoretical frameworks that explicitly takes the role of gamified IS designers into account.

In summary, we suggest that future research can help avoid the problem of inconsistent findings in the gamification literature by addressing the paradigmatic, conceptual, contextual, and design issues that underlie and fuel this problem. Further, we believe that IS is well-suited to guide this emerging scholarship, given our field’s depth in studying the design, implementation, use, and outcomes of IS in organisational settings. To illustrate this, we draw on one of IS’ wellestablished theories, namely AST, to develop a framework and research questions to help guide future research. ## 3. Moving forward: an agenda for organisational gamification

In this section we develop an agenda for future gamification studies by drawing on and extending adaptive structuration theory (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) as our underlying theoretical lens. AST seeks to explain how social structures of technology are mutually constituted, changed, and maintained through the appropriation of advanced information technologies (i.e., IT artefacts or information systems) by their users within a given context (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Social structures refer to rules and resources that lay the foundations and provide a template for human thoughts and actions. AST posits that modern information systems “bring social structures which enable and constrain interaction to the workplace” (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994, p. 125). These structures may complement, contradict, or substitute the structures provided by non-IS sources such as organisational hierarchies, knowledge, standards, and routines, as well as industry and societal norms.

AST is particularly appropriate for our purpose because it simultaneously addresses the proposed reasons for mixed efects, specifically additivedeterministic approaches, the incomplete conceptualisation of gamified IS, the inadequate consideration of context, and the assumption about the faithful enactment of designers’ intents by users. Relating to the first reason, AST represents the middle ground between deterministic views that emphasise the causal efects of (gamified) information systems and constructivist or institutional views that tend to downplay these efects in favour of human agency and social structures (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Markus & Silver, 2008).

That is, AST preserves “the predictive potential of a deterministic perspective, while accounting for interpretive flexibility of the process perspective” (Bostrom et al., 2009, p. 24). Concerning the second reason, recent extensions of AST’s notion of advanced information technologies allow us to provide a more complete conceptualisation that goes beyond game elements by including the symbolic and abstract layers of gamified IS (e.g., Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018; Markus & Silver, 2008). AST addresses the third reason by considering how IS interact with the individual and organisational environments of their users as additional sources of structures. For the last reason, AST considers how designers’ intents are “embedded” in IS and could be realised in diferent ways through users’ appropriation of IS. We extend AST with regard to this issue by presenting a dynamic account of how designers’ intents and interactions with users influence the adaptive structuration process in users’ contexts. This extension is consistent with prior utilisation of AST in IS research as a flexible meta-theory (Bostrom et al., 2009) that can be adopted and applied to diferent contexts and levels of analysis (e.g., Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018; Hardin et al., 2014; Peng & Guo, 2019; Romanow et al., 2018; Schmitz et al., 2016).

Given the central role of the gamified IS in the entire adaptive structuration process, we begin our analysis by taking a fresh look at various layers of gamified IS and their potential as sources of structure within organisations. We then proceed to discuss the emergence of new organisational structures as a result of gamified IS, taking into account the influence of contextual factors and the role of designers. These are summarised in our framework diagrammed in Figure 1 which we develop and discuss in the following sections.<sup>4</sup>

## 3.1. An enhanced conceptualisation of gamified iS

To extend past conceptualisations of gamified IS, we draw on AST and its extensions. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) originally proposed that the potential of IS to act as sources of organisational structure is manifested in their features (the system’s rules, resources, and capabilities) and spirit (the intent underlying the features). More recent work has refined and developed this conceptualisation further, with notable examples being that of Markus and Silver (2008) and Cheikh-Ammar et al. (2018). We draw on the latter to explain how gamified IS, comprised of game elements (i.e., AST’s features), symbolic expressions, afordances, and spirit, act as sources of organisational structures.<sup>5</sup> However, our conceptualisation extends Cheikh-Ammar et al. (2018) in two ways. First, he focuses primarily on the ontology of the IT artefact rather than on the adaptive structuration process.<sup>6</sup> Therefore, we integrate his work with DeSanctis and Poole (1994) to devise research questions from an AST perspective. Explicit theorising of the role of designers in the adaptive structuration process represents our second point of departure from his more usercentric view. We begin by examining the bottom layer of gamified IS, their elements.

## 3.1.1. Game elements

The most concrete component of an information system is called IT features (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018) or technical objects (Markus & Silver, 2008), defined as “building blocks or components of the technology” (Jasperson et al., 2005, p. 529). Similarly, in the gamification literature, gamification objects or game elements (diagrammed as the bottom layer of the gamified IS in Figure 1) are defined as “the basic building blocks of a gamified system” (Liu et al., 2017, p. 1013). Given their central position within the additive-deterministic paradigm (Deterding, 2014) and their roots in digital game studies, game elements have received considerable research attention. Several classifications of game elements exist (e.g., Bui et al., 2015; Ralph & Monu, 2015; Thiebes et al., 2014), many as modifications or extensions of the MDA framework (Hunicke et al., 2004).

Consistent with our theoretical lens, our interest lies in how such elements may act as sources of organisational structures. AST posits that IS features provide organisational structures in terms of both resources (e.g., data or data storage capacity) and the rules that govern the allocation and manipulation of these resources by users, and that such structures encourage diferent forms of interactions in organisations. Similarly, it can be argued that game elements provide both resources and rules, in the form of game objects (e.g., points, leaderboards, badges, rewards, etc.) and mechanics, which are rules on how these objects might be allocated and manipulated (Liu et al., 2017). In fact, some game mechanics such as rules governing player levels, privileges and limitations of each level and how players might be promoted to a higher level (e.g., by accumulating a certain number of points or badges) or demoted to a lower one (e.g., by ranking among the bottom 10% on a leaderboard) provide readily recognisable structures resembling organisational hierarchies and promotion rules. Therefore, the introduction of such structures through gamified IS into an organisational environment may encourage new or diferent forms of interactions among its members (e.g., a member ranking high on a leaderboard may expect more respect or wield more power compared to those at the bottom). Further, the types of interactions encouraged by IS features are contingent upon their specific design and arrangement in terms of restrictiveness, sophistication, and comprehensiveness or being core or optional features (DeSanctis et al., 1994). Similarly, it can be argued that the types of interactions encouraged by gamified IS will vary based on the bundle of game objects and mechanics incorporated in such a system. Therefore, an organisational researcher examining a specific gamified IS from an AST perspective should ask:

## RQ1: What (new) forms of interactions are encouraged by game elements incorporated into a gamified IS?

Game elements as objective and concrete IS features provide a solid foundation for examining the structures that can emerge as a result of introducing gamified IS into an organisational setting. However, the AST logic underlying RQ1 remains controversial, as some view the notion of structures being embedded within IT features as bordering on technological determinism (e.g., Jones & Karsten, 2008; Markus & Silver, 2008). Instead, they propose that it is more beneficial to examine how symbolic expressions and functional afordances of technical objects can give rise to new social structures (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018; Markus & Silver, 2008). Contrary to game elements, symbolic expressions and afordances are relational (Robey et al., 2013) and context-bound by definition (Markus & Silver, 2008), meaning that their structural possibilities can only be defined, activated and studied as a matter of the relationship between IS features, human agency, and the context within which these are embedded. Therefore, studying these additional layers provides an opportunity for gamification scholars to move beyond additivedeterminism and to paint a more complete picture of a gamified IS, grounded in their (organisational) context.

## 3.1.2. Symbolic expressions

Citing a number of conceptual and practical concerns with AST’s notion of spirit, Markus and Silver replaced spirit with symbolic expressions, defined as “communicative possibilities of a technical object for a specified user group” (2008, p. 623). However, Cheikh-Ammar et al. (2018) noted that this conceptualisation of symbolic expressions conflates two distinct types of communicative possibilities: one that conveys the functionalities of individual elements of an IS or a bundle of such elements, and one that communicates the values promoted or epitomised by the IS holistically. Therefore, we adopt his revised definition of symbolic expressions as “the possibilities of an IT artifact or of IT features to communicate impressions of functionality to a specific user group” (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018, p. 280). In other words, symbolic expressions can act as signifiers (Norman, 2008, 2013), indicating what potentials for actions (i.e., afordances, explained in the next section) are supported by IS features and how users might actualise these (see Figure 1). Note that symbolic expressions are potentials for communication that may or may not be perceived and actualised by a specific individual or group. For example, to those with a basic knowledge of computing, a small floppy disk icon embedded on a graphical user interface immediately communicates the possibility of saving one’s work within a software application. However, it may be meaningless to anyone new to using computers, and even younger techsavvy users might not know why this specific icon was selected to signify saving work, owing to the obsolescence of floppy disks.

Little research in either gamification or IS in general exists on symbolic expressions (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018). Yet there is some empirical evidence that symbolic expressions explain variance in outcomes such as object-based beliefs (e.g., perceptions of system and information quality) (Grgecic et al., 2015). Similarly, recent gamification work has begun to examine the design and impact of symbolic expressions of game elements. For instance, Matallaoui (2018) found that badges utilising symbolism related to the task for which they are awarded are more readily perceived by users compared to those using nonrelated symbolism. Van Roy et al. (2019) enumerated nine diferent “functionalisations” of badges for students enrolled in online courses by deriving functionalisation from the notion of “functional significance”, a core construct in self-determination theory (SDT: Deci & Ryan, 1985; Ryan & Deci, 2000). According to SDT, “events such as rewards, evaluations, or feedback have a particular meaning or functional significance that predicts the impact of these events on intrinsic motivation” (Ryan & Deci, 2017, p. 123).

Again, our concern here is how symbolic expressions can act as sources of structures and influence interactions in organisational settings. Jones and Karsten (2008) provide the example of a “white coat” as a signifier of medical expertise and the types of interactions expected or allowed by the person wearing it. Similarly, we argue that, in their role as signifiers of functionality for game elements, symbolic expressions can convey and reinforce the type of interactions encouraged by these elements. For example, a badge or an avatar that uses appropriate symbolism to clearly signify expertise or rank in a specific organisational task, process, or domain may better aford its holders a high status in formal or informal organisational hierarchies and social networks, and signal the types of interactions expected or allowed. Finally, the ability of symbolic expressions to convey or reinforce structures depends on their recognition by a collective in addition to individual members: “Things can only function as signs for members of shared culture or language communities” (Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 622). As such, symbolic expressions are contextualised by definition, as their communicative potential varies from context to context. Therefore, we propose the following question:

RQ2: What (new) forms of interactions are encouraged by the symbolic expressions of game elements incorporated into a gamified IS?

## 3.1.3. Afordances

Afordances comprise the next layer of a gamified IS. Markus and Silver (2008) introduced the notion of functional afordances in part to address the technological determinism concern with IS features as outlined earlier, and in part to address the problem of repeating decomposition of IS features. The problem, in DeSanctis and Poole (1994, p. 124) words, is that “there are features within features (e.g., options within software options) . . . So how far must the analysis go to bring consistent, meaningful results?”. Markus and Silver break down IS features into technical objects (e.g., game elements) and functional afordances, defined as “the possibilities for goal-oriented action aforded to specified user groups by technical objects” (2008, p. 622). They argue that, because afordances are context-bound by definition, they delimit the number and depth of features that need to be examined to those that are meaningful in a specific context.

Afordances provide a powerful lens to study information systems, because of their ambivalent, emergent, and context-dependent nature (Majchrzak et al., 2016). A burgeoning stream of research has emerged around this concept in IS and gamification research. For example, to address what he saw as the lack of theoretical perspectives that can explain the motivational efects of game elements, Deterding (2011, p. 3) proposed the notion of situated motivational afordances, defined as “the opportunities to satisfy motivational needs provided by the relation between the features of an artifact and the abilities of a subject in a given situation”. Suh et al. (2017) identified four specific afordances (rewards, status, competition, and self-expression) for gamified IS and empirically tested their impacts on users’ flow and aesthetic experiences.

How do afordances contribute to the production of organisational structures? Building on our previous example of player levels as game mechanics that have the potential to produce new organisational hierarchies, here we argue that organisational structures can arise only if a group or organisational unit perceives this potential and actualises it. In contrast, if these actors ignore or resist actualising this potential for any reason, it is unlikely that new structures arise just because a set of mechanics (e.g., player levels) have been incorporated into a gamified organisational IS.

This example also highlights another issue that is particularly salient to organisational contexts: that in order for afordances to produce structures in organisations, it is important that a majority of group or organisation members perceive and actualise afordances in the same way, resulting in shared afordances (Leonardi, 2013). In other words, points, leaderboards, badges or levels may aford status and recognition to employees based on their individual diferences and goals, but these individual afordances are unlikely to give rise to new organisational hierarchies if the majority of their colleagues or managers fail to recognise this. Therefore, organisational gamification research should examine:

RQ3: What (new) forms of interactions are encouraged by the afordances of game elements incorporated into a gamified IS?

## 3.1.4. Spirit: overarching goals and values

In addition to their features, IS provide structure through their spirit, defined as “the general intent with regard to values and goals underlying a given set of structural features” (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994, p. 126). To uncover the spirit of an IS, researchers must examine it as a “text”, guided by questions like “What kind of goals are being promoted by technology?” and “What kind of values are being supported?” (1994, p. 127). For example, the spirit of an IS might be deduced from a focus on eficiency (a goal) or by its support for equal participation of all stakeholders (a value). We will provide more gamification-specific examples of goals and values in the following discussion.

As explained earlier, Markus and Silver (2008) raised several conceptual and practical issues with the notion of spirit and replaced it with symbolic expressions. However, Cheikh-Ammar et al. (2018) makes an argument for the reintroduction of spirit and its conceptual distinction from symbolic expressions. He defines the spirit of an IS as “incorporeal essence of an IT artifact” and “an integrated expression of overarching values” that is revealed over time as users appropriate the artefact (2018, p. 287). We accept his conceptualisation of spirit but extend it in two ways. First, we highlight the role of both designers and users in the emergence of the spirit of IS, as originally stipulated by DeSanctis and Poole (1994). Second, we follow their logic in extending Cheikh-Ammar et al.’s (2018) conceptualisation to include goals in addition to values. This is in line with the representation of gamified IS in the extant literature as dual-purpose systems (Wu & Lu, 2013), designed to fulfil experiential as well as instrumental goals (Deterding, 2015; Liu et al., 2017). In fact, gamification scholars have argued that the use of goals is “a key component of most gamification interventions” (Landers et al., 2017, p. 508). Therefore, we define the spirit of an IS as an integrated expression of overarching goals and values promoted by an IS to a specific group of users. This definition represents our view that the spirit is context specific as it varies from one user group to the other, while at the same time it allows for the IS to promote the intended goals and values of its designers. Consistent with this definition, spirit in our framework is represented by the top layer in Figure 1. In the following, we explain how the spirit of gamified IS, manifested in the overarching goals and values promoted by a gamified IS, may provide organisational structures that encourage new interactions in organisations.

A large body of literature in disciplines such as organisational behaviour examines how goals influence human interactions. Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham, 1990) represents one of the most studied and supported areas of this literature (Robbins & Judge, 2017). Goal setting theory states that specific dificult goals, when committed to by an individual, group, or organisation, result in higher levels of motivation and eventually task performance, contingent on various contextual factors (Locke & Latham, 2006). Initial studies of goal setting theory in gamification have found similar results (e.g., Hamari, 2013; Landers et al., 2017).

Consistent with the definition of spirit within AST, our interest lies in the structuration efects of overarching goals promoted by a gamified IS. We suggest that a gamified IS that consistently rewards and punishes employees or motivates them in other ways, based on achieving a specified set of goals (e.g., hitting a quantity, quality or learning target), will encourage certain interactions favourable to achieving those goals. For example, employees may start to compete (or collaborate) on achieving a top spot on a leaderboard that ranks them individually or as a team based on eficiency metrics. Those with the knowledge and skills conducive to achieve such gaols might occupy central positions in informal organisational hierarchies and social networks, and might demand higher compensation for their work. Such interactions might over time give rise to new organisational structures.

We qualify this discussion by pointing out several conditions under which setting goals through gamified IS might not inspire the desired interactions and structures. First, DeSanctis and Poole (1994) caution us to the possibility of an incoherent spirit, where diferent components of information systems promote inconsistent or conflicting goals. This is of particular concern to gamified IS, which promote two diferent sets of goals (experiential and instrumental) by design (Liu et al., 2017). If experiential goals such as motivation, engagement, and flow are not aligned with instrumental ones such as performance, attitudes, and behaviour change (Seaborn & Fels, 2015), the gamified IS could fail to direct interactions in a definite direction or produce any stable structures. Second, similar to afordances and symbolic expressions, the structuration possibilities of goals are context sensitive. For example, prior research has found that moderate goals can be more motivating than dificult goals in collectivist and high-power distance cultures and assigned (as opposed to self-set) goals inspire more goal commitment in high-power distance cultures (Robbins & Judge, 2017).

Similar arguments can be made for values, which are fundamental beliefs about what goals or behaviours are personally or socially desirable (Rokeach,

1973). Even though values are more abstract compared to goals and do not consistently predict behaviour (Ruepert et al., 2015), they provide a stable system which influences individuals, groups, organisations and societies’ judgements, decision making, and behaviours (Robbins & Judge, 2017). Gamified IS may promote specific value systems. For example, it has been argued that game elements that aford and signify individual distinctions and accomplishments (e.g., badges) promote values that are rooted in individualistic cultures (Khaled, 2015). Gamification has been fiercely criticised as “exploitationware” (I. Bogost, 2015; Rey, 2015), that is, as a means to unfairly exploit the workforce, and alternately, as a means to achieve eudaimonia, or the good life (Deterding, 2014). By promoting these values, gamified IS are likely to encourage new and diferent forms of interactions and eventually structures. The perception and the influence of the values promoted by an IS are contextbound (Cheikh-Ammar et al., 2018). For example, the individualistic connotation of badges may become more visible and problematic in collectivist and egalitarian cultures. Therefore, we pose the question:

RQ4: What (new) forms of interactions are encouraged by the spirit of gamified IS, manifested in the overarching goals and values that its game elements promote?

## 3.2. Other sources of structure

In addition to gamified IS, the content and characteristics of tasks as well as organisational and social contexts may act as sources of structure, substituting, complementing, or contradicting the gamified IS in shaping user interactions (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). In the gamification literature, some researchers have contemplated how gamified IS might interact with task and organisational contexts. For example, Liu et al.’s (2017, p. 1019) gamification principle A states that “To be efective, gamification design elements must be congruent with the target task.” From an AST perspective, this means that any structure provided by gamified IS, and the types of interactions they encourage, should align with the types of rules and resources imposed by and required to accomplish the relevant task(s). Therefore, a gamified IS that afords and signifies competition where collaboration is more appropriate to accomplish the task provides a conflicting structure and encourages interactions that are misaligned with accomplishing that task. Whether this results in the abandonment and/or modification of the gamified IS structure or the adaptation of the task to align with the new structure depends on various contingencies (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994) and needs to be examined in its context. Therefore, researchers should consider:

RQ5: How do gamified IS interact with relevant tasks as complementary or competing sources of structure?

Additional structures might also be present in users organisational and social contexts. Diferent organisational and social contexts impose diferent forms of structures and interactions. We noted earlier that not all national cultures may be compatible with the individualistic culture within which gamification was first conceived. Parallels with diferent organisational cultures also exist. Of the four ideal organisational culture types of hierarchy, market, adhocracy and clan (Cameron & Quinn, 2011), the first two types might seamlessly integrate with the structure provided by a gamified IS that classifies employees into various player levels based on their performance, while the other two types might find it dificult to work within such a structure. Again, the question of whether these sources of structure complement or contradict each other and the types of structures and interactions that eventually emerge need to be examined by gamification scholars:

RQ6: How do gamified IS interact with users’ organisational and social contexts as complementary or competing sources of structure?

## 3.3. Appropriation of gamified iS structures

Users put the gamified IS to use through interactions with the technology, with other users and their contexts, and in so doing they enact the structures these systems provide. However, users often do not utilise an IS exactly as foreseen and presented to them by its designers. Rather, users’ encounters with IS trigger sensemaking and interpretations, and consequently users construct their own technology-in-use, that is, they incorporate the IS into existing work practices, bounded by the capabilities and constraints of IS as well as their organisational and social contexts (Grifith, 1999; Orlikowski, 1992). This is called the appropriation of structures provided by (gamified) IS (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Consistent with our enhanced conceptualisation, we extend the appropriation of structures to those provided by symbolic expressions, afordances, and the spirit of gamified IS.

Appropriation of structures happens through a series of appropriation moves, which are various ways in which users shape their own technology-inuse (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Revisiting our example of an IS that utilises leaderboards to rank employee performance, let us envision the following scenario. Imagine a set of employees who might be familiar with the concept of leaderboards from digital games or sports competitions (i.e., they understand the symbolic expressions of leaderboards), to whom leader boards aford achieving their personal or group goals (such as being recognised for hard work and receiving a promotion), and for whom the perceived spirit of gamified IS aligns with their personal goals and values (e.g., they do not perceive the spirit as an electronic stick because they value autonomy). These employees might readily accept the leaderboard’s rankings as a new form of organisational structure and enact it in their interactions (e.g., demand higher pay or status if they are at the top of the ranking or compete to achieve the top spot). Poole and DeSanctis (1992) call this scenario a direct appropriation. If one or more of these conditions is not true, employees might instead opt for some form of negation (e.g., refusing to recognise the leaderboard ranking outright), or for a combination of the leaderboard ranking with another source of structure (e.g., an existing organisational hierarchy), or for substitution of the leaderboard with an existing or new source of structure. In fact, Poole and DeSanctis (1992) identify 31 such appropriation moves for GDSS. We are not aware of any parallels for gamified IS. As explained in the following paragraphs, AST anticipates that the emergence of outcomes and structures vary by the type of appropriation moves; therefore, it is important that gamification scholars examine:

RQ7: What types of moves can be identified as users appropriate the various layers of gamified IS?

## 3.4. The outcomes of appropriation

Variations on appropriation moves result in diferent degrees of appropriation, which directly impact the outcomes of the appropriation and the overall structuration process (Bostrom et al., 2009). The degree of appropriation may be manifested in several dimensions, including faithfulness of appropriation, consensus on appropriation, and attitudes towards appropriation (Bostrom et al., 2009; Romanow et al., 2018). A faithful appropriation is one that matches the spirit of a gamified IS as intended by its designers (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). A gamified IS intended to promote a spirit of collaboration within an organisational unit but instead encouraging competition among individuals or groups is not faithfully appropriated.

Consensus on appropriation represents another dimension of the level of appropriation. It refers to the extent to which the members of a group or organisation agree on their interpretation of the symbolic expressions, actualisations of the afordances, and perceptions of the spirit of gamified IS. Whether the appropriation is faithful or not, it is also important to examine whether users’ appropriations coalesce into a recognisable pattern, in the form of a common understanding of the symbolic expressions, enactment of collective and shared afordances (Leonardi, 2013), and a unified spirit.

Finally, attitudes represent evaluative statements (Robbins & Judge, 2017) elicited by the appropriation of structures provided by gamified IS. DeSanctis and Poole (1994) suggest comfort, respect and challenge as the three main attitudes identified in the prior literature. Others (e.g., Bostrom et al., 2009) have adopted a more general valence-based classification (positive vs. negative attitudes). We have already noted that the concept of gamification has elicited fierce criticism, with one opponent famously branding it as “bullshit” and “exploitationware” (I. Bogost, 2011a, 2011b, 2015). In contrast, others have advocated for gamification’s potential to improve human welfare (e.g., Deterding, 2014). While not everyone might express such strong attitudes towards gamified IS, it can be expected that organisational members develop positive or negative attitudes of varying degrees towards such systems, dependent upon their individual and social contexts.

As noted above, the combination of these dimensions (i.e., faithfulness of appropriation, consensus on appropriation, and attitudes towards appropriation) helps determine the degree of appropriation, which in turn influences the outcomes of appropriation. DeSanctis and Poole identified three such outcomes: decision outcomes and (new) sources and types of structures. Consistent with their focus on GDSS, decision outcomes referred to the instrumental goals expected from such systems, such as increased decision quality and eficiency. For gamified IS, these goals will translate to the instrumental outcomes salient to the system under study and pursued by system designers (as many types of IS can be gamified, including but not limited to GDSS). However, gamified IS have the added complexity of experiential goals such as immersion and enjoyment, which are usually expected to advance the instrumental goals (Liu et al., 2017). AST predicts that an ideal level of appropriation, manifested in faithfulness, consensus and positive attitudes, will result in the realisation of the outcomes desired by system designers (Bostrom et al., 2009; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Romanow et al., 2018). Yet as we noted earlier, this may be viewed as a deterministic position (Jones & Karsten, 2008), and given our arguments concerning additive-determinism, some might question these conclusions. Therefore, we see this as an open question that needs to be empirically examined:

RQ8: What is the relationship between the degree of appropriation and the instrumental and experiential outcomes of gamified IS?

In addition to instrumental and experiential outcomes, AST proposes the emergence of new sources of organisational structures as well as new organisational structures (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). New sources of organisational structures refer to outputs of IS use that constitute new rules and resources, and therefore qualify as sources of structure in addition to the structures provided by the IS itself or its context. For an example, DeSanctis and Poole suggest “after the group enters data into the GDSS, the information generated by the system becomes another source of social structures” (1994, p. 128). For gamified IS, we might consider the rankings on a leaderboard an example of a new source of structure, as it only emerges after the appropriation and use of a gamified IS.

Sources of structure, whether already present in the gamified IS, its context, or emerging as a result of its appropriation and use, are expected to produce the final category of outcomes, namely, new organisational structures. Earlier we presented the example of new organisational hierarchies (a structure) based on diferent player levels in a gamified IS. Another form of such new structures could be a new employee remuneration system based on the outputs of a gamified IS (e.g., rankings on a leaderboard). The relation between (sources of) structures and appropriation is reciprocal: structures are enacted and institutionalised through appropriation over time, and once institutionalised, they influence future user interactions, and consequently, their appropriation of gamified IS.

Similar to the realisation of outcomes, the degree of appropriation influences the emergence of new structures. While faithfulness of appropriation and attitudes are important, we particularly highlight the role of consensus on appropriation. Individuals or small groups of organisational members might form nascent structures through their interactions with gamified IS and with each other; however, such structures are expected to be transient and unstable until they are enacted by a majority of (or by powerful) organisational members (Schmitz et al., 2016). It is only through shared interpretations of symbolic expressions, actualisation of afordances, and perceptions of spirit that these structures are (re)produced over time. Therefore, we suggest the following research question:

RQ9: What is the relationship between the degree of appropriation and the emergence of new organisational structures?

## 3.5. The role of designers in the adaptive structuration of gamified iS

In developing AST, DeSanctis and Poole (1994) noted the influence of designers in shaping the IS and the types of organisational structures these systems provide. However, this influence is not explicitly formulated in AST propositions. In this regard, Leonardi (2009) made an interesting observation about some of the most important theoretical perspectives used as alternatives to technological determinism. He noted that these theories either seek to explain technological change by theorising the development phase of a new technology (such as gamified IS) and stop at the point where it is ready for implementation (e.g., actornetwork theory), or seek to explain organisational change by theorising the use of technology after it has been implemented in an environment (e.g., AST). This creates an unnecessary “implementation line” that blinds these theories to “the important role that organizations play in the development of technologies, and that the material features of technologies play in the process of organizing” (Leonardi, 2009, p. 278). More important to our discussion, this divide also makes the role of designers of less concern for AST and other theories that seek to explain organisational change after IS implementations. However, recent empirical studies have demonstrated that IS designers “play a limited yet clearly consequential role” in the shaping of new organisational structures in the user environment (Holeman & Barrett, 2017, p. 902). Therefore, in this section, we examine the role designers and their organisational and social contexts play in the process and outcomes of adaptive structuration.

First, we examine the role of intent with which a gamified IS is designed. Designers’ intents are discussed to some degree in the development of AST (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994), even though they are not explicitly reflected in its propositions. We argue that examining this intent is particularly salient because, as explained earlier, as persuasive systems, gamified IS are usually designed with the explicit intent of encouraging a specific or desired set of interactions which, as discussed earlier, could eventually lead to the (re)production of organisational structures and influence the instrumental and experiential outcomes of gamified IS. However, designers’ intents cannot be studied in a vacuum, as these are shaped by their organisational and social contexts (Leidner & Kayworth, 2006). In other words, “[d]evelopers of a new technology are enabled and constrained by the institutional properties of their work . . . and they often inscribe those properties into the artifacts themselves” (Leonardi, 2009, p. 295). These artefacts then act as (new) sources of organisational structures and influence user interactions. Therefore, questions of interest to organisational gamification researchers are:

RQ10: How do designers’ intents shape the gamified IS as a source of structure within users’ organisational contexts?

## RQ11: How do designers’ organisational and social contexts influence their intents?

Second, as suggested above, AST-based models like our framework need to move from a static view of designers’ intents and users’ appropriations separated by the implementation line to a dynamic one that considers how designers and users negotiate these intents and appropriations over time (Leonardi, 2009), and how this influences the adaptive structuration process. This dynamic view becomes more important because of the increasing popularity of iterative design methodologies such as agile, which involve close collaboration between designers and users (Cram & Newell, 2016). These methods promote and facilitate rapid analysis, design, feedback and adjustment of prototypes through close interactions between designers and users (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2004; Conboy, 2009). Under these methods, negotiation of design intents and choices of elements, their symbolic expressions, afordances, and the spirit of IS take place continuously and frequently, for example, through daily stand-up meetings (Cram & Newell, 2016). Designers observe some of the initial appropriation moves and their outcomes as these unfold in users’ environments and incorporate necessary adjustments in their designs. A recent comprehensive study made similar observations and recommendations for the design of gamified IS (Morschheuser et al., 2018). Therefore, we propose this final research question:

RQ12: How do recurring analysis-design-feedback loops dynamically shape the gamified IS as a source of structure in users’ organisational contexts?

Our framework depicts how the various layers of gamified IS (individually and as a whole) encourage or discourage new or existing forms of interactions. These interactions over time influence how users appropriate the gamified IS within their social and organisational contexts and the (un)expected organisational outcomes and structures that may emerge as a result of this process. These new structures then reciprocally influence future appropriations and interactions. The framework further depicts how designers influence the adaptive structuration of gamified IS in users’ organisations by infusing their intents into the design of gamified IS guided through iterative analysis-designfeedback loops.

## 4. Implications and conclusions

Motivated by a considerable amount of mixed empirical results (Koivisto & Hamari, 2019) and the ongoing debate concerning gamification’s efectiveness in different contexts (Nacke & Deterding, 2017), this Issues & Opinions paper developed an agenda to help guide future gamification research. Supported with evidence from an umbrella review of the literature, this agenda should help future researchers avoid four major reasons underlying the mixed empirical findings.

Drawing on and extending AST, our framework explicates how new forms of interactions can emerge as a result of introducing a gamified IS into an organisational context (RQ1-RQ4). It demonstrates how new structures emerging from the introduction of a gamified IS may interact with other sources of structures such as relevant tasks and organisational and social contexts (RQ5-RQ6). It proposes that the introduction of a gamified IS can trigger a series of appropriation moves that unfold through interactions between users and a gamified IS over time (RQ7). It suggests a plausible link between the degree of appropriation of a gamified IS and experiential and instrumental outcomes of appropriation (RQ8). It also emphasises that experiential and instrumental outcomes are not the only outcomes that emerge as a result of appropriation of a gamified IS: new forms of organisational structures might be (re)produced as a result (RQ9). Lastly, it directs the attention of future researchers to the role of designers and design processes in the adaptive structuration of a gamified IS within an organisational context (RQ10-RQ12). We hope that future research will examine and conceptually refine our framework and create a set of finetuned, empirically testable hypotheses.

Our framework comes with a number of strengths and limitations that provide additional avenues for future research. First, this framework can help inspire a paradigmatic shift advocated by gamification scholars (e.g., Deterding, 2014), which in turn can stimulate new research and insights through the problematisation of extant research (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). An important strength of our framework is its balance between additive-determinism on the one side and constructivism on the other. Although it has been suggested that pure technological determinism may be too simplistic given the current state of our field’s cumulative knowledge (Leonardi, 2009), studying the causal efects of technology remains relevant to IS and supported by real-world observations (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Leonardi, 2012; Markus & Silver, 2008). AST as a form of “softline determinism” (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Markus & Silver, 2008) helps address these concerns; however, there are many other ways of achieving this balance, particularly in light of some critiques of AST (see debate in Jones & Karsten, 2008). Prior work (e.g., DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Leonardi, 2009) has suggested several such theoretical perspectives, including actor-network theory (Callon, 1984; Latour, 1987), the duality of technology (Orlikowski, 1992), sociotechnical systems theory (Bostrom & Heinen, 1977) and more recently, sociomateriality (e.g., Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Leonardi, 2012). Adopting these perspectives in future organisational gamification research could provide novel insights to propel the field forward.

Second, in developing our framework, we adopted Cheikh-Ammar et al.’s (2018) conceptualisation of IS, the most recent development in a stream of research tracing its lineage to AST. This conceptualisation opens up possibilities for future gamification research beyond our work. For example, conceptualising the spirit of gamified IS as the expression of overarching values and goals they promote would allow for critical studies of gamified IS (e.g., Cukier et al., 2009; Lin et al., 2015; Mingers, 1980; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). Such studies would be of interest to both gamification researchers and practitioners, as they would allow for further problematisation and generation of new research questions (Myers & Klein, 2011) and help practitioners to understand and mitigate some of the ethical and social concerns with gamified IS such as labour exploitation (Kim, 2018; Kim & Werbach, 2016). Similarly, other layers of gamified IS represent interesting opportunities for future research: we have already noted a burgeoning stream of research around afordances (Majchrzak et al., 2016; Volkof & Strong, 2013) and there have been calls for examining the symbolic and semiotic aspects of information systems (e.g., Aakhus et al., 2014; Mingers & Willcocks, 2017).

Considering alternative perspectives in conceptualising gamified IS might provide other fruitful research directions. Specifically, recent developments in the ontology of digital artefacts display some interesting parallels to our arguments. For example, digital artefacts are characterised as having a layered architecture (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Yoo et al., 2010) shaped into a meaningfully arranged array of signs and symbols known as form or structure. Forms in turn enable or constrain various functions, which are “the use that members of some community impose on that object in pursuit of their practical interests” (Faulkner & Runde, 2013, p. 807). Digital objects are also generative (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2012), meaning that they are malleable and can be used in ways not anticipated by their designers. Such characteristics are expected to have important implications for organisational studies (Yoo et al., 2012), providing another fertile ground for future gamification research.

A third area for future research lies in bridging the gap between design and use of gamified IS in organisational contexts. The processes of design and organising are intertwined and mutually influence each other (Leonardi, 2009), particularly within modern design methodologies. The formation of any new organisational structures as well as their causal (i.e. experiential and instrumental) outcomes are guided by both designers’ intents and users’ appropriations. Further, the role of designers could be examined in more depth by adopting other theoretical lenses. For example, from the perspective of organisational semiotics (Mingers & Willcocks, 2014, 2017; Stamper, 1973), gamified IS can be conceptualised as a means of communication of meaning between designers and users (McKay et al., 2012). The context-dependent appropriation of gamified IS in our framework parallels the notion of semiosis, which “consists of human and nonhuman interactions based on technologicallymediated communication in the social, material and personal worlds” (Mingers & Willcocks, 2017, p. 17). Taken together with a burgeoning stream of research known as the communicative constitution of organisation (e.g., Fairhurst & Putnam, 2004; Putnam & Fairhurst, 2015; Schoeneborn et al., 2014, 2019) advocating that “communication generates, not merely expresses, key organizational realities” (Ashcraft et al., 2009, p. 2), we see promising opportunities for organisational gamification research in this area.

Our work is subject to several limitations that sug gest additional research directions. First, the four rea sons we identified for mixed results do not represent an exhaustive list. For example, diferences in goals, expectations and methods across studies and domains might have contributed to the mixed results (e.g., conflating short-term and long-term results or com paring poorly designed studies with well-designed ones etc.). Future research can examine such alterna tive explanations by classifying and analysing studies along these lines. A second limitation relates to our use of an umbrella review to support our arguments. Although umbrella reviews provide important advantages such as summarizing research teams’ views of the evidence in an area (Aromataris et al., 2015), the review articles making up the umbrella review do not represent independent observations (Kitsiou et al., 2015) and need to be interpreted at a broad level (Aromataris et al., 2015). Our umbrella review also included review papers that contained diferent types of adult participants, comprised of employees, consumers, and/or university students (we did not include reviews consisting of children, like Chow et al., 2020). While some argue that university students are consu mers and thus are appropriate participants in research of interest to organizations, researchers in both mar keting (e.g., Ashraf & Merunka, 2017) and IS (e.g., Compeau et al., 2012) acknowledge that best practices need to be followed in using university students as proxies for other adults. Future gamification research needs to consider these practices for studying students and focus more on organizational participants when ever possible. Third, even though our framework extends AST in some areas, it does not include research questions related to all of the AST proposi tions. While this is in line with prior AST research (e.g., Bostrom et al., 2009), it represents another lim itation of our work. Specifically, we did not separately address AST’s proposition 3, which suggests the out puts of advanced information technologies, tasks, and the environment as emergent sources of structure. Rather, we merged it with the discussion on the out comes of appropriation and emergence of new struc tures. Further, we did not propose a link between the appropriation of gamified IS and processes or between a group’s internal system and its appropriation of a gamified IS (corresponding to AST’s propositions 5 and 6, which are aligned with its original focus on GDSS). In contrast, gamified IS encompass a range of instrumental systems, including but not limited to GDSS. We therefore suggest these as additional areas for future research. Fourth, we did not consider the role of other important organisational stakeholders, including managers, executives, shareholders, and customers. For example, some (top) managers may play a combination of designer and user roles. They may contract and sponsor designers to develop and implement a gamified IS that produces some desirable outcomes. In this regard, the intent that goes into the design of this gamified IS is primarily that of the managers, as perceived and implemented by the designers. Yet these same managers may also play the part of the users of the implemented gamified IS, appropriating it based on their social and organisational contexts. Therefore, examining the influence of managers in their dual designer-user roles represents another avenue for future research. Similarly, shareholder and customer expectations might represent additional sources of structure that may complement or compete with those of gamified IS. For example, research has shown that shareholders react positively to corporate implementations of business analytics systems (Teo et al., 2016). As large-scale gamified IS become more commonplace in organisations, it is conceivable that equally positive (or negative) reactions among shareholders (or customers) occur. To varying degrees, this could enable or constrain organisations in their interest and ability to implement gamified IS. Fifth, in developing our framework, we focused on the gamified aspects of a gamified IS, that is, game elements, their symbolic expressions, afordances, and the spirit they promote within IS. However, all gamified IS have non-gamified aspects which in fact represent the larger part and raison d’être of such systems, particularly in organisational settings. The interaction and (mis)alignment between the gamified and non-gamified aspects and their implications for the structures and outcomes enacted by gamified IS creates another important avenue for future research. Finally, in our analysis of the role of designers, we did not distinguish between outsourced, insourced, or of-the-shelf gamified IS. However, the role of designers might unfold diferently for systems developed inside or outside the organization, an issue that needs to be examined in future research

In addition to potential research directions, we see important practical implications of our work. For example, for the intended outcomes of a gamified IS to be realised, designers will need to monitor users to ensure that they are appropriating the system faithfully and consensually, and adjust course if needed. This is only possible through short and iterative analysis-designfeedback loops. Further, we recommend that monitoring and adjusting should not be limited to quantifiable experiential and instrumental outcomes. Rather, we also encourage practitioners to consider the (re)production of organisational structures as a result of introducing a gamified IS. Examples of these organisational structures include new power and status hierarchies and compensation and remuneration systems.

Of all the “non-game contexts” to which gamified IS might be introduced, large-scale adoption of such systems in organisations will be most consequential. This is crucial given both the potential of gamified

IS to challenge established organisational structures and produce new ones, as well as the paramount role that organisations play in the governance and operation of many aspects of our lives. Thus, organisational gamification endeavours need to be carefully considered if they are to be efective, eficient and ethical. Lessons of early gamification work should be carefully examined through the lens of organisational theories and empirical findings. The information systems field sits at the intersection of organisational and gamification research and thus is well-positioned to lead organisational gamification research eforts. We hope that our work represents an important step in this direction.
