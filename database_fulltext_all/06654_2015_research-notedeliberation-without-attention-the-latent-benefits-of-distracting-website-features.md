## Introduction

Website features, such as animated banners, popups, and floating advertisements, are frequently used by electronic commerce (e-commerce) practitioners to attract attention and convey commercial messages to consumers above and beyond the information for which they actively search (Davenport and Beck 2001, Gao et al. 2005). However, beyond these purposes, these features often increase the complexity of the websites (Nadkarni and Gupta 2007) and evoke frustration in users, resulting in unintended consequences (Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007, Venkatesh 2000). More specifically, these attention-attracting components on e-commerce sites have been found to distract consumers from their primary task and interrupt their natural cognitive flow, thus negatively affecting their informationseeking performance (Smith et al. 2006, Zhang 2000). Hence, earlier studies on Web design (e.g., Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007) typically caution that these distracting features should be used with care.

More recently however, a series of studies in cognitive psychology uncovered the deliberation-withoutattention 4D-W-A5 effect (see Dijksterhuis et al. 2006) that is relevant to this context. Specifically, the D-W-A effect suggests that when making a complex decision (i.e., when a large number of decision parameters have to be considered), being distracted from information processing and not thinking consciously about the decision appear to lead to better decision outcomes as opposed to thinking consciously (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006). Seeking to explain this effect, unconscious thought theory (UTT) was formulated to challenge the assumption (e.g., Simon 1955) that there should be a systematic and comprehensive process of evaluation before a decision is made (see Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). In particular, UTT suggests that the unconscious mind is capable of integrating large amounts of information outside of one’s rational awareness, and hence considerable unconscious thought can improve one’s decisions in situations of high complexity. The UTT has stirred much controversy (Acker 2008) and led to the emergence of a stream of studies seeking to corroborate (e.g., Ham et al. 2009), refute (e.g., Newell et al. 2009), or investigate the boundary conditions of the theory (e.g., Payne et al. 2008).

The D-W-A effect has important implications for Web design research. In particular, if the D-W-A effect holds in the context of e-commerce, it will challenge the traditional view on the use of distracting website features (e.g., Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007). In other words, rather than creating a distraction-free website to facilitate focused and conscious deliberation during prepurchase evaluation, the use of distracting features that prevent consumers from focusing on product evaluation could be beneficial. This is because they may not only serve to convey the intended commercial messages of the sellers (Davenport and Beck 2001, Gao et al. 2005) but also lead to better choices for consumers facing complex purchase decisions. This is especially relevant in the context of e-commerce today, where Web personalization technologies can allow online merchants to dynamically customize their websites based on the context of the interaction or transaction (Tam and Ho 2005, 2006). For example, distracting features can be hidden or presented based on whether the product being viewed is simple or complex.

In light of the findings surrounding the D-W-A effect and UTT, this study seeks to investigate whether the D-W-A effect can be triggered by distracting website features, in the form of pop-ups, in an e-commerce setting and, if so, explore the underlying mode of thought that gives rise to better decisions. Pop-ups refer to browser windows that appear on the surface of a Web page and obscure part of the original content of the page (Zhang 2006). We propose that if these features have a positive influence on consumers’ decision making, then the traditional negative perception of distracting website features (e.g., Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007) should be reassessed. As such, beyond contributing to the current debate in cognitive psychology literature on the D-W-A effect, this research would also corroborate a more recent stream of research in the human–computer interaction (HCI) literature that suggests the need for a balanced and contingency perspective of the use of these features (e.g., Brajnik and Gabrielli 2010, Schumann et al. 2014). In addition, it could add to the growing calls for research (see Hong et al. 2007) on how to make distracting website features more palatable and beneficial for online consumers.

More specifically, this paper addresses two research questions. The first question is, What is the effect of different types of pop-ups on the users’ feeling of annoyance? Although the majority of past studies consider distracting features as irrelevant information, a few studies have suggested that these features could be used to convey relevant information as well (e.g., Hong et al. 2004). To gain a more complete understanding, this study will examine the effects of pop-ups on annoyance under both task-related and non-taskrelated conditions. The second research question is, What is the effect of pop-ups on the quality of purchasing decisions of low and high complexity? In line with prior research on decision making (Olshavsky 1979, Payne et al. 1988), this study characterizes the complexity of decision tasks based on the number of product attributes that have to be considered and compared when making decisions (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006). In addition, decision quality is assessed objectively based on the proportion of desirable attributes possessed by the chosen product (similar to Haubl and Trifts 2000, Tan et al. 2010). More specifically, an alternative is deemed to be of higher quality than another if it has at least one attribute that is superior to that of the other alternative while none of its other attributes are inferior, assuming that all decision attributes are equally weighted (Payne et al. 1993).

## Literature Review

## The Deliberation-Without-Attention Effect

The notion that conscious deliberation is ideal for decision making is a fundamental assumption of both classical and contemporary perspectives on decision making (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006). For example, classical models of rational choice typically advocate a systematic evaluation of alternatives before a decision is made (e.g., Simon 1955). Similarly, contemporary studies of decision making have suggested that intuitive thought processes tend to lead to poorer decisions unless they are controlled by a deliberate and systematic reasoning process (Kahneman 2003). However, evidence from studies of the D-W-A effect and UTT have emerged to challenge this notion by demonstrating that not thinking consciously about a decision may lead to better outcomes in the context of complex decisions where the number of parameters that have to be considered is large (see Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006, Payne et al. 2008).

A review of prior studies on the D-W-A effect reveals an intense interest in validating and challenging the propositions of UTT (see the online supplement, available as supplemental material at http://dx .doi.org/10.1287/isre.2015.0566). The debate about the underlying cause of the D-W-A effect, in particular, is especially heated (Bargh 2011). At one end, proponents of UTT have attributed the D-W-A effect to the superiority of unconscious thought in complex decision making (e.g., Dijksterhuis et al. 2009, Strick et al. 2010). At the other end, opponents of the theory have proposed a number of plausible alternative explanations for the D-W-A effect, such as the mechanism of online judgments (i.e., impression formation during the initial information acquisition period; see Lassiter et al. 2009, Newell et al. 2009) and the disruption of nonproductive conscious deliberation (e.g., Rey et al. 2009). Straddling the middle ground between the two extremes, a number of researchers are of the view that UTT is neither universally true nor patently false, but is instead subject to a number of boundary conditions. Among others, the boundary conditions investigated in the literature include the adoption of a configural mindset (Lerouge 2009), the simultaneous presentation of decision parameters (Acker 2008), a time constraint (Payne et al. 2008), subject matter expertise (Dijksterhuis et al. 2009), and information quality and quantity (Gao et al. 2010).

Although the cause of the D-W-A effect is disputed, the existence of the effect itself is subject to less controversy (as revealed in 13 out of the 18 studies presented in the online supplement). Moreover, the nonsuperiority of conscious thought in complex decision making is generally acknowledged (with the sole exception of Acker 2008), even in the handful of studies where the D-W-A effect is not found (e.g., Newell et al. 2009, Thorsteinson and Withrow 2009). Collectively, both the D-W-A effect and the nonsuperiority of conscious thought in complex decision making (Bargh 2011) present important implications for the existing knowledge on website design.

## Implications of the D-W-A Effect on Website Design

One of the widely accepted principles in early Web design literature is that the use of distracting website features, such as pop-ups, animated banners, and floating advertisements, should be treated with caution (Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007). The intent of these features is to attract consumers’ attention to the various commercial messages that the online retailer hopes to convey (Davenport and Beck 2001, Gao et al. 2005). However, because of a human’s limited attention span (van de Heijden 1992), consumers may perceive this form of pushed communications to be distractions. This is because distractions direct their attention away from the primary activity (Baron 1986, p. 4), resulting in poorer task performance.

In particular, distractions are believed to bring about two negative consequences for online retailers. First, research has found that distractions that prevent consumers from focusing on the primary task of shopping can form a source of considerable annoyance (Bailey and Konstan 2006, Burns and Lutz 2006) and increase overall transaction costs (Chircu and Mahajan 2006). The increased transaction costs may even result in consumers switching to a competitor or deciding not to buy the product altogether (Carter et al. 2014). Second, there is an implicit assumption that a conscious and systematic phase of evaluation prior to making the purchase decision allows consumers to clarify ambiguities and focus their attention on the most relevant information (e.g., Smith et al. 2006), which enhances their product understanding and decision quality (Blackwell et al. 2001). Distractions are thought to disrupt this prepurchase evaluation process by increasing the consumers’ mental load (Kahneman 1973) and diverting attention away from the primary task (Speier et al. 1997), which limits the processing of relevant information (Bailey and Konstan 2006, Trafton et al. 2003) and consequently lowers the decision quality (Speier et al. 1999, Zhang 2000).

Recent findings related to the D-W-A effect should, however, give e-commerce practitioners a reason for pause. Because distracting website features can direct a consumer’s attention away from decision making, thereby preventing conscious deliberation (Li et al. 2002), these features may be potentially beneficial for consumers’ decision making in situations of high complexity. This would also corroborate an emerging stream of research in the recent Web design literature that suggests the need for a balanced and contingency perspective on the use of these distracting website features. In particular, these studies suggest that the effect of distracting website features on task performance and user experience may not always be negative, but instead vary under different conditions. The contingency factors of this effect that have been identified include task type, prior user experience, and nature of the objects (see Hong et al. 2004, 2007, Nadkarni and Gupta 2007).

To further investigate the implications of using distracting website features and the D-W-A effect, our study aims to examine the effects of a specific form of distracting website features (i.e., pop-ups) on consumers’ feeling of annoyance and the quality of their purchase decisions. In particular, although the early Web design literature generally suggests there is a negative effect of distracting website features on annoyance (Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007), we are interested in whether pop-ups can be designed to alleviate the consumers’ feeling of annoyance. For example, if website owners increase the value or relevance of the distracting features to consumers (Edwards et al. 2002), these features may then serve their intended purposes without causing much annoyance (Davenport and Beck 2001, Gao et al. 2005). Moreover, the D-W-A effect implies that the presence of distracting features may even have a positive effect on consumers’ decision making in situations of high decision complexity. Hence, better decision quality, in tandem with an increased acceptance of the featured content (Davenport and Beck 2001, Gao et al. 2005), could potentially offset the negative consequences of using these features.

## Hypotheses Development

## Distractions and Annoyance

Annoyance is an unintended outcome of using a website (Koufaris 2002) and is often caused by features that create feelings of confusion, distraction, or impatience (Chen and Wells 1999). Prior studies have found that certain forms of online advertising, particularly animated banners, pop-ups, and floating advertisements are capable of causing annoyance (Burns and Lutz 2006, Hausmana and Siekpeb 2009, McCoy et al. 2007). By disrupting the content of interest and interfering with the users’ primary goals, the presence of these forms of advertising limits the number of actions that users can take to attain their goals, which in turn gives rise to negative consequences such as irritation and frustration (Edwards et al. 2002, Li et al. 2002). Accordingly, we expect that the presence of these distracting features on a website will lead to greater annoyance for online consumers in general.

In addition, prior studies on distractions have revealed that the nature of a distraction has an important effect on annoyance as well. A number of researchers have found that distracting features perceived to contain information related to the primary task elicit less irritation and avoidance (Czerwinski et al. 2000, Gluck et al. 2007, Pasadeos 1990). In particular, animated features have been found to attract consumers’ attention more quickly when they are applied to relevant information than irrelevant information (Hong et al. 2004). This implies that when faced with distracting website features, consumers will assess and be influenced by the congruence between the distracting content and the task at hand. Congruence is defined as the degree to which the distracting information is relevant or complementary to the task goal (Edwards et al. 2002).<sup>1</sup> For example, for a consumer looking to buy a jacket in an online store, a pop-up related to fashion products would probably be perceived as more task congruent and less intrusive than a pop-up related to an online video game. Hence, we expect distracting features that are congruent with the context of the present cognitive activities to cause less annoyance than those that are incongruent (Edwards et al. 2002, Faber et al. 2004). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> A website with distracting features leads to more annoyance than a website without distracting features.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> A website with distracting features that are incongruent with the current task will lead to more annoyance than a website with distracting features that are congruent with the current task.

## Distractions and Quality of Product Choice: The D-W-A Effect

One of the dominant explanations for the D-W-A effect is proffered by UTT (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006), which suggests that the D-W-A effect is due to the different natures of conscious and unconscious deliberation. The six principles of UTT are summarized briefly in Table 1.

According to the rule principle of UTT, conscious deliberation will lead to better decisions compared to unconscious deliberation when the decision to be made is simple due to the precise, rule-based nature of conscious thought. However, UTT offers three explanations for why conscious deliberation is not ideal in the context of complex decision making. First, the capacity principle of UTT postulates that conscious deliberation leads to better decisions only if its capacity is not strained (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). Prior research has found that conscious thought can only temporarily store roughly five to nine items at any one time (Miller 1956) and process information at 10–60 bits per second, compared to the 11,200,000 bits per second processing capability of the entire human system (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). For example, one can easily consider a single attribute (e.g., breakfast is better at Restaurant A than at Restaurant B), but the simultaneous consideration of multiple parameters (e.g., location, service, price) may be challenging for conscious thought. Consequently, in circumstances of high complexity, low capacity of conscious thought may cause individuals to focus narrowly on a subset of relevant information in decision making while ignoring other pertinent pieces of information, resulting in suboptimal decision making (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006).

Second, according to the bottom-up-versus-topdown principle of UTT (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006, Sloman 1996), conscious deliberation is guided by expectancies and heuristics. As a result, under circumstances of high processing complexity, an individual may rely on these to form a judgment prematurely instead of considering all of the information relevant to the decision at hand. Unconscious deliberation, in contrast, is shaped by existing information, rather than guided by one’s preexisting schemas. It allows an individual to slowly integrate the available information into relatively objective summary judgments (Bos et al. 2011). Third, the weighting principle of UTT (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006, Wilson et al. 1993) postulates that conscious deliberation may lead to suboptimal weighting of the importance of attributes as it disrupts a natural weighting process. This can cause individuals to weigh attributes that are more accessible and plausible disproportionately more (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006). In contrast, unconscious deliberation has the ability to naturally weigh the relative importance of the various decision parameters, and the assigned weightings also tend to be consistent over time.

Overall, these principles imply that the quality of decisions made through unconscious deliberation is likely to be independent from the complexity of the context, and unconscious deliberation is thus expected to outperform conscious deliberation in making complex decisions (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). Indeed, similar findings have been reported in other domains as well. For example, Smilek et al. (2006) have found that difficult visual search tasks can be made more efficient and accurate by instructing users to “relax” (i.e., to give up active control over attention and rely on automatic processes for directing attention).

In accordance with the D-W-A effect, because distracting website features can prevent consumers from focusing on product assessment when making a purchase decision (Li et al. 2002, McCoy et al. 2007), we expect that the presence of distracting website features will negatively affect decision quality for lowcomplexity decisions, but have a positive effect for high-complexity decisions. This is because a lowcomplexity purchase decision will involve only a few decision parameters (i.e., product attributes). Hence, all of the relevant pieces of information can be processed within the cognitive capacity of the focal decision maker, which helps in systematically evaluating the pros and cons of each product alternative, leading to the best possible choice (Smith et al. 2006). However, the reliance on unconscious modes of decision making induced by distracting website features (Lerouge 2009) may be relatively less precise in discerning the finer differences between product alternatives in this context (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). Conversely, highly complex decisions may involve many attributes as well as complex trade-offs among them. Thus, conscious deliberation may require excessive processing that is beyond an individual’s cognitive capacity (Wilson and Schooler 1991), which may lead to pertinent pieces of information being dropped (Rey et al. 2009) or suboptimal weighting of product attributes subject to cognitive rules or biases (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). Unconscious deliberation, however, enables individuals to naturally integrate large amounts of information and assess the various product attributes efficiently and appropriately without subjective bias (Bos et al. 2011). In this case, distracting users from conscious thought could thus lead to a better summary judgment than a decision made without distractions (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). Thus, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2A</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> When decision complexity is low, a website with distracting features (regardless of the level of congruence) will lead to lower decision quality compared to a website without distracting features.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2B</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> When decision complexity is high, a website with distracting features (regardless of the level of congruence) will lead to higher decision quality compared to a website without distracting features.

## Research Method

We conducted two lab experiments to test our hypotheses. The purpose of Experiment 1 was to investigate the impact of distracting Web features, in the form of pop-ups, on users’ perceived annoyance and decision quality. In this experiment, the congruence between pop-ups and the task, as well as the complexity of the decision task, was experimentally manipulated. Experiment 2 sought to replicate the findings of Experiment 1 on decision quality as well as provide further evidence on the underlying mechanisms proposed by UTT.

## Experiment 1: The Effect of Distracting Website Features on Annoyance and Decision Quality

Participants. A total of 101 undergraduate and graduate students were recruited from a large university in Singapore. The participants were from a variety of countries and cultural backgrounds, as well as six different academic faculties, constituting a diverse sample. Among the participants, 46 (49.5%) were women. The ages of the participants ranged from 18 to 35 (mean, 22.3). In general, the participants felt very comfortable with Internet usage (mean, 6.25/7) and were familiar with mobile phones (4.87/7) and digital cameras (4.52/7), which were our experimental products (as explained in the next section).

Design and Operationalization of Variables. A 3 (no distraction versus congruent distraction versus incongruent distraction) × 2 (low versus high decision complexity) mixed experiment design was employed for this study. The nature of distracting features was manipulated as a between-subjects factor. Decision complexity, in contrast, was manipulated as a withinsubjects factor. Based on the principle that a conscious mind can only store around five to nine items at any one time (Miller 1956), and in keeping with the manipulation of decision complexity in prior studies (e.g., Acker 2008, Rey et al. 2009), a low-complexity decision was associated with 4 decision parameters (i.e., a purchase decision involving products with 4 attributes), whereas a high-complexity decision was associated with 12 decision parameters (i.e., a purchase decision involving products with 12 attributes) in this experiment. Because decision complexity was a within-subjects factor, the order of the two decision tasks was counterbalanced.

Product category was kept constant across the two levels of decision complexity because using a different product for each level of complexity might introduce product category as a potential confounding factor. This experiment adopted two product categories, mobile phones and digital cameras, each with two decision tasks (i.e., at low and high complexity). We selected these two categories because the existing mobile phones and digital cameras in the market could span a wide range of complexity. The decision attributes associated with the mobile phones and cameras at the two levels of complexity are presented in Table 2. For each attribute, the attribute value was set to be either desirable or undesirable for a product.

For each product category, there were four products at each complexity level. In line with the operationalization of product quality in prior studies (e.g., Acker 2008, Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Haubl and Trifts 2000, Rey et al. 2009), we varied the quality of the four products based on the amount of desirable attributes possessed by each product so that there was one best product, two mediocre products, and one worst product (see Appendix A.1 for details). In addition, the four products were associated with the same brand name but different model names, which were all fictitious and unknown to the participants. Product photographs and price were kept constant for products of the same complexity level in each category to prevent possible confounding effects of appearance and individual price sensitivity. Three websites were created to realize the three distraction conditions (i.e., no distraction (ND), congruent distraction (CD), and incongruent distraction (ID)) for each decision task. For both levels of decision complexity, the participants in the control (i.e., ND) group saw only static information pages that displayed the specifications of the four products. The CD group was distracted with a pop-up that asked the participant to complete a product-related survey to receive a 10% discount (see Appendix A.2 for the questions on the survey). This form of distraction remained congruent with the primary task of online shopping due to the survey content and the discount offered, while forcing participants to focus on answering the survey questions rather than product evaluation. The same discount was also applied to the other groups to ensure that the discount would not confound the results. The ID group was distracted with a pop-up that asked participants to do an IQ quiz, which had no relation to the primary task of shopping. Overall, the aim of the pop-ups in both the CD and ID conditions was to take the mind of the participants off of product evaluation by forcing them to focus fully on another task, which was expected to trigger unconscious mode of thought in decision making (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Lerouge 2009).

Manipulation checks were performed for both decision complexity and congruence of the distracting features with the primary task (for the CD and ID conditions only).<sup>2</sup> In addition, pretests and manipulation checks were performed to ensure that distractions were of sufficient intensity to make conscious deliberation unlikely.<sup>3</sup> The dependent variable, annoyance, was measured based on validated scales adapted from prior studies (Burns and Lutz 2006, Flavian et al. 2006). Procedure. The experiment was set in the context of an online auction, where a natural time constraint was imposed on decision making. Prior to the experiment, participants were randomly assigned to one of three distraction conditions, with 31 participants in each condition. Each participant was provided with an experiment task sheet (see Appendix B) and briefed on the products that they were supposed to purchase. In each decision task, they were told to evaluate each of the four products and then pick the “best” one (Lerouge 2009). An experimenter described each decision parameter to the participants and explained how to pick the optimal product based on the decision parameters. This explanation ensured that (1) the participants knew how to pick the best product (i.e., based on the percentage of desirable attributes possessed by the product) and that (2) the participants would use a rational decision strategy taking into account all available information and treating the attributes equally (Rey et al. 2009). Indeed, in our setting, a subjective interpretation of optimality was unlikely to differ from the objective optimality, because the alternative with the most desirable attributes had no attributes that were inferior to that of other alternatives. To further motivate the participants to make the best possible decision, they were told that they would be paid S\$4 if they picked the best product, S\$2 if they picked a mediocre product, and no money if they picked the worst product or if they made no selection before the auction closed. No additional forms of incentives were provided. For data analysis, if the participant chose the worst alternative, the decision quality was coded as 1; if the participant chose either of the mediocre alternatives, the decision quality was coded as $2 ;$ if the participant chose the best alternative, the decision quality was coded as 3. In this way, decision quality could be treated as an ordinal variable that reflected the relative utility value (i.e., the share of desirable attributes) of the chosen alternative (Tan et al. 2010).

Following the time limit in previous D-W-A studies (e.g., Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Lassiter et al. 2009, Lerouge 2009), the auction for this experiment was set to close in four minutes. At the start of the auction, the participants of all conditions were shown four static information display pages one by one for 15 seconds each (see Figure 1). Each page contained specifications of one product. The sequence in which the four product pages were presented and accessed was randomized to minimize the possibility of order effects. The amount of time allowed for each page (i.e., 15 seconds) was verified in pilot tests as adequate for absorbing all of the relevant information, but inadequate for systematic deliberation.<sup>5</sup> The timing was strictly controlled through explicit instructions from the experimenter.

Discussion. Experiment 1 provides interesting answers to the research questions set forth at the beginning of this paper. First, in relation to our first research question, we found that pop-ups gave rise to annoyance in general. However, employing more relevant distracting content might alleviate annoyance to some extent, as shown by the strong difference between the no distraction and incongruent distraction conditions, as well as the marginal difference between the no distraction and congruent distraction conditions. Nonetheless, the results did not indicate a significant difference between the congruent and incongruent distraction conditions in terms of the annoyance caused. A possible explanation is that although our participants did recognize the difference in the level of congruence of the distraction content (as indicated by our manipulation check), they were generally not concerned about the survey content or the product price (i.e., the high congruence distraction), as their primary motivation was only in identifying the optimal product. Accordingly, the potential of alleviating annoyance by increasing the congruence between the distraction content and the primary task was not strongly demonstrated.

Next, in relation to our second research question, we found that the presence of pop-ups led to poorer purchase decisions in the case of low decision complexity, but better decisions in the case of high decision complexity, regardless of the level of content congruence. In addition, for each product category, we conducted a separate analysis comparing the decision quality of the groups with pop-ups and the group without pop-ups. The results show that for both cameras and mobile phones, in the highcomplexity decision task, the groups with pop-ups made higher-quality decisions than the group without pop-ups (for mobile phones, mean $\mathrm { r a n k _ { P o p - u p } = }$ 52012, mean ${ \mathrm { r a n k } } _ { _ { \mathrm { N o P o p - u p } } } = 3 6 . 7 6 , z = - 2 . 9 2 , { \dot { p } } <$ $0 . 0 1 ;$ for cameras, mean $\mathrm { r a n k } _ { \mathrm { P o p - u p } } = 5 0 . 0 4 _ { \cdot }$ , mean $\mathrm { r a n k } _ { \mathrm { N o P o p - u p } } = 4 0 . 9 2 , z = - 1 . 7 7 , p = \dot { 0 } . 0 7 , \mathrm { m a r g i n a l l y } ) ,$ but lower-quality decisions in the low-complexity task (for mobile phones, mean $\operatorname { r a n k } _ { \operatorname { P o p - u p } } = 4 2 . 1 8 ,$ mean $\mathrm { r a n k } _ { \mathrm { N o P o p - u p } } = 5 6 . 6 5 , \ z = - 2 . 7 8 , \ \dot { p } \stackrel { . } { < } 0 . 0 1 ,$ ; for cameras, mean ran $\mathbf { \sigma } _ { \mathrm { { l F _ { P o p - u p } } } } = 4 2 . 3 9 ,$ mean $\mathrm { r a n k _ { N o P o p - u p } = }$ $5 6 . 2 3 , \ z = - 2 . 6 4 , \ p \ \dot { < } \ \dot { 0 . 0 1 } )$ . According to UTT, the enhanced decision quality in complex tasks as a result of using distracting features could be explained through the mechanism of unconscious deliberation. More specifically, the presence of pop-ups might have triggered the participants’ unconscious deliberation, which was less capacity constrained than conscious thought and better at integrating information and forming holistic impressions. Overall, putting the findings on annoyance and decision quality together, it appears that for complex shopping tasks, a website incorporating relevant distracting features, such as pop-ups, could be potentially beneficial to consumers.

Although Experiment 1 provides evidence for the D-W-A effect in an online auction context with popups, a review of prior studies on the D-W-A effect reveals several issues that deserve further attention. As mentioned earlier, although the mechanism of unconscious deliberation $( \mathrm { i . e . , U T T } )$ has been the most popular explanation for the D-W-A effect, several alternative explanations have also been proposed. Hence, to further investigate the mechanisms underlying the positive effects of pop-ups on complex decision making, we conducted a second experiment.

In particular, one alternative explanation for the D-W-A effect is that consumers might have already formed the decision while they were acquiring product information. In other words, they might have formed an online judgment before the distraction period (Cleeremans et al. 2009, Lassiter et al. 2009, Waroquier et al. 2010). A period of conscious deliberation thereafter might have distorted this online judgment, leading to a deteriorated decision in the end. A period of distraction, on the contrary, might have preserved the online judgment better. To ascertain if the higher decision quality after the distraction period should be attributed to the preservation of online judgment or further (unconscious) thought during the distraction period, Experiment 2 incorporated an additional condition where the participants made their decisions immediately after they acquired all of the product information. Such decisions should reflect their actual online judgments (Cleeremans et al. 2009, Lassiter et al. 2009, Waroquier et al. 2010). Comparing this immediate decision condition with a distraction condition (e.g., the conditions with a pop-up in Experiment 1) should reveal whether the users’ thoughts during the distraction period have indeed changed the online judgment. In fact, the difference between an immediate decision and one produced after unconscious thought is a crucial aspect of UTT; i.e., a period of unconscious thought should lead to an improved decision compared to the online judgment (Dijksterhuis 2004, Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006).

A second alternative explanation for the benefit of having pop-ups in making complex decisions is that consumers might have entirely stopped thinking about the product evaluation (consciously or unconsciously) during the distraction period and they returned to consider the decision again after this period. Previous studies have suggested that people often have a fresh, unbiased new start after putting aside the problem for a while (e.g., the process of set shifting, as described in Schooler and Melcher 1995). In particular, an interruption might have changed the users’ means of processing product attributes (e.g., Liu 2008), leading to a better decision compared to if they were continuously processing the information. To examine whether the higher decision quality after the distraction period was due to the mere disruption of the users’ decision-related thinking or their unconscious thought during the distraction period, Experiment 2 incorporated a condition where the participants were made to stop thinking about product evaluation altogether during the distraction period. Comparing this mere disruption condition with a distraction condition would thus reveal the difference between a fresh decision after a period without thinking about the problem and a decision derived from a period of active but unconscious thinking.

As a third alternative explanation, one could argue that participants might have continued thinking consciously during the distraction task, although their cognitive capacity was constrained due to multitasking (i.e., the decision task and the distraction task were conducted simultaneously). As a result, they might have adopted a simplified but effective decision strategy that differed from the strategy they would use when they had more cognitive resources. The decision strategy adopted under constrained cognitive capacity could be the cause of the superior decision quality after the distraction task. Experiment 2 was designed to address this issue in two ways. First, it adopted a more cognitively demanding distraction task that would leave little free resources for the participants to think about the product evaluation. Second, to more directly address this issue, Experiment 2 also incorporated a condition where the participants were explicitly asked to think hard about the product evaluation while performing the distracting task. Comparing this constrained conscious thought condition with a distraction condition would thus reveal the difference between a decision resulting from a conscious but cognitively limited thought process and one resulting from unconscious thought.

Overall, the focus of Experiment 2 was on consumers’ decision making in a complex shopping task, with an aim to corroborate the core findings of Experiment 1 (i.e., H2B). More importantly, Experiment 2 sought to ascertain the underlying mechanism of the positive effects of pop-ups on purchase decisions. We expect that if our hypotheses based on UTT are true, then the alternative mechanisms described above should not lead to the same level of decision performance as that produced by unconscious thought or outperform conscious deliberation.

## Experiment 2: Mechanisms Underlying the Positive Effect of Distracting Website Features on Complex Decision Making

Participants. A total of 127 participants were recruited from a large university in Australia. The participants were from seven different academic faculties, representing a diverse sample. Among them, 75 (59%) were women. The ages of the participants ranged from 18 to 34 (mean, 21.5).

Design and Procedure. Five conditions were created in Experiment 2 (see Table 6 for an overview). The participants were randomly assigned to one of five conditions, with approximately 25 in each condition. The conscious thought (CT) condition was similar to the ND condition in Experiment 1, and the unconscious thought (UT) condition was similar to the ID condition in Experiment 1 (albeit with a different distraction task, as explained below). Three other conditions were created to explicitly address the three alternative mechanisms aforementioned. These mechanisms include (1) online decision making during information acquisition before the distraction, (2) the mere disruption of decision-relevant thought by the distraction, and (3) conscious deliberation during the distraction period with constrained cognitive capacity. We termed the three corresponding conditions as the immediate decision (IM) condition, the mere disruption (MD) condition, and the constrained conscious thought (CCT) condition, respectively (see Table 6).

More specifically, in the CT and UT conditions, the participants were instructed to evaluate four complex mobile phone products (i.e., the same complex mobile phones used in Experiment 1) on an auction site and to choose the best one later. They were then presented with the information page for each product, which contained 12 product attributes. The sequence of accessing the four product information pages and the order of the 12 attributes on each page were both randomized. Each product information page was displayed for 15 seconds. After browsing through all four pages, the participants in the CT condition were told to carefully think about the product information and their decision, without any distraction, and to report their choice after two minutes. The participants in the UT condition, however, were prompted to do a distraction task presented in a pop-up. A twoback test was adopted as the distracting task, which has been widely used in prior research on the D-W-A effect (e.g., Bos et al. 2008, Thorsteinson and Withrow 2009). In the two-back test, participants were exposed to a continuous slideshow of pictures on the pop-up screen, with one second for each picture to display and half a second transition between consecutive pictures. If the picture they currently saw was the same as the picture they saw two steps before, they were supposed to click on the current picture. This test was not difficult to perform, but it has been shown to be continuously occupying, thereby preventing conscious thought on product evaluation to a large extent (Jonides et al. 1997). After two minutes had elapsed, the participants in the UT condition were automatically redirected to the product pages and asked to report their choice quickly.

Given that unconscious thought tended to integrate large amounts of information into a summary judgment whereas conscious deliberation focuses on specific attributes (e.g., Dijksterhuis 2004), we expected the conscious thought condition to lead to better recognition of the product attributes than the unconscious thought condition.

The accuracy of the participants’ attribute recognition was measured by the number of incorrectly recognized attributes, with a higher value indicating a larger number of incorrectly recognized attributes and, thus, a less accurate memory. One-way ANOVA tests on this measure revealed that the UT condition $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 7 . 4 8 )$ generally led to a higher number of incorrectly recognized attributes than the CT condition $( \mathrm { m e a n } =$ 5.63; $F ( 1 , 4 8 ) = 4 . 9 9 ; p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the CCT condition (mean = 5.84; F 411 465 = 2059; p = 0010, marginally). There was no difference between the UT condition and the IM condition (mean = 7.04) or the MD condition $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 7 . 6 1 )$ in this aspect. This result thus suggests that while the UT condition led to better decision quality, unconscious thought was not overly focused on the detailed attribute information compared to conscious deliberation. This finding is also in line with Dijksterhuis’ (2004) arguments that unconscious thinkers tend to integrate all of the information to make a summary judgment as opposed to deriving a judgment from the processing of specific pieces of information.

Interestingly, we found that among all of the correctly recognized attributes of the four products, the percentage of the desirable attributes of the best product was higher in the UT condition (mean = 0.32) than in the CT $( { \mathrm { m e a n } } = 0 . 2 2 ; \ F ( 1 , 4 8 ) = 2 . 9 6 ; \ p =$ 0009, marginally), IM $( { \mathrm { m e a n } } = 0 . 1 9 ; ~ F ( 1 , 4 6 ) = 5 . 4 0 ;$ $p < 0 . 0 5 ) .$ , MD (mean = 0.23; F 411 475 = 2046; p = 0010, marginally), and CCT $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 0 . 1 8 ; \ F ( 1 , 4 6 ) =$ 4090; $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ conditions. In other words, although an unconscious mind might not process the specific product attribute information in detail like a conscious mind, it appears to be able to weigh the relative importance of information better than the other thought modes. More specifically, unconscious thought seems to have made the desirable attributes of the best option more accessible than other information, and this has likely helped participants effectively distinguish the best option from the rest. This finding is in line with prior studies that suggest that unconscious thought may enable consumers to differentiate alternatives more easily than conscious deliberation can (Bos et al. 2011, Dijksterhuis 2004).

Discussion. Experiment 2 provides further evidence that the presence of distracting website features, in the form of pop-ups, can lead to better decisions given a complex decision problem. More importantly, pairwise comparisons between the unconscious thought group and the groups representing the other modes of thought reveal that, despite the small sample size, differences between unconscious deliberation and other modes of thought were within or close to statistical significance. In other words, Experiment 2 indicates the superior decision performance after a period of distraction could not be attributed to online judgment (via the comparison between the UT and IM conditions; Bos et al. 2011), or to the mere disruption of decision-related thinking (via the comparison between the UT and MD conditions; e.g., Rey et al. 2009), or to a cognitively constrained conscious thought process (via the comparison between the UT and CCT conditions). Therefore, unconscious deliberation is a more plausible cause of the positive effect of distracting features on decision making. Overall, Experiment 2 extends the first experiment by shedding light on the mechanism triggered by the distracting website features and provides further support for UTT.

## General Discussion

Both experiments have established that the D-W-A effect (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006) can manifest in a particular e-commerce setting. Although early Web design literature generally advocates creating distraction-free websites that facilitate a conscious, thorough decisionmaking process, the results of our two experiments suggest that although a distraction-free website may facilitate consumers’ decision making when the decision to be made is of low complexity, incorporating website features that distract consumers from prepurchase evaluation may lead to better purchase decisions when the decision to be made is of high complexity. Specifically, when only a few product attributes have to be considered, a precise, rule-based conscious deliberation enabled by a distraction-free website will improve decision quality because all of the information can be systematically processed (Rey et al. 2009). However, when the decision involves complex products with many attributes to consider and compare, such rule-based conscious deliberation may require excessive processing beyond the consumers’ cognitive capacity, hence resulting in suboptimal information integration and decision performance. Conversely, websites with distracting features that divert consumers’ attention away may, in fact, free them from imperfect heuristics or biases that stem from conscious thought and allow them to appropriately assess the various alternatives implicitly (Dijksterhuis and Nordgren 2006). This may lead to an abstract, but more accurate, summary judgment.

## Limitations and Future Research

A limitation of our work is that we have adopted a single form of distraction (i.e., pop-ups) in the context of a specific type of e-commerce transaction (i.e., an online auction). The specificity of our experimental context may limit the external validity of our work because it may not be representative of all online purchasing scenarios (e.g., those without a time constraint). Future research can extend the current work by varying the type of distraction and e-commerce transaction so that the external validity of the findings can be tested.

A second limitation is that, in both experiments, we attempted to induce rational decision making by imposing a definition of the “best” decision on our participants. We also quantified and imposed an equal weighting of all product parameters so that the differences between products were objective and clear. Yet, it must be acknowledged that not all decision parameters in reality can be objectively weighted and quantified. Indeed, online consumers often do not follow the rational decision-making paradigm faithfully (Devaraj et al. 2002). For instance, some consumers might simply have a strong preference over particular features to the extent that they do not even care about others. Although we found it necessary to introduce these objective conditions to establish the baseline for manifesting the D-W-A effect in the e-commerce context, replicating our experiments by varying these conditions could prove a fruitful avenue for future research. In particular, the inclusion of less objective or unquantifiable decision parameters and allowing for subjective weightings of decision parameters under different decision-making paradigms may reveal the boundary conditions of the D-W-A effect and extend the findings of the current study.

A third limitation specific to Experiment 1 is that the task-congruent distraction was realized by asking participants to perform a task to earn a price discount for the products, whereas the same discount was applied in other conditions by default. Although the price discount might be of less concern for participants in our studies (because their primary task was to identify the best product without considering price as a factor), the different means of presenting the discount could potentially affect participants’ motivation for performing the distraction tasks, which might affect the relative effects of the different conditions. Future research could seek to replicate our experiments with alternative distractions that remain congruent to the task of product evaluation. For instance, users could be presented with a survey concerning a different but closely related product that is about to be launched.

Beyond the limitations of our work, we hope that our findings can serve as a catalyst for a new line of inquiry that seeks to establish boundary conditions of our hypotheses. For instance, although the effects of unconscious versus conscious thought on decision performance were consistent in both experiments, we noted that the Singaporean sample (Experiment 1) performed better in picking the best product in the irrelevant distraction condition (i.e., 61.3%) than the Australian sample (Experiment 2) did in the equivalent unconscious thought condition (i.e., 48%). Future studies could thus further explore the influence of culture on the D-W-A effect and decision making. Future research could also extend the current study by testing the differences between unconscious deliberation and other modes of decision making using a larger sample in a real shopping environment, as well as examining long-term effects on factors such as consumer loyalty (e.g., Blackshaw 2003). In addition, actual consumer behaviors, such as attention to distracting features and brain activity, could be captured by eye trackers or functional magnetic resonance imaging in future research so that the precise mechanism by which unconscious thought forms a judgment might be more fully revealed.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our work makes a number of important theoretical contributions. First, our findings extend previous HCI studies on Web design and information search, and increase our understanding of the impacts of distracting website features on decision making. In particular, our results have raised concerns about the general view in early Web design literature that distractionfree websites should lead to better task performance (e.g., Burns and Lutz 2006, Smith et al. 2006). Consistent with the emerging stream of research on the D-W-A effect (see Acker 2008, Strick et al. 2011), our work has shown that for websites selling complex products, distracting features in the form of pop-ups can actually trigger the D-W-A effect, leading to better purchase decisions. By challenging the traditional view, our work contributes corroborating evidence to the more recent view in the HCI literature that suggests the need for a balanced and contingency perspective of the use of distracting website features (Faber et al. 2004, Hong et al. 2007). Overall, our work has advanced the state of knowledge concerning the use of distracting website features on consumer decision making and contributed to a more refined and complete view of the phenomenon.

Second, our research has made an important contribution to the growing debate surrounding the D-W-A effect and UTT. Our work has provided further evidence to the D-W-A effect via two experiments that closely simulate a particular form of e-commerce transaction. The results show that under the current setting, distracting website features do benefit users’ decision making if the decision task is complex. More importantly, in view of the inconclusive evidence on the debate about whether unconscious thought is the actual cause of the D-W-A effect, our work has tried to address several alternative explanations raised in the literature. In particular, our second experiment reveals that the enhanced decision performance resulting from the use of distracting features is unlikely to be due to consumers’ online judgment, a mere disruption from decision-related thinking, or a period of cognitively limited conscious thought. Indeed, a period of unconscious deliberation appears to enable an effective information aggregation process that results in an accurate summary judgment and help users focus on the more important and distinguishing attributes. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that other alternative explanations may yet be possible. Although we cannot exhaustively account for all alternative explanations within our study, the investigation of other potential explanations is a direction that future research could pursue. Overall, our work not only provides evidence in support of the D-W-A effect and UTT in a new and important context (i.e., an online auction) but also offers new insights into the underlying mechanism driving the D-W-A effect.

## Practical Contributions

Beyond its academic significance, our work makes a number of contributions to practice. First, it provides specific indications on the use of distracting website features such as pop-ups. For e-commerce retailers selling simple products with few attributes like toiletries or other grocery items, our work suggests that the use of distracting website features should indeed be minimized. This is because our findings have affirmed that when the purchasing decision to be made is of low complexity, the presence of distracting features could both create annoyance (Burns and Lutz 2006, McCoy et al. 2007) and lower decision quality (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006). Conversely, our findings give rise to an intriguing dilemma for e-commerce retailers selling complex products with many attributes like IT products or cars. In particular, our work suggests that when the purchasing decision to be made is of high complexity, although the use of distracting features might create annoyance (McCoy et al. 2007), these features may also benefit consumers by enabling better decision quality (Strick et al. 2010), especially when there are decision parameters that can be objectively compared. This may potentially offset the negative effect of annoyance in the long term.

Second, the specific settings of our experiments coupled with the findings of prior works on the boundary conditions of the D-W-A effect (e.g., Lerouge 2009) provide important indications on how these distracting features should be presented on websites. In particular, because the D-W-A effect is more likely to manifest under a time constraint (Lassiter et al. 2009), our work suggests that for better decision quality to arise through the mechanism of the D-W-A effect, consumers might have to be prevented from lapsing back into the natural tendency of conscious thought after they have been distracted. In both of our experiments, this was achieved through the auction closing mechanism. In the real world, the practitioners can possibly achieve the same effect through “limited-time offers” that expire soon after a distracting feature has been presented. Our study also suggests that a good time to present the distraction is after the consumers have acquired the product information.

Finally, our work hints at a couple of important caveats in triggering the D-W-A effect with distracting website features. In particular, because the D-W-A effect essentially suggests that consumers should think less about their decisions (Dijksterhuis et al. 2006, Ham et al. 2009), this might mean that the consumers may not even realize that they are making better decisions as a result of being distracted. As such, e-commerce retailers must make the link between the distraction and better decision quality explicit (i.e., find the means of demonstrating to their customers that the distractions are aiding their decision making) to maximize the resultant positive effect of better decision quality. This could, for example, take the form of a published postpurchase survey that captures the satisfaction rate of customers who purchased the same product vis-à-vis the other alternatives. Nonetheless, e-commerce retailers should still aim to minimize the annoyance caused by the distracting features, perhaps by increasing the relevance of the distracting content (Gluck et al. 2007, Pasadeos 1990) or introducing them at an appropriate stage of the purchasing process (Faber et al. 2004). This is so that with the dual action of amplifying the positive effect and suppressing the negative effect of the distracting website features, the desired results associated with their use can be achieved.
