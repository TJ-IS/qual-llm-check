# Telework vs. central work: A comparative view of knowledge accessibility

HongGirl Lee <sup>a</sup>, Bongsik Shin <sup>b,⁎</sup>, Kunihiko Higa <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Division of e-Business, College of Economics and Commerce, Kyungnam University, 449 Wolyoung-Dong, Masan, Korea <sup>b</sup> Department of Information and Decision Systems, San Diego State University, 5500 Campanile Drive, San Diego, CA 92182-8234, USA

<sup>c</sup> Center for Research in Advanced Financial Technology, Tokyo Institute of Technology, 2-12-1 O-okayama, Meguro-ku, Tokyo 152-8550, Japan

Received 30 November 2004; received in revised form 8 November 2006; accepted 12 November 2006 Available online 8 January 2007

## Abstract

With increasing popularity of telework arrangements especially among knowledge workers, effective knowledge access in the virtual setup is becoming vital to their performance. Despite the imperative, there has been a lack of theoretical and empirical efforts to investigate knowledge accessibility by distributed workers. Our study examines the gap between telework and central work in accessing knowledge with varying levels of tacitness (or implicitness), and in the use of communication media for knowledge exchange. Media theories offered a conceptual foundation for deriving relevant hypotheses. Necessary data were gathered from teleworkers working at companies with a large telework program in Japan. Both survey questionnaire and interviews were mobilized for balanced information gathering. Data analysis shows that, although information richness perception of a medium is largely rational, it is also partially re-structured by non-rationality factors. It also revealed significant discrepancies between telework and central-work in knowledge accessibility and in the patterns of media usage for knowledge access. Implications of the findings are discussed in terms of telework design and telework productivity © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Telework; Knowledge management; Media richness

## 1. Introduction

We are witnessing the ubiquity of distributed organizational structures, thanks to the advancement of information and communication technologies. The concept of telework in which people work away from the home office in various set-ups (i.e., mobile working, satellite offices, telecommuting) has received a great deal of attention in the business world, especially among knowledge workers. Successful telework that re-defines the spatial boundary of traditional organizations into a flexible structure (i.e., virtual-, network-, and flat organizations) was said to serve the knowledge society better [27]. Other alleged benefits include improved worker productivity, higher job satisfaction and worker retention, responsive customer support, reduced operational cost, and learning opportunities for new technologies [35,36,46].

In the meantime, knowledge has become the driving force of a new economy where effective use and management of organizational knowledge for formulating strategies, developing products, and designing services has become a key to success [19]. Naturally, sound accessibility of necessary knowledge by distributed entities could be instrumental for the success of a telework program and for improved worker productivity [25]. Its importance is further highlighted by the fact that knowledge-oriented jobs are increasingly performed in virtual settings [3]. For instance, more companies in Japan adopt telework for creative job performance and improved intellectual productivity rather than for the effectiveness of routine activities [52]. Efforts to investigate knowledge management issues (especially knowledge accessibility) from the perspective of telework have been lacking.

Effective use of knowledge presumes its easy availability and accessibility [37]. Depending on the complexity of knowledge to be acquired, the process of knowledge exchange may need rich communication channels such as face-to-face contacts [58]. Due to the geographical constraint, however, the conveyance of both tacit and explicit knowledge [37]<sup>1</sup> may have to rely heavily on electronic media in telework. Accordingly, the patterns of media usage to gain domain knowledge may be significantly different between telework and central-work. Such discrepancy may have considerable implications on the effectiveness of knowledge sharing, application, and re-production process, especially in a distributive setup. Meanwhile, counter-arguments can also be made that advanced technologies are able to effectively neutralize the spatial barrier in obtaining structured and unstructured knowledge.

The main research objective of this paper is to compare telework and central work in knowledge accessibility given various communication media. Here, we used knowledge accessibility as an umbrella concept that encompasses the technical (i.e., knowledge base access) and non-technical (i.e., inter-personal knowledge sharing) feasibility to acquire knowledge necessary to conduct organizational duties. The comparison was made from three different angles: (1) appropriation of communication media to locate experts, a source of domain knowledge; (2) accessibility of knowledge with varying levels of tacitness; and (3) usage of media as knowledge access tools. Prior to the comparisons, we examined perceived suitability of various media in supporting complex communication needs.

Theories of media richness offered the key conceptual foundation of our research. Survey data were collected from teleworkers who were working for Japanese companies that maintained a large-scale telework program and, therefore, a teleworker became the unit of analysis for the study. Additional information was gathered through the interviews of people associated with the telework program of surveyed firms. Data analysis revealed significant discrepancies between telework and central-work in knowledge accessibility and in the patterns of media usage for knowledge access.

## 2. Theories

Theories of media choice that depict how different social and non-social forces influence people's media usage are relevant to the research. Influencing forces include rational perceptions of media richness, social constructions, and situational constraints [12,16,43]. The classic media richness theory presumes that the information-carrying capacity of a medium is inherent because of its feedback capability, language type, and available communication cues. According to the theory, rich media should be real time-based for immediate feedbacks, and should offer visual and audio effects accompanied by various communication cues (i.e., facial expressions and body language). With the intrinsic characteristics of a communication medium, people have rational perception of its information richness. According to the richness criteria, natural language-based, faceto-face contact is the richest medium because it exposes communicating parties to synchronous verbal engagements with instant feedbacks and non-verbal communication cues [12]. On the other hand, email is deemed leaner than telephone or face-to-face because of its reliance on asynchronous and text-based correspondence.

According to other studies (i.e., [12,33]), information richness of a medium is not necessarily a logical perception, but subject to social construction and structuration processes. Contractor and Eisenberg [10] explained the process in terms of the recursive development of a communication network. Lee [29] explained that a seemingly lean medium becomes a rich channel through distanciation, autonomization, social construction, appropriation, and enactment processes. They argued that media richness and their effectiveness in supporting communication tasks are subjective and vary across groups and individuals. Various social factors that may attribute to the formation of unique media perceptions have been discussed. These include influences from coworkers, attitudes of superiors and managers, vicarious learning, group and/or organizational norms, existing characteristics of a communication network, social definition of rationality, organizational policies, and the existence of a critical mass [16,45]. They may directly or indirectly affect the re-definition process of media richness at both individual and group levels.

Knowledge access in both centralized and distributed settings requires communication channels. Extant media richness theories indicate that both rational media attributes and social elements are essential to understand the usage of communication channels for knowledge exchange and sharing among teleworkers. Empirical studies also support that an individual's recognition of media richness is the consequence of both cognitive rationality and social construction [21,45]. They seem to imply that social forces and the rationality perception interact re-producing new patterns of media adoption for knowledge access. In the circumstance, the initial perception of media about their role and utility can be dynamically transformed by the social context of their employment for knowledge access. This reconciling and dialectic process may be understood in terms of adaptive structuration [14]. The re-definition of media capability and their subsequent employment patterns becomes the ground on which we derive relevant hypotheses in the context of knowledge access and sharing.

## 3. Hypotheses

Four research hypotheses are proposed. With the first hypothesis, we examine the suitability of various media in supporting complex communication to validate the rationality of media richness perception. Then, 2 (2a and 2b), 3 and 4 (4a and 4b) compare knowledge accessibility between telework and central work in terms of: preferred mechanism to search domain knowledge expertise, knowledge accessibility, and the patterns of media usage for knowledge acquisition.

## 3.1. Media suitability for complex communication

From the classical media richness theory, a communication medium has its information carrying capacity logically perceived by people [12]. This makes certain media better prepared for supporting complex discourses than the others do. Complexity in communication may be directly affected by the ambiguity, equivocality, analyzability, variety, and prior determinability of messages to be exchanged [8,13,42]. If messages are for the sharing of domain knowledge, communication complexity may be largely reflected by its tacitness [57]. The complexity concept, therefore, could be an overall indicator in judging the difficulty of communication tasks at hand [21]. The media theory suggests that the effectiveness of communication is realized when a person adequately matches its complexity with a chosen medium. It is, therefore, hypothesized that:

Hypothesis 1. Teleworkers perceive that communication media differ in their suitability for handling complex communication.

## 3.2. Media usage for finding domain experts

The importance of social interactions and organizational networks for obtaining unstructured knowledge is well-documented. Effective knowledge access demands social interactions where individuals from different backgrounds (culture, organizational experience, disciplinary training, and preferred cognitive style) draw upon their pools of knowledge [26,30]. Organizational workers frequently consult domain experts to acquire knowledge necessary to undertake their duties. This is true regardless of whether they are working remotely or at a central location. To facilitate the search process, Ruggles [44] stressed internal-expertise mapping and creating networks of knowledge workers. Kouwenhoven [28] proposed the employment of knowledge stewards to provide a better linkage among knowledge workers.

The patterns of media usage for searching knowledge experts in telework may be significantly different from that of central work. Above all, people may recognize that the location of area specialty does not necessarily demand information rich media. In fact, a medium rich in functions (i.e., broadcasting and archive) can be more effective in the search process. It is, therefore, expected that email is used much in both telework and central work. In the meantime, conjecture is also possible that media usage may be significantly influenced by social norms established within a location or a group [16]. People may simply resort to face-to-face contacts in central work because of personal habits, convenience, or ingrained social norms. In telework, however, people may rely more on the formal procedure because of geographical limitations. As a result, technology solutions may play a bigger role in telework. The discussion leads us to propose that:

Hypothesis 2a. Email is used most in both telework and central work to locate domain expertise.

Hypothesis 2b. The other electronic media (i.e., telephone, DB/KB) play a larger role in telework than in central work to locate domain expertise.

## 3.3. Knowledge accessibility

Knowledge itself is a polysemous, multi-dimensional, and philosophical notion [39], which may be depicted by various key concepts such as “authenticated information,” “personalized information,” and “justified belief” [4]. Drawing on Polanyi's work [41], Nonaka [37] suggested that knowledge can be divided into explicit and tacit forms. Explicit knowledge (i.e., research reports, specifications, and simple software codes) needs little interpretation and can be communicated easily in words and numbers. It carries more declarative, contentoriented, and know-what elements [5]. In contrast, tacit knowledge (i.e., subjective insights, scientific intuitions) is highly personal and hard to formalize, and requires a high level of interpretation. It is more procedural and know-how driven, making it difficult to share with others [5].

Efforts have been made to formalize and codify both tacit and explicit knowledge to increase its accessibility. With the advancement of technologies, more organizational knowledge and business intelligence are expected to be codified allowing the storage and retrieval of facts, procedures, and experience through associative mechanisms [51,56,59]. Organizational memory information system [58], adaptive hypermedia information system [11], case-based knowledge modeling [9], and agent-based retrieval system [49] represent such efforts. Other approaches including Intranets, workflow, and groupware technologies may also be utilized for knowledge codification [31,40,50,60].

Despite the growing interest in technology solutions, they also pose significant challenges in facilitating effective knowledge access and sharing [22]. The solutions have to be adequately tuned for distinct types of knowledge users (i.e., shared work producers and practitioners, expertise-seeking novices, and secondary knowledge miners) as they have unique knowledge requirements [34]. System solutions should facilitate a process whereby knowledge held by individuals is amplified and internalized as part of an organization's knowledge base [24]. Accordingly, current system solutions fall short of user needs for effortless knowledge accessibility and transfer. When the theory of media richness is combined with current limitations in technical solutions for knowledge access, following proposition becomes possible:

Hypothesis 3. Knowledge accessibility is lower in telework than in central work and the gap increases as knowledge in demand becomes more tacit.

## 3.4. Media usage for knowledge exchange

According to the classic media richness theory that emphasizes rational choice, message synchronization and natural voice elements are vital in dealing with complicated communication. In the meantime, it is also true that other channels lacking these features can have their own strengths. For instance, email's distinctive advantages come from its functional richness including broadcasting ability, management and archiving of communication, access to resources, global accessibility, and flexibility [45]. With the strengths, it can change the patterns of information distribution at organizations [48], control the pace of communication [54], promote information dissemination through weak ties, and offer a unique channel for socialization and problem solving [15]. Rice et al. [43] summarized such benefits of email-based communication in terms of substitution-, communication-, and augmentation effects. The strengths might be able to offset weaknesses in terms of message synchronization and natural voice.

From a different theoretical angle, media theories explain that the usage of a communication channel can be constrained by contextual, social, and situational factors, not necessarily by the objectivity of its information richness. Individual differences in cognitive style, skill set, and training may affect media usage [18]. Distance between the sender and the receiver, accessibility to a medium, and the critical mass of technology users may affect media choice [32,55]. There are also factors that may contribute to the formation of a prevalent communication structure including influences from co-workers and peers, organizational norms in communication, and the characteristics of social networking. The circumstantial elements may make people in central work resort to more traditional face-to-face even when it is too rich (from the rationality perspective) to share target knowledge. Meanwhile, the influence of contextual forces along with the functional strengths of non-traditional media (i.e., email) may reshape the dynamics of media usage in telework. As a result, a medium, generally considered information lean, may be effectively adopted in telework to obtain tacit knowledge.

Hypothesis 4a. Email is used much to exchange tacit knowledge in telework, but telephone usage increases as shared knowledge becomes more tacit.

Hypothesis 4b. In central work, fact-to-face is a favored channel to share even explicit knowledge and its usage increases as shared knowledge becomes more tacit.

## 4. Research methodology

To test the hypotheses, a survey instrument was developed in Japanese using the seven-point Likert-scale response format as recommended by Hansen [20]. Due to the large volume of the survey, each variable was examined by a single question item (see Appendix A) and, therefore, much focus was placed on validating the content integrity of all question items. For this, qualitative pretesting of survey items was conducted based on iterative feedbacks from participating Ph.D. students and IS faculty members. Then, the question items were presented to industry practitioners for another round of review and their suggestions were incorporated. English translation of survey items is shown in Appendix A.

Survey data were gathered from seven large companies (10 regional offices) in Japan with a sizable telework program in place. For this, copies of the questionnaire were mailed to telework coordinators of those organizations and they distributed the questionnaire to teleworkers. Participating teleworkers answered both telework and central work question items because they also reported to the central office although the frequency of commuting varied considerably (Table 4). Study participants could return the completed survey either to their coordinator or directly to us. Additionally, the questionnaire was posted on the web and coordinators were asked to distribute the URL (uniform resource locator) to those who preferred on-line response. With indirect dissemination of the survey form through telework coordinators and additional usage of the web as an input channel, response rate was not measured, potentially constituting a weakness of this research. A total of 58 teleworkers returned the completed survey.

Given the ordinal nature of Likert-scale data, nonparametric methods including the Wilcoxon ranks test and the Friedman ranks test are used to validate the proposed hypotheses. As an alternative to the paired t-test for quantitative data, the Wilcoxon test compares the difference between two ranking values that are not independent [47]. In our analysis, average ranking values are sequenced from high to low, and then, the neighboring values are compared. The Friedman's ranks test is used to determine the equality of mean ranks among several (nN 2) groups. Unlike the Kruskal–Wallis test that assumes the independence of observations, the Friedman's test is used when ranking values are not independent (i.e., repeatedmeasure design) as our case [47].

## 5. Profile of studied firms

Although a teleworker constitutes the unit of analysis and all teleworkers become the population group of this study, we describe additional information on the surveyed firms to detail the context of survey response and to further the perspective of this research. Relevant information was gathered through interviews for which we contacted at least five persons from each firm. They included telework program committee members, managers, coordinators, and teleworkers. Through the interviews, we learned a great deal about the implementation mode, program size, job category, telework frequency, teleworker qualification, goals and objectives, realized benefits, and the provision of technologies. Some of the key information is summarized here. Table 1 shows that there were more teleworkers than non-teleworkers in company A and company G.

The characteristics of each telework program are summarized in Table 2. Company E had two different telework programs; one of which was implemented by a manager's order (top-down approach), and the other originating in the department of sales. The term “spot office” indicates a workplace where telework was in use temporarily. As shown in Table 3, all cases except two cases (td) had a clearly defined goal(s) for introducing telework. The most noticeable objectives and benefits of telework were increasing productivity and, to a lesser degree, curbing business costs and improving work flexibility. As for productivity changes, each case used its own measures to estimate the effects of telework. Interviews revealed that sales productivity of mobile workers in company A doubled since telework deployment. Case B witnessed a significant increase in productivity, measured by average interview time with customers. In Case C, the productivity increase was estimated through workers' thinking time and production volume of job-related documents. Case D translated commuting-time saved into cost savings.

## 6. Data analysis

## 6.1. Demographics of respondents

Most of the survey participants were male (84%) and only 9 were female (16%). Among the respondents, the majority held an information-system related job (64%), followed by research and development (12.5%) and finance and accounting (9%). This job distribution indicated that surveyed people were mostly professionals undertaking knowledge intensive tasks. The majority of respondents were either mobile workers or telecommuters. More than half of those surveyed were doing either fulltime or near full-time telework. General statistics of participants' demographics and their telework arrangements are summarized in Table 4.

## 6.2. Media suitability for complex communication

We examined teleworkers' perceptions on the suitability of popular media for complex communication (question item 2 in Appendix A). Face-to-face (5.84), telephone (5.67), and email (5.21) showed high mean values. To test Hypothesis 1 (H : All media are equal in their suitability for complex communication.), we relied on the Friedman's ranking test. It (Chi-square = 193.1, df= 5, n= 56, P = .00) confirmed association between complexity in communication and media suitability supporting Hypothesis 1. Even when only face-to-face, telephones, and email were considered as popular communication channels, test statistics (Chi-square = 22.34, df= 2, n= 58, P = .00) were identical to those based on all media.

According to the Wilcoxon ranks tests, teleworkers did not perceive much difference between face-to-face and telephone, but they recognized the limitation of email and other electronic media in handling complex communication (Table 5). Email was considered better than DB/KB and Fax. The tests largely validated the rationality of media richness perception by separating real time media (face to face and telephone) from nonreal time ones (email and others) in handling complex communication.

## 6.4. Knowledge accessibility

In this section, telework and central work are compared in terms of convenience in accessing knowledge at different levels of tacitness. For this, we borrowed the taxonomy scheme of organizational knowledge Wijayanayake [57] introduced based on Wijnhoven [58] and Bohn [7]. The scheme ranked organizational knowledge by eight complexity levels depending on the difficulty of codification (see Appendix B).

Rather than using the taxonomy scheme as it was, we first validated its integrity by asking teleworkers to rank the items by their perceived knowledge complexity (item 6 in Appendix A). The Friedman test (Chisquare = 233; df = 27; P = .00) confirmed a significant difference among mean values. Then, each pair of neighboring values was compared by the Wilcoxon test. The results mostly confirmed Wijayanayake's [57] taxonomy. Teleworkers, however, did not differentiate between level 1 and level 2 (Z = 0.77 and P = 0.44) (see Table 7). Thus, original values of level 1 and level 2 were collapsed in the new seven-layer scheme.

Then, telework and central work were compared in terms of convenience in accessing knowledge at different levels of tacitness (Table 8). The analysis was based on the comparison of survey items 4a and 4b in Appendix A. Statistics indicated that there was no difference between centralized and distributed work arrangements in accessing highly explicit form of knowledge (level 1) such as “data required for work” and “the method of identifying simple problems encountered in a job.” This may be rather natural in that explicit knowledge is easier to structure and codify with available technologies (i.e., knowledge base) and, therefore, geographical separation does not limit its accessibility.

## 6.5. Media usage for knowledge access

In this section, data analysis was made on the survey items of 5a and 5b (Appendix A) to understand teleworkers' media usage to acquire knowledge with varying levels of tacitness. Table 9 summarizes three most popular media chosen to acquire knowledge and their comparisons based on the Wilcoxon test. In telework, email was the premier medium for knowledge exchange at all levels. It was also recognized that telephone usage in telework rose consistently as subject knowledge becomes more tacit supporting Hypothesis 4a. The preference for telephone surpassed that of DB/KB from the third level. Face-to-face was not used much until knowledge tacitness reached the fifth level (knowledge of know-how).

In the central work, DB/KB and email were preferred when exchanged knowledge was at the first level. Faceto-face, however, passed them as the medium of choice when knowledge tacitness belonged to the 3rd level or higher and Hypothesis 4b was largely accepted. Telephone usage grew as well but it was not statistically larger than email usage in general. Overall, heavy usage of email in telework even for the exchange of highly implicit knowledge and much reliance on face-to-face interactions in central work even for the exchange of relatively explicit knowledge were recognized.

## 7. Discussion

Teleworkers recognized that studied media differed in their information richness and therefore in their suitability to support complex communication (Table 5). Face-to-face and telephone channels were regarded information rich and more suitable than any other channels in supporting complex communication. The result agrees with inherent rationality in the information richness of media [12]. The test also showed no statistical difference between face-to-face and telephone, a deviation from the theory in which the former is considered richer than the latter. It appears that non-verbal cues (facial expressions and body languages) from faceto-face were not that critical for teleworkers to have rich communication. This may imply that, in the formal business context, synchronous feedback and verbal expression are more important channel attributes than non-verbal cues to be a rich medium.

There was a significant divergence between telework and central-work in the way communication media were used to locate area experts (Table 6). Searching knowledge expertise mostly involves inquiries that are considered low in the hierarchy of communication complexity [23,45] and may not warrant an information rich channel. In telework, email was the primary choice followed by telephone and DB/KB, and face-to-face was less adopted than electronic media. In central work, however, both face-to-face and email were favored over the others. The result demonstrates that, in both telework and central work, email effectively supported the search task enabled by its functional richness [2,4,15]. Meanwhile, much reliance on face-to-face in central work may indicate that the channel selection was also heavily influenced by social and habitual elements rather than by the rationality of media richness. From the perspective of media theory, the mismatch between the task nature and media choice in central work might negatively affect communication effectiveness and worker productivity.

Respondents indicated that telework made it more difficult to access tacit knowledge. Geographical separation was not a significant barrier in sharing either level 1 or level 7 knowledge (Table 8). We reason that highly explicit (level 1) knowledge can be easily codified and therefore can be conveniently shared through traditional or non-traditional electronic media. Also, highly tacit (level 7) knowledge may be hard to be conversed regardless of media channels being used and may require prolonged engagement for its articulation and relegation. When knowledge tacitness fell between level 2 and level 6, its accessibility was better in the centralized setup, underscoring the importance of improved knowledge accessibility in telework.

Existing studies offer possible avenues to enhance knowledge accessibility among teleworkers. An organization may have to be more active in creating Ba(s) [38], an abstract space for promoting knowledge dissemination. For teleworkers, special emphasis may be placed on offering interacting Ba(s) where tacit knowledge is made explicit and thus support(s) the knowledge externalization (or explication) process [38]. This requires individual or group-based message synchronization through such measures as video/audio conferencing and instant messaging. The accessibility may also be strengthened by increased usage of IT-based corporate knowledge resources. Such system solutions advance information redundancy as a vital element for sharing organizational knowledge [37]. An agent-based information retrieval system [49], case-specific integration of knowledge resources [1], and concept or case-based knowledge modeling [6,9] are among the technical attempts that may enable the sharing of elusive tacit knowledge.

It was shown that the tacitness of shared knowledge affects media choice behavior in both telework and central work. Exchanging highly tacit knowledge resulted in the usage of richer media. Although the patterns of media appropriation support the rationality aspect of media richness, the analysis (see Table 9) also indicates that the richness perception is re-defined by the context and the manner a medium is appropriated [16,17]. That the usage of email was on par with that of telephone in both telework and central work bespeaks growing potential of the former in moving up the media richness ladder. It seems to imply that, in telework, the capacity of an electronic medium to facilitate tacit knowledge sharing becomes more effective when it reaches utility status. As related, embedding or linking collaborative knowledge management functions to a utility application (i.e., email) may be an effective way to improve teleworker productivity. The analysis results also highlight that personal engagements play a supportive role in telework as an access channel for highly unstructured knowledge. The supportive role might be necessary until the technology infrastructure (multi-media applications and network bandwidth) matures enough to make the face-toface option unnecessary and costly.

The discrepancy between telework and central work in the patterns of media usage might have implications on worker productivity. According to the media richness theory, the effectiveness of a communication task is reached when the task at hand matches the type of medium used. This may imply that over-reliance on faceto-face contacts in central work or on electronic medium (especially email) in telework may be counter-productive. The non-discriminating media usage in central work is exemplified by the heavy dependence on face-to-face for searching domain expertise (Table 6) and for accessing explicit knowledge (Table 9). This may be difficult to change because face-to-face interactions have been the cornerstone of organizational communication for so long. Besides, excessive reliance on personal contacts in central work may paradoxically hamper opportunities for knowledge capturing, codification, and explication process via information and communication technologies (ICTs).

Meanwhile, telework may be in a position to better capitalize the strengths of both electronic media and face-to-face encounters. With the distributive work design, with adequate managerial guidance and telework policy, and with unique social structuration of media adoption, teleworkers may become more discrete in media choice to optimize their knowledge work and productivity. Such discretion in media choice may actually reduce the need for frequent face-to-face encounters as electronic media increasingly integrate rich functions in the form of synchronous and verbal connections, and electronic search of knowledge sources. Improved productivity of telework (Table 3) bespeaks the prospect.

## 8. Conclusions

There are elements that might have limited the external validity of our findings. We adopted a single question item for studied constructs to control the volume of the survey; however, this might have lowered their measurement reliability. Teleworkers' dual response for both telework and central work situations could have biased their judgment. The justification was that teleworkers also reported to the central office and accordingly could offer balanced inputs on the studied variables. To avoid the possibility of common method bias, however, future studies can gather survey responses separately from teleworkers and non-teleworkers. Besides, the sample size may not be large enough to claim the generalizability of findings. With larger observation points, we could have conducted more in-depth analysis such as the comparison between overall test results and those based on firms with a high percentage of teleworkers.

Also, the empirical data were gathered in Japan with its own unique cultural and geographical characteristics. Cross-cultural studies will not only improve external validity of the results but also advance understanding of cultural implications on related issues. Lastly, although our study relied on communication media broadly adopted, there are other emerging information and collaboration technologies (i.e., [3,53]) moving up the richness ladder. At the moment, these applications may not be as pervasive as email and telephones, and therefore making a sound judgment on their utility may be difficult. In fact, there was the “Others” category in the survey to include all the other media unspecified. However, responses on their role was weak and the lack of their ubiquity could have been the main reason for the limited recognition. With a good prospect for knowledge sharing, however, the emerging technologies will have to be incorporated into the future research design.

Telework is increasingly becoming an integral part of organizational design. It has a potential to benefit firms in various manners as we are entering the knowledge society. We compared telework and central work in the accessibility of organizational knowledge and in the use of electronic media for knowledge acquisition.
