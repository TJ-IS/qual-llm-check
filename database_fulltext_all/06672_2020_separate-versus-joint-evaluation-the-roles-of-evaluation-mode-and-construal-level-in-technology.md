# SEPARATE VERSUS JOINT EVALUATION: THE ROLES OF EVALUATION MODE AND CONSTRUAL LEVEL IN TECHNOLOGY ADOPTION<sup>1</sup>

Candy K. Y. Ho School of Business, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong SAR CHINA {candyho@hkbu.edu.hk}

Weiling Ke Southern University of Science and Technology, Shenzhen 518055, CHINA {kewl@sustech.edu.cn} and David D. Reh School of Business, Clarkson University, Potsdam, NY 13699 U.S.A. {wke@clarkson.edu}

Hefu Liu School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, Anhui 230026 CHINA {liuhf@ustc.edu.cn}

Patrick Y. K. Chau University of Nottingham Ningbo China, Ningbo, CHINA {Patrick.Chau@nottingham.edu.cn}

Nowadays, people are faced with choices between or among alternative systems (joint evaluation, or JE mode), as opposed to evaluating only one system in isolation (separate evaluation, or SE mode). This research investigates how users make technology adoption decisions in these two contexts. Based on the context-specific theorization framework proposed by Hong et al. (2014) and integrating general evaluability theory and construal level theory, we theorize how evaluation mode (JE versus SE), as a usage context variable, interacts with other aspects of IS contexts to influence technology adoption. The IS contexts considered in this work include a user characteristic variable (construal level) and technology-related factors (perceived ease of use, PEoU, and perceived usefulness, PU). In two studies (N = 272 and N = 112), participants were presented with two new e-learning systems either in JE or SE mode. Because study (1) was conducted in two natural settings and study (2) had participants randomly assigned to manipulated evaluation modes, these two studies complement each other to offer internal and external validities. We found that in JE a higher construal level strengthened the effect of PEoU but weakened that of PU on technology adoption. These effects were reversed in SE. The findings suggest that users’ technology choices and preferences can be reversed by simply presenting the target system in different ways.

Keywords: Evaluation mode, separate evaluation, joint evaluation, construal level, technology adoption

## Introduction

The accelerating technological changes and widespread adoption of technological applications have increased the availability of alternative systems for organizations and indi viduals. For instance, a survey showed that organizations considered multiple software suppliers when choosing an enterprise resource planning system, including SAP and Oracle (Bernroider and Koch 2001). Similarly, for example, when users search for “expense manager” applications in the Apple Store, the search results in over 50 similar applications from which they can choose. Having users evaluate alternative systems side by side (joint evaluation or JE mode), rather than evaluate individual systems separately and in isolation (separate evaluation or SE mode) (Hsee 1996), has become the more prevalent and realistic context of technology adoption (McMurtrey 2013).

Technology adoption has a long history in information systems (IS) research, with a rich understanding of various facets of the phenomenon (for a review, see Venkatesh et al. 2016). Previous studies on technology adoption have not distinguished between JE and SE modes and primarily examined technology adoption only in the context of SE (for reviews, see Venkatesh et al. 2003; Venkatesh et al. 2012, 2016). However, according to general evaluability theory (GET), these two modes of evaluation lead people to apply different evaluation strategies that can lead to varying preferences and even reversals (Hsee 1996; Hsee et al. 1999). Therefore, research investigating how technology adoption decisions are made in JE, which is the more prevalent and realistic context in today’s world, and how such a decision-making process may differ from existing knowledge about technology adoption, which is mostly gained in SE contexts, will enrich our understanding of the domain of technology adoption.

To gain an understanding of the influence of evaluation mode (JE versus SE) on technology adoption, we draw on the contextualization approach suggested by Hong et al. (2014). Specifically, instead of viewing evaluation mode as a standalone contextual variable, we treat it as a construct characterizing the usage context and then examine how it interacts with other aspects of the IS context (i.e., characteristics of technology and user) (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hevner et al. 2004). We use the technology acceptance model (TAM) as a general theory<sup>2</sup> and contextualize it to account for the effects of usage context and user characteristics based on GET (Hsee and Zhang 2010) and construal level theory (CLT) (Trope and Liberman 2010). Drawing on GET, we regard evaluation mode as a usage context that leads users to employ different strategies to evaluate the target system (Hsee 1996; Hsee et al. 1999). Anchoring on CLT, we argue that the ways by which users construe the target system affect the effects of system attributes (perceived ease of use, or PEoU, and perceived usefulness, or PU) on technology adoption (Förster et al. 2008; Liberman and Trope 1998; Trope and Liberman 2010). Further, we contend that in JE, a higher construal level weakens the effects of PU but strengthens the effects of PEoU on users’ attitude toward the focal system. In contrast, we argue that the moderating effects of construal level on the relationships of PEoU and PU with attitude toward the system are reversed in SE. These hypotheses were supported in two experiments. Our findings suggest that simply presenting a target system in different ways induces users to undergo different decision-making processes, which can lead to varying system choices and preferences.

This research makes four major contributions. First, we provide a nuanced understanding of how the technology adoption decision-making may differ between JE and SE. As prior technology adoption research has primarily been conducted in the context of SE, our work sheds new light on the applicability and relevance of existing knowledge to the increasingly prevalent context of JE. Second, as a response to the call for research on context theorization (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hong et al. 2014; Te’eni 2015), we enrich TAM by incorporating multiple aspects of the IS context into theory building. Distinct from prior studies, which have identified the boundary conditions of TAM (e.g., Sun and Zhang 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2003), this research incorporates variables characterizing the usage context and users into TAM and theorizes how these factors interplay with key technology constructs (i.e., PEoU and PU) to influence technology adoption. This approach leads to a more complete model with greater predictive power across situations (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hong et al. 2014). Third, our work contributes to the user resistance literature by identifying evaluation mode and users’ construal level as variables that influence technology adoption. Our findings suggest that construal level differences between users and the management of an organization can help explain user resistance to a system selected by management and that different system presentation strategies can promote system acceptance (Polites and Karahanna 2012; Rivard and Lapointe 2012). Finally, we meaningfully couple GET with CLT to predict how cognitive appraisal aroused by construal level affects the decision making about technology adoption in different evaluation modes. With an increasing number of studies applying CLT to understanding technology adoption (e.g., Huang et al. 2013; Kankanhalli et al. 2015), our work opens up new avenues for investigating the joint influence of construal level and evaluability on technology adoption.

## Theoretical Background and Research Model

Technology adoption is an extensively researched topic in IS discipline. The extant literature has developed various models to determine technology adoption, including but not limited to TAM, unified theory of acceptance and use of technology, diffusion of innovation theory (Rogers 1962), and motivation model (see Venkatesh et al. 2003). Collectively, these models denote three types of factors affecting technology adoption: technology-related variables (e.g., PEoU, PU, technology complexity, and purpose of using technology), user characteristics (e.g., computer self-efficacy and age), and usage context factors (e.g., social norms and voluntariness) (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010; Davis et al. 1989; Morris and Venkatesh 2000; Titah and Barki 2009; Venkatesh and Davis 2000). These determinants constitute the IS context (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hevner et al. 2004), where context refers to “the surroundings associated with phenomena which helps to illuminate the phenomena” (Cappelli and Sherer 1991, p. 56).

Recognizing that context should play a central role in theory development (Johns 2006), researchers have incorporated IS context variables into general technology adoption models (e.g., Brown and Venkatesh 2005; van der Heijden 2004; Hong and Tam 2006; Kulkarni et al. 2006). In particular, past studies have investigated the boundary conditions for the adoption of various IT applications and systems (e.g., text editors, spreadsheet packages, graphics software, groupware, e-commerce, and telemedicine) (e.g., Sun and Zhang 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2003). Prior studies have also examined how different context factors may affect technology adoption as direct antecedents (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2012); indirectly through core system attributes, such as PU (e.g., Agarwal and Prasad 1999; Burton-Jones and Hubona 2006); or by moderating the relationship between core system attributes and user behavior (e.g., Sun and Zhang 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2003). The identified context factors include variables on technological (e.g., technology’s purpose, individual application versus groupware), individual (e.g., age, gender, experience, and computer self-efficacy), and organizational (e.g., voluntariness, and task design) characteristics (Sun and Zhang 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2003).

Our understanding of the effects of IS context still remains limited (Hong et al. 2014; Te’eni 2015). As mentioned earlier, prior studies have been primarily conducted in a context where users evaluate a single system in isolation (e.g., Adams et al. 1992; Agarwal and Prasad 1999; Davis and Venkatesh 1996; Jackson et al. 1997; Tan and Teo 2000; Venkatesh et al. 2003), which is a context that resembles SE. Although a few studies have been conducted in a context where users compare systems, which is a context similar to JE (e.g., Chin and Gopal 1995; Choudhury and Karahanna 2008; Karahanna et al. 2002; Polites and Karahanna 2012; Sun et al. 2016), they have focused on the effects of relative advantage of the new system and not considered how users’ decision making in the JE mode is different from that in the SE mode. Thus, the usage context of evaluation mode (JE versus SE) has not been theorized or tested in the research model in prior research. This gap has not only caused scholars to underestimate the potential influence of evaluation mode in technology adoption, but also limited the applicability of knowledge from the context of SE to the currently prevalent context of JE. Indeed, a recent study found that the influence of system attributes on technology adoption in a JE context is contingent on the construal level at which users represent the technology (Ho et al. 2015). Such insight has not been found in the SE mode. Therefore, we need to formally establish evaluation mode as a usage context factor, incorporate it in a model of technology adoption, and systematically examine how and why it may affect technology adoption decisions. Several dependent variables have been used (e.g., attitude toward using technology, behavioral intention, and usage) to study technology adoption. We use attitude toward using technology, defined as an individual’s overall affective reaction to using a system (Ventakesh et al. 2003), as it is well suited to the voluntary use context (Brown et al. 2008).

A systematic research approach carries importance to IS theory building. According to Hong et al. (2014), theory building requires an examination of the interplay of key aspects of IS context to understand technology adoption. As most previous studies did not examine the joint effects of multiple aspects of IS contexts (i.e., characteristics of technology, user, and usage context), the influence of IS contexts has been underappreciated or unrecognized (see Johns 2006; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). Consequently, a critique about previous studies investigating the influence of IS contexts is that they are often “made in a piecemeal fashion … few have collectively examined how factors pertaining to multiple aspects of the contexts—i.e., technology, user, and usage context—can shape beliefs and behaviors” (Hong et al. 2014, p. 112). Other concerns have also been raised about inadequate attention to context theorization (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hong et al. 2014; Orlikowski and Iacono 2001). Context theorization requires sacrificing the comforts afforded by staying within the paradigm that is most tightly linked to the phenomenon of interest (i.e., technology adoption), identifying surrounding or nested variables typically associated with other paradigms that are likely to influence the focal constructs and relationships (e.g., variables on users and usage context), and explicating how these variables affect the phenomenon under investigation (e.g., examining the mechanisms through which situational characteristics affect technology adoption) (Bamberger 2008). Accordingly, in his call for research on context theorization, Te’eni urges scholars to “get a greater sense of context in questionnaire-based papers, as well as to use other research genres that lend themselves more easily to contextualization” (2015, p. 362).

In response to these calls, the current research investigates variables in three aspects of contexts: PEoU and PU (technology), users’ construal level (user), and evaluation mode (usage context). We use TAM and draw on GET and CLT to theorize how evaluation mode and users’ construal level interact with technology-related factors in influencing technology adoption. In doing so, we aim to develop richer theories that provide accurate, context-specific, and actionable advice (Benbasat and Zmud 2003; Hong et al. 2014; Te’eni 2015).

## General Evaluability Theory and Evaluation Mode

The central principle of GET is that attribute evaluability greatly influences an individual's evaluation and choice. An attribute refers to a quality, character, or characteristic ascribed to an object, event or a person (e.g., income level, price, or ambient temperature; Hsee and Zhang 2010). Evaluability is the extent to which changes in an attribute’s level can be sensed by people and then mapped onto their evaluation of the target (Hsee and Zhang 2010). The evaluability of attributes depends on available reference information, which can be obtained from three sources, namely, evaluation mode, knowledge, and nature. Evaluation mode refers to the way of presenting options, namely whether alternatives are presented side by side (JE mode) or only one option is presented for evaluation in isolation (SE mode). Evaluation mode influences evaluability by providing different reference information about alternative options (explained in detail later). Knowledge refers to the information of an option acquired through learning (e.g., system knowledge or expertise). Nature refers to whether people own an innate physiological reference system to evaluate an option’s attributes (Hsee 1996; Hsee et al. 1999).

We focus on evaluation mode for these reasons. First, evaluation mode concerns two different ways of presenting alternative options resembling the two usage contexts we investigate and thus, this factor is central to our research. Second, we focus on the situation wherein alternative systems are new to users, which means users’ knowledge about these options is limited and should have little impact. Third, nature information is innate and can hardly be manipulated. Therefore, evaluation mode is a context factor that can be readily manipulated to influence evaluability and hence, option choice and evaluation.

In the technology adoption context, there are two possible types of JE: one for running old and new systems in parallel with the intention of switching to the new system and the other for presenting two or more systems with the objective of choosing one out of them. Switching is different from adoption (Carter et al. 2014; Furneaux and Wade 2011). Switching happens at a later stage of the focal technology’s life cycle; it represents a change in the status quo or a natural progression along the continuum of technology adoption (Schwarz and Chin 2007). Therefore, the driving forces for switching are different from those forces for adoption (Furneaux and Wade 2011). We focus on JE for choosing, rather than JE for switching. This is because we investigate users’ adoption decision making when the target system is presented with other options in different manners.

According to GET, evaluation mode influences the evaluation of alternative options by providing different reference information. In JE, individuals can take an option as a reference point to evaluate another option (Goldstein and Einhorn 1987; Hsee and Zhang 2010). This setting drives an evaluation strategy of comparing options by using common attributes such as comparing two systems based on PEoU or PU. In contrast, in SE, individuals lack the corresponding attribute information of another option as reference (Goldstein and Einhorn 1987; Hsee and Zhang 2010). The value of another attribute of the same option is more readily available and becomes the reference point, directing people to evaluate an option by comparing its various attributes (e.g., this system is useful but not easy to use) (Hsee 1996). Many empirical studies have demonstrated that the different ways of evaluation from the two evaluation modes cause systematically different or even reversed option choices (Hsee et al. 1999; Hsee and Zhang 2010). Hence, people do evaluate options differently in JE versus SE modes.

## Construal Level Theory

Individuals can represent an object or event at different levels of concreteness or abstraction (Trope and Liberman 2003, 2010). A high construal level leads individuals to represent an object in terms of (1) abstract, superordinate, and decontextualized features that convey the general core information and (2) the why aspect or desirability features. In contrast, a low construal level drives individuals to represent an object in terms of (1) concrete, subordinate, and contextualized features that convey specific details and (2) the how aspect or feasibility features (Trope and Liberman 2003). For instance, studying can be viewed as an act of gaining knowledge (abstract representation) or taking notes (concrete representation). Individuals can also construe studying by its value (why, desirability aspect) or its means (how, feasibility aspect).

According to CLT, representing an object or event in abstract/concrete features can have a profound influence on evaluation. Specifically, a higher construal level leads people to construe the object in a more schematic and abstract manner by which only the fundamental core characteristics are retained (Trope and Liberman 2010). Such abstraction makes the object less disparate and more similar to other alternative objects in terms of their core, primary features (Förster et al. 2008; Liberman et al. 2002; Malkoc et al. 2005; Nussinson et al. 2011). This effect of increased similarity among objects resulting from abstract representation is known as the abstraction hypothesis of construal level (Goodman and Malkoc 2012) and has been demonstrated in many studies. For instance, Levy et al. (2002) showed that when people have a higher construal mindset, they view higher similarity between themselves and others (see also McCrea et al. 2012). Similarly, a higher construal level induces more inclusive categorization (Lieberman et al. 2002; Wakslak et al. 2008), such as categorizing tablets and digital cameras, two apparently dissimilar objects, into a common, super-ordinate category of electronic devices (see also Förster et al. 2008; Isen and Daubman 1984).

Nevertheless, when people construe an object in terms of feasibility/desirability features, a higher construal level drives them to focus more on desirability features (e.g., whether the lecture topic is interesting) and to evaluate the object primarily based on these features. Meanwhile, a lower construal level induces people to focus more on feasibility features (e.g., whether the lecture is held at a convenient location) (Liberman and Trope 1998) and to rely more heavily on them when evaluating the object. This effect, termed the feasibility/desirability hypothesis of construal level (Goodman and Malkoc 2012), has empirical support (e.g., Förster et al. 2009; Liviatan et al. 2008). For example, Förster et al. (2009) showed that presenting a product as novel (i.e., relatively unfamiliar, less probable and hence, of higher construal level), rather than old, induces participants to assign greater weight to the product’s desirability features and less weight to its feasibility features in product evaluation.

As the abstraction and feasibility/desirability hypotheses of CLT have empirical support, a question that arises is: Under what circumstances would each hypothesis prevail? In the context of technology adoption, we argue that evaluation mode is the contextual factor that determines when each hypothesis of construal level is more predictive of user evaluation and choice. As we articulate below, simply presenting alternative systems in SE versus JE modes can induce different effects of construal level that can lead to different, or even reversed, technology preferences.

## Interplay among System Attributes, Construal Level, and Evaluation Mode

Based on the characteristics of high- and low-level representations stated in CLT, PEoU and PU can be considered as system attributes with different construal levels (Ho et al. 2015). PEoU, as a process expectancy variable (Venkatesh 1999, 2000), is a secondary, feasibility-focused system attribute. In contrast, PU, as an outcome expectancy variable (Venkatesh 1999, 2000; Venkatesh and Davis 2000), is a primary, desirability-focused system attribute. With this difference in construal level between PEoU and PU, we propose that their effects on technology adoption can be moderated by users’ construal level that can be further moderated by evaluation mode.

Central to this research, evaluation mode is posited as the factor that determines when the abstraction or the feasibility/ desirability hypothesis of construal level prevails. As mentioned above, the abstraction hypothesis of construal level influences the perceived similarity of the primary features of different objects. An implicit assumption is that more than one object should be present in a situation for the abstraction hypothesis to show effects. Indeed, a prior study has found that when alternative options are presented simultaneously for choice, the abstraction hypothesis prevails (Goodman and Malkoc 2012).

Specifically, in JE, users have information about alternative systems that are juxtaposed for comparison. As suggested by GET, users tend to conduct cross-system comparisons based on common attributes (Hsee 1996; Hsee and Zhang 2010). This context enhances the evaluability of common attributes across alterative systems, making users sensitive to the similarities and differences in the levels of common attributes across these systems (e.g., system X and Y are similarly useful, but system X is easier to use than system Y). In addition, according to the abstraction hypothesis of construal level, when alternative systems are represented in higher, more abstract terms, they should be seen as more similar in terms of their primary features (i.e., PU) (Förster et al. 2008; Liberman et al. 2002; Malkoc et al. 2005; Nussinson et al. 2011). Taking e-learning system as an example, different e-learning systems should more or less achieve the high-order goal of facilitating teaching and learning; thus, they can be perceived as similarly useful in this aspect. In contrast, the perceived similarity of the secondary attribute of alternative systems, PEoU, is not influenced by the abstraction hypothesis. PEoU will become a more diagnostic attribute on which users can rely to evaluate the systems. Therefore, in JE, a higher construal level decreases the effect of PU but increases that of PEoU in shaping users’ system evaluation and acceptance decisions. Indeed, this prediction has received empirical support in prior studies conducted in the JE context. For example, Ho et al. (2015) found that users with higher construal level rely more on PEoU and less on PU in system evaluation. In addition, Sun et al. (2016) demonstrated that mindfulness, a state of mind focusing on here and now and pays attention to the details in the immediate surroundings (Butler and Gray 2006; Thatcher et al. 2018), which is consistent with a low construal mindset, that in turn can enhance the importance of PU when evaluating alternative systems.

In contrast, SE is a usage context in which the feasibility/ desirability hypothesis of construal level prevails. In SE, users evaluate each alternative system one at a time. According to GET, users in this situation tend to evaluate one system attribute with reference to the other attribute of the same system (Hsee and Zhang 2010). For example, users may think a simple and basic e-learning system is easy to use but not very useful. This kind of cross-attribute, same-system evaluation renders comparisons between desirability and feasibility attributes (i.e., PU versus PEoU) salient. Prior research has shown that, in this kind of situation, the feasibility/desirability hypothesis becomes dominant and drives people’s evaluation (Goodman and Malkoc 2012). Therefore, we predict that, in SE, users with a higher construal level rely more on desirability features (i.e., PU), whereas users with a lower construal level emphasize feasibility features (i.e., PEoU) when making system evaluation and acceptance decisions.

Our model, shown in Figure 1, comprises two hypotheses:

H1: In JE, a higher construal level strengthens the positive effect of PEoU on attitude toward using the focal system. In contrast, in SE, a higher construal level weakens the positive effect of PEoU on attitude toward using the focal system.

H2: In JE, a higher construal level weakens the positive effect of PU on attitude toward using the focal system. In contrast, in SE, a higher construal level strengthens the positive effect of PU on attitude toward using the focal system.

## Methods and Results

## Overview of Research Design

To test our hypotheses, we conducted two studies. In Study 1, we tested our predictions in two natural settings that resembled the contexts of JE and SE. This design allows the deployment of the research in a real context, but lacks the random assignment of participants into the two evaluation mode conditions. Consequently, this design might cause concerns about possible sample bias and internal validity of findings. Therefore, we conducted Study 2 in which we manipulated evaluation mode with the random assignment of participants into either the JE or SE mode. The two studies complement each other to offer reasonable levels of internal and external validities.

## Study 1

## Method

Design, Participants, and Procedure: Study 1 had a 2 (evaluation mode: JE versus SE) × 2 (construal level: low versus high) between-subjects design. We operationalized evaluation mode by conducting the study in two universities. University A had adopted a new e-learning system with which participants did not have any experience, allowing us to ensure that knowledge information would not cause any effects on the evaluability of the system. Both the old and new systems were running concurrently; users could select either system to use. Although University A planned to phase out one of the e-learning systems eventually, it neither had a predetermined decision on which system to phase out nor a specific cut-off date. Such decisions were to depend on the responses of users. The context in University A constitutes a JE mode. Data collected from University A were reported in Ho et al. (2015). In comparison, University B did not have an existing e-learning system. We presented University B participants with the new e-learning system that University A had adopted and asked them to evaluate it. Under this condition, participants from University B were in an SE mode. A total of 272 participants (131 from University A and 141 from University B; see Appendix A for demographic information) participated in the study for a monetary incentive of about U.S. \$5.

The participants were informed by the investigator that the study aimed to gather student opinions on a new e-learning system recently adopted by the university. They were required to try out the new system following an instruction sheet on how to access and use the system. We manipulated construal level (high versus low) by randomly distributing one of the two versions of the instruction sheet to the participants (see the “Construal Level Manipulation” section). The participants had 12 minutes for the tryout. Observations from the investigator indicated that all participants found this duration sufficient.

After the 12-minute trial, all participants filled out an online questionnaire that contained measures pertaining to construal level, PEoU, PU, attitude toward the new system, and demographic information (see the “Measures” section for details). Each participant spent a total of approximately 30 minutes. Finally, they were debriefed, paid, and thanked for their participation.

Construal Level Manipulation: Following the work of Freitas et al. (2004), we instructed participants in the high and low construal level conditions to think about the objectives of using the new e-learning system for their study and to think about the ways in which they would use the new e-learning system. The instructions are shown in Appendix B. After reading the instructions, participants in the high construal level conditions were asked to report the value of using Blackboard (i.e., the new e-learning system) to (1) obtain course information and materials, (2) prepare for the tests and assessments of the course, and (3) interact with the course instructor and other students in the course by completing the sentence “WHY? I would use Blackboard to do so because” for each objective. In the same manner, the participants in the low construal level conditions were asked to report the ways of using Blackboard to achieve the three objectives by completing the sentence “HOW? I would use Blackboard to do so by” for each objective.

Measures: All items (listed in Appendix C) were adapted from existing literature. The items measuring construal level were on a scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much), and the items measuring PEoU, PU, and attitude toward using the system were on a scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

## Analysis and Results

We used SPSS (version 22) for our analyses.

Preliminary Analyses: To test the effectiveness of the construal level manipulation, we calculated the relative construal level score of each participant. This score is calculated by subtracting the mean of the low construal level items from the mean of the high construal level items. A positive score indicates a relatively high construal level, whereas a negative score indicates a relatively low construal level. We ran a 2 (evaluation mode) × 2 (construal level) ANOVA on the relative construal level score. The results revealed only a main effect of construal level, F(1, 270) = 9.47, p < 0.01. Participants in the high construal level condition construed the e-learning system at a significantly more abstract level (M = 0.04) than those in the low construal level condition (M = -0.26). All other effects were non-significant. Thus, the results confirmed the effectiveness of the construal level manipulation.

Supplementary Analysis: In addition to analyzing the change in the effects of PEoU and PU on attitude in different evaluation modes and construal levels (i.e., H1 and H2), we compared the magnitude of the effects of PEoU and PU in these different conditions by using multi-group analysis. Conversely, at a lower construal level (one standard deviation below the mean), the effect of PEoU was significantly weaker $( \Delta \beta = . 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ whereas that of PU was significantly stronger $( \Delta \beta = 0 . 2 0$ $p { < } 0 . 0 1 $ ) among the JE mode participants than that among the SE mode participants. Consistent with the hypothesis, participants with high (low) construal level primarily evaluated a system based more on PEoU (PU) in JE but based more on PU (PEoU) in SE. Therefore, participants’ system choice could be reversed between JE and SE modes.

## Discussion

Study 1 confirmed that system attributes, users’ construal level, and evaluation mode collectively affect technology adoption decision. Participants’ level of construal moderated the positive effects of PEoU and PU on attitude toward using the system. In addition, such moderating effects were contingent on the usage context. In JE, a higher construal level strengthened the effect of PEoU but weakened that of PU. In contrast, in SE, a higher construal level weakened and strengthened the effects of PEoU and PU, respectively. These effects could result in reversed system preferences between JE and SE modes.

Study 1 has a limitation in that we did not manipulate evaluation mode but only inferred it from real-life situations resembling JE and SE modes. Although findings from reallife situations generally have good external validity, their internal validity may be uncertain. In particular, without random assignment, numerous systematic differences may exist between these two groups of participants that may have been confounded with the JE and SE classification and driven the effects in our predicted direction. For instance, University A participants were mostly undergraduate students, whereas University B participants were mostly postgraduate students. One could speculate that the flip in the result patterns between the two evaluation mode conditions may be due to this difference between the two groups of participants. To address this issue, we conducted Study 2, in which evaluation mode was manipulated and the participants were randomly assigned to treatment conditions. In addition, we developed two hypothetical alternative systems, whereby participants could not have prior knowledge about the system. Moreover, the random assignment of participants eliminated the potential differences of nature reference information caused by physiological systems of participants in different conditions and other potential sample biases.

## Study 2

## Method

Design, Participants, and Procedures: Study 2 had a 2 (evaluation mode: JE versus SE) × 2 (construal level: low versus high) × 2 (system version: X [high PEoU but moderate PU] versus Y [moderate PEoU but high PU]) mixed design, in which evaluation mode and construal level were manipulated between-subjects and system version was manipulated withinsubjects. In JE mode, if a higher construal level increases the importance of PEoU and decreases that of PU, then the participants in the high construal level condition should show a higher relative preference for System X (the PEoUdominant system) over System Y (the PU-dominant system) than those in the low construal level condition (i.e., a construal level × system version interaction). Conversely, in SE mode, if a higher construal level increases the importance of PU and decreases that of PEoU, then the participants in the high construal level condition should have a higher relative preference for System Y over System X than those in the low construal condition (i.e., also a construal level × system version interaction). These results will be evidenced by an interaction effect of all three manipulated variables on attitude toward using the system.

A total of 112 students in a major Asian university participated in the study for course credit. Among these participants, 83 (29) were undergraduates (postgraduates); 23 (60) participants were men (women) and 29 participants did not provide their gender information. The mean age of participants was 21 years.

The participants were told they participated in a “study on e-learning system.” They were randomly assigned to one of the four (construal level × evaluation mode) conditions. First, the participants were subjected to construal level manipulation by completing either the high or low construal level version of the instruction sheet on evaluating e-learning systems (see the “Manipulations” section). Then, they were randomly given either of the two versions of the questionnaire. Both questionnaire versions contained identical information about two e-learning systems but only differed in the way of presenting the information of the two systems (see “Manipulations”). Subsequently, the participants responded to items measuring construal level (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.70 for high construal level and 0.79 for low construal level), attitude toward using System X (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) and System Y (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85), and demographic information. The items were similar to that in Study 1, with slight modifications to fit the current context (see Appendix C). Each participant spent a total of approximately 30 minutes. Finally, they were debriefed, paid, and thanked for their participation.

Manipulations: Manipulation of construal level followed that of Study 1, with three exceptions. First, the instructions were slightly modified to fit the context of Study 2. Second, after reading the instructions, participants in the high construal level conditions enumerated three important life goals that making good use of an e-learning system could help to achieve. The participants were then required to rate the extent to which making good use of an e-learning system could help them meet each of the three goals on a scale from 1 (a little) to 7 (very, very much). Similarly, participants in the low construal level conditions were asked to enumerate three ways in which they could make good use of an e-learning system. For each way listed, they rated how much engaging in each activity would help them make good use of an e-learning system on the scale described above. Third, to strengthen the manipulation, we followed Freitas et al. (2004) and asked participants to complete a diagram that guided those in high construal conditions to think more abstractly about how making good use of an e-learning system could help them achieve their important life goals, and those in low construal level conditions to contemplate how they could make good use of an e-learning system in more concrete terms.

To manipulate system version, in the e-learning system questionnaire, System X was described as “excellent” (rated five out of five stars) in terms of operation and ease of use but “moderate” (rated three out of five stars) in terms of functionality and usefulness. System Y was described as “moderate” in terms of operation and ease of use, yet “excellent” in terms of functionality and usefulness. Appendix F provides the details of the system description.

We manipulated evaluation mode by developing two versions of the e-learning system questionnaire, the SE and JE versions. They featured the same system descriptions but presented the information differently. In the SE version, the two e-learning systems were presented sequentially. The participants responded to questions about a system immediately after its information was presented. The order of presenting the two systems was counterbalanced. In the JE version, the two e-learning systems were presented side by side on the same page, followed by the measures about these two systems in tandem. The position (left versus right) at which the two systems were presented was also counterbalanced.

## Analysis and Results

We used SPSS (version 22) to analyze the data.

Preliminary Analyses: We calculated the relative construal level score of each participant by employing the method described in Study 1 and subjected the score to a 2 (evaluation mode) × 2 (construal level) ANOVA. The results revealed only a main effect of construal level, F(1, 108) = 26.73, p < 0.001. Participants in the high construal level conditions (M = 0.35) reported a higher relative construal level score than those in the low construal level conditions (M = -0.72). No other effects were significant. The construal level manipulation was deemed effective. The descriptive statistics of the variables are shown in Table 2.

Hypothesis Testing: A 2 (evaluation mode) × 2 (construal level) × 2 (attitude toward using system: System X versus System Y) repeated-measures ANOVA with the last factor as the within-subject factor revealed that the attitudes toward using the two systems were significantly different, F(1, 108) = 9.63, p < 0.01. Participants had a more favorable attitude toward System X (M = 4.88) than System Y (M = 4.54). Importantly, this effect was qualified by a significant threeway interaction, F(1, 108) = 22.36, p < 0.001. Consistent with our prediction, in JE mode, the interaction between construal level and attitude toward using system was significant, F(1, 108) = 7.05, p < 0.01. Participants in the high construal level condition had a significantly more favorable attitude toward using System X (M = 5.06) than System Y (M = 4.22; difference = 0.84; F(1, 108) = 8.33, p < 0.001). In contrast, participants in the low construal level condition had comparable attitudes toward using System X (M = 4.83) and System Y (M = 4.84; difference = 0.01; F < 1, NS). In SE mode, the interaction between construal level and attitude toward using system was also significant, F(1, 108) = 16.61, p < 0.001. Although participants in the high construal level condition had a marginally more favorable attitude toward using System Y (M = 4.82) than System X (M = 4.47; difference = 0.35; F(1, 108) = 2.76, p = 0.10), those in the low construal level condition had a significantly more favorable attitude toward using System X (M = 5.16) than System Y (M = 4.27; difference = 0.89; F(1, 108) = 16.34, p < 0.001). Collectively, the results confirmed our predictions and supported H1 and H2.

## Discussion

Study 2 showed that, in JE, high construal level participants exhibited a more favorable attitude toward using a PEoUdominant (versus PU-dominant) system than low construal level participants. Conversely, in SE, high construal level participants had a more favorable attitude toward using a PUdominant (versus PEoU-dominant) system than low construal level participants did. Such results are consistent with those of Study 1, thereby limiting the possibility of sample bias.

## Discussion

Having users evaluate alternative technologies side by side (JE mode) versus evaluate individual technology separately and in isolation (SE mode) are two widely prevalent but distinct technology adoption contexts. Yet their influences on user decision making has been under-researched. This research fills this gap by taking a context-specific theorization framework proposed by Hong et al. (2014) to study the contextual influence of evaluation mode, together with construal level (a user characteristic variable), PEoU and PU (technology-related factors) in technology adoption. Two studies in which the participants were presented with two new e-learning systems either in JE or SE mode showed that, in JE, a higher construal level strengthened the effect of PEoU but weakened that of PU on technology adoption. These effects were reversed in SE. This research is among the first that systematically incorporates evaluation mode into a research model and shows how and why users’ technology choices and preferences can be reversed by simply presenting a target system in different ways.

## Theoretical Contributions

This research makes four key theoretical contributions. First, it enriches the technology adoption literature by providing a nuanced understanding of how technology adoption decisions are influenced by the way a system is presented (i.e., evaluation mode). We theorized and showed that PEoU and PU, the technology-related determinants of technology adoption identified in TAM, remain influential in different usage contexts (JE and SE) and among users with different construal levels. However, their relative effects vary across these user and usage contexts. Evaluation mode and construal level are new and increasingly popular concepts in the study of technology adoption (e.g., Huang et al. 2013; Kankanhalli et al. 2015). By introducing these concepts into TAM and examining their inter-relationships, we enrich the literature by increasing the predictive power of TAM across different usage contexts and among different users, and by offering a theoretical explanation for why and how user decision-making processes vary in different situations. In addition, our findings shed new light on the applicability of existing knowledge, which is derived from a plethora of studies conducted in SE mode, to the situations in which users evaluate a system juxtaposed with one or more alternative systems. As the JE usage context becomes increasingly prevalent, our research enhances the relevance of the body of work on technology adoption.

Second, this research responds to the call for context theorization made by Benbasat and Zmud (1999), Hong et al. (2014), and Te’eni (2015). Benbasat and Zmud (1999, p. 9) claimed that “it is imperative that the IS research community produce cumulative, theory-based, context-rich bodies of research.” Building on GET and CLT, the present research is distinct from previous contextualization efforts in IS research by its theorization and examination of how all three aspects of

IS context, namely technology, user, and usage context, collectively affect user behavior. By such situational linking, our work makes the “research model more accurate and our interpretation of results more robust” (Rousseau and Fried 2001, p. 2) as it allows the audiences to better assess the applicability of the technology adoption theory and our findings.

Third, our findings provide new directions to the user resistance literature. Prior research has suggested various reasons for user resistance to technology systems such as changes in job conditions, shift of power, and job characteristics (Bala and Venkatesh 2013; Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005; Boudreau and Robey 2005; Kim and Kankanhalli 2009; Lapointe and Rivard 2005; Robert and Sykes 2016; Sykes 2015). However, research examining why users and the management have different attitudes toward a newly adopted system is insufficient. Our findings extend this line of research by highlighting the effects of construal level on the cognitive process of system evaluation. The levels at which users and the management construe the system typically vary because of their different status within the organizational hierarchy and structure. Our research offers a plausible explanation of how such an incongruence could result in user resistance to a system selected by the management.

Fourth, our research contributes to the extant literature by coupling GET with CLT. Although both GET and CLT provide predictions of preference reversals, the mechanisms they articulate vary. GET is primarily applied to and tested in hypothetical or relatively trivial judgment contexts with objective information of options provided to individuals (Hsee and Zhang 2010). Accordingly, GET focuses on comparing the evaluability of options in different evaluation modes. In contrast, CLT considers how the representation of alternatives affects judgment and decision making. In CLT, people appraise the options and construct their understanding of attribute values. This situation is more complex but may be more realistic than the phenomena investigated by previous studies applying GET. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first to propose that GET and CLT can complement each other to hypothesize how cognitive appraisal aroused by construal level affects decision making in different evaluation modes. The number of IS studies applying CLT to understand the effects of construal level on technology adoption is increasing (e.g., Huang et al. 2013; Kankanhalli et al. 2015). We hope our attempt to integrate GET and CLT may open up new avenues for research investigating the joint influence of construal level and evaluability on technology adoption.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

We note two key limitations. First, the realism of Study 2 may be questioned because we measured participants’ attitude after they went through the system descriptions. Although such a study design could decrease the external validity of our findings, it was necessary for the sake of the internal validity. Manipulating the levels of PEoU and PU by providing written descriptions allowed us a tight control over participants’ perceived values of these attributes, enabling us to compare the effects of PEoU and PU based on participants’ system preferences. The reduced external validity of Study 2 was complemented by Study 1, which allowed participants to try out the system before forming an attitude. Despite having their own limitations, these two studies complement each other to ensure that our findings have a reasonable level of internal and external validities. Second, as the first study examining how user decision making differs between the situation of evaluating the focal system in JE and SE modes, this research focuses on how evaluation mode and users’ construal level interact with system-related determinants of technology adoption. Although doing so allows us to obtain a parsimonious model, this model does not consider the effects of other possible critical factors and misses the chance to offer a more complete articulation of the evaluation and decisionmaking processes involved. Future research can explore how other sources of reference information suggested by GET (e.g., knowledge and nature) may interact with factors influencing construal level and jointly affect the effects of technology adoption determinants. For instance, one meaningful direction for future research is investigating how user experience (i.e., one type of learned knowledge) may interact with construal level resulting from similarity with important others (e.g., peers) to jointly influence the effects of social norm, an established determinant of technology adoption (Liviatan et al. 2008; Sun and Zhang 2006; Venkatesh et al. 2003). In addition, in JE mode, reference options affect individuals’ evaluation of the target system, which implies that there could exist anchoring bias. The current research did not examine the effects of such a bias. We suggest future research examine how the anchoring bias can affect individual decision making.

## Practical implications

This research guides system implementers on how to promote technology adoption in different situations. Managers can provide training to adjust users’ construal levels that match the implementation strategy to facilitate users to accept a system. For example, when an organization is adopting a complex system, a single system implementation strategy, together with training that primes users with a high construal level, can lead users to rely more on PU and less on PEoU in their decision making. Subsequently, this strategy can facilitate the formation of a favorable attitude toward using the system. Other than priming users through trainings, the management can leverage system design to arouse construal level conducive to adoption. For example, pictorial expression, rather than verbal description, in the interface can guide users to represent the system more concretely (Amit et al. 2013). In addition, a black-and-white interface design can lead to a higher construal level than a colorful interface can (Lee et al. 2014). Therefore, based on the implementation strategy (single versus parallel) and the nature of the system (simple versus complex), the organization can utilize appropriate design features to promote technology adoption.

Our findings can also help reduce preference incongruence in organizational system adoption. A time delay usually exists between the time when the management selects a new system and the time when users adopt the system. The former constitutes a distal decision made by the management, whereas the latter is a proximal action by users. The difference in temporal distance, together with the difference in psychological distance caused by the power of the two parties in an organization, can make management construe the system at a higher level and users construe the system at a lower level (Zhao et al. 2007; see also Venkatash et al. 2006). Therefore, management may select a PU-dominant system, but users may prefer a PEoU-dominant system. Our findings suggest that, in order to align the focus of the users with that of the management, organizations can adopt a parallel system implementation strategy (i.e., running alternative systems in parallel at the beginning of implementation stage). Such a strategy could guide users to place greater emphasis on the PU of the system and be more inclined to accept the system chosen by management.

Finally, this research offers practical implications for system designers in applying a prototyping methodology. Our findings suggest that user feedback collected from beta testing without alternative systems in the background (i.e., a SE mode) differs from the evaluation of users when they are deciding whether to adopt a system, which is typically in a JE mode. For instance, users who focus more on PU in prototype testing would value PEoU more in an adoption decision.

Hence, system designers can benefit from considering the possible effects of evaluation context when using user feedback to improve the system. System designers can also obtain data on how users represent the prototype when they are performing the evaluation. With such information, system designers can acquire precise understanding of user experience and expectations (Brown et al. 2012; Ventakesh and Goyal 2010) that can then improve the efficiency and effectiveness of prototyping.

## Instructions for Construal Level Manipulation: Study 1

Instructions for the high construal level condition:

For everything we do, there always is a reason why we do it. Moreover, we often can trace the causes of our behavior back to broad life goals that we have. For example, you are about to learn using a new e-learning platform that supports courses. Why are you doing this? Perhaps to take and complete courses. Why do you take and complete courses? Perhaps because you want to earn a college degree. Why do you want to earn a college degree? Maybe because you want to find a good job or because you want to educate yourself. And perhaps you wish to educate yourself or find a good job because you feel that doing so can bring you happiness in life

Similarly, in this study, we would like you to think why you would use Blackboard, a new e-learning platform, for your study at the University.

Instructions for the low construal level condition:

For everything we do, there always is a process of how we do it. Moreover, we often can follow our broad life goals down to our very specific behaviors. For example, like most people, you probably hope to find happiness in life. How can you do this? Perhaps finding a good job or being educated can help. How can you do these things? Perhaps by earning a college degree. How do you earn a college degree? By taking and completing courses. How do you take and complete courses? In some cases, you need to learn using an e-learning platform that supports courses

Similarly, in this study, we would like you to think how you would use Blackboard, a new e-learning platform, for your study at the University.

## Common Method Bias, Reliability, Validly, and Multicollinearity Analyses: Study 1

Common Method Bias: Common method bias may have occurred because the data were collected simultaneously from a single source. We employed two methods to test the possible common method bias. First, Harmon’s single-factor test was employed. The results showed that all items can be categorized into three constructs with eigenvalues above 1.0, accounting for 73.20% of the total variance. The first factor only accounted for 28.34% of the variance, which did not constitute the majority of the total variance. These results indicated that the common method bias was not a concern in the current study. Further, following Podsakoff et al. (2003) and Spector (2006), we used a three-item scale for prevention focus as a method variance (MV) marker to assess common method bias. Prevention focus measures the extent to which an individual is concerned about security, safety, and fulfillment of responsibilities (Higgins et al. 2001). This construct, as a trait variable, was not theoretically related to other constructs in our analysis. The results showed that the MV marker had the lowest positive correlation with PEoU (r = 0.004). We used this correlation to adjust the correlations among the constructs and compare the adjusted correlations (presented above the diagonal of Table 1) with the unadjusted correlations (presented below the diagonal of Table 1). All the 31 correlations remained significant after adjustment, indicating that common method bias was not a serious concern for the study. To diminish the potential effects of common method bias, we included the MV marker as a control variable in hypothesis testing.

Reliability and Validity: We used LISREL 8.7 to test the reliability and validity of our scales. Results from confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that the measurement model fit was acceptable (÷<sup>2</sup>/df = 328.08/155 = 2.12; CFI = 0.98; IFI = 0.98; NFI = 0.97; NNFI = 0.98; RMSEA = 0.064). The reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of the measures were tested as well. Table 2 shows that the Cronbach’s alphas of the constructs ranged from 0.616 to 0.927, which are higher than the benchmark value of 0.60. The constructs’ composite reliabilities ranged from 0.797 to 0.949, which were all above the recommended level of 0.70. These results reflected a good reliability of the scales. Convergent validity was tested based on the value of the loading and average variance extracted (AVE). The loadings of all items were higher than 0.70, with the significant t values at the p < 0.01 level. The AVEs ranged from 0.567 to 0.861, which were above the recommended level of 0.50. Thus, the scales exhibited good convergent validity.

To assess the discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE of each construct was calculated and then compared with the correlations among the constructs. As shown in Table 1, the square roots of the AVEs of the constructs, which are presented on the diagonal of the table, were greater than the correlations among the constructs, which are presented below the diagonal. Therefore, the measures achieved adequate discriminant validity. Given that several inter-construct correlations were high, we conducted chi-square tests to compare the chi-square differences between the constrained and unconstrained models. All chi-square differences for the highly correlated constructs were significant, indicating good discriminant validity.

Multicollinearity: Table 1 also shows that several of the inter-construct correlations were higher than 0.60, indicating the potential of multicollinearity. Multicollinearity is evidenced by a variance inflation factor (VIF) value that is higher than 10 or a tolerance value less than 0.1 (Mason and Perreault 1991). The multicollinearity test showed that the highest VIF value and the lowest tolerance value were 3.57 and 0.28, respectively. Thus, multicollinearity was not a concern in our study.
