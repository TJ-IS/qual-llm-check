# Blogging for ICT4D: reflecting and engaging with peers to build development discourse

Julie Ferguson,\* Maura Soekijad,<sup>†</sup> Marleen Huysman<sup>‡</sup> & Emmanuelle Vaast<sup>§</sup>

\*Faculty of Social Sciences, Department of Organization Sciences, VU University Amsterdam, De Boelelaan 1081, Room N-320, 1081 HV Amsterdam, the Netherlands, email: julie.ferguson@vu.nl, Faculty of Business Administration, Research group Knowledge, Information & Networks, VU University Amsterdam, De Boelelaan 1105, Room 3A-15, 1081 HV Amsterdam, the Netherlands, email: <sup>†</sup>m.soekijad@vu.nl, <sup>‡</sup>m.h.huysman@vu.nl, and <sup>§</sup>Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Bronfman Building. 1001 Sherbrooke Street, West Montreal. Quebec H3A 1G5. Canada

Abstract. Information and Communication Technology-enabled Development (ICT4D) discourse relies upon the idea that ICTs can foster development in particular by encouraging wider participation in development initiatives. In this paper, we question how the blogging practices of development professionals shape such ICT4D discourse. Through a combination of interviews and analyses of blog contents, we examine two major purposes of blogging: reflecting upon development practices and engaging with a self-selected audience. Our analyses reveal that these two purposes were interwoven in ways that contributed to making bloggers ICT4D discourse innovative but oriented towards a small community of peers rather than a larger audience. Through blogging, development professionals refined their expertise on ICT4D. As they did so, they also generated a personal speaker’s corner that primarily attracted like-minded peers rather than promoting larger participation in ICT4D discourse. This research contributes to the emerging literature on socia media practices by showing how blogging practices enable the formation of what a discourse is about, and by highlighting differences between perceived and actual levels of interactions between bloggers and their audience. The paper also adds to the ICT and development literatures by revealing that blogging practices can deepen ICT4D discourse, but that they do not necessarily enhance participation in development. Such insight is crucial for development professionals to develop realistic expectations of blogging for ICT4D.

Keywords: blogging, development professionals, discourse, ICT4D, participation, social media

The first two authors have contributed equally to this paper.

## ICT4D AND ITS DISCOURSE

Information and Communication Technology (ICT)-enabled development, or ‘ICT4D’ promotes technology adoption to support international development (Thompson, 2004; Datta, 2011). A key assumption of ICT4D is that ICTs can empower people in developing countries by amplifying their voices and strengthening their participation in decision-making processes (Hickey & Mohan, 2004; Walsham et al., 2007; Ferguson et al., 2010; Zuckerman, 2010). In such a perspective, making ICTs available to marginalised populations can help them access more resources and gain new opportunities (Ganesh & Barber, 2009). While previously Information Systems (IS) literature seemed predominantly biased towards North American and Western European environments, more recently a field of ICT4D is developing. Discussions on ICT4D emerged in the late 1990s and early 2000s in the wake of the publication of a seminal World Bank (1998) report and the first internet boom. The World Bank Development report emphasised access to information as a condition for development, and highlighted ICT as an important enabler in this regard. In 2002 and 2005, the United Nations convened the ‘World Summit on the Information Society’, a multilateral forum that also strongly recognised the significance of ICT4D.

ICT4D brings together heterogeneous development professionals, including donors, non-governmental organisations’ (NGOs) activists, government policy officers, consultants, academics, intended beneficiaries and so forth, who are active in various development aid organisations distributed all over the world (Covey, 1995; Heeks, 2002). These professionals share an interest in institutionalizing ICT4D as a policy priority of governmental and nongovernmental organisations. As such, ICT4D can be conceptualised as a discourse, i.e. as a set of texts produced by multiple actors connected around a topic of focal interest (in this case, how ICTs can foster development) (Phillips & Hardy, 2002; Avgerou, 2008). ICT4D discourse is still being defined and negotiated as it is characterised by multiple perspectives, ideas and technologies to support development, ranging from the use of social mapping (or ‘maptivism’) to e-government and from open source to mobile technologies (Avgerou, 2001; Dewan & Riggins, 2005; Ganesh & Barber, 2009). Key to ICT4D discourse is the idea that, through ICT use, greater participation in development can be enhanced.

Whereas originally access to technology (such as mobile phones and computers) was the key focal point of ICT4D, weblogs or blogs, and related social media of the ‘Web 2.0 generation’ (O’Reilly, 2007) have recently become recognised as new ways to transform and democratise development and to promote participation in its processes (Heeks, 2008; Thompson, 2008; Kleine & Unwin, 2009). The affordability and user friendliness of blogs, in particular, have spurred their popularity (MacDougall, 2005; Murthy, 2008; McKenzie & Özler, 2011).

Much of the prior literature on ICT4D has focused on empirical examples of specific tools in a given setting, providing accounts of tool design and implementation. This has provided useful insights into innovations and challenges related to ICT4D in practice, but the generation of theoretically informed insights has remained relatively neglected (Avgerou, 2008). In this paper, we build on recent developments of theorizing ICT4D (Walsham & Sahay, 2006;

Avgerou, 2008; 2010; Silva & Westrup, 2009) by providing an understanding of the entanglement of blogging and the generation of ICT4D discourse.

## ICT4D AND BLOGGING

Blogs consist of low to no-cost Web-based publications with dated entries posted in reverse chronological order (Davidson & Vaast, 2009; Wattal et al., 2010). Since the mid-2000s, blogging has become increasingly popular with over 180 million blogs registered in December 2011 (Blogpulse.com). Features of blogging make it possible for anyone involved in ICT4D and with internet access to express ideas and experiences and engage with others interested in these issues, irrespective of organisational or geographical boundaries. Through blogging, development professionals who often work in a highly distributed environment can participate in and collectively generate ICT4D discourse. As ICT4D discourse has become increasingly recognised in the development field (Kuriyan et al., 2008; Unwin, 2009), and as blogs have become more widely available, development professionals have started to integrate blogging into their own work practices. Recent instances of civil unrest have brought a surge in blogging activity in the development field, as blogging offers ways for activists, journalists and citizen journalists to share real-time updates in difficult conditions (Preston, 2011). An example of an activist blogger in development is Wael Abbas [http://misrdigital.blogspirit.com/], who has reported on developments in the Middle East in general and Egypt in particular. ‘White African is a popular opinion blog of an individual development professional on technology and development [http://whiteafrican.com]. Another example is ‘Africa Can End Poverty’ [http:// blogs.worldbank.org/africacan], a blog by the World Bank Chief Economist for Africa who has advocated for and contributed to more openness and transparency of World Bank operations and perspectives.

While blogs have been hyped as new media that development professionals can (and should) use to promote and sustain ICT4D discourse (Kleine & Unwin, 2009), the influence of blogging practices on ICT4D discourse has so far remained under-explored. Such insight can contribute to an improved in-depth understanding of the interaction between blogging as a specific (low cost) tool and its social context, as indicated as important area for further study in Management Information Systems Quarterly special issue on ICT4D (Walsham et al., 2007). This paper therefore aims at bridging this gap in the literature. It in particular examines development professionals’ blogging practices and questions whether these practices indeed facilitate participation in ICT4D discourse. To pave the way for our empirical investigations, we first characterise ICT4D blogging practices.

## ICT4D BLOGGING PRACTICES: REFLECTING AND ENGAGING WITH PEERS

Blogging practices are discursive practices through which people rely upon blogging to produce and disseminate a discourse (Vaast et al., forthcoming). The budding literature on blogging practices has examined why people blog individually and collectively (Nardi et al., 2004b; Pantelli et al., 2011). Bloggers – people who write blogs – use blogging technologies for multiple purposes (Blood, 2004; Dearstyne, 2005), such as to collect their persona thoughts (Nardi et al., 2004a; Gregg, 2009) or to organise and publish professional insights (Bar-Ilan, 2005; Silva et al., 2009). Very often, bloggers end up producing discourse related to specific, specialised topics and, so doing, some have become influential (Dhar & Chang, 2009; Droge et al., 2009). Moreover, as some bloggers engage in and with similar topics, they may develop ‘shared practices’ (Barnes, 2001). The idea of shared practices suggests that individual bloggers come together around similar topics (such as ICT4D) and that the discourse they produce is not only to be considered at the individual level but also at the collective, or community, level (Barnes, 2001; Brown & Duguid, 1991; Vaast et al., forthcoming).

Our focus on the blogging practices of development professionals allows us to distinguish two relevant purposes for ICT4D blogging: reflecting and engaging with peers. First, blogging can sustain the activity of reflective practitioners (Wopereis et al., 2010). Reflecting corresponds to the cognitive process of (re)structuring experiences, existing knowledge or insights (Korthagen, 2001) and involves making sense of one’s own process of learning in various contexts (Schön, 1983). As they reflect, people explore and learn from their experiences. Popular tools such as journals or logbooks (Korthagen, 1999) focus on externalizing and capturing reflective thinking on paper; or thinking by writing. Through writing, one may step out of the ongoing practice and engage in reflection on action, or ‘the active process of making sense of experiences for the purpose of orienting oneself for current and/or future thought and action’ (Ertmer & Newby, 1996, p. 17). This is often done by publicly documenting thoughts and by stating plans to an audience (Pantelli et al., 2011). Through their blogging practices, development professionals can thus reflect upon ICT4D.

Second, thanks to its open and interactive nature, blogging can also help development professionals engage with an audience on the basis of shared interests and experiences (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010; Preston, 2011). As they blog, people with shared interests can connect through features such as comment functions, content syndication, message forward ing and invisibly, by tracking page views (Silva et al., 2009). Such features enable interaction, and diffusion of ideas to a potentially large and often anonymous audience (Senges et al., 2008; Wattal et al., 2010). Blogging features and practices support asymmetrical but bilateral relations between bloggers and their readers (Herring et al., 2005). Blogs center on bloggers voices and their practices (Blood, 2004), but bloggers can interact with their audience by trying to influence them or to seek their opinions and feedback (Pantelli et al., 2011).

Many ICT4D professionals share an optimistic and often passionate confidence in the positive effects of ICT for development (Heeks, 2008). In fact, our respondents all believed that blogs have a powerful potential to strengthen participation in development, an essential goal of ICT4D (Unwin, 2009). With the goal of substantiating theoretical and analytical contributions (Avgerou, 2008), we investigated how development professionals actually use blogs as a medium to strengthen such participation. The following research question guided our empirical investigations: How do development professionals’ blogging practices of reflecting and engaging with peers shape ICT4D discourse? In what follows, we detail our methods before presenting our findings and their implications for ICT4D discourse and blogging practices.

## METHODS

Given the exploratory nature of this research, we adopted a qualitative research design. We developed in-depth investigations of the practices of ICT4D bloggers that belonged to a leading international development online network on ICT4D of around 1500 participants who use an online list serve as their primary means of communication. To select the bloggers whose practices we wanted to understand better, we relied upon theoretical sampling and as such, focused our investigations upon members of the network who were frequent participants and active bloggers rather than occasional ones (as in Java et al., 2007). Our sampling resulted in focusing our investigations on the practices of 18 development professionals who worked as policy officers, project officers, communication officers, directors, division heads, program managers or advisors. These bloggers were affiliated with different development organisations, such as NGOs (13), development agencies (3) and research institutes (2). Their blogs reflected their personal views on ICT4D, not their organisations’. Many of them were based in Europe, namely Italy (1), Portugal (1), Germany (1) and specifically in the Netherlands (5), which actively adopted ICT4D as a policy priority in the early 21st century, and therefore yielded a proportionally high number of ICT4D bloggers (Lovink, 2008). Other bloggers were based in the USA (2), Surinam (1), Indonesia (1) and in Africa (Ethiopia (1), Burkina Faso (1), Ghana (2) and Uganda (2) ). All bloggers were development professionals who had lived in and travelled to a wide range of countries in both Western and developing regions.

All selected bloggers agreed to participate in the research. We performed semi-structured interviews with them to get a sense of their own perspectives upon and intentions behind their blogging practices (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). Interviews, conducted either face-to-face or by phone, lasted between 1 and 3 hours, and were fully transcribed. We used an interview guide for backup and consistency, asking the bloggers for their development experiences and motivations; their reflections on their use of social media in support of their work; their perceptions on the development of their professional community through their blogging practices; and to what extent their audiences influenced their postings. Finally, we gave them the opportunity to add their own topics to the discussions. Overall, we took an open approach, encouraging storytelling (Alvesson, 2003) based on personal experiences in order to gather a wide range of experiences, events and happenings. The interviews helped us to develop sensitizing concepts that informed a further inductive analysis of our findings.

We then selected a subset of interviewed bloggers and examined their blog posts in order to understand better their actual blogging practices. ## FINDINGS

Our study corroborated our expectations that ICT4D blogging practices reflected the two key blogging purposes of reflecting and engaging with peers (cf. Table 3). Reflecting encompassed blogging practices such as critically reflecting upon experiences, expressing opinions, sharing expertise, summarising a discussion and expressing plans for future postings. Engaging with peers involved interacting with an audience, and included practices such as asking for feedback, personal messaging, making compliments and providing updates in status. More unexpectedly, we also found practices interweaving reflecting and engaging with peers. In what follows, we expand upon reflecting and engaging as well as upon how they were interrelated in blogging practices.

She introduced her blog as a way ‘to track and record some of my reflections and found materials, without having the pretension that it will be exhaustive about everything happening in [this] field’ (Jenny, blog entry Oct. 2005). Shortly thereafter, she posted musings about ICT4D in Ghana, seeking examples of its application and its contextual relevance. ‘The point’, she wrote, ‘is to work with the existing structure rather than trying to restructure communications in terms of a new ICT. . . . The most important lesson I draw from this is the importance of looking at existing patterns of communication, and link/add/build on the existing pattern rather than introduce some new alien media’ (Jenny, blog entry Oct. 2005). As ICT4D bloggers articulated ideas and produced a discourse online, they simultaneously made sense of their own local context and practices. In this sense, ICT4D bloggers performed a role of ‘reflective practitioners’ (Schön, 1983) who built their own thinking as they typed.

Other bloggers exhibited similar practices. Ana, a policy officer in an agricultural development agency, contemplated in one of her early posts how ICTs could be specifically used to disseminate knowledge and improve rural livelihoods. Remarking that people had had to travel a full day to become informed on rural land reform policies, she indicated ‘only recently I’ve become convinced of the prominent and crucial role that ICTs can and should play to improve the livelihoods of poor rural people. . . . ICTs have been and are crucial for reducing poverty especially when farmers and rural stakeholders participate in decision making processes, are kept informed, so that they can make informed decisions to improve their livelihoods’ (Ana, blog entry March 2007).

Overall, through such blogging practices, these ICT4D professionals thought about ICT4D practices and opportunities. Blogging provided a way for them to contemplate their professional practices according to their own particular interests, experiences and skills. For instance, ‘I blog a lot . . . I reflect a lot on the patterns I observe in my work . . . to try to unpack the concepts and experiences that we make with that work. . . . It has also given my own practice real evidence of what has worked for me’ (Eric, interview). While they usually declared that they blogged mostly for themselves, they also had an audience in mind when they blogged. Since an audience needs to be able to understand one’s perspective, ICT4D bloggers exerted some effort to explicate their thought processes clearly: ‘you really need to think things through more than if you were talking to someone. You really need to articulate very much more explicitly’ (Eric, interview). In this manner, blogging involved an incremental and public sensemaking process of developing an idea and of associating it to ICT4D practice.

Blogging enabled our professionals to cultivate and transform their personal view on ICT4D over time. Ana for instance started blogging on issues related to ICT4D for rural agriculture. In her early posts, she shared many concrete examples of endeavours she was involved in, such as a project aimed at stimulating entrepreneurship among small-scale farmers (Apri 2007) or another one dealing with artisanal fisheries (October 2007). While many of her postings were primarily about concrete activities related to daily practices, others referred to the more abstract or general debate of ICT4D and reflections on how this could support organisational needs. One theme that emerged over time concerned leadership issues that eventually appeared in 16 of her posts (e.g. ‘How leaders create and use networks’, October 2007; ‘Different types of leadership’, April 2008, etc.). Then, as she became involved in a major event in her organisation aimed towards sharing ICT4D knowledge, Ana posted: ‘we are taking an important step to bring about change by demystifying ICT4D and showing that [it] is not a fad but a fact and a way of living’ (January 2009). Over time, one of the main themes of he blog became how ICT4D could influence the way her organisation operated (Ana, blog entry September 2010: ‘Enough with pilots: let’s get serious and start investing in mobile development.’). Such insights were often accompanied by a strong opinion or activist standpoint: ‘I blog mostly for where I want to put my take on certain things, or my opinion on certain things’ (Luca, interview).

These examples illustrate a trend perceptible among all our bloggers. Blogging helped them to make sense of their experiences and practices, to develop their personal take on ICT4D, thereby shaping their professional thoughts and expertise. As they blogged, they situated and interpreted multiple facets of ICT4D in relation to individual professional practices. Writing down their ongoing thoughts led them to reflect on what ICT4D meant for them as practitioners, and helped them develop and express their opinions.

## Engaging: connecting with a like-minded audience

‘These [audience’s] opinions are the “gravy”, the opinions about what really happens. This is really stimulated by blogging’ (Jenny, interview).

The second main purpose of blogging, also recognised by the bloggers we interviewed, was to interact and actively engage with an interested audience. This was for instance illustrated by blogger Eric who often prompted his readers for contributions: ‘Here comes a list of statements you might avoid to say when you want to convince colleagues or clients for such changes. Please share more as I am sure you have plenty’ (blog entry, December 2009). ICT4D bloggers had specific issues on which they sought to engage their audience. Namely, most investigated bloggers felt that their ideas and opinions required a like-minded audience, which they often did not find in their own organisation: ‘We’ve got such different discourse around this’, said Eric as he referred to his colleagues and local office mates. Consequently, they tried to converse with like-minded people by addressing them directly in their posts. ‘I think at a certain moment like-minded people are going to look for each other because you’re not “forced” to, well in the past you had to engage with, like your neighbors, you were at the mercy of others and they might have been really different people and nowadays we travel lots and on the internet, you just seek out the ones who think the same as you. I think it works like that, it may be that it is even more an in-crowd, with groupthink processes, that they strengthen each other even more’ (Jenny, interview). The like mindedness was often reflected in the shared daily practices that a blogger was participating in and in the engagement or passion they displayed when debating these practices and ICT4D in general. They expressed enjoying sharing ideas with these peers in exchanges that usually remained exclusively computer mediated. For instance, Eric indicated: ‘The unique thing that I really enjoy is that I’ve been having conversations with a whole bunch of people that I really don’t know, that I’ve never met and I probably will never meet’ (Eric, interview).

Most of our bloggers considered blogging a more all-encompassing way of communicating than traditional organisational communications. What is more, to them, audience’s contributions provided opinions that were ‘less formal and abstract’ than the official documents and memos that circulated within their organisation. In fact, bloggers sometimes described these official documents as ‘completely disengaged from the real practice’ (Jenny, interview). Bloggers felt that their blogging practices stimulated the expression of new and multiple voices as alternatives to the existing and institutionalised voices of development organisations. Luca considered that ‘one of the fundamental things that is different [in blogging] versus traditional communication, is that in the past you would need to have one corporate message, right, if you had dissonant voices it was perceived to be a weakness. I think finally the maturity has reached the [organization] and others, showing that there are internal debates, people have different opinions, taking into account criticism’. A comment from Ana corroborated this view: ‘It’s a different way of thinking, from corporate communications department with a certain message versus people posting messages and perhaps informing policy that way.

Bloggers thus often felt that they explicitly distanced themselves from their organisation through their blogging practices. In fact, Eric mentioned: ‘My colleagues are very skeptica about the use of social media, are very skeptical of knowledge management, don’t really engage with it. . . . I’ve decided to invest less in my colleagues and invest more where it actually reaps benefits. I see it in the projects I’m involved with, in the communities I’m involved in, and I just focus on that. I get much more external feedback anyway. I’ve half given up on my colleagues’ (Eric, interview). Other bloggers expressed similar opinions and reflected upon what this could mean for their relationships with their colleagues: ‘You become very outward looking, maybe even so that you forget your own colleagues’ (Jenny, interview).

In relation to the position of bloggers within their organisation, several of our interviewees insisted upon the innovative character (‘there is clearly a forefront in this domain’, said Harry) of their practices and opinions published through their blogs. They viewed their blogging discourse as ahead of their organisations’: ‘It’s a whole new way of looking at things’ (Ana, interview). They presented themselves as frontrunners on ICT4D, and felt that the dialogue developed with those who read and reacted to their blogs was often richer and more informed than those they had within their own organisations.

## Interweaving reflecting and engaging

‘Now with blogs, it is even easier, it becomes more of a conversation, you know, of kind of half finished ideas’ (Jenny, interview).

Through blogging, ICT4D professionals reached a potentially large audience that could read and comment on the written thoughts and reflections, with various consequences. ‘(I like to) test the water, developing a workflow in your head, like, I have an idea, okay shall I continue with this, add it to that and that’ (Harry, interview). Harry even considered that blogs constituted laboratories for ideas that could lead to changes in actions: ‘like an incubator that needs to be translated into the organization, but is nurtured by individuals. Simply individuals translating experiences into an interpretation of our policy of the future, it’s quite similar. Venturing ideas, waiting for someone to pick it up’. In this way, bloggers practised what they preached in using blogs as ICT4D to aim for increased policy influence and participation by supporting the articulation of a voice.

One blogger said that one of the things that appealed to her was that blogging allowed her to reach ‘a far larger network than the one directly related to my daily practices’, while at the same time facilitating ‘intimate conversations about what you’re doing, in a many-to-many way of communicating’. These conversations, she indicated: ‘opened my eyes very much, widened my vision’ (Jenny, interview). Ana expressed confidence in her blogging practices as she felt supported by her audience: ‘If I don’t know something, there are thousands of people out there [outside the organization] who can help me, I have learned tremendously from everyone. Somebody can tell me what I need to know, or can help me if I’ve made a mistake, or can provide me with something I need’. Luca, a senior policy advisor at a multilateral agency, illustrated the potential reach of such engagement: ‘One of the reasons we blog is it is the only way to reach out to the constituencies we want to change in Africa, to hear what they want, think, and have to say. . . . And I know for a fact I’ve had several responses and I know these people tend to follow me and I know it makes really quite a difference. I mean this is open for everybody right, some people can jump in with their completely different perspectives and that’s amazing’ (Luca, interview). Luca thus considered that his blogging actually enabled the participation of constituents so that they could collectively build a reflection on ICT4D through comments and blogging responses.

Our bloggers therefore often expressed a belief that their interactions with the audience were very important for the richness and value of the ICT4D discourse. Our own examination of actual blog posts and their comments, however, revealed that the audience was usually not very engaged – at least on the blog. As Table 2 shows, our bloggers had a rather low (1.2) average number of comments per posting, even as we had selected these seven bloggers as the ones who had the most comments in our sample of interviewees. And since 63% of commenters to posts only commented once, it might seem a stretch to consider that a blogger really formed a tightly knit community with his or her audience. Moreover, as Table 4 shows, most comments seemed to express agreement with blog posts, either via information sharing, referring to posts or actual positive feedback. Comments were usually brief, and commentators often merely expressed satisfaction that the bloggers’ opinions resonated with their own views and development practices as in the following example of comment: ‘Hi Stephan, what a great post! Thank you so much for mentioning our project. Btw: I admire the way you can link many valuable resources into one post. It is always a treat to read your blog’ (blog comment, March 2010).

Comments thus did not usually display the depth of interactivity or engagement that ICT4D bloggers considered appealing to their blogging practices and encouraging of their reflection process. However, it was exactly the support that bloggers perceived to get from a like-minded audience that encouraged them to pursue certain ideas and topics related to ICT4D. In fact, when they did not receive positive feedback, they tended to close the topic or alter the discussion, taking a more ‘popular’ angle, as Ana said: ‘I do pay attention to what [post] people positively comment on and who is doing that. I must admit: if they did not do so, I would alter my message, yes’ (Ana, interview). Eric also indicated: ‘I do pay attention to who’s listening to me. [. . .] I won’t hide the fact that, I think like most bloggers and tweeters there is an interest in popularity. To a degree. Do they like what you’re saying, how do they respond? To an extent, it forces you to come up with better content, so I think it’s a useful dynamic; it’s not just an egotistic, ego-tripping point of view, but actually does help everyone else’ (Eric, interview). Thus, bloggers felt encouraged by an audience of respondents that did not comment often, but that reacted positively to their postings and often seemed to share similar practices and opinions. Such encouragement translated into posting specific types of posts or orienting their ICT4D discourse into a direction that bloggers deemed most popular among their audience.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

ICT4D discourse promotes the idea that ICTs can empower people in developing countries by amplifying their voices and strengthening their participation in decision-making processes (Ferguson et al., 2010; Zuckerman, 2010). Blogs have recently become recognised as new ways to promote sustainable development (Kleine & Unwin, 2009). Having analysed the blogging practices of highly active ICT4D bloggers, we first summarise our key findings before we discuss their main implications.

Our study revealed how blogging involved two key purposes of reflecting and engaging with peers but also that these two purposes were highly interwoven in actual ICT4D blogging practices. Through blogging, development professionals engaged in a reflective process of ‘public sensemaking’, generating a personal view of their own development practices and of the role of ICT in this regard. At the same time, through blogging, they addressed an audience, articulating their personal views in such a way that others could understand them, thereby refining and expressing their perspectives and opinions more clearly over time. Our observations thereby highlighted a tension hinted at in prior studies (Nardi et al., 2004a; Papacharissi, 2007; Chang & Chuang, 2011) in that blogging is an inherently personal medium in content while its reach extends to a potentially broad, public and largely anonymous audience.

Our findings further revealed that ICT4D bloggers engaged with a like-minded audience consisting of peers with shared professional practices. It should be noted that the audience we refer to here is not the overall audience of these blogs, that also includes lurkers and silent ‘listeners’, but consisted of the actively reacting audience. This self-selected audience fre quently expressed agreement with blog posts and actively encouraged the bloggers in thei blogging practices. In fact, ICT4D bloggers considered that their blogging audience was different from and more supportive than their organisations and direct colleagues.

Intriguingly, our analyses provided evidence of deep intertwining of reflecting and engaging with peers in blogging practices, especially as ICT4D bloggers experimented with ideas through blogging, widened their vision and reached out to the audience. ICT4D bloggers believed that engaging with an audience deepened and increased the value of their own ideas. They therefore paid great attention to their audience and to any (positive) signal it gave them.

As a consequence, a self-reinforcing mechanism became evident. An audience with shared professional practices and interests, expressed their agreement with specific posts through the comments section, which was consistent with studies that have suggested that ‘blogs create communities of like-minded people’ (Lovink, 2008, p. 21). In turn, bloggers felt encouraged to continue talking about these issues in part to further satisfy their readers’ expressed interests. At the same time, though, our study revealed that ICT4D blog audiences were much less engaged than ICT4D bloggers perceived them to be.

## Theoretical implications and suggestions for further research

This research holds implications for the emerging IS literature on social media in professiona settings, as well as for the development and ICT4D literature.

With regard to social media practices, this research contributes to growing scholarship that has focused on some behavioural aspects of blogging such as the motivations for these practices (Nardi et al., 2004b; Pantelli et al., 2011) or their impacts (McKenzie & Özler, 2011). Our focus on blogging practices allowed us to discover that reflecting and engaging with peers are not independent aspects of blogging, but, rather, that they mutually reinforce each other. When ICT4D bloggers enacted both practices simultaneously, engaging with a like-minded audience became an inherent part of their sensemaking activities. Such a finding helped us to understand better how blogging practices build upon a perceived sense of shared community and might contribute to changes in the positions and relations of bloggers with regard to their organisation and to their informal network of peers. Even more broadly, this finding contributes to the literature on the formation of discourse through blogging by revealing not only that blogging practices constitute discourse (in the present case, development discourse) but also that blogging practices contribute to the formation of what the discourse is about. This paper added to the literature on blogging and discourse by uncovering some of the individual and collective dynamics of blogging. More specifically, it showed how the actual process of writing a blog post on a specific issue, intertwined with the process of making it available publicly through the internet, actually shapes what the issue is about and what the author’s position on it ends up being.

We also found that ICT4D bloggers perceived a ‘sense of community’ when blogging and when engaging with their perceived audience. ICT4D bloggers expressed this sense of community by noting feelings of belonging to a group of peers and of sharing connections based on similar interests, i.e. ICT4D. This perceived sense of community has been noted in Blanchard (2004). Yet, our investigations revealed an intriguing counterpoint to this in that, when looking at the actual blogs and the online discussions they generated, the level of interactivity and the number of people adding comments were actually fairly low. This research revealed that this sense of community therefore more aptly applies to bloggers’ perceptions, regardless of whether such a community actually exists or is very active. This distinction between the high level of interactivity that bloggers perceived and claimed to be essential to shaping their blogging practices, vs. the low level of interactivity actually observed constituted an intriguing and new insight of this research. Such a finding contrasts highly with emerging scholarship on blogging practices in other fields (in particular, Vaast et al., forthcoming, on bloggers in the technologica field) where bloggers have been shown to engage more actively with one another and with readers through blog posts and comments. We explain this difference by noting that the ICT4D blogging community is a relatively small one, whose ideas and mobilising mottos have not fully reached societal mainstream and therefore need to be ‘nurtured’. Future research could investigate bloggers’ practices in additional fields, related to bloggers’ actual interactions and their perceived sense of communities, in order to further develop theory on this issue.

Furthermore, our findings allowed us to discover that blogging practices contributed to the emergence of new ways of developing and mobilising development ideas beyond traditional organisational boundaries. Our research revealed in particular that rather than transmitting a uniform, formal message from the organisation, in their practices ICT4D, bloggers created a space that allowed for the expression of individual voices that sometimes contrasted with that of their organisations. ICT4D bloggers blogged to form and express their own opinions on ICT4D (Gumbrecht, 2005; Lovink, 2008) and, as they did so, they took more control of ICT4D discourse and often moved away from their already institutionalised organisational structures and existing professional routines of their institution (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Their selfselected audience of like-minded professionals encouraged them to intensify their blogging practices and, through faint but noted praise, oriented their ICT4D discourse as well. This exposed that blogging can influence discourse in unexpected ways, by intertwining two main blogging practices of reflecting and engaging with peers.

With regard to the development and ICT4D literatures, our research also brought intriguing and counter-intuitive findings. In particular, we found that blogging practices can contribute to deepening ICT4D theories and concepts, but that they do not necessarily enhance partici pation in development, which is, however, traditionally presented as at the core of the ICT4D discourse.

For one, concerning blogging practices deepening ICT4D theories and concepts, we found that, through the interweaving of reflecting and engaging with peers, ICT4D bloggers cultivated their thinking on ICT4D and oriented their discourse towards certain topics they considered of great relevance and importance to them and their perceived audience. As they did so, they became more and more attuned with certain topics of ICT4D and developed strong expertise on these issues. They considered their thinking as innovative and ‘avant-garde’ as compared to traditional ICT4D theories and concepts. Yet, as their thinking on ICT4D was deepening, bloggers were also potentially building ‘knowledge silos’ (Wattal et al., 2010), ‘information cocoons and echo chambers’ (Sunstein, 2008) or ‘in-crowd dynamics’ (Lovink, 2008). Thus, while blogging practices helped bloggers refine and deepen their thinking, proposing innovative approaches to ICT4D, they might also have made it more difficult for this thinking and such approaches to reach broader audiences. Future research would in this regard be needed to examine to what extent and how blogging communities may be able to become involved in the interpretation, legitimisation and mobilisation (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997) of ICT4D discourse in the broader development field.

Second, and consequently, we found that blogging does not automatically contribute to broadening participation in development, which has however been an important goal of ICT4D.

Indeed, much ICT4D research has been implementation-oriented and has addressed the use and potential of technology for people living in developing countries (Avgerou, 2008). This is based on the idea that ICTs facilitate access to information, which is critical for strengthening people’s voices in decision-making forums. While recognising the significance of this potential, we explored blogging practices of development professionals and examined how these shaped the actual ICT4D discourse. This research found ICT4D blogging had the potential to strengthen in-crowd dynamics and to deepen ICT4D discourse at the expense of a broader appeal for this discourse and of greater participation. We therefore argue that caution needs to be heeded in terms of the expectations of social media in general and blogging in particular, as contributing to participatory development.

In a more general sense, our research is not aimed at developing insights into ICT in one specific context or addressing a particular theme, such as health, which is often the case in empirical studies of ICT4D. While our research was conducted with acute sensitivity to the significance of context, we took a more theory-oriented approach to ICT4D, seeking to generate an improved understanding of how a tool such as blogging can influence ICT4D discourse. So doing, our study disclosed empirically derived insights that are in line with a recently developed socio-materiality lens. Socio-materiality focuses on affordances and constraints of technologies in practice, highlighting not only the technology, not only behavioural aspects, but the manner in which the two are inextricably intertwined (Orlikowski, 2007; Zammuto et al., 2007; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008; Davidson & Vaast, 2010). We see much potential for future research to build upon a socio-material perspective to further theorise ICT4D, through its emphasis on contextual and behavioural implications of a technology that are so critical in achieving development objectives.

A limitation of socio-materiality theory is the recognised challenge of empirically analysing phenomena from a socio-material perspective (Phillips & Oswick, 2012; Thompson, 2012). Without having fully delved into the materiality of blogging, such as the features and affordances of blogging sites or tools, our findings hinted at an intertwining of blogs and blogging practices with unexpected outcomes for ICT4D discourse. Our study can therefore be perceived as an empirical manifestation of this perspective, providing some guidance to others interested in empirical analyses of socio-materiality, in the IS domain and beyond.

Our study carries limitations in that we examined the practices of a fairly small number of bloggers who actively belonged to an online network. Future research would be useful for developing additional insights into the formation of discourse through blogging practices. In addition, we investigated ICT4D professionals’ overall blogging practices; future research might zoom onto specific topics within the general ICT4D discourse, such as safety, gender or HIV/AIDS, in order to develop a more fine-grained understanding of the formation of ICT4D discourse through blogging.

## Practical implications

There are several practical implications of our research for ICT4D. In particular, our findings suggested that blogging for ICT4D might hold adverse effects on participatory development.

Namely, the ability to permeate, let alone influence, debate at a policy level depends on forging strong liaisons with a broad number of constituents and allies in order to access decisionmakers in relevant policy domains (Haas, 1990; Biggs & Smith, 1998). ICT4D bloggers, however, through their blogging practices, tended to reach an external network of peers rather than their internal organisational discourses. Through blogging, they felt more disengaged from organisational discourse, developing their own thinking and discourse instead, within a diffuse group of like-minded peers. This contributed to ICT4D bloggers ‘preaching to the choir’, as a small blogging community was mostly talking to itself about development and was seeking approval for ICT4D discourse and practices with supporters rather than engaging with dissi dents or with the intended beneficiaries of ICT4D projects. Despite expectations, more participatory development processes through blogging in such cases remain an elusive ideal. We believe that such a situation may only be avoided if intended beneficiaries and targeted policy makers become explicitly involved as part of the blogging audience. We hope that our research will help ICT4D bloggers become aware of the importance of broadening the reach of their blogs and of making them more widely available and accessible to more diverse development stakeholders.

One aspect that makes such a wider involvement challenging is the relatively low percent age of people with reliable internet access in many developing countries. Recent estimates put internet users in Africa at only 5.7% of the world’s total (InternetWorldStats.com). With such low numbers, the likelihood that people in developing countries will find their way to blogs offering opinions on changing local realities through ICT4D is not very high. This limited reach of ICT4D discourse is in fact echoed in findings by development researchers, that ICT growth is only weakly correlated to poverty reduction (OECD, 2005).

Despite our observation of the weak participatory aspect of ICT4D blogging and of its limited influence on the broader ICT4D discourse, our research indicated that development professionals felt encouraged to deepen their thinking and enhance their professional knowledge through their blogging practices. The ability to reflect on their professional practices and to engage with people with shared interests can provide professionals enrichment that is likely to benefit their performance and expertise. Blogs also offer new opportunities to strengthen organisational transparency by providing a look behind the scenes of what happens in the field of development. In a world where the call for aid transparency is becoming louder (Rocha Menocal & Rogerson, 2006; Easterly & Williamson, 2011), blogging therefore still holds promises for ICT4D. Along with Walsham’s (2012, p. 92) ambition to ‘making the world a better place with ICTs’, we would urge more IS researchers to examine these fascinating issues and to grapple with the difficult and ambivalent implications of ICTs for development.

## Biographies

Julie Ferguson is Assistant Professor of Organization Sciences at the Faculty of Social Sciences, VU University Amsterdam. Her research focuses on knowledge, power and the interplay between new technologies and socia practices, particularly in the development sector. She is associate member of the ICT4D Collective. Roval Holloway University of London, and was previously responsible for knowledge management and ICT4D in various development NGOs. Her work has been published in World Development and Knowledge Management for Develop ment Journal, in an edited volume and in numerous trade journals. and has been presented at international conferences including the Academy of Management, EGOS and OLKC.

Maura Soekijad is Associate Professor of knowledge and organisation at the Faculty of Economics and Business Administration at the VU University Amsterdam. She is trained as ethnographer, and her research interests include knowledge sharing among professionals in competitive alliances, innovation in networks and managing networks of practice. Her publications appeared in outlets such as Organization Studies, Journal of Management Studies, Journal of Information Technology, and she published a chapter on competition in an edited volume by Routledge.

Marleen Huysman is full Professor of knowledge and organisation at the Faculty of Economics and Business Administration, VU University Amsterdam, and Head of the Department of Information, Logistics and Innovation, Her research focuses on issues related to social media, knowl edge management, organisational learning and networks.

On these topics, she has written various papers and chapters published in international journals and books and has co-authored several books.

Emmanuelle Vaast is Associate Professor of Information Systems at Desautels School of Business, McGill Uni versity. She holds a PhD from the Centre de Recherche en Gestion, Ecole Polytechnique, Paris (2003). Her research examines how social practices emerge and change with the implementation and use of various new technologies, and how these new practices lead to changes in organisational and field dynamics. She has in particular investigated the knowledge and learning dynamics taking place at different levels through the use of new technology. Most of her research is qualitative, case based and interpretive, but she is also interested in the quantitative analysis of electronically collected evidence. Her research has been published in, among other outlets: Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Organization Science, Information and Organization, Journal of Management Information Systems and Journal of Strategic Information Systems.
