RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Towards the improved treatment of generalization of knowledge claims in IS research: drawing general conclusions from samples

Peter B. Seddon<sup>1</sup> and Rens Scheepers<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>The University of Melbourne, Victoria, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Deakin University, Victoria, Australia

Correspondence: Peter B. Seddon, Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, 111 Barry St, Victoria 3010, Australia. Tel.: þ 61 3 8344 1576; Fax: þ 61 3 9349 4596; E-mail: p.seddon@unimelb.edu.au

## Abstract

This paper presents a framework for justifying generalization in information systems (IS) research. First, using evidence from an analysis of two leading IS journals, we show that the treatment of generalization in many empirical papers in leading IS research journals is unsatisfactory. Many quantitative studies need clearer definition of populations and more discussion of the extent to which ‘significant’ statistics and use of non-probability sampling affect support for their knowledge claims. Many qualitative studies need more discussion of boundary conditions for their sample-based general knowledge claims. Second, the proposed new framework is presented. It defines eight alternative logical pathways for justifying generalizations in IS research. Three key concepts underpinning the framework are the need for researcher judgment when making any claim about the likely truth of sample-based knowledge claims in other settings; the importance of sample representativeness and its assessment in terms of the knowledge claim of interest; and the desirability of integrating a study’s general knowledge claims with those from prior research. Finally, we show how the framework may be applied by researchers and reviewers. Observing the pathways in the framework has potential to improve both research rigour and practical relevance for IS research.

## Introduction

Although empirical researchers almost always study samples (as opposed to populations) from the real world, the information systems (IS) research community actually has very little interest in the samples studied, per se. Rather, the research community is normally interested in lessons that are applicable in other settings. These ‘lessons that are applicable in other settings’ are called generalizations, the subject of this paper.

Since making general knowledge claims is the goal of almost all IS research, our goal in this paper is to provide a comprehensive framework for justifying generalizations in IS research. In doing so, we (a) provide evidence that generalization practices even in our top journals are often unsatisfactory, (b) lay out a framework showing various chains of logical argument that may be used to justify generalizations, and (c) using the framework, discuss some examples of how the process of generalizing may be improved in future IS research.

In this paper we define a generalization as a ‘researcher’s act of arguing, by induction, that there is a reasonable expectation that a knowledge claim already believed to be true in one or more settings is also true in other clearly defined settings’. As summarized in Table 1, underpinning this definition are one ontological assumption, three epistemological assumptions, and three knowledge claims about key characteristics of generalization. These three key characteristics (at the end of Table 1) are discussed and justified in detail later in this paper. The ontological assumption is that objective reality exists. The three epistemological assumptions are that (1) all human knowledge is bounded; (2) all knowledge claims, including generalizations, are subject to revision; and (3) truth beyond reasonable doubt is sufficient. These four assumptions have been made to try to sidestep philosophical debate about whether reality exists, whether we can ever know that anything is really true, and the fact that it is logically impossible to prove knowledge claims true by induction (Hume, 1758; Papineau, 2003). Making these four assumptions enables us to get on with the business of discussing how generalization-based knowledge claims may be justified, which is the subject of this paper.

Please note that epistemological assumption 3 – that ‘Truth beyond reasonable doubt is sufficient’ – is our way of sidestepping the impossibility of proving anything true by induction, the problem that Lee & Baskerville (2003) call ‘Hume’s truism’. An alternative, but equally valid, way of sidestepping Hume’s truism is the Bayesian approach of focusing on one’s degree of belief in the truth of a knowledge claim. From a practical point of view these two approaches are tantamount to the same thing: saying that something is ‘true beyond reasonable doubt’ (our epistemological assumption 3) is very similar to saying that one has ‘a high degree of belief that something is true’ (the Bayesian approach). Either approach enables the researcher to sidestep Hume’s truism, which is essential for making any generalization.

Finally, as noted above, the goal of this paper is to present a comprehensive framework for justifying generalizations in IS research. To achieve that goal, the remainder of this paper is structured in three parts. Part 1 provides evidence that generalization practices in two leading IS research journals in 2007 and 2008, and by inference, all leading IS research journals, can be improved. Our goal here is not to be disrespectful of others’ work, nor to argue that all papers are unsatisfactory, but to provide evidence that existing practice can be, and should be, improved. Part 2 presents our framework for justifying generalization from samples of things. This is a significant extension of the thinking that we presented in Seddon & Scheepers (2006) (where we used the term ‘other-settings generalization’ to refer to what we call simply ‘generalization’ in the current paper), and hopefully, provides a useful framework for discussion of generalization in many future papers. The flowchart in this second section summarizes the key contribution of this paper. Part 3 shows how the framework from Part 2 may be applied. In addition to helping researchers achieve improved understanding of the role of generalization in IS research, it is argued that using one or more of the generalization justification pathways proposed in this paper should lead to both (a) improved rigour in IS research and (b) research that is more understandable to readers, and therefore more relevant to practice.

## Part 1: evidence that the treatment of generalization in leading IS research journals could be improved

To provide a fact base for the arguments that follow, we analysed a sample consisting of all papers published in Information Systems Research (ISR) and MIS Quarterly (MISQ) during the 2 years 2007–2008. In those 2 years, 48 papers were published in ISR and 64 in MISQ, a total of 112 papers. The analysis of these 112 papers was performed by the first author, paper by paper, over the course of about 2 weeks. After a sample of eight papers had been independently reviewed by the second author, we met to discuss coding guidelines. After some discussion we decided that, because most of the coding required so little judgment, no further second coding was necessary. For example, it is not hard to decide if the authors of a quantitative study have reported P-values or explicitly discussed populations or not. Likewise, it is not hard to decide if a paper is qualitative, quantitative (or both) in approach. The coding of whether generalizability was implied in a paper was more subjective, for example, see the Appendix for a quite difficult example, but after discussion it was decided that further second coding was not required.

The results of the analysis of the 112 papers in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 are summarized in Table 2. These results are similar to those reported by Seddon & Scheepers (2006) for ISR and MISQ in 2003–2004, but the current analysis is more detailed. For example, Table 2 reports the percentage of quantitative studies that use P-values $( \mathrm { e . g . } , \mathrm { \Omega } ^ { \prime \star } P { < } 0 . 0 5 ^ { \prime } )$ , but no comparable statistic was reported in Seddon & Scheepers (2006). In the current sample of 112 papers, 87 were empirical studies that collected and analysed data in some way. All 87 were written as if some aspects of their results were generalizable to settings outside the sample they actually studied, yet only 53% actually discussed the generalizability of their studies’ findings, and only 24% made any attempt to discuss the boundary conditions beyond which their theories or findings might not apply. Further, of the 87 empirical papers, 66 (76%) presented primarily quantitative analyses, and 21 (24%) – which includes 6 literaturereview articles in MISQ – were primarily qualitative. Of the 66 primarily quantitative studies, 64 (97%) reported P-values, yet only 33 (50%) discussed the generalizability of their findings, and only 22 (33%) attempted to specify the populations in which the null hypotheses assumed in their P-value tests applied. Frequently it seemed as if P-values were being used as evidence of strong (or important) relationships between variables in the sample of data, rather than as inferential statistics for use in generalizing to other settings.

## Problems with generalization in the 66 quantitative studies

## Frequent absence of discussion of the general implications of findings from individual quantitative studies

Since IS is an applied discipline, where researchers seek to publish lessons that may be applied in other settings, the absence of discussion of populations, boundary conditions, and generalizability of findings in 50% of quantitative studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 is a problem. The reason is that although P-values were reported in 97% of the 66 quantitative studies, P-values, by themselves, do not tell readers what they want to know about likely relationships in settings beyond the sample. Cohen (1994, pp. 997–998) sums up the problem with ‘null hypothesis significance testing’ (NHST), that is, the use of P-values, as follows:

What’s wrong with NHST? Well, among many other things, it does not tell us what we want to know, and we so much want to know what we want to know that, out of desperation, we nevertheless believe that it does! What we want to know is ‘Given these data, what is the probability that $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 }$ is true?’ But as most of us know, what it tells us is ‘Given that $\mathrm { H } _ { 0 }$ is true, what is the probability of these (or more extreme) data?’ These are not the same, as has been pointed out many times over the years by the contributors to the Morrison & Henkel (1970) book, among others, and, more recently and emphatically, by Meehl (1978, 1986, 1990a, b), Gigerenzer (1993), Falk & Greenbaum (1995), and yours truly (Cohen, 1990).

Given the strength of claims like Cohen’s, it is important to understand what P-values do and do not tell us. According to Lehmann (1993, p. 1242), the ‘formulation and philosophy of hypothesis testing as we know it today was largely created during the period 1915–1933 by three men’, namely, Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson. Of these, Fisher (1925, 1926, 1935, 1959, 1966) was the primary advocate for the use of P-values. According to Hubbard & Bayarri (2003, p. 172):

‘Fisher believed that statistics could play an important part in promoting inductive inference, that is, drawing inferences from the particular to the general, from samples to populations’.

In Fisher’s approach the researcher sets up a null hypothesis that a sample comes from a hypothetical infinite population with a known sampling distribution. The null hypothesis is said to be ‘disproved’, as Fisher called it, or rejected if the sample estimate deviates from the mean of the sampling distribution by more than a specified criterion, the level of significance. According to Fisher (1966, p. 13), ‘It is usual and convenient for experimenters to take 5 per cent as a standard level of significance, in the sense that they are prepared to ignore all results which fail to reach this standardy’. Consequently, the Fisherian scheme of significance testing centres on the rejection of the null hypothesis at the P≤0.05 level.

Fisher, who Ziliak & McCloskey (2008) describe in positive and negative terms as both a genius and a wasp, has been hugely influential. He knew that P-values only assess the probability of finding the pattern observed in the data, given that the null hypothesis is true in the population, Pr(data|H ). He also knew that this statistic is not the same as the probability that the null hypothesis is true in the population, given the pattern observed in the data, that is, $\mathrm { P r ( H _ { 0 } | d a t a ) }$ , which is the question that generalizability seeks to address. But for Fisher, addressing this second question – the inference from the sample to the population – seems to have been a matter of researcher judgment, informed by Pr(data|H ). The problem with 33 of the 66 quantitative studies from ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 is that having reported P-values, that is, $\mathrm { P r } ( \mathrm { d a t a } | \mathrm { H } _ { 0 } )$ , they did not go on and exercise the inductive judgment that tells us what we all want to know, that is, $\mathrm { P r ( H _ { 0 } | d a t a ) }$ . It could be that the authors of these 33 studies believed that readers could make inductive judgments themselves, but we argue that authors of these 33 studies had so much more knowledge of their data than readers that they were much better placed than readers to discuss the generalizability of their knowledge claims.

In fact, there are so many unsatisfactory things about P-values, particularly their dependence on sample size (Royall, 1986), that many statisticians (e.g., those in the books edited by Morrison & Henkel (1970) and Harlow et al (1997) that debate the weakness of null hypothesis significance testing) recommend abandoning the use of P-values in quantitative studies or at least supplementing them with discussions of the substantive meaning of those statistics (Ziliak & McCloskey, 2004, 2008). Supplements and alternatives to P-values recommended in these and other books and articles include reporting ‘confidence interval estimates of parameters and effect sizes’ (Mulaik et al, 1997), using Bayesian statistics (Berger, 1985), reporting likelihood ratios (Royall, 2000; Gregoire, 2001), and the use of meta-analysis (Schmidt, 1992).

However, since the use of P-values is so entrenched in the IS literature (at least in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008), our goal in this paper is not to discuss or promote alternatives to P-values; there is ample information available to researchers on these topics, for example, see Harlow et al (1997), and Ziliak & McCloskey (2008). Our goal is simply to point out that because P-values only tell us Pr(data|H ), not Pr(H |data), researchers who report P-values need to go on and discuss the applicability to other settings of the sample-based knowledge claims in their papers. It is this absence of discussion of general knowledge claims in 50% of the 66 quantitative studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 that is the first reason that we argue that the state of generalization practice in these two journals during 2007–2008 was unsatisfactory. (Note that this is a sample-specific knowledge claim, it is not a general claim about all IS research. We make that general claim shortly.)

## Drawing inferences from non-probability samples

The second major problem with the 66 quantitative IS research studies reviewed in Table 2 follows directly from the first. If it is accepted that the purpose of almost all IS research is to make inferences about populations, and that the basis for all statistically based inference relies on the use of probability samples (e.g., random samples) drawn from those populations, it is also clear that failure to obtain a probability sample renders all subsequent statistical analysis void. Yet because of non-respondents and other sampling difficulties, this is what happens in almost all survey-based quantitative IS research today! For example, the mean response rate for the 22 surveys in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008, where response rate reported was 37% (median 26%). This is close to the average response rate of 30% in the studies reviewed by King & He (2005). The problem with non-response in surveys is that it is likely that those who choose to respond or not respond do so for systematic reasons (Rogelberg & Stanton, 2007). This means that samples from surveys with any non-respondents are not probability samples, which in turn means that so-called ‘inferential’ statistics such as t-statistics and P-values cannot be relied upon. Equally, experimental researchers with human subjects rarely discuss how representative their sample is of some generalization-target population. Yet without evidence of representativeness, it is similarly hard to draw sound conclusions about the applicability of findings from the experiment to other settings.

Our recommended solution to these two problems, or to any situation where the researchers have a nonprobability sample, is that researchers might reasonably use ‘inferential’ statistics, such as P-values, if they can show that their sample is representative of the population of interest. A representative sample, we argue, is one where ‘For the variables relevant to the study, statistics calculated from the sample are similar to those of the population from which the sample was selected’. Thus if one’s theory proposes that X causes Y, the sample would be judged representative if it could be shown or argued that sample statistics related to X, Y, and the association between X and Y, are similar to, or are likely to be similar to, those of the population of interest. Alternatively, if the researcher has no basis for claiming representativeness, it is helpful for readers to know that (because it reduces the reader’s confidence in the study’s knowledge claims).

Only 22 (33%) of the 66 quantitative studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 presented evidence that their sample was either a true probability sample (e.g., a true random sample) or representative of the population. Yet without such argument or evidence, no meaningful inferences can be drawn safely from any of the P-values that 97% of quantitative studies reported. This is the second reason that we argue that the state of generalization practice in quantitative studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 is often unsatisfactory. (This, too, is a sample-specific knowledge claim, it is not a general claim about all IS research. Again, we make that general claim shortly.)

## Room for improvement in treatment of generalization in qualitative IS research

As shown in Table 2, 21 of the 112 papers published in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 were studies that collected and analysed data in a primarily qualitative way. Six of these 21 papers were literature reviews; the other 15 were case studies. Reflecting the different emphases of the two journals, 20 of these 21 qualitative papers were published in MISQ. Of these 21 papers, only 13 (62%) actually discussed the generalizability of their knowledge claims, and only seven of these (33%) made any attempt to discuss the boundary conditions beyond which their theories or findings might not apply. On a positive note, although Lee & Baskerville (2003) were able to document 12 papers published during 1992–1999 that inappropriately applied a ‘statistical, sampling-based conception of generalizability to non-statistical, non-sampling research’ (p. 223), none of the qualitative papers we reviewed suffered from this problem.

In terms of room for improvement of qualitative IS research practice, since IS is an applied discipline, where readers typically hope to apply lessons from one study to other settings, the above-mentioned lack of discussion of generalizability (by eight of 21 papers), and boundary conditions (by 14 of 21 papers) means that the published research in our sample is probably not as useful as it potentially could be to readers seeking to apply study findings elsewhere. At the minimum, a brief discussion of generalizability and boundary conditions would (a) guide readers on the soundness of any generalizations made, (b) remind readers of the possible risks of assuming that lessons, say, from a single case study, also apply in other settings, and (c) help readers understand better the authors’ goals in publishing the paper.

## Generalizing from the sample above to all IS research

The purpose of this first part of this paper (Part 1) is to convince the reader that, taken as a whole, the state of generalization practice in all IS research needs to improve. Let us call this Claim 1 for this paper. Claim 1 is a generalization of the findings from our sample of 112 studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008. Further, it is expected that the reader will feel uncomfortable about the huge leap we just made in generalizing from a sample of 112 papers from two US-based journals to all IS research. We have done this deliberately, for two reasons. First, we want to illustrate the nature of the problem with generalizations, namely that they are normally based on limited samples, and always have the potential to be shown to be incorrect (Campbell & Stanley, 1963). Second, we want to present an example of the type of justification logic that can be used to support a general knowledge claim. The logic we use to justify Claim 1 (which corresponds to path ABC the framework discussed shortly) is as follows:

Step A: In the previous three sections, we have shown that the state of generalization practice in many papers in the sample of 87 quantitative and qualitative empirical papers in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 is less than ideal. (This argument corresponds to step A in our generalizationjustification framework, presented shortly.)

Step B: (1) ISR and MISQ are top-ranked IS journals (Saunders, 2006; Straub & Anderson, 2010). (2) Papers in the top-ranked journals are supposed to be reviewed to the very highest standards. (3) It is unlikely that papers in lower-ranked journals exhibit higher methodological standards. Therefore, the finding of less-than-ideal practices in many papers in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 is likely to apply to all IS journals, say, for the past decade. (This argument corresponds to step B in our generalization-justification framework, presented shortly.)

Step C: Further, and consistent with the Bayesian thinking discussed shortly, Claim 1 is buttressed by the knowledge that earlier studies by other researchers have also drawn attention to problems with generalization of findings from both IS and non-IS research studies. First, in the IS literature, Pinsonneault & Kraemer (1993), Lee & Baskerville (2003), King & He (2005), and Sivo et al (2006) have also pointed to problems with generalization practice. Second, with respect to quantitative studies more generally, the authors of many articles and books advocating the use of (a) Bayesian statistics, for example, Gelman et al (2004), Berger (2003), Hubbard & Armstrong (2006), and Gill (2008), (b) law of likelihood, for example, Royall (2000) and Gregoire (2001), and (c) exploratory data analysis (Cohen, 1994), have described the inadequacies of both likelihoodist (Fisherian) and frequentist (Neyman–Pearsonian) statistics, particularly the use of P-values, for statistical inference. Thus although Claim 1 is based primarily on the sample data in Table 2, similar conclusions from these prior studies increase one’s confidence in Claim 1. (This argument corresponds to step C in our generalization-justification framework, presented shortly.)

## Summary

In this section of this paper (Part 1), we have sought to convince the reader that the state of generalization practice in IS research needs to improve. The evidence offered to support this claim was an analysis of all 112 papers in 2 years of two leading IS journals, plus – equally importantly – supporting evidence from prior studies that have also drawn attention to problems with generalization in IS research. Parts 2 and 3 of the paper discuss our suggested solution to this problem.

## Part 2: a framework for justifying generalizations in IS Research

## What are generalizations?

Building on the definition and assumptions in Table 1 and the arguments in Seddon & Scheepers (2006), we argue that generalizations in IS research have the following characteristics:

1. What we have termed ‘generalization’ in this paper is what Campbell & Stanley (1963), Cook & Campbell (1979), and Shadish et al (2002) call external validity. It also corresponds to Lee & Baskerville’s (2003) second type of empirical-to-theoretical generalization, that is, ‘generalizability of the resulting theory beyond the sample or domain that the researcher observes’ (p. 236).

2. Generalization claims may apply to both the population from which the sample was or might conceptually have been drawn, and populations beyond the sampling population, for example, those in other countries, cultures, or time periods, where the results of the study might also apply (Cronbach, 1982; Shadish et al, 2002).

3. The things generalized are knowledge claims, for example, propositions, models, or theories, that the researchers believe explain the facts in both past research and their present studies.

4. Generalizing knowledge claims is meaningful and useful for researchers of all ontological persuasions – positivist, interpretivist, and critical – except those who believe that each contextual situation is so unique that generalization to other settings is not possible.

5. Generalization claims are made by researchers; transferability judgments about the applicability of knowledge claims to a particular new setting are made by readers (Barnes et al, 2005).

6. Contrary to Yin’s (1989, 1994, 2003) claims that ‘analytic’ and ‘statistical’ generalization are different, his two ‘types’ of generalization can more usefully be viewed as two alternative logical pathways for justifying the one thing, namely, generalizations. As a rough approximation, Yin’s two terms correspond to the leftand right-hand sides of Figure 1 (steps AB and DF), respectively, discussed shortly.

7. The intuition behind what it meant by a ‘representative’ sample (a key concept in the discussion that follows) is that in a quantitative study:

‘For the variables relevant to the study, statistics calculated from a representative sample are similar to those of the population from which the sample was selected’.

In a case-study context, this becomes:

‘Cause-and-effect relationships in representative case studies are similar to those in the population from which the case studies were selected or for which generalization claims are made or implied’.

8. Useful guidelines for assessing or demonstrating representativeness are in Cronbach (1982), Shadish (1995), and Shadish et al (2002). These are based on Cronbach’s Units, Treatment, Observations, and Settings framework. In particular, following Shadish et al (2002), we recommend that researchers should use the theories discussed in their papers as the logical basis for discussions of generalization, and that representativeness should be demonstrated for the key factors related to the knowledge claim being generalized (Table 1, Key characteristic 2).

Points 7 and 8 are so important that further explanation is warranted. The following example shows that a sample may be representative of a population on some dimensions, and not on others. Suppose that the population of interest is all the fruit in a supermarket. Further suppose that the sample of interest is the fruit a shopper has selected in his or her shopping trolley. Finally suppose that the shopper is planning to make a red-berry fruit salad and has selected strawberries, raspberries, blackberries, boysenberries, blueberries, and cherries for the fruit salad. In what sense, if at all, is the fruit in the shopper’s trolley representative of the fruit on sale in the store? Our claim is that the answer depends on the attributes of interest. If the attribute of interest is freshness, the fruit in the trolley may well be representative. However, if the attribute of interest is fruit type or colour, the sample is highly skewed. As this simple example makes clear, a sample may be representative of a population in some respects and not in others. Representativeness should therefore be assessed in terms of the attributes of interest in the study. This is a key insight that informs the generalization-justification discussion that follows.

## Justifying generalizations in IS research

The position taken in this paper is that the combination of Table 1 and the preceding eight points provides a useful characterization of what generalization means for the IS research community. However, that characterization does not explain the logic through which generalizations should be justified. The claim we now make – the key contribution of this paper – is that there appear to be eight distinct pathways, or logical chains of argument, available to IS researchers to justify generalization claims. These are pathways ABC, DBC, DFC, DFG, DEFC, DEFG, DH, and DEH in Figure 1. Key parts of Figure 1 are now discussed in turn.

## Analytic generalization of results from a single study (step B in Figure 1)

The first two pathways for supporting generalizations from a single study, paths ABC and DBC in Figure 1, use what Yin (2003) calls ‘analytic generalization’ as a key step in their generalization-justification logic. Analytic generalization (step B in Figure 1) means arguing, based on similarities between relevant attributes of things in a sample and things in other (broader) settings, that knowledge claims based on the sample are also likely to hold true in those other settings. For example, based on their case study in the US Residential Mortgage industry, Markus et al (2006, p. 461) developed four propositions that they claimed are (a) true in the US Retail Mortgage industry, and (b) likely to be true for all vertical industry standardization (VIS) efforts, for example, grocery manufacturers and retailers, automobile assemblers and suppliers. Their justification for their more general claim, point (b), is that ‘heterogeneity of interests among participating user organizations’ is the driving force for the observed phenomena in their sample, and further, that these same forces also exist in the other settings, so threatening the success of VIS standards development in those other settings, for example, by inhibiting collaboration. Note the twostep, AB, generalization logic Markus et al (2006) used is as follows:

![](/api/attachments/JG5MXZCK/fulltext/images/ee80df4f324752608f2a52987132d2f6dabb777d3f912493de08905729cea317.jpg)  
Figure 1 Alternative logical pathways for justifying generalizations.  
Note: Boxes for steps G and H have dotted lines to emphasize that they are relatively unusual in IS research. Step G, meta-analysis, is unusual because there are often insufficient studies of similar phenomena to conduct a statistical meta-analysis. Step H, Bayesian statistical analysis, is unusual because, as documented in Table 2, the tools and techniques for Bayesian statistical analysis are currently not widely applied in the IS research community.

Step A: Based on careful analysis of their sample, Markus and colleagues developed propositions 1–4. These are sample-based (i.e., sample-specific, in this case casespecific) knowledge claims.

Step B: Because the forces that motivate the behaviour of people and organizations in their sample are also likely to exist in other settings, they argue that their findings are also likely to apply in those other settings, that is, in ‘all VIS efforts’.

Step A is what might be termed local theory building. Step B is generalizing analytically, that is, using reasoning, to clearly defined other settings. Step B in Figure 1 can be used to justify generalizations from single samples in both qualitative and quantitative IS research, that is, paths AB and DB, respectively, in Figure 1. For example, survey researchers do not have to use inferential statistics (step F in Figure 1, discussed below) to justify their generalization claims. They can simply combine steps D and B and justify their general knowledge claim analytically. In fact, step B is the only option – though admittedly an uncomfortable one – for researchers with quantitative data that are not a probability sample or where the researcher is unable to provide a convincing argument for treating the sample as like a random sample (step E2), and so analysable using inferential statistics. Use of inferential statistics, such as P-values with such samples cannot be justified.

Those who frame their thinking about generalization in terms of the arguments of Yin (2003) will probably argue that our description of generalizing from case studies via path AB is wrong, that case-study researchers ‘generalize to theory’, and that all this talk of samples and populations shows that the authors have a limited understanding of what qualitative research is about. With respect, we disagree. When one examines the process of theory building from case studies carefully, one finds that – as shown in the Markus et al (2006) example above – local theory comes first (step A), then generalization follows (step B). However, Yin’s term ‘generalizing to theory’ implies that theory is the result of generalization. Clearly, under our definition of generalization, it is not. Therefore, we argue, the term ‘generalizing to theory’ should not be used in the context of the generalization-justification framework proposed in Figure 1. The two views are incompatible.

Yin (2003) clarifies the meaning of his term ‘generalizing to theory’ on page 37 when he says:

A theory must be tested by replicating the findings in a second or even a third neighbourhood, where the theory has specified that the same results should occur (emphasis in italics added by us)

Here we are in full agreement. As the words in italics make clear, for Yin (2003) (and Gregor (2006), too), all theories contain statements of boundary conditions. The problem is that, as shown in Table 2, many IS researchers – quantitative and qualitative – present theories without defining boundary conditions.

As shown in Figure 1, once the step B argument has been presented, researchers seeking to justify their generalization knowledge claims are advised to discuss the fit between their study’s findings and those of earlier studies. Good fit strengthens the reader’s confidence in the truth of the researchers’ knowledge claims. This corresponds to step C in Figure 1, discussed in the section below headed ‘Generalizing from multiple studies’.

## Statistical generalization from a single probability sample (paths DF and DH in Figure 1)

The remaining six pathways for supporting generalizations from a single study are variants of what Yin (2003) calls ‘statistical’ generalization, and what statisticians call inferential statistics. Of these, three paths, DFC, DFG, and DH, rely on the availability of true probability samples as an essential requirement of their generalizationjustification logics. In these three paths the researcher first draws a probability sample (e.g., a random sample) from a clearly defined population of interest, then:

Step D: Observes patterns of relationships in the sample and makes local, that is, sample-specific, knowledge claims based on those patterns of variation. (For example, the researcher might compute path coefficients and variance explained (R<sup>2</sup>) in the sample.)

F or H: uses patterns of variation in the sample to make inferences about the truth or otherwise of the local, that is, sample-specific, knowledge claims in the population.

\- If step F is chosen, adoption of Fisherian P-value logic would involve (i) estimating the probability, Pr(data|H ), that the patterns of relationship observed in this sample could have been drawn from a population where there was no relationship between the variables of interest (the null hypothesis), then (ii) using judgment to make an inference about Pr(H |data), that is, whether the relationships found in the sample imply that there is a non-zero relationship in the population. (After step F, researchers seeking to justify their generalization knowledge claims should discuss fit between their study’s findings and those of earlier studies. This can be done using steps C or G in Figure 1, discussed in the section below headed ‘Generalizing from multiple studies’. Since step G requires a lot of work it is expected that most researchers will use step C logic to complete the justification of their generalizations.)

\- If step H is chosen, data from the sample would be used to revise the prior probability that the knowledge claim is true in the population in the light of the new evidence from the sample. (Bayesian step H automatically includes recognition of prior studies.)

Note, again, the two-step generalization logic in these three logical pathways (DFC, DFG, and DH). Step D is local theory building or testing (usually the latter in quantitative research). Knowledge claims at this stage are limited to claims about relationships between variables in the sample, for example, the path coefficients or coefficient of determination (R<sup>2</sup>). Steps F and H are generalizing to well-defined other settings. However, steps F and H are valid only if the population is well defined and the sample is a probability sample from that population. As van Belle (2002, p. 2) says:

The Achilles’ heel of statistical practice and inference is the selection of the sample. A probability model is required to draw inferences (emphasis added)

However, as shown in Table 2, even in the two leading IS journals analysed in Part 1, few samples used in IS research are probability samples. Therefore, strictly speaking, many published quantitative IS research studies are logically incomplete.

## Statistical generalization from a non-probability sample (step E in Figure 1)

Because few studies have access to true probability samples, few studies can use Paths DF or DH for justifying their generalizations. This brings us to the final three pathways for justifying supporting generalizations from a single quantitative study, namely, paths DEFC, DEFG, and DEH. All three pathways include step E in Figure 1, which itself consists of three steps: E1, E2, and E3. Step E is our suggested solution to the difficulties of using inferential statistics with non-probability samples. It requires researchers to exercise judgment about sample representativeness, just as is required for step B.

Step E in Figure 1 relies on (i) the concept of a representative sample, defined earlier, and (ii) the principles underlying the central-limit theorem, which show that as the size of a random sample increases, that sample is increasingly likely to be representative of the population from which it was drawn. Using these two concepts, the logical chain of argument that needs to be inserted after step D, and before steps F or H, is as follows:

Step E1: Provide sufficient evidence to show that for the variables of interest in the study the sample is representative of the clearly defined larger population of interest. Due to their intimate knowledge of the data-collection process, a study’s authors are likely to be in a much better position to make such judgments about target populations and representativeness than their readers.

Step E2: Argue that a representative sample is similar to a random sample.

Step E3: Argue that inferential statistics (e.g., P-values) developed for probability samples may therefore be applied to guide inferences about the population from a representative sample.

These three steps rejoin the otherwise-broken logical chain of argument needed to justify the use of inferential statistics with real-life non-probability samples. Provided representativeness can be demonstrated – a big proviso – steps E1–E3 may be used before steps F or H to try to justify the use of inferential statistics with, say, data from a survey with a o100% response rate, or data from a convenience sample. The key to step E is to demonstrate representativeness for the constructs of interest in the study. After step E, the final steps in the generalization justification then follow paths FC, FG, or H, as discussed above. The logics required for steps ${ \mathrm { C } } , \ { \mathrm { G } } ,$ or H are discussed in more depth below under the heading ‘Generalizing from multiple studies’.

## Bayesian statistics (step H in Figure 1)

Although none of the 66 quantitative studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 used Bayesian statistics, authors such as Gill (2008), Lynch (2007), Gelman et al (2004), Berger (2003), Goodman (1999a, b, 2005), and many others argue that (i) Bayesian methods are superior to today’s likelihoodist (Fisherian) and frequentist (Neyman–Pearsonian) NHST orthodoxy, and (ii) with the rapidly increasing availability of computer programs supporting Bayesian analysis (e.g., using Markov-chain, Monte-Carlo and Gibbs-sampling techniques), there is little to stop researchers from using Bayesian methods. An oft-perceived limitation of Bayesian statistics is the subjectivity of the prior-probability estimates, but the authors cited above argue that this limitation is not insurmountable. Gill (2008, pp. 21–29, 62–65), for instance, argues that Bayesian disclosure of priors actually provides the reader with important subjective information that is implicit but hidden in typical Fisher/ Neyman–Pearson analyses (step F in Figure 1).

In this paper we take a position that Bayesian methods are a viable alternative to P-value logic, and that although they are not yet common, Bayesian methods may start appearing in leading IS journals in future. For that reason, we included step H as an alternative to P-value generalization logic (step F) in Figure 1, though because it is so little used at present, we have shown step H in a box with a dashed-line border. Note that Bayesian methods still rely on data from probability samples for making sound inferences, so step E logic will often still be necessary for drawing conclusions from nonprobability samples.

## Generalizing from multiple studies

We are now ready to present the final key argument underpinning the generalization framework in Figure 1. As Campbell & Stanley (1963) have explained, the limitation with generalizations as knowledge claims is that they rely on assumptions such as the uniformity of nature (Lee & Baskerville, 2003); they may later be proven to be wrong. Further empirical testing of generalizationbased claims is therefore important because it provides new evidence for, or against, such claims. Once a series of studies has been conducted, the question arises: Does knowledge from prior theory and prior studies strengthen knowledge claims from a later study?

To illustrate, assume that studies 1 to 3 in Figure 2 have each (a) built on some or all of theories P, Q, and R, (b) concluded that X probably causes Y in their respective samples, and (c) independently asserted as generalizations Gen1 to Gen3 (e.g., on the basis of Path AB or DEF logic) that X probably causes Y for all members of the population in the large rectangle in Figure 2. Now suppose that, informed by the prior theories and studies, the researchers in Study 4 conclude that there is evidence that X probably causes Y in their sample, then use a pathway such as AB, DF, or DEF in Figure 1 to argue, as Gen4, that X probably causes Y in the population in Figure 2.

Is generalization 4 (represented by the dark arrow between Study 4 and the population) stronger because of the support from the prior theory and studies? Commonsense says it should be, but traditional Fisherian and Neyman–Pearsonian analyses provide no mechanism for recognizing the additional weight of evidence provided by Studies 1–3. However, Bayesian analysis does. This is why Bayesian analysis (Goodman, 1999a, b, 2005; Berger, 2003; Gelman et al, 2004; Gill, 2008), step H in Figure 1, is so attractive. Bayesian analysis is used to revise the prior probability that an hypothesis is true, in the light of new evidence. Thus a Bayesian will argue that as each study in Figure 2 provides further evidence that X probably causes Y. one's degree of belief that the hypothesis is true in the population increases. This means that with a Bayesian analysis, the conclusions from Study 4 will reflect quantitatively the lessons from prior theory, Studies 1–3, and the information in sample 4. By contrast, Fisherian P-values and Neyman–Pearsonian methods ignore the evidence from prior studies in their quantitative measures.

Figure 2 Generalizing from multiple studies.

However, as mentioned earlier, none of the studies in ISR and MISQ in 2007–2008 used Bayesian statistics. If researchers choose to restrict themselves to non-Bayesian analysis, they have two other mechanisms for combining conclusions from the three preceding studies in Figure 2 with insights from their own sample in Study 4. These are (a) Bayesian-compatible, literature-review-style comparisons of findings from one’s own study with findings from prior studies, as depicted in step C in Figure 1, and (b) meta-analyses, as depicted in step G in Figure 1. Such generalizations are both represented by the arrow Gen5 in Figure 2. Since readers will be familiar with both systematic literature reviews (Webster & Watson, 2002; Rousseau et al, 2008) and meta-analyses (Glass et al, 1981; Schmidt, 1992; Hunter & Schmidt, 2004), they are not discussed further. Generalization claims based on any of these three techniques – Bayesian analysis (step H), Bayesian-compatible systematic literature reviews (step C), and meta-analyses (step G) – are highly valued because the evidence supporting such claims is based on conclusions from many studies in many different contexts, and are therefore more likely to be true.

From a practical perspective, step C is the most likely choice for IS researchers. Step G, meta-analysis, is unusual because there are often insufficient studies of similar phenomena to conduct a meaningful statistical metaanalysis. Step H, Bayesian statistical analysis, is unusual because, as documented in Table 2, the tools and techniques for Bayesian statistical analysis are not widely applied in the IS research community. For that reason, as noted under Figure 1, the boxes for steps G and H have been drawn with dotted lines. The intention of this notation is to emphasize that these two steps are currently relatively unusual in IS research. Thus, in a typical research report on an empirical study, it is expected that most IS researchers will choose to use step C logic in preference to step G or H to complete the generalization logic in their paper.

Further, since the literature review at the start of each paper should have already attempted to summarize the best that is already known about the topic of interest, step C can probably be confined to a brief discussion of how results from the current study add to findings documented in the literature review. This is not as comprehensive as a full literature review (Webster & Watson, 2002; Rousseau et al, 2008), but it is probably sufficient for most purposes. In addition to completing the logic of any sound generalization process, such a paragraph would also help to highlight the contribution of the paper.

One example of a step C-style argument was presented towards the end of Part 1. A second example of how a step C argument might be written is in the discussion section in Markus et al (2006, pp. 460–462), the single-case-based study mentioned earlier. In that section, Markus et al (2006) develop four general propositions about ‘VIS standardization efforts’, and comment that they based their propositions on the ‘prior theoretical literature’ and triangulated their results ‘against those of other case studies, such as Brown (1997)’. (Brown (1997) looked at the development of standards for barcodes in the retail stores.) They then ask whether their results might generalize further, to various other types of industry standardization efforts and after considering 18 other studies, conclude that they would probably not. This sort of attempt to assess the generalizability of one’s conclusions by comparing one’s own knowledge claims to those of the prior literature, and explain how one's own results are consistent with, or differ from the prior literature, is consistent with the spirit of our recommendations for step C.

## Part 3: future discussions of generalization in IS research

Having defined ‘generalization’ in terms of Table 1 and the eight points at the start of Part 2, and discussed the eight logical pathways shown in Figure 1 that may be used to justify generalizations, this third part of the paper now asks: How should generalization be discussed in future IS research reports? In other words, what should IS researchers say about the generalizability of their research findings, how much should they leave to their readers to make their decisions about the relevance of the findings to the reader’s own situation (transferability), how should generalizations be justified, and what criteria should reviewers adopt in assessing generalizability claims from empirical studies?

Our answer to these questions is that it will help readers of all empirical IS research papers to understand the implications of those studies if the authors include discussions of generalizability of their knowledge claims in their papers, including a discussion of the likely boundary conditions for any general knowledge claims. Further, it is suggested that the generalization logic used to justify generalizations in IS research should be based on one of the eight paths in Figure 1. Qualitative studies can only use path ABC. Quantitative studies will use one of the remaining seven paths. Further, because (a) P-values are so widely used, (b) yet most samples for quantitative analysis are not true probability samples, (c) meta-analyses (step G) require so much additional work (Schmidt, 1992), and (d) Bayesian analysis (step H) is so little used (at least at present), we expect most of today’s quantitative researchers will choose to use path DEFC to justify their generalization-based knowledge claims. So the two most popular generalization-justification logics for IS researchers in the near future are likely to be ABC and DEFC.

To show how the principles from the framework in Figure 1 can inform research practice on generalization, five common examples of generalization issues are now discussed.

## Statistical generalization

As discussed earlier, statistical generalization is when authors attempt to use Fisherian, Neyman–Pearsonian, or Bayesian inferential statistics (steps F or H) to justify their generalization knowledge claims. For statistical generalization, researchers need to specify the target population, that is, boundary conditions, for their general knowledge claims. Whenever a sample is not a probability sample, or a group of subjects is chosen for an experiment, researchers need to include step E logic in their statistical-generalization justification argument. This means they need to demonstrate the representativeness of their sample for the variables and interrelationships of interest in the study. To do this, it is not sufficient to compare, say, early and late respondents to a survey on some arbitrary variables that happen to be available in the study, for example, sales revenue. Rather, the variables used to assess representativeness must be those of primary interest in the study. One approach to demonstrating representativeness that does not seem to have been widely used might be for the authors to show that their sample exhibits patterns of variation of key variables and relationships among the key variables similar to those in other studies that have explored similar phenomena for the same target population.

Concerns about statistical generalization issues are evident in reviewer comments such as ‘This paper should be rejected because the response rate is too low’ or ‘ This paper should be rejected because it uses a convenience sample’. When reviewers have concerns such as these, they will find it helpful to frame their comments based on step E logic, that is, in terms of the representativeness (or otherwise) of the sample for the purposes of this particular study, rather than focusing on the low response rate or use of a convenience sample, per se.

## Generalizing from a single case study

As Walsham (1995), Yin (2003), Lee & Baskerville (2003), Markus et al (2006), Eisenhardt & Graebner (2007), and many others have argued, there is no question that sound generalizations – providing useful insights to guide future practice – can be based on knowledge claims from a single case study. First, as Popper (1992) has argued so clearly, a single case can be all that is required to disprove a theory or force redefinition of its boundary conditions. Second, on the theory-affirmation side, if the forces within an organization that drove observed behaviour are likely to exist in other organizations, it is likely that those other organizations, too, will exhibit similar behaviour. Researchers can never prove that their generalized knowledge claims are true, but they can – with appropriate caveats – provide arguments that their truth is highly likely. That said, generalizing to other settings based on findings from a single case always needs to be done with great caution: it is so easy to jump to the wrong conclusions!

Authors of papers using the AB chain of justification logic in Figure 1 to support generalizations from case studies need to make clear which factors are salient, and why those factors might also apply in other settings. As discussed in Part 2, it is recommended that as an alternative to Yin’s (2003) notion of ‘generalizing to theory’ when justifying theory-building generalizations from case studies, it is clearer to (1) formulate a local theory to explain phenomena in the context(s) studied, then (2) discuss the extent to which this local knowledge claim is likely to hold in other broader settings.

Finally, as the discussion of generalization from multiple studies in Part 2 makes clear, consistency of a case study’s findings with prior knowledge (step C) adds weight to case-based general knowledge claims. This is particularly important with single-case studies, because of the concern that things unique to the case mean that knowledge claims that are true for that case may not be true in other settings.

## Generalizations from ‘old’ data, that is, over time

It is not uncommon for authors to submit papers that rely on data collected perhaps 10 or more years ago. This causes difficulties for reviewers who write comments such as ‘This paper should be rejected because the data are too old’. The real concern is not that the data are old, but that knowledge claims based on those old data may not be valid today. Such generalizations may be called ‘generalizations over time’. Assuming the study is internally valid, that is, that the sample-based knowledge claims are sound, the key question is whether knowledge claims that are true in that old data set are still true today. Though the age of the data must also be considered in steps C, G, and H, discussion of the likely impact of old data in a single sample on the generalizability of a study’s conclusions is particularly important in steps B and E in Figure 1.

Authors seeking to generalize from an old sample of data using step B or E reasoning need to (a) explain that they are trying to generalize over time, (b) provide readers with details of the technologies, economic conditions, and possibly even institutions at the time data were collected, then (c) provide convincing arguments to explain why knowledge claims based on those old data are still likely to be valid today. For example, in a study of ‘employee resistance to change’ knowledge claims based on 10-year-old data should still be valid today because human behavioural characteristics of 10 years ago are likely to representative of those today. However, in a study of e-Commerce strategies, knowledge claims based on 1997 data are unlikely to be useful today (because e-Commerce strategies of 1997 are unlikely to be representative of e-Commerce strategies today). Our advice to reviewers who find themselves having to comment on such papers is that rather than saying that the data are ‘too old’, it would be more helpful to explain that for the topic being studied, the data from yesteryear are not likely to be representative of the situation today, so the knowledge claims based on those old data are not valid today.

## Generalizations from student subjects to managers

It is also not uncommon for authors to submit papers that rely on data collected from students. This causes difficulties for reviewers who write comments such as ‘This paper should be rejected because it uses student subjects’. The real concern is not that the data were collected from student subjects, but that knowledge claims based on the behaviour or responses of those students might not be valid for the target population of interest. As in the previous example, the implications for generalizability of using student subjects need to be discussed particularly carefully in steps B and E of the various generalization-justification logic pathways in Figure 1, and the key argument will again be around representativeness. If the goal of the study is to explore the way experts use technology, students are unlikely to be good proxies. But if the goal of the study is to explore how managers solve unfamiliar problems with a new piece of technology, knowledge claims based on the students are likely to be valid for managers too. (The reason is that both students and managers are likely to have the similar cognitive limitations, so both groups are likely to exhibit bounded rationality in decision making.) In short, researchers who use student subjects have to clearly define their target population, then explain to their readers why the students are representative of individuals in that target population for the topic studied.

## Generalizations from one country to another

Studies in different countries and cultures are particularly helpful in testing the boundary conditions of knowledge claims from earlier studies. They are valuable because contextual differences such as culture, institutions, and levels of economic development mean that knowledge claims based on data collected in one country do not automatically hold in another.

Researchers who make knowledge claims based on data collected from any one country therefore have an obligation to define clearly the country where the data were collected, the nature of the respondents, the population of things to which the researchers believe their knowledge claims apply, and to justify those claims for the topic that they have studied. Equally, to reduce the risk of unsound transferability judgments by readers, the authors should also document any underlying contextual factors that may limit the more general applicability of their sample-based knowledge claims to other settings. As with the earlier examples, such discussions will normally be presented in steps B and E in Figure 1 (and step C where appropriate).

## Applicability of the arguments in this paper to other disciplines

Finally, do the knowledge claims made in this paper apply to other disciplines? The position taken in this paper is that although evidence of unsatisfactory generalization practice presented in this paper – including the prior arguments and evidence from Lee & Baskerville (2003), King & He (2005), Sivo et al (2006), and the analysis reported in Part 1 of this paper (Table 2) – is limited to IS research, many IS researchers are also very familiar with literatures from other disciplines (e.g., psychology, management, economics, and accounting), so it is unlikely that generalization practices in IS are very different from those other disciplines. Further, none of the key characteristics of generalization presented in this paper are specific to IS research. Therefore, the generalization-justification framework in Figure 1 probably applies to any social science research. However, this is a very preliminary general knowledge claim, based on step B-style thinking. Clearly, it still needs both (a) further testing, and (b) further step C discussion (both of which we treat as out of scope for the current paper).

## Conclusion and contributions

There are two main conclusions we can draw from the above research. First, the state of generalization practice in both quantitative and qualitative IS research needs improvement. An analysis of generalization practices in a sample from two leading IS journals shows that nearly all quantitative studies reported P-values, yet only half discussed generalizability of their knowledge claims, and only a third discussed populations of interest. Since P-values only estimate the probability that the data came from a population where the null hypothesis is true, and most researchers want to know the reverse, more discussion in many published papers is needed about whether relationships in the population are likely to be non-null given the evidence provided by the data. It is because of (a) the absence of this discussion in many papers, as well as (b) the use of non-probability samples (e.g., as the result of non-responses to surveys) without discussion of sample representativeness, that we argue that the state of generalization practice in many quantitative IS research studies needs to be improved. In addition, our evidence shows that many qualitative IS researchers discuss findings from their studies as if they have general applicability beyond their samples, yet do not discuss boundary conditions for their knowledge claims.

Second, generalization from almost any empirical IS research study – quantitative or qualitative – requires careful qualitative (i.e., analytic) justification and human judgment. In other words, for all but quantitative studies with true probability samples from well-defined populations, generalization is a much more subjective process than the P-value-based logic described in most quantitative-analysis texts and used in many empirical IS research papers.
