# Consumer’s decision to shop online: The moderating role of positive informational social influence

Matthew K.O. Lee <sup>a,b,</sup>\*, Na Shi <sup>a</sup>, Christy M.K. Cheung <sup>c</sup>, Kai H. Lim <sup>b</sup>, Choon Ling Sia <sup>b</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

While much of the current literature tends to focus on the direct effect of social influence on consumer online shopping behavior, our study drew heavily on social influence theory and argued for an alternative theory focusing on the moderating role of social influence. In particular, we explored how positive messages in online discussion forums may affect consumers’ decisions to shop online. We used a laboratory experiment in which the treatment group was required to read positive messages about online shopping experience in an online discussion forum. Positive social influence was found to reinforce the relationship between beliefs about and attitude toward online shopping, as well as the relationship between attitude and intention to shop. We believe our alternative theory provides new insight into the complex processes through which social influence is brought to bear on consumers online shopping decisions. Implications of our work are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Web 2.0 facilitates collaboration and sharing among users through social media, such as online discussion forums, electronic bulletin board systems, blogs, etc. Such applications are significantly changing our life and the relationship between consumers and retailers. Customers can easily obtain information about goods and services from a large, geographically dispersed population, who have bought and used possible products or services. The resulting volume of electronic word of mouth (eWOM) available and its easy access through Web 2.0 applications are increasingly important in affecting shopping decisions.

Consumer theorists have long recognized the influence that friends and reference groups have on consumer decision making. Prospective online shoppers can easily access large volumes of information about a particular topic from those who have recent experience with it. This is a special type of social influence: informational social influence, which involves accepting information or advice from a person who may not have previously been known as a friend or colleague.

Informational social influence is especially important when a consumer is faced with time constraints, possesses limited knowledge, perceives high risk in the action, or simply lacks interest in making the decision alone. Given the perceived risk involved in online shopping [6,13], we believed that prospective online shoppers will ask the opinion of their friends or online consumer groups before they make an online purchase decision. While informational social influence could be positive (successful cases) or negative (bad experiences), we focused on positive informational social influence in our study, because we were interested in facilitating online shopping.

TAM and its variants have been widely used to examine consumers’ decision to shop online [14]. We attempted to introduce the concept of informational social influence to the process and investigate its role in affecting the beliefs, attitude, and intention to shop on the Internet. We believed that informational social influence provided more of a moderating, rather than a direct, role.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Intention models

TAM has been widely adopted to explain user behavior across a broad range of end-user computing technologies and populations, while at the same time being both parsimonious and theoretical [11]. It was based solidly on Ajzen’s Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), which postulated that attitude toward a particular object depended on the direct effects of beliefs (perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEoU)) about the object, and that attitude also had a direct positive impact on behavioral intention (BI) toward the object (see Fig. 1).

Fig. 1. One version of TAM.

PU (the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system enhances job performance) and PEoU (the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system requires little effort) thus affect user attitude and behavior intent [8].

The role of social influence in technology acceptance decisions still remains unclear in IS research. Venkatesh et al. [17] built on the eight commonly used theoretical models of TAM and proposed UTAUT. Their model included four core determinants and four moderators of key relationships, with social influence being one of the four determinants of intention and usage. However UTAUT included only one aspect of social influence: subjective norm. With the growth and public popularity of Web 2.0 applications, social influence has become particularly important in explaining group and collective behavior. It thus seemed crucial to include other types of social influence.

## 2.2. Social influence

Opinions of others influence consumers’ purchasing intent. Normative intent involves social pressure from colleagues, friends, etc. to conform to their expectations, while informational social influence involves accepting information from another person without question.

Identification occurs when an individual adopts a behavior derived from others and it is associated with a satisfying selfdefining relationship. Compliance occurs when an individual conforms to the expectations of important others in order to receive a reward or avoid a punishment. Informational social influence entails accepting information from others to facilitate problem solving or cope with some aspect of the environment.

Prior intention models among IS research have considered only one of the social influence processes: compliance (subjective norm). For example, some people may feel that by not adopting a particular technology, they may be perceived by others as ‘‘old fashioned’’. Coupled with the popularity of Web 2.0 applications, consumers can easily communicate and exchange online shopping experience with each other.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 2 depicts our research model. Consumers’ beliefs of online shopping features (PU and PEoU) determine their attitude toward online shopping, and this then affects their intention to shop. PEoU also posits a positive relationship with PU. In addition to these basic variables, in most circumstances, prospective online consumers get information about the product they are considering buying before they make the purchase. With the connectivity of the Internet, they can easily interact and exchange shopping experience with others. In our study, we attempted to explain how positive informational social influence affected the relationships between beliefs, attitude, and BI in online shopping.

## 3.1. PU and PEoU in online shopping

Beliefs are a person’s feelings about some aspect of the world; they deal with the person’s self understanding and the environment. Beliefs are the building blocks of a person’s conceptual structure and serve as the informational base that ultimately determines the person’s attitudes, intentions, and behavior. In ecommerce, studies have shown that PU and PEoU of the online shopping platform are the two beliefs that determine the likelihood of online shopping [18,19]. We believe that these relationships are robust and form a baseline for our research model. Empirical evidence [1,4] has shown that PU and PEoU result in a favorable feeling toward online shopping.

## 3.2. Attitude toward online shopping

Attitude is a predisposition to respond in a consistent manner to a particular situation. An individual’s attitude is basically determined by the set of his or her beliefs about an object or person. However, only a relative small number of beliefs serve as major determinants of consumer attitude at any one time. Consistent with the findings of most IT adoption studies, research in consumer-based e-commerce has found that attitude has a strong, direct, and positive effect on BI of online shopping [9].

## 3.3. BI of online shopping

In the context of e-commerce, BI refers to the likelihood that a consumer actually buys online. There is a strong relationship between attitude and intention. The more favorable an individual's attitude, the more likely he or she will shop online. Also, people will use a technology when they believe that it will increase their job performance.

Intention to shop is treated as the dependent variable in our research, since understanding BI is important in predicting behavior: consumer’s intention to shop is an important factor in determining a consumer’s online shopping behavior.

## 3.4. Positive informational social influence

Informational social influence is a process in which people determine the successful experience of their social group in using an innovation, before deciding to adopt it. Information from social groups only serves as a supplementary function: the potential user may or may not follow what was suggested. Thus the information serves only to modify existing attitudes by moderating the impact of the beliefs on the formation of the attitude. Thus, we believed that positive informational social influence would exert a moderating impact on innovation adoption decisions.

Information from external sources can enhance consumer’s confidence in their beliefs and attitudes; the more confidence an individual has in a belief, the more likely it is that the belief will influence attitude formation. We assumed that potential online shoppers who found support for their opinions on an online discussion forum, would have more confidence in their beliefs. Therefore:

Hypothesis 1. Positive informational social influence would strengthen an existing positive relationship between PEoU and attitude toward online shopping.

Hypothesis 2. Positive informational social influence would strengthen an existing positive relationship between PU and attitude toward online shopping.

In a similar fashion, the more confidence individuals have in their attitude toward some object, the more likely it is that this attitude will guide later behavior toward the object. Thus, we believed that when online shoppers have more confidence about their attitude toward online shopping, they will be more likely to buy online. Therefore:

Hypothesis 3. Positive informational social influence would strengthen an existing positive relationship between attitude toward online shopping and intention to shop online.

## 4. Research method

The prime objective of our study was to explore the moderating role of positive informational social influence on a consumer’s decision to shop online. To control for potential confounding factors, such as connection speed and interruptions when working at home or in a dormitory, we conducted the experiment in a controlled laboratory setting.

## 4.1. Experimental task and procedures

The experimental task required participants to decide whether to make online purchases of tickets at a cinema website. To augment realism in the experimental task, we used a real cinema website (http://www.cinema.com.hk). A screen shot of the homepage of the cinema website is shown in Fig. 3.

We conducted six sessions of the experiment (three for the control group and three for the treatment group). Two of the sessions (one for each type of group) were held at the same time, and each session in our computer laboratory had about 20 participants and lasted for about 45 min. Participants were randomly assigned to participate in experimental group.

After all the participants had arrived and each had been assigned to a computer in the laboratory, the experiment administrator spent about 10 min introducing the tasks and the context in which the tasks would be performed. We stated that the context of the experiment was buying a particular movie ticket and the participants had to choose between buying the ticket on the cinema’s website or through other means (such as telephone or from the ticket office at the cinema). The same administrator conducted all the sessions to ensure consistency in the instructions.

![](/api/attachments/VBKUVKMD/fulltext/images/9ed88da0e5da027f58b1333c30c0b3b0cf34add9681f1ecdca69e3dfa73044b5.jpg)  
Fig. 3. Screen shot of the homepage of the cinema website.

For the control group, participants were told to browse in a local cinema website for about 15 min to decide whether they would make online purchases through that website. The participants then completed an online questionnaire containing measures of research variables and questions asking them for demographic information.

For the treatment group, as well as browsing the cinema website, participants were told to login and browse through an online discussion forum for 10 min before they filled in the same questionnaire. To ensure that participants browsed the cinema website before the online discussion forum, our experimental web page directed participants to the cinema website. Participants were required to click a confirmation button after they finished browsing the cinema website, and they were then directed to the online discussion forum. Similarly, they were required to click a confirmation button before being directed to the online questionnaire.

The online discussion forum and the messages were created by three research assistants. Each message was checked and amended to make sure that the messages provided positive online purchasing experience as if written by prospective users of the website (i.e., teenagers and young people) and thus seemed realistic and trustworthy. Participants were not allowed to discuss material on the discussion forum. A screen shot of the online discussion forum is shown in Fig. 4.

No communication or messaging was allowed with one another person during the experiment. Participants sat in every other seat in order to make it difficult for them to see another person’s computer screen. Responses were monitored to determine the time spent by participants on the website, the online discussion forum (for the specific treatment group), and the completion of the subsequent online questionnaire.

## 4.2. Participants

An invitation to participate in this study was sent to students in a university in Hong Kong via email, posters, and flyers inside the campus. This population is the most active class of Internet users in Hong Kong; thus we believe that our use of a student sample was appropriate and that the behavior of our participants represented typical online consumers.

![](/api/attachments/VBKUVKMD/fulltext/images/cc7610620cf1f41587467715e257d78a764cd42a17f341700210d52410076e2d.jpg)

To encourage participation, students were given about US\$7 remuneration for their time spent. We only allowed individuals who had not previously bought cinema tickets from www.cinema.com.hk to participate in our study, to avoid potential bias. A total of 104 individuals were finally included in our study; 48% were male and 52% female. More than 50% of were aged 20–25. Among the subjects, 52% were business students most others were majoring in Sciences and Engineering (24%), or Social Studies and Languages (15%). Undergraduate students were about 90% of the participants. Over 80% using the Internet for more than 4 years and all used the Internet regularly, with 94% using it more than once per day. Over 70% participants had online shopping experience. Table 1 summarizes the profile of the participants.

## 5. Analysis and results

Data analysis was carried out using SEM. Before we tested our research model, we performed manipulation checks found that the treatment was successful.

## 5.1. Manipulation checks

Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they agreed with a number of statements designed to test the effect of manipulation. For instance, we expected that the treatment group would be more likely to agree with our assumption that the online consumer group generally posted positive messages about the website (t = 6.7, p < 0.001), and that the online consumer group had satisfactory online purchase experience with the cinema websites (t = 5.7, p < 0.001). Indeed, the results show that the experimental manipulation was successful.

## 5.2. PLS analysis

We chose PLS-Graph version 3.00 to perform our analysis. It is an implementation of SEM that can model latent constructs under conditions of non-normality and small to medium sample sizes. Following a two-step analytical procedure, we first examined the measurement model, then the structural model.

## 5.2.1. Measurement models

A composite reliability of 0.70 or more and an average variance extracted (AVE) of more than 0.50 are deemed acceptable convergent validity. Table 3 summarizes the factor loadings, composite reliability, and AVE of the measures for the control and treatment group. All items had significant path loadings at the 0.01 level and fulfilled the recommended levels of the composite reliability and AVE, with composite reliability at 0.78 or more and average variance extracted at 0.64 or more. The measures of the constructs in this study therefore had adequate convergent validity.

Testing for discriminant validity involves checking whether the items measure only the construct or also measure other constructs. We verified that the square root of the AVE for each construct was greater than the correlations between it and all other constructs; Table 4, shows that this was true.

## 5.3. Hypotheses testing

Hypotheses on were tested by statistically comparing corresponding path coefficients between the two structural models. The statistical comparison was carried out using the procedure (see Appendix A) suggested in Keil et al. [10]. Table 5 summarizes the comparisons. Results show that positive informational social influence enhances the relationship between respondents’ PEoU and attitude toward online shopping. The path coefficient from PEoU to attitude for the treatment group was significantly stronger than the corresponding path coefficient for the control group, supporting Hypothesis 1. However, the path coefficient from PU to attitude for the treatment group was weaker than the corresponding path for the control group. The results therefore did not support Hypothesis 2. Results also show that positive informational social influence significantly enhanced the relationship between respondents’ attitude toward online shopping and their intention to shop online. The path coefficient from attitude to BI for the treatment group was significantly stronger than the corresponding path for the control group, supporting Hypothesis 3. It was interesting to find that the path between attitude and BI was so strong that PU was no longer significant in determining BI.

To further explore how positive informational social influence affected consumer’s intention to shop online, we performed a t-test on the mean difference between the treatment group $( { \mathrm { m e a n } } = 4 . 5 0 ,$ , standard deviation = 1.24) and the control group (mean = 3.88, standard $\mathrm { d e v i a t i o n } = 1 . 3 1 )$ . The two groups had a significant difference in terms of their intention to shop online $( t = 2 . 4 , p = 0 . 0 2 )$ . Thus, we believe that positive informational social influence can enhance consumer’s intention to shop online.

## 6. Conclusion and discussion

Motivated by the need to better understand consumers’ decision of online shopping, we explored the moderating effect of positive informational social influence on the relationships among consumer’s beliefs, attitudes, and BI of online shopping. Our findings showed that positive informational social influence strengthened the relationship between consumer’s attitude toward online shopping and their intention to shop, as well as on the relationship between consumer’s PEoU and their attitude.

## 6.1. Implications for theory and research

Our main theoretical contribution was that an exogenous variable, positive informational social influence, affects consumer decision to shop online. Bagozzi [2] argued that ‘‘Much of human behavior is not best characterized by an individual acting in isolation’’. There is a need to incorporate social factors, such as social influence, in the study of online shopping behavior. In our study, we further demonstrated that social influence significantly affects an individual’s beliefs, attitude, and BI to shop online. We provided significant empirical support to our alternative theory emphasizing the moderating role of positive informational social influence between belief and attitude, as well as attitude and BI. The positive messages in online discussion forums reinforce consumers’ preexisting positive beliefs toward attributes relevant to online shopping and enhance their confidence in these beliefs. It is also interesting that the relationship between attitude and BI to shop online was significantly enhanced by positive eWOM.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our study also provided material for Internet marketing and website design practitioners. Many retailers have realized the power of the Internet, however they still struggle to determine which strategies will be most effective in influencing consumers. Consumer reviews play an important role in enhancing consumer purchase decisions. Our study provided empirical evidence that successful experiences of other consumers positively influence online shopping decisions; messages in online discussion forum are effective in affecting consumer online purchase decision through the social influence internalization process. Internet merchants should therefore, target their marketing efforts at the beliefs and attitudes of potential shoppers, emphasizing the successful experiences of other consumers. A recent study showed that consumers trusted blogs for purchase decision and found the information on blogs helpful in making purchase decision.

Finally, PU of online shopping is consistently a dominant factor affecting the formation of consumer’s attitude toward online shopping. Internet merchants should therefore emphasize how the Internet offers a range of advantages that can collectively attract general interest in online shopping. For instance, the Internet allows consumers to shop at any time of the 24 h/day, all year around from almost any location. It also provides consumers more choices and allows them to make quick comparisons.

## 6.3. Limitations

One potential limitation of this study is related to the fact that data was collected from relatively homogeneous student subjects of a collectivistic culture, which can have a significant impact on country-level online shopping rates [12]. In addition, people from such cultures tend to view in-group members more positively than out-group members. Sia et al. [15] found that customer endorsement can build consumer trust in online shopping more effective in the collectivistic culture than in the individualistic culture (e.g., Australia). Thus, it is possible that the impact of positive informational social influence on consumer’s decision of online shopping may be stronger in the collectivistic than in the individualistic culture.

Another limitation is that the relatively small sample size could limit our ability to determine statistical significance in the testing of the hypotheses. For instance, PU is no longer significant when the impact of attitude on BI has been significantly enhanced through positive informational social influence.

Finally, our study only focused on the impact of ‘‘positive’’ informational social influence on consumer’s decision in online shopping. In conventional shopping, negative word of mouth travels farther and faster than positive word of mouth. There is a growing interest of negative asymmetry in the field of IS [3]. Recent literature has suggested that events that are negatively valenced will have longer lasting and more intense consequences than positive ones of the same type [5,7]. Further, the generated effects tend to display nonlinearity because of the diminishing sensitivity in individuals’ evaluations [7]. Such complex asymmetry and nonlinear mechanism could be detected using nonlinear model estimation and comparison techniques (e.g., logarithmic regression, structural equation modeling with quadratic latent variables) with large measuring scales.
