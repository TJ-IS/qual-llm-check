# An exploratory study of the impacts of upgrading packaged software: a stakeholder perspective

## Abstract

Once an organization has adopted packaged software, upgrades to newer versions are inevitable. Anecdotal reports suggest that software upgrades are extremely costly and may not bring actual benefit to stakeholders in the organization. Because software upgrades recur periodically, it is important to understand the impacts of software upgrades on organizational stakeholders. In this study, we examine the impact of packaged software upgrades from the perspectives of IS staff and IS users. A dual case study was conducted to understand the impacts of an SAP upgrade and a Windows upgrade. Our study shows that there are two essential costs in each upgrade, the cost of implementation and the costs of users’ learning. Moreover, not all users experience positive impacts from software upgrade. Users’ experience of beneficial impact is contingent upon users’ explicit adoption of useful new features and improvement in features that users use.

## Introduction

he use of packaged software to meet system and application software needs of organizations has steadily increased (Sommerville, 2008). According to a report by the Business Software Alliance, the worldwide market for packaged software was \$297 billion in 2008 (BSG, 2009). Although packaged software has been defined in various ways (Morisio and Torchiano, 2002; Xu and Brinkkemper, 2007), the term generally refers to a product developed by vendors and either sold as tradable product or licensed to adopting firms (Sawyer, 2000). We consider packaged software to include system and application software that is pre-built and sold to generic customers, in contrast to customized software written to meet the requirements of a single specific customer. Packaged software may be configured to varying degrees depending on the features available in the software. Packaged software is also referred to as ‘shrink-wrapped’ and ‘commercial-offthe-shelf (COTS)’ software (Carmel and Sawyer, 1998).

Adoption of packaged software presents both benefits and drawbacks (Oberndorf, 1998). On the benefit side, organizations adopt packaged software solutions to save costs (Oberndorf, 1998; Voas, 1998; Light and Sawyer, 2007), improve user capabilities (Yang et al., 2005), and reduce system development time (Yang et al., 2005). Nonetheless, one of the key drawbacks that organizations face when using packaged software is lack of control over its development (Oberndorf, 1998; Vigder and Dean, 1998; Boehm and Abts, 1999; Basili and Boehm, 2001). On an average, COTS products are upgraded every 6–12 months (Oberndorf, 1998). With many vendors only providing active support for the latest three releases (Basili and Boehm, 2001), firms that adopted packaged software have no choice but to eventually upgrade, unless they abandon the software or choose not to receive support.

In general, upgrading is an expensive and potentially disruptive endeavor because it may involve an overhaul of existing systems (Sahin and Zahedi, 2001). According to AMR research, the average ERP upgrade carries a price tag of \$1.5 million (Bonasera, 2002). Gartner estimates that the migration from Windows XP to Windows Vista costs between 2.3 and 5 million dollars for 2500 users (Silver, 2007). Although a few studies have reported that ERP upgrades could reduce client enhancement modifications, as well as future Legal Change Patch (LCP) maintenance (Ng, 2001), upgrade to a heavily customized package can be costly due to the need to re-implement custom codes after the upgrade (Light, 2001). With the cost of software maintenance amounting to 10 percent of total purchase price per year (Reifer et al., 2003), it is important to understand the impact of upgrade.

Unfortunately, the impact of an upgrade is difficult to assess, and few studies of packaged software have focused on upgrades. Studies of packaged software impact have been dominated by a focus on initial implementation, especially for ERP systems (Mabert et al., 2000; Markus et al., 2000; Francalanci, 2001; Gefen 2002; Hitt et al., 2002; Gattiker and Goodhue, 2005; Light and Sawyer, 2007). Although general IT impacts are widely studied (e.g., Weill 1992; Belcher and Watson, 1993; Finlay and Mitchell, 1994; Barua et al., 1995; Melville and Kraemer, 2004), the impact of packaged software upgrades has not received the attention that new system implementation commands. Like other ‘less glorious’ post implementation maintenance activities (Swanson and Beath, 1989), few studies have focused on the impact of software upgrades (Ng, 2001; Nah and Delgado, 2006; Khoo and Robey, 2007).

To redress this neglect, we conducted a qualitative study to shed light on this important but understudied area. To refine the objective of our investigation, we considered upgrade to be a migration from one version release to another version release. Thus we intentionally overlooked the impacts of minor upgrades, such as security patches, and other enhancements within a single version. The study examined two research questions:

(i) How does a packaged software upgrade affect stakeholders? To answer this question, we investigate how an upgrade of particular software affects two main stakeholders, IS staff and users.

(ii) What influences the impact of a packaged software upgrade? To answer this question, we focus on the processes that influence the impacts that users experience.

We conducted two qualitative case studies at a Fortune 500 company located in the United States. The first case studied Windows operating system upgrades, and the second case studied SAP upgrade.

## Understanding the impacts of packaged software upgrades

Following Orlikowski and Iacono’s (2001) advice to pay attention to the computational capabilities and functions of specific technologies, we briefly describe the software packages examined in this study. Both SAP and Windows fall under the classification of independent commercial products, but they have different modification requirements (Carney and Long, 2000). SAP requires ‘necessary tailoring and customization,’ and Windows needs ‘very little or no modification.’ As independent commercial products, both SAP and Windows necessitate in-house testing to supplement the vendors’ evaluation reports (Carney and Long, 2000). In addition, software that requires customization (i.e., SAP) should experience the more challenging upgrade, because the changes that organizations make to the software will not be preserved during subsequent upgrades (Light, 2001). In addition, SAP is a type of enterprise application software, and Windows is considered system infrastructure software (Xu and Brinkkemper, 2007). As enterprise software, SAP is often associated with business process changes. It is mainly used to integrate, automate and support day-to-day business operations (Hitt et al., 2002). By contrast, an operating system is usually in the background running applications on top of it, so any upgrades may not be immediately visible to users. Windows is mainly a platform that allows users to open applications, and little attention is paid to Windows itself unless it malfunctions. On the basis of these technology differences, we expect to observe different impacts of package upgrades.

Organizations usually upgrade to the newest version of software to achieve efficiency and improved functionality (Paine, 2000). Nevertheless, an upgrade is more complicated than overlaying new code over the old. Most software package releases contain known bugs, and one out of seven software development firms deliver code without prior testing (Minasi and Garde, 1999). Vendors also introduce changes to user interface and add or delete functionality (Boehm and Abts, 1999). These changes can have potential implications for the testing, implementation, and use of software upgrades. According to Lassila and Brancheau (1999), implementation of packaged software involves mutual adaptation between commercial software packages and organizational processes. In an upgrade, the affected system is already stabilized and has an existing user base. Thus, an upgrade can be a trigger that potentially disturbs the equilibrium of the information system. Although it is unclear whether an upgrade will introduce significant change to the deep structure of the organization, it is likely to bring disruptions to organizations and their stakeholders, leading to a transitional period for stakeholders. Our study attempts to understand impacts that originate from an organization’s decision to upgrade.

We focus on two stakeholder groups: IS users and IS staff. When a system is upgraded, normally users expect the upgraded system to remain operational and perform at least as well as the prior version (Charette et al., 1997). Although IS staff can also be the users of a particular software that is upgraded, their roles are more often associated with implementing an upgrade, sometimes spending over half of their time on maintaining software (Sahin and Zahedi, 2001). Thus, the perspective of IS staff on upgrades should differ from those of users. Hence, we observe the impact of upgrade for both groups of stakeholders.

To date, no study has specifically examined the upgrade impact from the stakeholders’ perspectives. Although many studies have reported the costs of upgrade (Bonasera, 2002; Silver, 2007), impacts on stakeholders remain unexplored. Understanding the impact that IS staff experience is critical to IS project management. Likewise, studying upgrade impact is important to understand how use patterns change (Jasperson, Carter and Zmud, 2005). Torii laboratory data reports that Microsoft Word and PowerPoint users adopt only 12–16 percent of available software features (Basili and Boehm, 2001). Thus, functionality added in new versions of software may remain unexplored (Basili and Boehm, 2001).

## Relational foundation model

We use Swanson and Beath’s (1989) relational foundation model as a conceptual framework for examining the impacts of packaged software upgrade on users and IS staff. Swanson and Beath identified a set of six relationships that exists both within and between three elements involved in software maintenance: systems, IS staff, and users. As shown in Figure 1, these relationships are (1) amongsystems, (2) among-IS staff, (3) among-users, (4) between systems-IS staff, (5) between systems-users, and (6) between IS staff-users. These relationships constitute the relational foundation of system maintenance. Each of the six relationships in the framework represents a particular aspect of system maintenance that requires management attention to issues arising from interconnected systems.

Although the relational foundation model is fairly simple, it serves as a frame to guide our investigation of the positive and negative impacts of software upgrades. In particular, the model draws attention to the effects of upgrades on both human stakeholders (IT staff and users) and application system features. Additionally, the framework adds a degree of realistic complexity to our analysis by drawing attention to the relationships between the three elements. However, the relational foundation model is relatively ‘bare in its specification of the nature of the six relationships. In the absence of hypothesized relationships or descriptions of social processes involving these relationships, our study necessarily remains exploratory rather than a test of the model. Our aim is to flesh out the bare bones of the model by moving from a rich description of the upgrades to a more theoretical account of the upgrade process.

## Method

We used the case study method to investigate two upgrade projects within a single site. According to Creswell (1997), a case study is ‘an exploration of a “bounded system” or a case over time through detailed, in-depth data collection involving multiple sources of information rich in context.’ The case study method is particularly suitable to answer questions related to what, how and why (Yin, 1984). In our study, a case is defined as an upgrade project in the organization. Bounding the case around an upgrade project allows the study to focus on the details of a particular upgrade, to uncover similarities and differences between technology projects, and to investigate the circumstances that influenced the impacts associated with upgrades.

Although bounded, an upgrade project is not confined to a single level of analysis. The relational foundation model includes relationships within and among multiple stakeholders as well as system characteristics. This forces a more inclusive perspective involving both individuals and groups. Moreover, case studies must be sensitive to elements in the context surrounding the case, such as organizational policies and software vendors. Software vendors are likely to be, especially, significant not only to the maintenance of packaged software (Hirt and Swanson, 2001), but also to packaged software upgrades.

## Site selection

We adopted a purposeful sampling approach (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Creswell, 1997). A large organization that had conducted multiple software upgrade projects in different technologies was selected. To conceal the identity of the organizations, it will be referred to as SE Co. At the time of the study, SE Co. was a Fortune 500 company, with its headquarters located in the Southeastern part of the United States. SE Co. employed 75,000 employees worldwide and was made up of two large divisions: Consumer Products (CP) and Industrial Products (IP). CP had approximately 120 manufacturing facilities throughout North America, and its annual sales exceeded \$3 billion for the years 1999–2001.

Creswell (1997) advises researchers to choose cases that are promising and useful. Accordingly, we selected two major software upgrade projects at CP: an ERP systems upgrade from SAP 3.0F to 4.6C and an operating system upgrade from various versions of Microsoft Windows to Windows 2000. The SAP upgrade was selected because it was a major upgrade that affected both IS staff and users. The SAP upgrade was completed in December of 2000, 15 months before the beginning of the study. The Windows 2000 upgrade was a major upgrade that spanned the entire CP division, and it was almost completed at the time of the study. Both projects involved users and IS staff.

## SAP case

The SAP upgrade involved a leap of 13 versions and took 6 months to complete. There were two principal stakeholder groups involved: the SAP Support Department (SAPSD)

and the users. The SAPSD included 65 full-time employees and contractors. Only full-time employees were directly responsible for maintaining the SAP software. The majority of the employees were classified into one of two categories: functional or technical. Functional personnel were IT employees who had good SAP knowledge and extensive understanding of business processes. Technical personnel were shared resources who focused most of their time on solving technical issues for the entire SAP department. Almost every SAP project involved collaboration between the two groups of IT personnel.

In addition, SAPSD worked closely with affiliated IS staff in charge of maintaining the UNIX and database infrastructure supporting SAP. SAP was managed as a centralized system at CP headquarters. Users affected by the upgrade included not only those in the CP headquarters, but also those in 44 of CP’s 120 field offices and manufacturing facilities.

## Windows case

The Windows 2000 upgrade took over 1 year to complete. There were two principal stakeholder groups involved in the Windows 2000 project: IS staff in the Windows Support Department (WSD) at CP, and users. The WSD had over 60 full-time IT employees and was responsible for the operating system’s needs at the main headquarters and three smaller sub-headquarters in other parts of the country. The senior manager also had ‘dotted line’ authority over all manufacturing facilities and warehouses. However, the management of day-to-day IT operations and support was described by the senior manager to be ‘very decentralized’ within CP. While divisional headquarters set strategic direction, and managed the operations of four headquarters locations, each manufacturing facility had its own IT department. The upgrade was a huge project that involved users from 120 locations and over 9000 desktop computers.

WSD also worked closely with Corporate Shared Services (CSS), a team of IS staff located in SE Co. headquarters. CSS’s main task was to evaluate and support operating systems for the entire company. Windows was considered a common technology used by all divisions. As a result, it had to go through a rigorous evaluation process before it could become a corporate standard and be adopted by the divisions. SAP on the contrary, was not a standard product because it was only employed by CP. Although WSD was responsible for making the decision to upgrade the entire division to Windows 2000 and deploying the upgrade, it relied on CSS to provide technical support on some of the technical issues that they could not solve.

## Data collection

Our approach to data collection and analysis was based on interpretive principles (Klein and Myers, 1999). Interpretive research depends upon the subjective reports of respondents directly involved in the phenomenon under study. Thus, although our conceptual framework includes software artifacts (systems), our knowledge of them and their influence is mediated through the interpretations of human stakeholders (Taylor, 1971). Accordingly, we used semi-structured qualitative interviews as the main source of data, so that we could access stakeholders’ perceptions of how technology upgrades affected their work. Working with the director of SAP department, we identified the heads of three departments: Infrastructure Support Department, Database, and UNIX Operating Systems group. After speaking to the manager in charge of these departments, we were referred to project leaders and ultimately other members of the project groups. As interviews progressed, a snowball sampling technique was used to solicit interview subjects.

Two stages of data collection were conducted. One began in April 2002 and lasted 9 months. During that period, both first-time and follow-up interviews were conducted. A second data collection stage was conducted in May 2004 for 2 days. Over the course of the research, 57 respondents were interviewed generating a total of 72 interviews, each lasting between 60 and 90 min. Of those interviews, 10 were phone interviews with people at two manufacturing facilities. We interviewed 33 people for the Windows upgrade and 24 people for the SAP upgrade. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed for later analysis. A summary of the respondents interviewed is presented in Table 1.

In each interview, we asked generally about the upgrade, the respondents’ roles in the upgrade, and how the upgrade affected them. Sample questions are shown in the Appendix. Depending on the subjects’ roles, relevant questions were asked. The semi-structured nature of the questionnaire permitted respondents to expand on topics of their own choosing. Our aim was to generate rich accounts of respondents’ experiences with the software upgrades, which would later be subjected to data analysis. No attempt was made to match questions with specific theoretical constructs. Indeed, the application of Swanson and Beath’s (1989) conceptual framework followed the data collection to support our analysis and interpretation of the data.

In addition to interviews, we also attended a data center tour, an interdepartmental meeting between the SAP group and its support groups, user training sessions, and observation of order entry on SAP. We also collected documentation from SE Co.’s intranet and sat in technology governance committees meetings to observe discussions on a later Windows upgrade. Although some of these activities were relevant to forthcoming projects, the opportunities to observe provided context useful in understanding stakeholders’ accounts of the upgrades.

## Data analysis

Interpretive data analysis is fundamentally a hermeneutic process involving a circling between parts and wholes (Klein and Myers, 1999). In specific terms, individual data elements (parts) are examined in relation to a theoretical account (whole). The objective is to ensure that the finished theoretical statement is grounded in the data elements, and this is achieved by refining the theory over multiple rounds of data analysis.

We began the analysis by looking at each of the relationships in the relational foundation model (Swanson and Beath, 1989) and coding changes due to upgrade and their impacts. We also examined contextual influences on the impacts of upgrade projects. Using a conceptual framework to establish a set of initial codes is a preferred method for approaching qualitative data analysis (Miles and Huberman, 1994). However, further rounds of coding are necessary to complete the hermeneutic circle and modify the initial conceptual framework (Klein and Myers, 1999).

## Results

## SAP case

The SAP 4. 6C upgrade was a mandatory upgrade undertaken to avoid the software’s sunset date. Because the SAP upgrade was conducted on an operational system, the biggest concern of IS staff was to minimize any potential adverse impact on users. Thus, IS staff did not implement any new functionality to reduce the risk of upgrade. The upgrade was likened to a ‘second implementation’ by the project manager. Even though the upgrade was a massive project, no external consultant or contractor was employed to assist the SAP Support group. It was strictly an internal project, and the only external party involved was the vendor.

## Impact of upgrade on system

Relationships between vendor and systems: new software interface and functionality

SAP usually introduces added functionality and changes the user interface in new versions of software. SAP 4.6C was a very different version from the version that CP was on. In particular, user interfaces were dramatically different in the new version.

Relationships between IS staff and vendors: coordination on software issues

During an upgrade, the relationship between IS staff and SAP centered on support. SAP often responded immediately to problems that SAPSD submitted, ‘sometimes it may take a day or two and that depends on if it has to go to their technical support over in Germany’ (Technical Team Lead One). However, the director recalled some nerve-racking experiences in which the vendor waited until the last minute to resolve their problems. ‘And that can be back and forth, back and forth trying to convince them that it’s them. y They will not believe a lot of time that their code y it can’t possibly be their code. And we are sure it’s their code.’

To address any potential issues that might arise during the upgrade, extra time was allocated in the upgrade schedule in anticipation of delayed response from the vendor. Overall, the SAP upgrade created a surge of support needs and a responsive vendor could reduce the extra time in a contingency plan.

## Relationships among systems: interconnectivity issues

The SAP upgrade introduced many changes that affected the relationship among systems. The biggest change was between the new and old version of the software. The upgrade was a 13-release leap and, according to the error report returned by SAP reporting tools, 1100 objects were changed. Most of the errors pertained to core modifications or user exits. The project manager recalled, ‘The technical group had a large effort to go through and correct programs and change programs based on database changes.

When the SAP application was upgraded on the server, the SAP client had to be upgraded too, generating a cascade of needed upgrades. Due to interconnectivity between systems to be upgraded and other interfaced applications and drivers, upgrade was not an easy plug and play. During 4.6C upgrade, the new version of SAP rendered some printers and faxes inoperable.

When we did the upgrade there were also technologies that we were using before that had to be upgraded as well along with SAP that we didn’t know about because SAP doesn’t necessarily know what other software we’re using. (Technical Team Lead Two)

## Impact of upgrade on IS staff

The greatest impact for SAPSD was their time and labor in implementing the new version of software. SAPSD did not track the actual financial cost of upgrade because it was not a capital project. However, the upgrade took 6 months from start to finish.

## Relationships between IS staff and systems: testing

Execution of the software upgrades was not confined to just one group of IS staff within a department but also involved affiliated IS departments. Thus, upgrades increased the demand on all IS resources. For example, due to the changes introduced by the new version, IS staff had to make sure that the new software would still allow users to perform their tasks. To ensure this, SAPSD adopted a labor intensive and time-consuming test case approach to make sure all functionalities worked like before. IS staff also carefully planned the migration to preempt any foreseeable issues. Several rounds of mock upgrades were conducted to make sure that system rollout went smoothly.

y the two biggest issues of upgrading, was, number one, the amount of testing effort to go through and make sure you weren’t going to have problem when you upgraded. y We have a very structured testing methodology. We have approximately 1100 to 1200 test plans. (Project Manager)

The second big issue facing IS staff was user training, which wasn’t limited to a small group of end users, according to the project manager.

You had to train customer service, you had to train customer account reps, you had to retrain the pricing rep, you had to retrain all the individuals at the mills, you had to train your finance people. (Project Manager)

The next section describes these support and training efforts.

## Relationships between IS staff and users: support and training

The relationship between IS staff and users was one that dealt with support. IS’s role was to support users with their IS needs. Besides ensuring that the system was implemented properly, IS staff also gave users the support that they needed after the system migration. Although SAPSD always had a help desk to answer users’ questions, when SAP was upgraded, there was a need for additional support from IS staff personnel. Besides staffing the regular help desk, SAPSD personnel also ‘walked the floor’ for a few days immediately after the upgrade. Business User One explained: ‘If we do it over the weekend, that Monday they come in and walk the floor to see if everything is okay. y and they usually do that like the first maybe two or three days.’

In the past, IS staff also trained the users. However, due to severe lack of resources, IS staff were unable to train business users as they usually did. In order to deliver training to all business users, a ‘train the trainer’ method was devised. IS staff trained business expert users, who in turn provided training to all business users. Team Lead One pointed out that it was taxing on them because they had to train users while attending to their daily tasks.

It’s not like, ‘well I’m not going to do that, I’m going to a meeting.’ No it doesn’t work like that. You’re expected to do both and you have to figure out how to get both accomplished. (Team Lead One)

Consequently, expert users took on more work during the upgrade.

## Relationships among IS staff: surging work demands Relationships among IS staff: surging work demands

Besides having to work with the vendor to resolve problems, IS staff also had to work with each other to ensure a successful execution of the project. In the SAP upgrade, two affiliated groups (database and UNIX) became involved. As the director explained, the groups would usually go along with her decision to upgrade.

We do need to get the other IT groups to agree because we interface to all [their] systems so we can’t upgrade without their help for testing, particularly for EDI and things like that. y Anyone of us wants to upgrade an application, we come in with a proposal, typically the same proposal we’re going to make to the business and get them to agree. Obviously, it’s an easier sell with them because they don’t want to hold back another IT group. (Director)

The manager of UNIX group who provided infrastructure support for multiple groups explained that once a group made its case, he would extend the resources.

Where there is an application group proposing an upgrade, and they have already justified in their project plan, you know, I am sure, the same as we do. y When it comes to the requirement from an application, we help them through that process. But that is more of a business driven proposed solution where they have made that a priority they will perform and we help them through. (UNIX Group Manager)

Besides getting support from other groups, different IS staff within SAPSD also had to work with each other to ensure a smooth execution. Two groups of SAPSD personnel, the technical team and the functional team, were involved in the upgrade. Technical team members would correct technical issues returned by SAP error reports, after which the system was passed on to functional team members for testing. ‘We reapply any core mod and user exits and at some point the functional team will start executing their test plans,’ said Technical Team Lead One.

## Impact of upgrade on users

## Relationships between systems and users

After SAP was upgraded, users found their interaction with the new version of software distinctly different due to major user interface changes. ‘That’s when all the screens completely changed and that was the first time that we did have to tab,’ said Team Lead Three. The business manager explained that the change was difficult initially due to uncertainty of whether they were processing the job correctly.

y at first there was a lot of complaints from all of us, you know thinking, oh, we have to go in and do all of these clickings y sometimes, it was overwhelming because we were not sure, we weren’t knowing where to go for all this information. (Business Manager)

Team Lead One recalled, it took the user approximately 3 months before they felt comfortable again.

You have your learning curve. With people trying to remember ‘did I learn that?’ Because you can have information overflow, of course. y They’re looking in the book trying to find out where it’s in the book and asking one another and then of course someone has it all wrong and gets everybody all confused. But normally people are back to operational without making mistakes in about 90 days. (Team Lead One)

Although the user interface changed dramatically leading to a long learning period, users did not experience any functionality improvement. This was due to SAPSD’s decision not to adopt any new functionality when implementing upgrade.

What we were doing, what we called our technical upgrade only, meaning we weren’t changing business processes or changing the way that they used the system, we wanted them to be able to do the same thing in a newer version. (Functional Team Lead One)

As a result, users did not experience any functionality or business process changes.

Again the process was the same. All the terminology was the same. It was just the screen looked totally different and the people were so used to dropping down from the tool bar and just clicking but it was always right there in front of you. But, you could still drop down from the tool bar and do it and people were still caught up doing that, and then the transaction codes were different like from screen to screen. (Team Lead Three)

## Relationships among users: user interactions

To transition to version 4.6C, users helped each other out. The person who was proficient in a certain area would spend time helping other members in need.

You become that floater on the floor. Helping people when they’re stuck. Helping people get through. (Team Lead One)

Team work became very, very important because each team had one person that was really good at one part of it and somebody else that was really good at another part and we had to rely on each other. Things that you didn’t do every day like creating returns or credits or any of that type of thing. We were really having to rely on each other say ‘ok help me, I don’t remember how to do this. I know I went through training but I don’t remember how to do it.’ (Team Lead Two)

Relationships between users and vendor: IS mediation At CP, the users’ interaction with the vendor was mediated by the IS staff. Business users did not interact with the vendor but relied on SAPSD for their SAP needs.

The reason being we don’t know SAP, what they migrate to. We don’t even know what version is out there because that’s their area of expertise. (Business Director)

## Windows case

Windows 2000 was upgraded as a result of the need to standardize applications after CP acquired a company twice their size, and also to take advantage of backend improvement enabled by a technology component added to Windows 2000. Windows operating system was managed by Windows Support Department (WSD), a department made up of three groups of IS staff. The first group is the desk side support personnel who oversaw day-to-day users’ support. The second group is called the server group, which were highly technical personnel who focused on server and network level tasks. The third group was the project group. This group was comprised of extremely technical IS staff who focused on one-time projects.

## Impact of upgrade on system

## Relationships between vendor and systems: new software features

Windows 2000 was a much improved product compared to its predecessor. It was a much more stable product with a more user-friendly interface. It also came standard with net-meeting, a feature that could allow IS staff to remotely access users’ desktops. But what truly set the Windows 2000 apart from the previous version was a technology component called Active Directory, which allowed system people to manage distributed computing environments centrally. Active Directory was used mainly to manage server and networking objects. Although Windows 2000 could be deployed without implementing Active Directory, an organization could not fully capitalize on the benefits of Windows 2000 technology without it.

## Relationships between vendor and IS staff: support

Active Directory was such a novel technology that IS staff at corporate who evaluated and approved new technology sought help from Microsoft in Redmond, Washington.

It was such a big technology shift we felt we needed assistance for that earlier than normal. So, actually before as we started doing that proof of concept we had brought [Microsoft resources] in to help us with that at that point. Prior to that, we were certainly doing our research in the technologies areas, we were talking to Redmond, getting a feel for that where it was going. (CSS Director)

The vendor brought its experiences in working with other beta users in designing Active Directory infrastructure. Besides that, IS staff at corporate also relied on Microsoft to help them tackle technical issues that they couldn’t solve.

We have access to the engineers in Redmond so for the real detailed ‘what if ’ questions that maybe the inside people don’t know, we’ll actually talk to Redmond about that. (CSS Director)

Their working relationship with Microsoft gave them the support they needed to deploy the new technology.

Relationships among systems: interconnectivity issues Due to the nature of operating system, the Windows upgrade resulted in more compatibility issues with application software and system drivers. In one instance, the shipping software was not compatible with Windows 2000, a problem that almost held up the upgrade.

So, I was instrumental in testing our applications on Windows 2000 and I had to raise the red flag and say we can’t do this. Either we don’t, as a division, y go to Windows 2000 or we got to find another solution, which is what caused us to come up with Citrix. Because the only reason Windows 2000 project got to go forward is because I cooked up the Citrix idea which allowed us to take the applications off user’s desktop. And so then they could be upgraded to Windows 2000. (System Administrator, Logistic Group)

In another instance, at a manufacturing site, one IS administrator recalled that he had to replace scanners because it didn’t have the necessary driver.

There were some scanners that connected through payroll port hardware devices that just, they just wouldn’t run on windows 2000. The vendor doesn’t provide a driver for them. You can’t do anything, your hands are tied, you can’t do anything. So we ended up having to throw away probably 10 scanners but they were cheap. They were like \$100 scanners. (IS staff, Field site B)

## Impact from upgrade on IS staff

Relationships between IS staff and systems: testing, ‘build and swap,’ and active directory

To cope with system compatibility issues, IS staff spent a tremendous amount of time testing. Corporate Shared Services (CSS) spent a full year evaluating and testing Windows 2000 against common software that all employees at SE Co. used. WSD then spent another 6 months testing the software on CP division software.

Anything that’s enterprise wide that everybody has on their desktop we will evaluate and test each of those corporate standard applications to make sure they work (CSS Personnel Two)

Besides making sure that the Windows upgrade would not cause adverse impact on peripheral applications, WSD also adopted a ‘build and swap’ strategy that would minimize downtime on users. The migration strategy required IS staff to pre-build computers and swap them for the users’ old computers.

After the upgrade, one of the most immediate impacts that IT observed from the new operating system was how stable it was compared to its predecessor. IT people found Windows 2000 ‘crashes less’ and ‘works better with our hardware.’ According to the IS planning manager, that ‘is one of the largest advantages because we were always replacing PCs.’ Prior to the Windows 2000 upgrade, one of the problems that IT people faced constantly was the instability of previous operating systems.

Something, either software wise or hardware the NT platform didn’t coexist with, and it would automatically shut down your PC y we would get what they call the blue screen of death. Well, the screen goes blue. And it’s like nothing works. (IS Planning Manager)

Furthermore, Active Directory brought tremendous backend operation improvement. WSD could now distribute software centrally and save tremendous amounts of time.

Over the last few months, there’s been a whole bunch of security patches, for OS, browsers, and Outlook. y Through Windows 2000 Active Directory, we have been able to apply these patches automatically, actually within half a day we have applied the patches to 9200 desktops vs weeks doing by hand. (Senior Manager)

Nevertheless, Windows 2000 was not an overnight success. In the middle of the rollout Active Directory went down, producing a disaster, according to the project leader.

The rollout completely stopped for about a two, three week period. We had to cancel trips, we had to come home from where we were at, we had to pack up and come home. It got very ugly. We had some ugly conference calls. People saying ‘somebody needs to get fired, we’re losing millions of dollars a day.’ (Project Leader)

When Active Directory crashed, both IS staff at WSD and CSS had to adjust the way they had been handling the system. CSS began to monitor the backend process, and some WSD personnel no longer were allowed access to Active Directory.

So we had to do a lot of disaster recovery, we had to make a lot of phone calls, a lot of apologies, we had to get Microsoft in here to help us get it back up and functioning. It was just a mess for about 3 weeks. (Project Leader)

Overall, the migration was a massive effort, with over 9200 PCs across 120 locations, it took over a year to implement. Although the financial costs were not tracked, the upgrade was highly labor intensive.

Relationships among IS staff: surging work demands The Windows 2000 upgrade involved employees from all three groups within WSD and also field IS staff. The initial evaluation of the technology was conducted at the corporate level by CSS. At the CP division level, desk side support personnel were responsible for the rollout of all PCs while attending to daily PC support. Progress was slow and a new employee was hired to focus on the upgrade. ‘We really needed somebody whose sole focus was on the rollout and allowed the normal support people to do their normal support duties.

Because this was the first time that the entire corporation had to upgrade to a standard OS, headquarters IS staff suspected that the field IS staff who normally ran their own shows might resent the decision to upgrade and the added responsibility. The project and server people flew to field sites to assist site-local IS staff, because upgrade was a project that they had to carry out on top of their daily responsibility. A project team staffer explained his perception of the field IS staff.

Well, you can almost say that we did impose that. We said ‘ok, this is the standard and you will be on this standard by this date.’ With that being said, we’ll help you with it. You do it any way you want to do it but you have to get it done. y Well, everybody these days they already have enough work to do so when you bring them something else to do there’s always that resistance. (Project Team member)

Most IT staff saw their workload increase depending on their role in the upgrade. For IS staff at headquarters, there was a clear division of labor. Although the upgrade brought increased workload, there was no visible discontent. Nevertheless, not all IS staff at manufacturing sites welcomed the upgrade.

You better understand, the folks in [divisional headquarters] what they do is projects. When they figure out the project, they do this project, they do that project, they do another project. They don’t have the users that they really have to support in addition to projects. Our team here, our IT team still has to do everyday mundane work of supporting the [manufacturing] environment, in addition to the initiatives [divisional headquarters] says that we have to do like everybody’s set up a newer release operating system or a newer computer. (Senior Technical Analyst, Field Site A)

## Relationships between IS staff and users: support

In the Windows 2000 upgrade, different IS staff had different approaches to swapping users’ computers. One desk side support staff advocated the least contact method, explaining it’s best to have ‘minimal contact’ with users. On the contrary, another desk side supports person strongly believed in easing users’ transitions by only swapping out the machine when users were at their desks.

I wouldn’t just kind of show up and say, ‘OK I’m switching out your machine now.’ Because I wanted to give them a chance, a time to get acquainted with it. Some people would just go and switch out the machine, and all of a sudden when the person comes back to their desk and all of a sudden and they’ve got this brand new machine and they don’t know where it came from. (Desk Side Support One)

To assist users with their transition, technical support was also available for user to call in.

Meanwhile, user reaction to the upgrade also affected the impact that IS staff experienced. During the upgrade, IS had to upgrade printer drivers, but some users took the matter into their own hands, helping other users to connect to the new printers. They ‘probably didn’t do it right,’ according to Desk Side Support Three. There was also confusion when a user sent out an e-mail message with misleading information that the print server for NT 4.0 was no longer available. Soon, help desk personnel were flooded with calls about printer problems. ‘At that point, the cat’s out of the bag, so you just go ahead and you wait for the problems to come in and you go fix them,’ said Desk Side Support Three.

## Impact of upgrade on users

Relationships between users and systems: minimal impact For most users, Windows 2000 upgrade didn’t bring much impact. According to IS Staff Three at field site A, there was no training because the change was minimal: ‘No we didn’t because it looks pretty much the same as what they were used to so there were no big special training before it happened.

Although the impact was minimal, a few users noticed some differences after the upgrade. User One at Field Site B explained that he could no longer set the clock after WSD tightened security with Windows 2000. Desk Side Support One reported that some users noticed a slower shutdown after the upgrade. The slower shutdown was a result of a new feature that synchronized users’ files from all locations. Even though it was a feature meant to help users, it came at the price of longer shutdown.

During the Windows 2000 upgrade, WSD also upgraded users to Office 2000. Like Windows 2000, many users didn’t notice much difference except for user interface change. User One at Field Site A recalled some minor modifications in the interface, ‘It’s a little bit different as far as the interface went but it was still easy enough to understand. It wasn’t a major issue at all.’

Among all applications, the most visible change came from Access. One user explained the user interface changed dramatically in Access. According to User Five, ‘The appearance changed. If I went into Access, whatever the old version was vs 2000, you had them up on two screens you could tell a big difference.’

Despite WSD’s best effort to deliver a smooth migration, a few users experienced lost files. Business Team Lead Two reported her files were missing, and had gone to ‘la la land. User One at Field Site A, explained that IS staff warned him that files may get lost and he experienced the problem.

I did have a problem finding some of my folders and my files. And they had explained to me before hand that this seemed to be a common problem as they did the upgrades and I might experience it and I would have to kind of look around in the system and try to find them ‘cause the upgrade placed my files in different folders and subfolders under different primary folders and if I needed help they would come over and help me do it. But, I did experience that and in fact there are a few files that I have yet to find. (User One, Field Site A)

Overall, it took users approximately 2 weeks to become accustomed to the upgraded software.

Relationships among users: sharing across versions and helping each other

Because the rollout was phased in, not all users were upgraded at once. For many users, this was not a problem, but for users who were using Access, sharing files became an issue. One user recalled the difficulty he encountered and the tactic he used to cope with the situation.

If you’re in 2000 and you try to work backwards it won’t work. So, you’ve got to upgrade that old file. You’ve got to modify it to the new version. That was a problem. A lot of the stuff we use is shared. We have a public server, public drive and a lot of the files were shared and for me to work on a shared file I would have to go get it, save it on my drive as a different name, modify it to the new version and do my work then, and then send it back down and save it back in the old version, old format. That was very inconvenient. (IS Manager)

As a temporary solution, IS staff installed both versions of Access for users so they could continue to work with files from Access 97 and Access 2000.

In addition, power users also helped out other users.

y they just call me up and say ‘hey I need help on this.’ I usually say ‘hey, I see you’re doing something there you could do it a lot quicker.’ (User, Field Site B)

Relationships between users and vendor: IS mediation Again, the users’ interaction with the vendor was mediated by IS staff. Users did not interact with vendor but relied on WSD for their operating systems’ needs.

## Discussion

We conducted this case study to answer two related research questions:

1. How does a packaged software upgrade affect stakeholders?

2. What influences the impact of a packaged software upgrade?

Our results show that upgrade impacts arise from the relationships mapped out in the relational foundation model in Figure 2. The four elements in the model (systems, IS staff, users, and vendors) are interdependent, and the impacts experienced by specific stakeholders are not determined by any one independent element. Importantly, we have included the technical features of systems as part of the interdependent set of elements.

However, the model suggests that IT does not directly determine upgrade impacts, and no other element in the model is capable of direct determination of impacts. This key insight from the relational model is explained below, first for the two software packages studied. Following, we address the research questions directly by explaining relational impacts on each of the two main stakeholders studied: IS staff and users.

## How software upgrades generate impacts

Our findings link changes in software features directly to vendor decisions to release a new version of an existing package. According to Basili and Boehm (2001), ‘adding features is an economic necessity for vendors,’ so the vendor-system relationship generates changes such as user-interface improvements, the addition or deletion of functionality (Boehm and Abts, 1999), internal code changes, and revised data structures. From the vendor perspective, Windows 2000 was a much improved operating system than its predecessors, bringing more user friendliness and stability. It also had many added features like net meeting, which allowed IS staff to remotely control users’ desktops. Most importantly, Active Directory, a new technology that greatly improved back end administration for IS staff. SAP 4.6C, by comparison, brought a completely different user interface and the potential for new functionality. However, the IS staff decided to defer exploration of new functionality so that the SAP upgrade would be less disruptive to users. Thus, the relationship between vendors and IS staff, in part, produced the specific features of the both upgrades.

Because software systems are interconnected, any change to one system can potentially trigger a chain reaction in others and bring compatibility issues to the surface. Due to the lack of vendor-to-vendor coordination, upgrades almost always raise compatibility issues with interconnected software. Two types of compatibility were observed: compatibility with different systems, and compatibility between two versions of the same system.

Certain software is more prone to create compatibility issues than others. As an operating system, Windows 2000 was host to other software applications; hence, the probability of incompatibility with other software was magnified. By contrast, ERP systems are integrated sets of software modules connected to a common database to perform basic business functions like materials management, sales, distribution, and finance (Robey et al., 2002). As a distributed application system, most of its interactions with interconnected software are limited to its client and peripherals. As a result, the SAP upgrade had fewer compatibility issues with interconnected software.

However, due to vendors’ independent development practices, compatibility issues are inevitable. The new version of any software package may render peripheral devices like fax or printer drivers inoperable. Thus, a software upgrade can easily affect so-called ‘familial’ relationship among system elements (Swanson and Dans, 2000).

An upgrade can also create internal problems due to the changes that the vendor introduced. Because vendors always introduce changes in the new version, the change can affect customized code within the software as in the SAP case. Furthermore, in the SAP case, an upgrade of server software can also lead to the need to upgrade client software. In addition, as an application system that runs business critical functions, compatibility between old and new versions is extremely important to ensure that existing functions are intact.

As stated earlier, relationships within technology components are not the sole driver of system upgrade impacts.

Understanding how technology relationships affect stakeholders must be extended to include primary stakeholders, and their relationships with systems and with each other. We consider next the impacts of upgrades on IS staff.

## Impact of upgrades on IS staff

In both cases, IS staff saw upgrades as huge projects that consumed a tremendous amount of their resources. Windows 2000 upgrade took more than a year and SAP took over 6 months. IS staff conducted a tremendous amount of testing to ensure no incompatibility between Windows 2000 and application software, and they tested extensively to ensure that business functionality remained intact in the SAP case. SAPSD’s experience was consistent with the experience of other SAP customers in their industry, who also see upgrades as difficult, costly, and time consuming (Kremers and van Dissel, 2000). Although upgrades are sometimes seen as smaller projects compared to initial implementations, they may still consume large amounts of resources because new versions involve technology and functionality that are profoundly different from existing versions (Weston, 1997; Kremers and van Dissel, 2000; Kumar and Hillegersbery, 2000).

Although the scope of implementation was influenced by the amount of changes in the new versions, the impacts of implementation were greatly influenced by a strategy that called for a careful implementation. Exercising greater care produced better outcomes and reduced adverse impacts, but it took longer and consumed more resources.

Some of the impacts of software upgrades on IS staff arise from the relationship between vendors and IS staff, who are the intermediaries between the vendor and the organization. The impact of the vendor on the IS staff can be high because IS staff span the boundaries between the vendor and the client organization, and bear the brunt of vendor decisions. In an upgrade, this relationship mainly revolves around the need for the vendor to support the implementation of the upgrade. Owing to the novel technology, when features are introduced in a new version more vendor support is needed. Although our results show that the degree of needed support differs between the two cases, vendor support was essential to the success of upgrade projects.

The SAP case illustrates an important point about impacts arising from relationships. During the SAP testing phase, IS staff identified many problems that they attributed to SAP software, but the vendor countered that the problems were related to client configuration decisions. Since SE Co. worked on the configuration task without the aid of external consultants, IS staff assumed the burden of demonstrating that the vendor was responsible before they could get the vendor to correct the problem. In addition to extensive testing and user support, IS staff generated additional time-consuming work in its relationship to SAP. In part, this additional impact can be attributed to relationships with both the software (system) and the vendor.

In the case of Windows 2000, dependence of the IS staff on the vendor varied. The CP IS staff conducting the rollout experienced minimal dependence. Windows was not being configured; the upgrades affected the functionalities of the IS staff, but imposed no special demands; and users did not face new functionality changes, only relatively minor interface changes. However, the corporate CSS Staff relied on vendor resources to help design Active Directory, and vendor resources were brought in to fix the Active Directory when it crashed. Thus, the relationships affecting IS staff impacts differed because of technology and vendor relationships. The relational foundation model thus helps to explain the source of stakeholder impact.

Relationships within IS staff also account for the impacts of the upgrades on IS staff. Both upgrades involved multiple groups of IS staff within the department responsible for the upgrade and also between the department and IS groups outside of the department. These working relationships had two effects: (1) they required coordination among IS staff; and (2) they produced ripple effects impacting IS staff who may otherwise not be involved in the project. For example, in the SAP case, error reports were generated by the database group; the technical group made fixes based on these error reports; and the functional group tested the repaired software. In the Windows upgrade, the CSS group did the initial testing and the IS staff on site did the roll out with corporate support. Thus, both upgrade projects depended on coordination between different IS groups, thus spreading the impact.

The ripple effect can be observed in the effect the SAP upgrade project had on the UNIX and data base groups. Neither the UNIX nor the database group was directly involved in the SAP upgrade, but the upgrade caused perturbations, requiring these other groups to be alert to possible problems. The ripple effect is also seen in the IS group’s inability to keep up with the roll out of the Windows upgrade. The initial plan was to have the IS group undertake the project without any additional help, i.e., discharge their regular responsibilities while implementing the upgrade. This proved to be difficult, resulting in the hire of an additional person to help with the rollout.

## Impact of upgrades on users

Two types of impact can be observed from the use of new version of software. First, there is the learning curve; then the elective benefits. Learning curve is seldom mentioned in the IS literature. However, it is always present when users experience software upgrade. In the SAP case, users experienced a 2–3 months learning period before they felt comfortable. In the Windows case, the average learning curve was approximately 2 weeks. Overall, the degree of the learning curve that users experienced was affected by the degree of change in user interface and user adoption of new features.

In the SAP 4.6C upgrade, the user interface changed from menu-based to icon-based, and the front end looked dramatically different after the upgrade. Although the changes were seen as improvements by many business managers interviewed, most users merely saw them as ‘changes’ and not as improvements. User interface changes can create very significant impacts on users because the software interface is where users interact with the system. In the world of the electronic workspace, a new interface changes the work environment, and the impact can be substantial. For many, keystrokes are ingrained in their daily routines. When a button is moved from its usual place, users have to think where to click instead of just clicking in a familiar place. On both technologies, user interfaces changed. A few users noticed the login screen changed for Windows 2000, and the user interface was slightly different in Office applications like Word and Excel.

Besides user interface changes, software upgrade can introduce new functionality intended to improve user’s work. In both cases, the improvements that stakeholders perceive vary depending on their roles and the type of software that they use. In SAP, IS staff made a decision not to adopt any new functionality. As a result, no new functionality was introduced, and users did not experience any new features through the upgrade.

For the Windows upgrade, IS staff and users experienced very different outcomes. This comes as little surprise because different aspects of software typically affect various stakeholders differently (Brinton et al., 2002). In general, the impacts that stakeholders perceived were closely linked to the roles that they played in the upgrade, either as an implementer, user, or both. As an implementer, IT staff’s experience was often linked to changes in the back end, while users experienced changes at the front end.

Windows 2000 was a huge technology improvement from the IS stakeholders’ perspective. The operating systems were much more stable, and Active Directory completely overhauled back end software management, eliminating the need to install service packs manually. By contrast, most users did not notice much improvement from the upgrade. The discrepancy of perceptions may seem strange at first, but in order for functionality improvements to make an impact, new features and affected functionalities need to be adopted and used. Thus, an upgrade can only impact stakeholders if the change is relevant to their software use, and the impact is visible. In the Windows 2000 upgrade, most of the features that brought improvement to IS staff were not relevant to users and were therefore not perceived.

Users’ key focus in an upgrade is their ability to perform previous tasks. Whether new features add value or not was a secondary concern to the users compared to regaining their ability to perform routine tasks. From both cases, the biggest impact that business users perceived from the upgrade was not the benefits received from improved features, but the time and effort invested to return to their normal work processes. This is similar to Lassila and Brancheau’s (1999) finding that there is a ‘transitional period between equilibrium states before and after IT implementation in which users strive to get back to normal.

Relationships among different users also account for the impacts of upgrades on users. Two types of impacts can be seen. One impact arose from files or documents that different users shared. In the Windows case, the phased rollout introduced file incompatibility among users with different versions of the Access application. File sharing impacts were not an issue in the SAP case because SAP was a centralized system and every user was upgraded at the same time.

The second impact is related to the support that users provided to each other during the upgrades. During SAP upgrade, a group of expert users who trained standard users were also tasked with support for standard users after the upgrade. Although the arrangement was meant to ease the transition of standard users, it added work to the expert users. Although there was no formal arrangement for users to assist users in Windows 2000 case, standard users also approached power users for questions. On the positive side, there is group affinity among users, and they are likely to help each other learn the intricacies of the software. Their physical proximity to each other furthers the opportunities for mutual help. On the negative side, the transitional period usually places more demand on expert users due to their knowledge and ability to navigate the new software.

Impacts arising from relationships between IS staff and users We conceptualize the relationship between IS staff and users as support. During the transitional period of upgrade, users’ demand for support surges, thereby increasing IS staff’s workload. For example, SAP personnel ‘walked the floor’ to help users cope with change. Some Windows users also needed more support due to issues arising from version incompatibilities. The support that IS staff provided to users is meant to solve issues and lessen the negative impact that users experienced. Nonetheless, they usually increase the workload for IS staff.

WSD staff spent extra time during rollout to make sure users knew how to login to the new system. The support that IS provided was meant to help users’ transition to the new system. Nonetheless, a different approach was used due to different types of system. SAP was rolled out as a centralized system; hence, the support was only needed when it was up and running. Windows staff decided to give support to individual users as they brought new computer to them.

Overall, our findings demonstrate the value of focusing on relationships as the source of the impacts of packaged software upgrades on two primary stakeholders: IS staff and users. These relationships include software vendors, IS staff, users, and the technology itself. We, thus, provide answers to our research questions, explaining how a packaged software upgrade impacts stakeholders, and identifying the various influences on those impacts. Befitting the complexity of the process of upgrading packaged software, these answers leave much room for local interpretation rather than general application. Nevertheless, we believe that similar relationships exist in many packaged software upgrade settings. Our contribution is not to oversimplify the understanding of upgrade impacts but rather to offer an empirically supported framework to cope with complexity.

## Conclusion

The main contribution of this interpretive case study of packaged software upgrades is the observation that impacts arise from interdependent relationships among software and different stakeholders, particularly IS staff, users, and vendors. As such, our research is one of the first studies of packaged software impacts to trace the source of impacts through the subjective experiences and accounts of relevant stakeholders. The nuances, contextual details, and complexity of software upgrades are unveiled in our study, demonstrating the strengths of qualitative research that relies upon subjective data drawn from participants in actual upgrades. Our contributions are presented as an extension of Beath and Swanson’s (1989) relational foundation model of software maintenance.

While contributing in these ways, our study also is limited by aspects of our research design. By choosing to study two upgrades in a single company, we forego opportunities to generalize to a larger sample of software upgrade situations. However, our findings and conclusions are not necessarily localized to the SE Co. case. Lee and Baskerville (2003) and Walsham (1993) argue that interpretive studies afford types of generalization that differ from the more familiar generalizations, from sample statistics to population parameters. Specifically, our research method allows generalization from raw data to abstract descriptions by virtue of the hermeneutic process, wherein data elements are interpreted as parts of an emerging whole. Our analysis also affords generalization from description to theory. As Walsham (1993) argues, the progression from descriptions to concepts to theory results in specific implications and rich insights that can apply beyond the local research setting. In this way, our insights into the impacts of packaged software upgrades would have application to similar cases.

The adoption of an interpretive perspective, in which data occur in the form of subjective accounts of individual experiences with software upgrades, prevents a more ‘realist’ analysis. For example, we did not attempt to evaluate the actual costs of implementation. When asked about costs, respondents typically replied that such costs were either unavailable or irrelevant to the decision to upgrade, leaving us with the impression that formal costbenefit analyses were not undertaken. In the absence of such objective data, a realistic analysis of costs, staff qualifications, and user proficiency would be difficult to complete. Moreover, our acceptance of subjective accounts of technical issues, such as software bugs or testing procedures, limited a more realistic appraisal of these actions. These limitations must be traded off against the benefits of our interpretive approach.

Although limited in these respects, our study does bear practical implications for organizations undergoing packaged software upgrades. Returning to our main contribution, practitioners are advised not to seek the ‘magic bullet’ for controlling upgrade impacts. Due to the relationships between and among software systems and various stakeholders, no single cause is responsible for positive or negative impacts. Thus, managers seeking to minimize upgrade risks must examine stakeholder interactions with software characteristics. In addition, managers should acknowledge that IS staff absorbs many negative impacts (e.g., surges in support needs, testing, training) in order to reduce the negative impacts on users. IS staff also span the boundary between the organization and vendors, negotiating issues pertaining to software errors and vendor support contracts. Although IS staff may take on extra burden during a software upgrade, they are also instrumental in easing the burden on users.

In conclusion, our study unveils many important facets of a relatively neglected phenomenon: the periodic upgrade of a vendor’s packaged software application. Given the steady increase in packaged software solutions, organizations need to be prepared for managing the impacts of upgrades on IS staff and users. By focusing on the relationships between these stakeholders and characteristics of the technology itself, organizations can minimize the risks of major upgrades.
