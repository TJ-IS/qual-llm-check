ABSTRACT: Advances in online technologies and bandwidth availability have opened new vistas for online distribution of digital goods, but potential benefits for consumers are juxtaposed against challenges for retailers. Here, we investigate one type of digital experience good—music—whose market environment includes the very real presence of online piracy. Although arguments abound for and against online distribution of such digital goods, little research exists in this area. We develop a model of consumer search for such an experience good, and study different emerging market environments for retailers, where consumers can pirate music online. Retailer cost to publishers is modeled using a variety of licensing schemas. Survey results, together with data from online sharing networks, are utilized to validate a key assumption. Finally, computational analysis is used to develop insights that cannot be obtained analytically. Our results indicate that decreasing piracy is not necessarily equivalent to increasing profit, and online selling strategies can provide additional profits for a traditional retailer even in the presence of piracy. We show that leading strategies for business in such goods should include pricing options, provision of efficient search tools, and new licensing structures.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: digital experience goods, license, music, online channels, piracy, sampling, search.

EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES OFTEN RESULT in new opportunities, choices, and possibilities. Advances in Internet technologies have opened new channels for online retailing of digital goods. But the same technology can be utilized for unauthorized copying and dissemination. The online retailer of a digital good thus faces two challenges: (1) determining a business model in a new environment and (2) analyzing the impact of piracy and different license structures on profit.

Here, we present a formal analysis for retailers of one digital good—music. The digital nature of today’s music offers convenience and portability, as predicted by Alexander [2], but also raises the specter of piracy and lost sales. In fact, the economics of music, the market structure, and the impact of piracy has been the subject of current studies [1, 3, 4, 11, 16, 17, 27, 28, 45, 47]. Music is an experience good, a type of hedonic product whose valuation is based on the experience it provides to a consumer [18]. A music item must be experienced (heard) to be accurately valued by a consumer. Moreover, each music item, even from the same artist, is potentially unique. While physical piracy of digital music (through CDs) may be common in certain markets, our focus is on those markets where increasing broadband connections provide the means for legal online markets while at the same time enabling online sharing and piracy (mainly through peer-to-peer networks, or P2P).

Retailer business models and pricing options in such online digital experience good markets have not been widely studied. The work reported here is not intended to address legal issues or viewpoints regarding the efficacy of online piracy—our goal is to contribute to the debate on electronic markets. We examine the conditions under which online sharing networks might benefit retailers. Could it be the case that electronic music sharing/piracy options actually foster increased searching by consumers seeking to experience the product prior to making the purchase decision? Is it possible that increased searching is linked to increased sales and thus increased benefits for retailers? In fact, there is anecdotal evidence pointing toward online sampling as increasing sales [13, 33, 38]. In addition, there is some indication that the music industry is realizing that these networks cannot be closed down by legal means alone. In short, the industry needs to develop new business models, pricing strategies, and licensing schema [7, 30, 53].

We develop a model of consumer search for such a hedonic product where each item might produce a unique experience. We validate a key assumption regarding the overall lower quality of experience from an illegally obtained shared music item versus a legally obtained original using (1) data we gathered on sharing activity on a major P2P network and (2) results of a survey we conducted. Utilizing this consumer search process, we analyze a series of market models for a monopolistic retailer of such digital goods, which represent differing environments, pricing options, and licensing arrangements. Our models include the traditional brick-and-mortar retailer, an online retailer offering per unit pricing, an online retailer offering subscription pricing, and an online retailer offering both types of pricing options. Our theoretical results suggest that, under certain conditions, a retailer may actually do better in an environment with piracy, largely due to consumers’ use of downloads as a means of prepurchase sampling. We find that revenues from an online model offering both subscription and per unit pricing dominate other online models studied. However, pricing and retailer profits do not have closed-form solutions under various licensing structures and piracy environments. We conduct computational analyses to study the interactions of these retailer controllable factors and environment variables—in the absence of publicly available empirical data—to gain insights into these and other emerging online market scenarios, some of which are not yet available to consumers. Key results from these experiments are: (1) online selling strategies for an existing traditional retailer can provide additional profits even under piracy environments, (2) efficient search techniques are essential to attract consumers to legal online markets, (3) maximum profit solutions do not occur in the absence of piracy, and (4) licensing cost structures of online retailers with music publishers have a significant impact on pricing and profits.

## Literature Review

PREVIOUS RESEARCH ON EXPERIENCE GOODS, shared goods, software piracy, and consumer search processes all helped to shape our modeling and analysis, but there are critical differences that set our work apart. In the following, we note limited similarities with these bodies of work while emphasizing critical differences that led us to develop new models for analyzing digital music markets.

## Experience Goods and Assumption of Restrictions on Sharing

Existing research on experience goods primarily focuses on models related to physical goods at a given location—that is, conditions where sharing or redistribution is not easy. Focusing on a monopolistic market of entertainment experience goods, Oi [43] implicitly assumed that sharing or redistribution of the good across different locations was difficult. Shapiro [50] focused on a personal experience good, but dealt with a scenario with impediments to the resale and redistribution of the product. Additional related studies include Gale and Rosenthal [19], Holbrook and Schindler [31], Jedidi et al. [32], Liebeskind and Rumelt [36], and Riordan [48]. While the digital music markets we study involve experience goods, the possibility of broad sharing and redistribution makes these market dynamics quite different from those in previous studies.

## Economic Models with Subscription Pricing

Models on economics of subscription pricing and shared goods have usually focused on products where private sharing occurs among only a small group of consumers. In analyzing the journal subscription market, Glazer and Hassin [22] and Ordover and Willig [44] assumed that individual customers or subscribers do not share journals. Coyte and Ryan [15] extended the subscription model and examined a consumer’s decision to purchase or renew a subscription of an information good—a book. Borrowing a library book has low cost, but such borrowing has a time limitation, and sharing can only occur in a limited way. On the other hand, a downloaded digital music file from a subscription service potentially can be owned permanently and shared widely.

## Software Piracy—Issues of Quality and

## Ease of Dissemination

While several studies on software piracy have examined consumer decisions to purchase or pirate the product [14, 21, 24, 25, 26], several key factors distinguish music from software. Music files are typically much smaller (and thus easier to copy) than computer software. Another difference important in our study is the quality of the shared good. The acoustic quality of original music (high-quality recording media) is better than that of a compressed digital music file, the format typical for music items shared or pirated online. Shared computer software, on the other hand, requires lossless compression for proper functioning [8]. Finally, given the extremely large volume of music files available online, a search process should be an integral part of market analysis and research in this arena. As the literature indicates (see, e.g., [8]), this does not appear to be the case for online sharing or piracy.

## Search Process and Costs—Why Is Digital Music Different?

Search costs have been widely studied in the literature (see, e.g., [12, 20, 51, 52]). Nelson [42] modeled a consumer who searches through different brands of canned tuna fish (a prototypic experience good) to find her favorite brand. Once the consumer finds the brand she likes, she no longer needs to search further for her next purchase since the same brand would be expected to have the same taste over time. While there has been increasing interest in the marketing of hedonic products [34, 46, 49], we suggest that the search process for digital music is significantly different from a prototypical search process. Each piece of musical performance contains its own unique characteristics. For a consumer, searching and finding a music item by a particular artist that the consumer likes is no guarantee that the next music item by the same artist (read brand) would be similarly liked by the consumer (have the same “value”). This variability, together with a large volume of music items available, suggests that consumers are likely to perform additional searches for additional products, a process quite different from that modeled in earlier studies.

In sum, differences relating to ease of sharing, potential permanence of the product, characteristics that set music files apart from computer software, and a rather different search process lead us to the development of new models specifically for the evolving digital music market.

## Modeling Music Market Environments

A MUSIC RETAILER FACES CONSUMERS who search to evaluate and obtain music. The search process involves consumers who may decide to purchase or pirate a number of music items, which can directly affect the profit-maximizing choices of the retailer.

## Models of Consumer Search Process

Consumers search to evaluate and obtain music. Each search involves a consumer searching for, identifying, and experiencing a new music item. Our consumer is not pursuing a directed search<sup>1</sup> but may start the search process with recommendations by friends or music reviewers about music items that he or she should consider. A consumer starts the search process in order to identify and experience a new music item. In our representation, if a consumer completes n searches, he or she would have experienced n different music items. This differs from the typical best option, singleitem purchase represented in the literature using concepts of a consideration set, reservation utilities, and an ordered search [41]. Following basic economic theory (e.g., [37]), we assume that the consumer is seeking to maximize utility by choosing a set of music items while remaining within a budget constraint specific to that type of good.

In each model we study, there are (1) nontrivial costs to search, evaluate, and obtain music and (2) opportunities for consumers to pirate music (except in the base case model). We assume that each consumer has a range of values they would place on the music items under consideration. Notationally, $\nu _ { i j } = \mathrm { v a l u e }$ that consumer i places on music item j after experiencing, where $\nu _ { i j } \in [ 0 , V _ { i } ^ { \operatorname* { m a x } } ]$ and takes on a discrete set of values in the set [0, V <sup>max</sup>]. $V _ { i } ^ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ is individual i’s maximum value for any music item offered by the seller.

We assume that the music retailer offers CD-quality music, either electronically or on a physical CD. We also allow differential utility of a music item if it is obtained legally versus illegally based on a “quality of experience” factor. Notationally, we use δ as a multiplier if the music item is procured illegally, and assume it to be homogeneous across consumers (see the Validation of Modeling Assumption section, where we offer initial validation of these assumptions). For example, if consumer i downloaded song j from an online illegal channel, he or she would receive a utility value of $\delta \nu _ { _ { i j } } .$ The factor δ can be viewed as capturing (1) lower acoustic quality of music at illegal sites due to compression technologies, (2) value-added services that a retailer might offer that are not offered at illegal sites, (3) the fact that a user is undertaking an illegal activity, or (4) possible disutility from downloading polluted files (see, e.g., the discussion in [35] about possible producer-created polluted music files). We consider values for δ over the range $0 < \delta < 1$

## Search Cost

Searches are, by their nature, uncertain activities. Because the goods on which we focus are experience goods, a consumer can attach individual value only after experiencing (listening to) an item. In addition, the volume of commercially available music is vast. The major labels by themselves release over 27,000 new albums every year [23]. Hence, we assume that no consumer is aware of all music items available. The limiting case is where an individual somehow knows (has identified) a set of music items and has sufficient knowledge (i.e., consumer already knows the song and just wants to obtain it) so that uncertainty is zero.

Each search involves processing a consumer query with a search tool. Potentially, a consumer may use three different channels to conduct a search, each channel having a different search cost. We consider each in turn:

• Traditional: The consumer performs the search in a typical brick-and-mortar setting (hereafter referred to as BM). Consumers search and sample in a traditional channel and either purchase or do nothing. Let the search cost for this traditional channel be $\psi ^ { B M }$

• Illegal: The consumer performs the search online at an illegal source, where consumers can download and sample a copy of the music item for free, without authorization from the seller. A consumer can search in an illegal channel and download and keep the illegal version or do nothing. We hereafter refer to this online illegal channel as ONI and the search cost as $\psi ^ { O N I }$

• Legal: This online channel is set up by a legitimate seller (hereafter referred to as ONLG). The seller allows consumers to sample, evaluate, and purchase music legally. Of course, a consumer cannot download and obtain a music item illegally from this channel. A consumer can search in a legal channel and either purchase or do nothing. Let the search cost be $\psi ^ { O N L G }$

Notice that a consumer can pirate music only from ONI, but can purchase music legitimately from either BM or ONLG channels. Given the limited time available at a brick-and-mortar store and the effort required to access it (visit and search at the store), compared to illegal online channels, we assume $\psi ^ { B M } > \psi ^ { O N I }$ . Also, given a greater incentive from legal online sellers to provide better search tools that return more relevant results, we assume $\psi ^ { O N L G } < \psi ^ { O N I }$ . Hence $\psi ^ { B M } > \psi ^ { O N I } > \psi ^ { O N L G }$ . Violations of this assumption are investigated later in our computational analyses (see the Computational Results section).

Of course, a consumer can perform a search on one channel and then switch to another channel to procure the music item. We summarize our representation of such two-stage searches in Figure 1. For instance, starting at the BM channel, a consumer performs the nth search and evaluates the music, incurring a cost of $\psi ^ { B M }$ . He or she then goes to ONI and performs a directed search and downloads an illegal version of the same song, incurring a cost of $\psi ^ { B M  O N I }$ . The cost for this two-stage search would be $\psi ^ { B M } + \psi ^ { B M \to O N I }$ . Similarly, starting at $O N I ,$ the consumer searches and incurs a cost of $\psi ^ { O N I }$ . Given his or her consumer surplus, the consumer might choose to terminate the search here, or switch to BM and buy the item, incurring an additional cost of $\psi ^ { O N I  B M }$ . The other options and related costs are depicted in Figure $1 . ^ { 2 }$ Note that a consumer who switches channels is assumed to do so after gathering the information necessary to make a specific nth item procurement (legal or illegal). When moving to the new channel, the consumer has the information necessary to move as directly as possible to finalize a transaction. More formally, we make the following assumptions about search costs:

1. $\psi ^ { O N I  B M } = 0$ and $\psi ^ { O N I  O N L G } = 0 ;$ that is, once a consumer has identified a piece of music using the illegal channel, a directed search is performed when switching to and using BM and ONLG. We assume better cataloging features of legal channels drastically reduce directed search costs.

2. $0 < \psi ^ { B M \vartriangle { } } \overline { { { \varphi } } } ^ { O N I } \leq \psi ^ { O N I } ;$ ; that is, a customer having the information gathered in the nth search at BM is more efficient when switching to ONI than when making the nth search initially at ONI.

3. $0 < \psi ^ { O N L G  O N I } \leq \psi ^ { O N I } ;$ that ${ \mathrm { i s } } ,$ a customer having the information gathered in the nth search at ONLG is more efficient when switching to ONI than when making the nth search initially at ONI.

## Search Efficiency

A search can be viewed as a draw over a set of finite music items. The quality or efficiency of a search tool (e.g., a music recommender system) will affect the probability of identifying a high- or low-value music item for a given consumer. If there are N music items available to search, then a “random search tool” is equivalent to a draw with each outcome having 1/N probability of being selected. A “better” search tool will make it more likely that a high-value music item is identified by the search.<sup>3</sup> We argue that, for legal online channels, a retailer has a monetary incentive to provide a more “helpful” search tool, whereas the illegal channel has no comparable incentives. Even with a rudimentary search tool, searches in each of the channels are more efficient than a random search. From previous discussion, we know that consumer i is able to place a value on a music item only after experiencing it. For the nth search, there is a true probability distribution across consumer $i \mathrm { \ ' } _ { \mathrm { s } }$ value assignments to each of the finite set of songs to be searched. This probability distribution of values, not known a priori (before experiencing items) by the consumer, is modeled as

\sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} \phi_ {i j} ^ {\Lambda , n} (v _ {i j}) = 1,

\sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {\Lambda , n} \left(v _ {i j}\right) > \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {\Lambda , n + 1} \left(v _ {i j}\right).
$$

In music item search, new searches occur without replacement of the previous music item. Thus, the nth search in a channel may identify a music item whose value to the consumer would be above the mean value for all music items in the channel’s search space. Subsequent evaluation and removal of that item will result in a lower expected value of all remaining items for the n + 1th search. We argue that electronic searches tend to be more efficient than manual/physical searches. Previous research has shown online search and activity to be less costly and more efficient [5, 6, 10, 17, 28, 29]. In addition, legal online sellers have incentives to develop and enhance customer service, whereas illegal online sites have no such incentives. Hence, we posit the final assumption (on the expected value of search from each channel):

$$
\begin{array}{c} \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {O N L G, n} \left(v _ {i j}\right) > \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {O N I, n} \left(v _ {i j}\right) \\ > \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} \left(v _ {i j}\right). \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Assumption (1) indicates (1) the online legal search is more efficient than search on an illegal network, and (2) search on an illegal site is more efficient than search in a brick-and-mortar store, which requires both a physical visit and a physical search. Specifically, a search on an online legal site is more likely to result in a music item with a higher value to the individual consumer. Note that a search in different channels does not necessarily result in obtaining the same music item. After some number of searches, the efficiency of the legal online search channel can be expected to lead to the identification of mostly high-value music items. The same number of searches in the other channels would be expected to lead to identification and removal of relatively lower-valued music items. At some point, the set of music items remaining to be searched in the legal online channel will consist of mostly low-valued items. Our assumption is that the consumer, with a budget constraint and certainly some time limitations, will stop searching before violations of assumption (1) occur.

Our analysis focuses on a retailer facing a set of consumers who differ in their V <sup>max</sup> values. Each of the steps that follow can be generalized where a retailer faces a distribution of consumers with varying $\phi _ { i j } ^ { \ : \Lambda , n } ( \nu _ { i j } )$ forms. Notationally, we center on a representative consumer $^ { \dag } i ,$ utilizing $\nu _ { i j }$ to indicate the value consumer i assigns to music item j resulting from a given search in channel BM, ONLG, or ONI. We maintain the subscript i to emphasize that individual consumers will differ—that is, we do not make the common simplifying independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption.

The last three involve differing pricing options for emerging online market settings.

With this information, the consumer makes rational choices on the number of searches to initiate (a consumer would continue to search until the expected cost exceeds the expected benefit of the next search), channels to search, and whether to obtain a song legally or illegally. ## Case 2: BM Model with Piracy Option

Our second case still involves a BM but introduces an online illegal channel. The consumer who wishes to search music items can start the search process from either BM or ONI. Again, if the search costs exceed the expected value of searching, no search would occur (Figure 2, case 2).

Let a consumer, whose $V _ { i } ^ { \operatorname* { m a x } }$ is sufficient to justify at least one search, be at the starting point of the nth search, and start this search process at the illegal channel, incurring a search cost of $\psi ^ { O N I }$ . The consumer’s subsequent behavior depends on the value, $\nu _ { i j } ,$ of the music item experienced at the end of the nth search. The three consumer choices and the net benefits are: (1) switch to BM and buy: $\nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } - \psi ^ { O N I  B M } =$ $\nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } ; ( 2 )$ pirate: $\delta \nu _ { i j } ^ { \phantom { } } ,$ or (3) do nothing: 0.

If $\nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } > \delta \nu _ { i j } ,$ the consumer would switch to BM. If not, the consumer would pirate the music item. Thus, the consumer’s net a priori expected benefit from an nth search that starts with the illegal channel is

$$
\sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, \frac {p _ {u}}{(1 - \delta)}\right)} \delta v _ {i j} \left\{\phi_ {i j} ^ {O N I, n} (v _ {i j}) \right\} + \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ \frac {p _ {u}}{(1 - \delta)}, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} \left\{\phi_ {i j} ^ {O N I, n} (v _ {i j}) \right\} (v _ {i j} - p _ {u}) - \psi^ {O N I}.\tag{2}
$$

Now, consider another consumer who starts the nth search at BM and incurs a search cost of $\psi ^ { B M }$ . The consumer choices and the net benefits are: (1) buy from BM: $\nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } ;$ (2) pirate: $\delta \nu _ { i j } - \psi ^ { B M \to O N I } ;$ or (3) do nothing: 0.

The consumer will buy from BM if $\nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } > \delta \nu _ { i j } - \psi ^ { B M \to O N I }$ <sup>I</sup>. Thus, the consumer’s a priori expected benefit from an nth search that starts with BM is

$$

$$

where $a = \psi ^ { B M \to O N I } / \delta$ and $b = ( p _ { u } - \psi ^ { B M  O N I } ) / ( 1 - \delta )$ . The first term is the expected value over the range where pirating the music item is optimal. The second term is the expected value over the range where buying the music item from BM is optimal. The third term is the search cost in BM. Finally, the fourth term is the switching cost (BM to ONI) times the probability of the consumer switching from BM to ONI to obtain the music item identified in the nth search at BM. ## Unit, Subs, and Mixed Models with Piracy Option

In the next three cases, we consider an online music retailer who offers one of three types of pricing strategies: per unit pricing with price $p _ { u }$ (unit model) (Figure 2, case 3), subscription pricing with an up-front price p (subs model) (Figure 2, case 4), or a mix with both per unit and subscription pricing offered (mixed model) (Figure 2, case 5). Modeling of cases 3, 4, and 5 follows the same steps utilized in cases 1 and 2 above. For brevity, we do not repeat details, choosing instead to provide the summary information in Table 2. We denote $d = \psi ^ { O N L G  O N I } / \delta$ and $e = ( p _ { \scriptscriptstyle u } - \psi ^ { O N L G  O N I } ) / ( 1 - \delta )$ .

In the unit model, before conducting each search, the consumer must take into account the expected net benefit from searches on the legal and on the illegal channels in deciding his or her course of action. In the subs model, the consumer decides whether to pay the subscription fee, $p _ { s }$ , and gain access to all music items on the retailer’s site or pirate all music items he or she wants to obtain. Once subscribed to the retailer’s site, the consumer would choose to search for additional music items if the expected benefit from the search exceeds the search cost. Note that once the consumer subscribes to the retailer’s site, the consumer will no longer have incentives to pirate, because he or she already gains legal access to all music items. Finally, in the mixed model, the consumer would choose whether to subscribe to the retailer’s site, purchase music items individually, or pirate the music according to the expected net benefit from each of the three options.

## Licensing Cost Structures and Retailer Profits

The marginal cost of physical reproduction of digital music is near zero. But there are licensing costs incurred by the retailer that may significantly affect overall profit. Wingfield and Smith [54] reported that Apple pays 65 to 79 cent licensing fees for each 99 cent download it sells through iTunes. In addition, in online music retailing, license structures are becoming quite flexible [40, 54]. With this in mind, our analysis considers three distinct cost structures: (1) a lump sum payment (c ) by retailer to music publisher based on the number of songs offered (N), (2) a payment set as a percentage $( c _ { p } )$ of overall retailer revenue (R), and (3) a per download fee $( c _ { d } )$ for a music item (most common market structure currently).

The retailer incurs the following costs: $N c _ { l }$ under (1) above; $R c _ { p }$ under (2); and a fixed marginal cost per download of $c _ { d }$ under (3) every time a consumer downloads a music item (in unit, subs, or mixed models). However, under (2), if a music item is sold on a per unit basis, the retailer faces a fixed marginal cost per download of $p _ { u } c _ { p } .$ If a subscription service is offered with (2), the retailer incurs a fixed marginal cost per subscriber of $p _ { s } c _ { p }$ per subscriber. Note that the license fees with (2) are independent of the number of downloads by a subscriber. The following results highlight the profit implications of license structures (proofs are in the Appendix):

## Validation of Modeling Assumption

IN OUR ANALYTICAL MODEL (see the Models of Consumer Search Process subsection), one factor reflected in δ is the lower acoustic quality of music at illegal sites due to losses from compression technologies. In addition, we suggested that δ is homogeneous across consumers. Here, we provide validation for our use of δ.

A number of factors influence the acoustic quality of compressed music, including bit rate of the compressed file, compression equipment and algorithms, and the user’s ability to discern various audio outputs. Among these, bit rate, which indicates how many bits are used to capture one second of music, is a widely utilized proxy for acoustic quality.

We monitored bit rates of music files available on WinMX, a popular P2P network for 17 weeks, from October 28, 2002, to February 20, 2003. This included daily searches for songs that were in the top 100 albums on the weekly Billboard 200 chart. From 4.3 million music files observed, we found that less than 10 percent were of the level considered “high” or “near CD” in the www.slyck.com ratings (Table 3). There were, however, about 75 percent that had bit rates in the “very good” or above categories. Thus, although only a small percentage would be viewed as roughly equivalent to CD quality, a substantial percentage could certainly be viewed as relatively close, at least from a technical bit rate perspective.

Note that δ represents the “quality of experience” factor, and there remain other forces that would tend to diminish this quality of experience including polluted music files [35] and disutility of undertaking illegal activity. Refined testing of our assumptions regarding δ are important directions of future research.

## Computational Results

TO GAIN ADDITIONAL INSIGHT INTO THE THREE emerging online market models (unit, subs, and mixed) operating under a piracy scenario, we conducted extensive simulations to address each of the following questions: How do retailer pricing strategies differ under each of the three models? How do licensing structures affect pricing and profits? What is the piracy activity associated with maximum profit solutions? Empirical data for these interacting variables are not publicly available, and simulation provides a rich test bed to study the combined effects of the consumer search process, availability of illegal channels, and retailer pricing and licensing strategies to maximize profit.

We simulated 100 consumers with V <sup>max</sup> drawn over U[1, 100]. The retailer offers 250 songs. An online illegal channel exists and offers “pirated” copies of the same songs. The experimental setup involved a range of search costs $( \psi ^ { O N L G }$ and $\psi ^ { O N I } )$ switching costs, quality of experience (δ) values and prices as shown in Table 5. These provide a wide array of possible choices for consumer and retailer decision making. For each experimental design point, one set of values for prices $( p _ { u }$ and $p _ { s } )$ search costs, channel-switching costs, and δ is chosen. We then calculate the number of searches conducted by each consumer, the number of songs each consumer purchases, the number of songs each consumer pirates, and the retailer’s revenue under each of the pricing strategies for that design. As indicated in Table 5, we also simulated two scenarios where our assumption on search cost ordering was violated $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ $\psi ^ { O N L G } \geq \psi ^ { O N I } )$ . Each design point required approximately three days to complete on dedicated racks of Intel Pentium III servers, for a total of 61,820,000 observations. We then performed an exhaustive search among all design points (brute force solution by considering every possibility) for the following information: (1) maximum profit for each of the three models; $( 2 ) p _ { u } , p _ { s } ,$ and $p _ { u }$ and $p _ { s }$ (as appropriate) for each profit-maximizing solution; and (3) piracy activity associated with each profitmaximizing solution.

As discussed in the Modeling Music Market Environments section, using the more efficient legal online search service would provide the consumer with a higher value for the outcome (music) of a search. We formalize this by assuming that a consumer searching on the more efficient legal online search service would obtain a song with a value from the top half of available songs, whereas a search on the illegal channel would yield a song from the consumer’s top two-thirds of values of available songs. Once a song is chosen, that particular song is removed from consideration in subsequent searches, resulting in a diminishing expected return from both types of searches as the number of searches increases.

For the unit model, each consumer can start a search either through a legal or illegal channel or not initiate a search. If the consumer eventually chooses to acquire the song from the legal channel, the revenue to the retailer would be $p _ { u } .$ . For the subs model, we calculate each consumer’s net benefit from subscription and piracy (according to Table 2). The consumer would subscribe if the expected benefit from subscription exceeds that from pirating (with retailer revenue $p _ { s } ) ;$ otherwise he or she would pirate. For the mixed model, we compare each consumer’s benefits from subscription and per unit purchase. The consumer would subscribe if the benefit is positive and exceeds that from purchasing individual songs; otherwise he or she can either purchase or pirate individual songs. In this case, the consumer’s decision becomes similar to that in the unit model. The consumer can also choose not to acquire any song at all if the expected benefits in all cases are negative.

In subsequent discussion, we will only refer to the percent licensing cost structure for the retailer. Since a payment under the percent licensing cost structure can always be converted into an equivalent lump sum payment, the results presented in the next section will hold equally for the lump sum structure. Note that both the lump sum and percent cost structures can be represented as a fraction of the retailer’s revenue and are not dependent on the actual number of downloads from the retailer’s service. We will mainly contrast these with the per download fee cost structure, which is dependent on the number of downloads and not on the retailer’s total revenue. ## Profit Implications

Result 3 shows the mixed model yields higher profit than unit and subs in most cases (under percent and lump sum cost structures), and computational results suggest that this is considerably affected by the quality of experience (δ) from the illegal channel. As $\delta  1$ , the difference between the maximum profit from mixed and subs models decreases and the two converge, as consumers no longer find it beneficial to purchase individual songs and those who can still benefit from making the purchase would only do so through subscription. Similar trends are observed for profits from mixed and unit models with respect to δ. On the other hand, as the value of legal search cost $( \psi ^ { O N L G } )$ increases, the subscription option becomes less attractive and consumers are more likely to purchase only individual high-valued songs. Hence, the profits from the mixed and unit models converge at a high value of legal search cost $( \psi ^ { O N L G } )$

The profit increase from the mixed model is derived from the retailer’s ability to fine-tune the value of the per unit $( p _ { u } )$ and subscription $( p _ { s } )$ prices. In many cases, the retailer can increase $p _ { s }$ to extract additional revenue from subscribers while lowering $p _ { u }$ to encourage nonsubscribers to purchase individual songs (Figures 3 and 4). This policy shows significant difference from unit and subs pricing, especially at lower values of δ. Complete comparisons can be found in Bhattacharjee et al. [9].

## Piracy Implications

We find that at the maximum profit prices, piracy levels are lower (or similar) with a percent structure than per download fee for the unit model (Figure 8). The piracy ratio shows similar patterns for different $c _ { d }$ values, and the lower piracy benefit decreases with δ. This directly flows from the previous discussion of prices charged to consumers. Lower prices lead more consumers to purchase than to pirate under the percent structure.

Another significant result is that the retailer’s profit-maximizing position occurs in an environment with piracy activity above minimum levels (Table 6). In general, the retailer obtains higher profit under the mixed model than other models. However, the piracy level in the mixed model is higher than in the subs model in most cases, while the piracy level in the mixed model ranges from 8 percent to 45 percent of the piracy level under the unit pricing model (except when $\delta = 0 . 9 5 )$ . This demonstrates that retailer strategies that lower piracy do not necessarily increase profits. As noted before, the complete set of computational results are available at Bhattacharjee et al. [9].

To test whether there exist conditions under which the retailer reaps higher profit in the complete absence of piracy, we considered the base case scenario (case 1) where the music retailer operates in a world without a music piracy option for consumers. Here, the brick-and-mortar retailer is assumed to offer a search channel whose efficiency is identical to the online legal music channel, and $\psi ^ { B M }$ ranges from 0.1 to 1.2, in increments of 0.1. At the completion of each search in the no-piracy scenario, the consumer either purchases the music item at a price of $p _ { u }$ or decides to not purchase the item. We derived a variety of implications for the differing environments and provided validation for “quality of experience” (δ), a key modeling construct for pirated music items. Finally, results from extensive computations are presented to offer insights for emerging music markets.

Our results indicate that decreasing piracy does not necessarily imply increasing profits. Rather, maximum profit outcomes occur in the presence of piracy. Seeking regulatory means to stop piracy is likely to be a self-harming strategy. Part of the access to unauthorized networks enabling music sharing may well be prepurchase sampling, an activity aiding consumers to make better purchases. Our analysis also suggests that online music sales is a dominant strategy for retailers.

Our results also indicate that the mixed model, where a consumer can either subscribe or purchase music à la carte, is the dominant strategy for the retailer. Interestingly, this dominance continues to hold even when the search cost in the legal channel exceeds that in the illegal channel. License fee structures with music publishers is a significant factor affecting pricing and overall profits. We show that a lump sum or percent of revenue payment model dominates a per download cost structure, suggesting a need for new licensing models for online music sales.

Various companies, including Apple, Roxio, Musicmatch, Wal-Mart, Sony, and Microsoft, have or are planning online music services [39]. Our analysis suggests that some of them may be missing a dominant strategy—the mixed strategy. As we continue to study online digital markets and the impact of piracy, the long-term viability and popularity of such market offerings will always be of great interest as a complement of our theoretical results. Online music retailing is a challenging enterprise. Our goal here has been to initiate a formal analysis of alternative pricing and licensing structures. Technology has forever changed the way consumers sample and procure music items, and the pot of gold awaits retailers who can understand the new landscape and adapt appropriately.

## Proof of Observation 1

NOTE THAT THE COST INCURRED FOR PERFORMING a search in the BM model with piracy is lower than in the base model. This follows from the inequality below. Considering the expected benefit from the nth search at BM, we have:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in [ a, b)} \delta v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} (v _ {i j}) + \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in [ b, V _ {i} ^ {\max} ]} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} (v _ {i j}) (v _ {i j} - p _ {u}) \\ = \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in [ a, b)} \delta v _ {i j} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} (v _ {i j}) + \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in [ b, c)} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} (v _ {i j}) (v _ {i j} - p _ {u}) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in [ c, V _ {i} ^ {\max} ]} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} (v _ {i j}) (v _ {i j} - p _ {u}). \end{array}
$$

When $b \leq \nu _ { i j } \leq c ,$ then $\begin{array} { r } { \delta \nu _ { i j } \leq \nu _ { i j } - p _ { u } . } \end{array}$ Therefore,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} \phi_ {i j} ^ {B M, n} \left(v _ {i j}\right) \max \left\{\delta v _ {i j}, v _ {i j} - p _ {u} \right\} \\ \leq \sum_ {v _ {i j} \in \left[ 0, V _ {i} ^ {\max} \right]} \phi_ {i j} ^ {O N I, n} \left(v _ {i j}\right) \max \left\{\delta v _ {i j}, v _ {i j} - p _ {u} \right\}. \end{array}

$$

$$

## Proof of Result 4
