## Abstract

IT investment by organizations in India has increased significantly over the last 10 years, as Indi an firms have deployed IS for modernizing and reengineering their processes. This paper analyzes, in an exploratory vein, organizational and strategic imperatives that have influenced IS assimilation and evolution of the IS application portfolio in Indian firms. It first identifies three categories of organizations with respect to IS assimilation, and describes strategic and organizational factors characteristic of each group. Next, it traces the evolution of the IS application portfolio in each of the studied firms and analyses accompanying changes in strategic and organizational factors. The paper builds on IS assimilation studies in organizations from developed societies, and describes issues unique to IS assimilation in Indian firms. It is based on data collected from case studies of IS deploy ment in nine Indian organizations.

Keywords: IS assimilation; IS application portfolio; India; Developing nations; Organizational factors; Strategi factors; Qualitative research

## 1. Introduction

Recent studies (Wolcott and Goodman, 2003; e.g.) indicate that firms in India have significantly increased their investment in $\mathrm { I T ^ { 1 } }$ . IT investment increased by 56% in 2001-2002 (Ranganathan and Kannabiran, 2004). Additionally, E-Commerce is also evolving as an emerging business phenomenon. The volume of B2B E-Commerce activity in India increased from \$30 million in 1998–1999 to \$225 million in 2001–2002. B2C E-Commerce activity increased from \$5 million to \$50 million during the same time (Wolcott and Goodman, 2003). The total volume of E-commerce transactions is expected to be \$100 billion by 2008<sup>2</sup>, due in part to economic liberalization during the past decade. This period has been characterized by de-regulation and easing of imports and exports. Several industry sectors have been faced with globalization and the entry of foreign competition. Historically government-controlled sectors have opened to private enterprise. Changes in financial regulatory systems, tarif structures, and industrial and commercial laws have altered industry structure and intensified competition for firms (see for example, Saxena, 1996). As a result, Indian organizations have turned to $\mathrm { I S } ^ { 2 }$ for modernizing and reengineering their processes (Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Saxena and Sahay, 2000; Banerjee, 2001). Increased availability of telecommunications facilities and penetration of the Internet (Wolcott and Goodman, 2003) have also enabled organizations to invest in Internet-based commerce. In addition, Indian organizations have been exposed to advanced uses of IT in organizations from more developed countries, through joint ventures and technology transfer initiatives made possible by increased international trade and commerce (Dasgupta et al., 1999). In many cases, this has compelled them to invest in compatible IT platforms and applications.

These developments have raised the question of how Indian firms have managed the introduction and assimilation of IS. How have firms responded to the changing environment in terms of the nature of IS applications, for instance? What strategies have they adopted in transiting from the use of basic administrative applications to strategic applications? How have organization-specific factors influenced IS deployment? India is widely regarded as one of the leading providers of IT services in the world, and a number of recent articles have discussed various aspects of the IT services and business process ofshoring industries in India (see for example, McKinsey Quarterly, special issue, 2005, 4), as well as issues related to software development and software project management (Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Walsham, 2002). However, there is very little investigation and a dearth of research on issues relating to the adoption and assimilation of IS in Indian firms. Studies have suggested (see Kanungo et al., 2001; e.g.) that there is a need to understand how diferent strategic and organizational factors have influenced IS deployment in Indian firms, given the recent changes in the Indian economy and the consequent changes in the business environment. Such understanding is necessary for managers to frame appropriate IS deployment strategies and to develop organizational capabilities necessary for implementing them.

This paper explores IS assimilation in Indian organizations. It is based on data collected from case studies of IS deployment in nine firms. More specifically, we examine IS assim ilation by tracing the evolution of the IS application portfolio in these firms during the years of economic liberalization. Further, we also study the key strategic and organizational imperatives that influenced IS assimilation in these firms. Through this longitudinal investigation of IS assimilation and associated factors in the Indian context, we contribute to the understanding of IS management issues in organizations operating in developing country environments.

## 2. Literature review

The evolution of organizations with respect to their deployment of IS can be understood in terms of two parameters. One, their IS application portfolio and two, the extent to which the technology becomes routinized in activities related to their diferent work processes. IS assimilation refers to the extent to which a system or technology becomes diffused in organizational processes (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999; Chatterjee et al., 2002; DeLone and McLean, 1992), from adoption to general deployment (Fichman and Kemerer, 1997). It is determined by the extent to which an information system is used in diferent functions, as well as how efectively it enables the conduct of activities associ ated with them. As firms achieve higher levels of IS assimilation, information systems are deployed in an increasing number of functions (Chatterjee et al., 2002). In addition, the IS application portfolio also changes and develops, from the initial use of IT for automation, to gradually more advanced uses such as decision making, process transformation, and altering relationships with customers and suppliers. One of the ways in which IS assimilation can be understood, therefore, is by examining the evolution and changes in the IS application portfolio.

Assimilation of IS is the result of appropriate actions by managers in a firm. These actions take place within the framework of organizational strategy, norms and structures. A number of studies, conducted primarily in organizations in developed nations, describe various imperatives that influence IS assimilation. They can be broadly classified as those pertaining to the strategic context and to the organizational context. Strategic imperatives include factors which impact IS assimilation through their influence on the organization’s business strategy (e.g., factors related to the external environment, characteristics of the industry and competition, and firm business strategy orientation). Organizational imperatives describe management related factors that influence IS assimilation (e.g., top management support, characteristics of managers and IS professionals, and firm specific IS department related aspects). We describe these factors below and summarize them in Table 1.

## 2.1. IS assimilation studies in a ‘‘developed country’’ setting

In the context of IS assimilation, the strategic imperatives mentioned above include three factors. First, environmental factors such as economic legislation and technology policies influence the degree of IS assimilation (Iacovou et al., 1995; Premkumar and Ramamurthy, 1995; Chwelos et al., 2001). Second, competitive factors such as characteristics of the industry, customers and partners have been found to drive a firm to deploy IS, especially when other firms in the industry have done so (King et al., 1994; Iacovou et al., 1995; Chwelos et al., 2001; Crook and Kumar, 1998). Adoption decisions with respect to inter-organizational systems (Grover, 1993) and Executive Information Systems (Rai and Bajwa, 1997), for example, have been influenced by competitive pressures. Customers expectations regarding product and service diferentiation and timely information can lead to assimilation of IS (Hart and Saunders, 1998; Watson et al., 1997). Pressures from suppliers have led to the deployment of B2B systems by firms (Reich and Benbasat, 1990; Crook and Kumar, 1998; Hart and Saunders, 1998; Payton, 2000; Chwelos et al., 2001; Mehrtens et al., 2001). A third factor that influences IS assimilation is the strategic stance of an organization. Organizations efect change in products and processes in three ways. According to Nadler and Tushman (1990), they can respond reactively to conditions that afect their competitive position, and change products or process in response to these conditions. Organizations can also proactively develop new technologies and processes, apply existing technologies and products to new markets, or create completely new products and industries (Kaplan, 1999). Additionally, firms can change in order to turn around from situations of decline. Such situations can be caused by top management failure and inabil ity to upgrade products and processes (Hambrick and D’Aveni, 1988; Hofman and Klep per, 2000), among other things. Recovery measures from these situations include product and service repositioning, process re-engineering, and changes in control systems (Kanter et al., 1992; Barker and Duhaime, 1997). All of these factors have implications for the deployment of IS (McKenny, 1995).

A number of organizational factors influence IS assimilation. The first is the approach of top management towards IS. For instance Raghunathan (1992) suggests that CEO participation in IT steering committees encourages the acceptance and assimilation of IS. Armstrong and Sambamurthy (1999); Premkumar and Ramamurthy (1995); Zmud (1982); Dewar and Dutton (1986); and Payton (2000) have suggested that top management can provide intellectual stimulation for the initiation of IS projects. Support to and championship of new IS projects by top management facilitates and encourages assimilation (Premkumar and Ramamurthy, 1995; Payton, 2000; Chatterjee et al., 2002). Top management can also ensure the availability of technical and human resources and eliminate unnecessary bureaucratic procedures (Grover, 1993; Kempis and Ringbeck, 1998; Crook and Kumar, 1998; Beatty et al., 2001). Second, organizational support for IT innovation and experimentation positively influences the assimilation of IS. IT champions for example (Beath, 1991; Sutclife, 1999), can facilitate the difusion and widespread use of new technologies. The CIO’s business and IT knowledge significantly enhances IS assimilation (Armstrong and Sambamurthy, 1999), as does a healthy rapport between the CEO and CIO (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998; Stephens et al., 1992). Third, characteristics of the IS department such as size, extent of involvement in the organization’s IS adoption and implementation eforts (Rai and Bajwa, 1997), and willingness to experiment with new technologies (Rai and Patnayakuni, 1996) are also important. Fourth, IT literacy of managers (Slywotzky, 2000) regarding advanced technology helps in the assimilation of IS. IT literacy may arise from interaction with vendors and professional associations, sophistication of existing IS infrastructure (Iacovou et al., 1995; Mehrtens et al., 2001) and existing knowledge related to IT innovations (Fichman and Kemerer, 1997).

## 2.2. IS assimilation studies in a ‘‘developing country’’ setting

Many developing nations have undergone economic policy deregulation in the last two decades (Saxena, 1996). In India, following economic liberalization, international firms have set up joint ventures with local organizations or have installed their own factories and units. This has raised the level of investment in infrastructure in general and in IT in particular, as described in Ranganathan and Kannabiran (2004) and Kanungo et al. (2001). In the government sector, a major initiative for computerization was undertaken in the early 1990s. This has resulted in large scale IT investments in many public sector organizations and government departments over the past fifteen years (Banerjee, 2001; Kanungo et al., 2001).

Strategic imperatives for IS assimilation include two factors specific to firms in developing nations. The first is the availability of infrastructure. Infrastructural resources determine the extent to which organizational deployment and assimilation of IS are feasible. Such resources include networking and telecommunications capabilities (Markus and Soh, 2002; Tractincky and Jarvenpaa, 1995), security provisions (Wresh, 2003), availability of hardware and software (Walsham and Sahay, 1999; Wolcott and Goodman, 2003), logistics and distribution facilities (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991; Bin et al., 2003; Wresh, 2003), electronic payment systems (Ein-Dor et al., 1993; Wresh, 2003) and elements of the National Information Infrastructure (Meso and Duncan, 2000). The second factor relates to government policies. In India, until the early 1990s, the economy was protected. The government played an important role in framing regulatory and legislative policies, and provided the competitive and strategic context for firms to make decisions related to IS deployment (Jain, 1997). A similar situation has also been documented in China (Kunnathur and Shi, 2001), where government policies determine the business environment to a large extent and have considerable influence on modernization and IS assimilation activities of firms. Government policies that afect IS assimilation include legal and economic regulatory frameworks (Markus and Soh, 2002; Tractincky and Jarvenpaa, 1995; Gallupe and Tan, 1999), industry regulations (Mennecke and West, 2001) and national developmental priorities for allocation of resources for IT development (Palvia, 1997; Ein-Dor et al., 1993).

Organizational imperatives for firms in developing nations include three factors. The first relates to resource acquisition. Since technology and personnel have not been easily available in the past, acquiring, mastering and managing hardware and applications has been dificult (Montealegre, 1998; Ranganathan and Kannabiran, 2004). Second, with respect to the IS department, efective management of the IS function is critical to the successful deployment of IS. A good CEO-CIO relationship and a formal IS planning process in which the IS personnel have a say, are necessary (Ranganathan and Kannabiran, 2004). This is because employees are in general less IT literate and more culturally diverse (Montealegre, 1998). The third factor is management style, which includes organizational attitudes towards information sharing, lateral communication between departments (Myers and Tan, 2002; Walsham and Sahay 1999), work norms and the nature of control systems (Walsham, 2002; Palvia, 1997; Myers and Tan, 2002). For instance, in many organizations, data is regarded as a source of power (Myers and Tan, 2002). Hence it is not easily transferred and members often hold and barter information in a clandestine manner (Bannerjee, 2002). This influences the extent to which inter-functional and electronic inter organizational information networks are implemented. The foregoing discussion has been summarized in Table 1.

Recent studies (Aladwani, 2001; Monlealegre, 1998; Kanungo et al., 2001; Ranganathan and Kannabiran, 2004; Wolcott and Goodman, 2003; Walsham and Sahay, 1999) have identified a need to better understand the processes and outcomes relating to IS deployment in developing nations. Our attempt to study the strategic and organizational factors influencing IS assimilation in Indian organizations, can be categorized as part of this research stream.

## 3. Research methodology

We adopted a qualitative approach (Walsham and Waema, 1994; Klein and Myers, 1999; Guba and Lincoln, 1994 and Kerlinger, 2000) and used the multiple case study method (Eisenhardt, 1991; Yin, 2003; Lee, 1989). We studied IS deployment in nine firms from the manufacturing (metal casting, telecommunications equipment, fast-moving consumer non-durables, hydraulic equipment) and service (financial services, banks, consulting, hospitality and courier) sectors, as described in Table 2.

For each organization, we focused our data collection on two aspects. First we studied the factors related to the strategic and organizational imperatives that influenced the assimilation of IS over time. Second, we looked at how the IS application portfolio changed over the period from before economic liberalization (prior to 1991) to after economic liberalization (after 1997). A structured, open-ended interview schedule was used. The questionnaire was divided into three sections. The first section contained questions about the background of the organization and details of the applications that were adopted and implemented (e.g., major landmarks of IS adoption programs, choice of applications, reasons for adoption and expected benefits). Questions in the second section addressed the external and competitive environment of the organization, such as changes in industry structure, political and economic changes and critical factors for the business. The third section included questions about the role of organizational factors. The questions are reported in Appendix A.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews, with 55 middle and senior managers from the IS and other departments. We first contacted a senior manager in each firm (from either a functional department or the IS department) to explain the nature and objectives of our study. Subsequent interviews were conducted using the ‘‘snowball’’ approach, whereby the initial contacts identified appropriate people for further interviewing. Each interview lasted for 45 to 60 minutes and was conducted by one of the co-authors. In addition to the interviews, we looked at archival company documents for records of existing and past information system configurations. We also collected industry data from CMIE (Center for Monitoring Indian Economy) databases, business and technology press reports and company web-sites.

We analyzed the data using across-case and within-case analysis (Yin, 2003; Miles and Huberman, 1994; Dube and Pare, 2003). First, using across case analysis, we identified, across all firms, the strategic and organizational factors that influenced the assimilation of IS, as well as the nature of the application portfolio. We matched common aspects among the firms, based on the application portfolio and the factors, and identified three diferent kinds of organizations. Next, using within-case analysis, we traced the evolution of the IS application portfolio in each firm and analyzed corresponding changes in the factors.

## 4.1.1.1. Factors relating to strategic imperatives

1. Environmental factors: For Innovative IS Users, environmental factors were not necessarily evident in every case. In the case of HOS such factors were absent prior to 1995 and again after 2000. They were present from 1995 to 1999, when there was a recession in the hospitality industry. For CND and FIN they were absent throughout the period of study.

2. Competitive factors: Similarly, the presence of competitive factors also was not necessarily witnessed in every case. For FIN and CONS, they were absent. The hospitality industry was opened to foreign competition in 1998–1999, after which competitive factors became significantly important for HOS.

3. Strategic stance: Innovative IS Users proactively pursued business excellence irrespective of any immediate competitive or environmental factors. This led to IS innovations as well, and these companies were often the first in the industry to deploy particular applications. HOS was one of India’s premier hospitality firms, and part of one of the largest corporate houses in the country. The company had introduced a number of innovative cuisine and dining concepts to Indian customers and regularly received national and international awards. A senior manager explained – ‘‘Pressures to excel are very high.’’ An IS manager said – ‘‘We adopted yield management software, similar to that used by companies [in the advanced economies] in our industry for predicting and managing demand. This helped us in demand forecasting, which is one of our most crucial activities (. . .). We were the first company in India, to do so.’’ They also implemented an enterprise system especially designed for the hospitality sector, which was later adopted by other companies as well. FIN was established in 1992 as India’s first stock exchange to provide electronic trading. Its IT infrastructural set up was the single largest in the country; it had the largest wide area network in India and one of the largest VSAT networks in Asia. CND was one of India’s oldest multinational companies. It was on Forbes’s list for the ‘‘Best Big Companies’’ and was one of India’s leading and innovative private sector firms. The company had a manufacturing process that required complex production scheduling. They implemented advanced demand forecasting, materials planning and scheduling software, similar to what was used by their UK based parent company.

## 4.1.1.2. Factors relating to organizational imperatives

1. Top management approach: Top management was strongly in favor of IS deployment. CND and HOS attracted some of the best technical and managerial talent in the country. Their CEO’s were prominent members of industry associations, educated at some of the best universities in India and abroad. They regularly interacted with their peers at other multinational/progressive companies in India. The CEO of CND had won a number of industry leadership awards. Top management at FIN consisted of a board of directors drawn from prominent members of other banks and financial institutions in India. They considered IT to be critical to their business success and were keen about maintaining IT leadership in the industry. One of the regional heads of HOS mentioned, ‘‘Our CEO considers IT to be a [central] part of [our business]. He knows where the industry is moving internationally, in terms of the use of IT. He communicates frequently with the head of IS and exchanges ideas about functions where IT can be adopted’’. At FIN, the CEO was personally involved in planning and monitoring IT projects and attended committee meetings. According to a senior manager there, ‘‘Our [CEO] sees to it that he is updated on major IT projects. He also attends some of the committee meetings. When he is touring regional ofices, he talks to the IS people, asks about their plans and makes sure that they have what they need’’.

2. IS department: The IS departments in these firms were headed by a CIO or a Vice President who was part of top management and who actively influenced the assimilation of new IT. At HOS, a middle level IS manager said, ‘‘Our department’s head interacts frequently with our CEO, and he can ensure that we have the resources we need and that the [senior] managers in other departments co-operate with us.’’ The IS heads interacted with peers from other companies and professional organizations. At FIN for example, the IS head was a member of the Computer Society of India<sup>5</sup>. Middle level IS managers had bachelors/masters degrees in computer related fields and had good technical knowl edge. A middle level function manager in CND said, ‘‘The IS people generally have a good knowledge about [our] IT applications.’’ They are also good at solving our technical problems. IS managers interacted closely with end users and managers, had a good understanding of the business, actively participated in IS deployment eforts, educated end users and supported the IT champions. At FIN, a regional head of operations said, ‘‘Our IT professionals understand what we need and provide solutions at our regional branch (.) If any of us have some ideas, they help us to develop [the ideas] further and give us technical support.’

3. IT literacy of managers: Given that Innovative IS Users deployed advanced IT applications, managers had a good knowledge of IT and were enthusiastic about its use. At FIN, a functional middle manager said, ‘‘We try to innovate with IT and like to work with the latest technologies.’’ An interesting observation at CND was that some of the senior managers interacted closely with the parent company based in the UK, and knew about IT applications being used there. One senior manager said, ‘‘We get help from our parent company. They tell us where the [potential] pitfalls and problems are.’’

4. Management style: The management cadre in HOS, CND and FIN was drawn from some of the best universities in India, and many of them had graduate degrees in busi ness administration. These firms had a professional management style that encouraged documentation, formal work processes and electronic information sharing.

5. IT champions: IT champions were present in the functional areas. They directed IT deployment eforts, encouraged employees to use applications and interacted with IS managers and senior managers to identify requirements for resources and end user training. In CND, for example, even though there were many semi-skilled employees working in the factories, production managers encouraged them to use production scheduling applications. A senior functional manager at CND described, ‘‘The senior departmental managers, especially the production managers in the factories were person ally involved in fostering user acceptance’’.

6. IT resource acquisition: Financial and human resources necessary for acquiring IT and developing applications were available.

## 4.1.2. Enlightened IS users

The second category, ‘‘Enlightened IS Users’’ (described in Table 4) deployed IS reactively, in response to the environmental factors and competitive factors. Examples of

Enlightened IS Users included the manufacturing company (HYD), the courier company (COUR) and the consulting company (CONS).

## 4.1.2.1. Factors relating to strategic imperatives

1. Environmental factors: These factors were present at COUR and CONS. CONS was a large private sector firm specializing in infrastructure consulting. More than 80% of its clients were from the steel industry. In 1995, the Indian steel industry entered a recessionary phase and decreased its infrastructural spending. Opportunities for new projects therefore decreased. At COUR, government policies with regard to modernization were the primary driver of IT deployment. A senior manager described, ‘‘In 1992–1993, we and other similar organizations were instructed by the government to modernize and introduce computers into our operations’’. COUR adopted computerized package booking, tracking and delivery applications and developed accompanying network infrastructure. These were similar to applications that were being used by private firms in the industry.

2. Competitive factors: These were present at HYD and CONS. HYD was a medium sized family owned firm. It faced competition and pressure from customers for reducing cycle times and lowering costs. It deployed IS for modernizing critical activities, as a reaction to these pressures. A senior manager at HYD said, ‘‘We automated [several functions], we developed an in-house enterprise system integrating material procurement, material requisition for production, inventory control, dispatches and invoicing. Better information availability reduced cycle time and inventory, [so that we] reduced costs and delivery times’’. CONS faced demands for better customer service. A senior functional manager said, ‘‘We started delivering drawings electronically to our clients. We built a central database where we stored our designs-in-progress. Consultants from diferent departments could simultaneously work on these designs. Solution design and development took less time’’.

3. Strategic stance: The strategic stance of these firms was reactive. IS deployment was in reaction to the environmental factors in COUR, and to competitive factors in HYD. It was in reaction to both in CONS.

## 4.1.2.2. Factors relating to organizational imperatives

1. Top management approach: Top management at HYD and CONS were members of the families that owned these firms. They were not professional managers, did not see the strategic significance of IS and were not enthusiastic about IT leadership in their industries. However, they were aware of the operational usefulness of IT and were willing to follow other firms in their industries, in deploying IS in key processes. The IS head at HYD explained, ‘‘The Managing Director (MD) of the company (. . .) is not averse to the use of IT per se, but her awareness of IT applications [is] limited to traditional payroll and accounting software. She does not think that we should be investing a lot of money in buying expensive software unless we can justify the costs’’. COUR was a public sector organization and top management was not proactive about IS deployment.

2. IS department: The head of the IS function in these firms was not part of top management and did not participate in strategic planning. At HYD he was a middle manager who reported to the Vice President (Finance). At COUR the IS department was headed by a senior manager. It had technical staf, as well as middle and junior managers from other functions who had been trained and re-deployed. This was a common practice in pubic sector firms in India. In all the three organizations, the head of the IS function interacted frequently with other functional heads, as well as with top management. They also influenced and championed IT deployment decisions. The IS head had auton omy in recruiting and training IS human resources. IS managers interacted closely with end users to provide technical help and training, and encouraged the use of diferent applications.

3. IT literacy of managers: At HYD and COUR, more than 80% of the employees were semi-skilled shop floor workers or staf, and were not IT literate. At HYD, managers were mostly engineers, working in the production or design functions of low complexity engineering technologies. A senior manager at HYD said, ‘‘Although we do not actively resist using computers, we do not think they make too much of a diference’’. At COUR they were career bureaucrats and were not IT aware, again a common feature of many public sector organizations in India. At CONS, although the IT awareness of managers was relatively higher, they used existing applications only to the extent required.

4. Management style: The management style at COUR was bureaucratic and there were structured work norms and control systems. At HYD, it was entrepreneurial and information was often shared through non-written, verbal means, especially among shop floor employees. Information was not always documented. At CONS, employees in the design and engineering department were engineers and the management style was professional. The administrative departments had a bureaucratic management style.

5. IT champions: IT Champions were from the IS department in all the three firms.

6. IT resource acquisition: At HYD and CONS, the availability of financial and human resources with regard to IS deployment depended on top management’s judgment of whether or not a system was required. At COUR it depended on resource allocation directives and policies of the government, and budgets for the particular financial year.

## 4.1.3. Reluctant IS users

The third category, ‘‘Reluctant IS Users’’ deployed IS primarily in response to serious and continuing performance problems such as declining profits and market share, or because of operational ineficiencies such as high cycle time and high inventory. Reluctant IS Users included a bank (BANK), a communications equipment manufacturing company (COM) and a metal manufacturer (MET).

## 4.1.3.1. Factors relating to strategic imperatives

1. Environmental factors: These factors were evident in the case of BANK. BANK was a public sector bank, and an important environmental factor was the directive from the Ministry of Finance for modernization and computerization of various transaction processing activities.

2. Competitive factors: These factors were present for COM, MET and BANK. COM was a state government owned manufacturer of radio-based communications equipment. Customer preferences shifted towards new multinational competitors, which entered the market in 1996. This was because COM had a long cycle time, and poor marketing and demand forecasting. MET was a family owned medium sized firm in a competitive, cost driven market. For BANK, there was competition from private banks, multinational banks and non-banking financial institutions.

3. Strategic stance: In all the three cases, the strategic stance was geared towards recovery. BANK had registered consecutive years of financial losses and decreasing market share because it could not ofer products and services comparable to its competitors. Operational ineficiencies, primarily due to poor information processing, led to a decline in profitability. A middle manager said, ‘‘We have high volume transaction processing requirements, which require computers. We also had problems with integration. We introduced applications for transaction processing in payroll, accounting and ledger posting’’ At COM, the R&D manager said, ‘‘Earlier, we had a glut of orders, more than we could fulfill. Once the foreign competitors entered, we lost customers. We had losses for five consecutive years’’. There was no co-ordination between functions because diferent departments did not share information. Hence, order fulfillment processes were ineficient. A basic enterprise transaction system spanning sales, accounts, purchasing, logistics and production was implemented. At MET the CEO said, ‘‘A lot of cheaper substitutes are imported from south east Asian countries. Between 1995 and 1997, we lost market share by 20–25%, we did not have enough orders to produce to capacity, and we had high inventory’’. To stem the decline the company implemented an inventory tracking and control application, and an accounting package.

## 4.1.3.2. Factors relating to organizational imperatives

1. Top management approach: The top management at BANK consisted of public sector bureaucrats who had functioned in a protected environment for a long time. They did not consider IS to be important in any way, except in automating financial and accounting transactions. An IS manager in one of the regions said, ‘‘Our senior oficers don’t think that investments in IT are useful. There have been no proactive initiatives taken, to modernize [the] operations’’. At MET, the CEO was also the owner-entrepreneur, and according to him, ‘‘For us, the shop floor and production activities are most important. [We do not think] computers are at all necessary’’. COM had been a state government owned monopoly manufacturer for a long time and the CEO was a research and development oriented person who was more comfortable working in the design laboratory. Even though he was aware of IT in general, he did not understand its applicability to the firm. Nor did he have the managerial and administrative skills for taking decisions regarding the deployment of IS. Decision making was centralized at MET and COM, and hence the CEO’s attitude towards IS defined the organizational stance as well. There was very little support for IS deployment.

2. IS department: At COM and MET there were no separate IS departments. There was one person in charge of maintaining PC’s and desktop departmental applications, developed on packages like FOXPRO. At MET he reported to the CEO. At COM he reported to the finance manager. They did not interact much with end users or the CEO, except when there was a technical problem. The CEO of MET said, ‘‘He reports to me. He does not usually interact with user departments – there is actually no need to, because we do not have complex applications’’. BANK had regional EDP departments stafed by clerical staf and junior managers who had previously worked in other functions and had subsequently been re-trained, primarily to install and maintain the ledger posting transaction systems. They reported to administrative departments in the regional ofices. The corporate EDP department was managed by middle managers who reported to the head of the finance department. It did not have any interaction with top management, or the capability or resources to develop and support strategic IT solutions.

3. IT literacy of managers: At all the firms, more than 80% of the employees performed low -skilled and semi-skilled technical and clerical tasks. Their IT literacy was low. BANK and COM had employee unions. The IS manager at COM said, ‘‘Shop floor workers are against the introduction of computers. Managers are indifferent’’. The employee union at BANK was part of the union of public sector bank employees in India. Both these unions feared that computerization would lead to retrenchment. Hence they were determined to block the introduction of IT. Junior and middle managers were apathetic to IT deployment. Although there was no union at MET, according to its CEO, ‘‘[Our] employees are mostly unskilled workers who work on the shop-floor. Most of them are completely unaware of IT.’’

4. Management style: The management style was bureaucratic at BANK, with structured processes and extensive documentation. The management style at MET was entrepreneurial and often ad-hoc. At COM it was somewhat bureaucratic. At COM and MET in that there was little documentation and information was often transferred verbally.

5. IT resource acquisition: At COM and MET, IS were not considered important and there was no perceived need to acquire IT related resources. At BANK, apart from deploying applications specified by the government and training employees to maintain and operate them, there was no additional resource allocation.

6. IT champions: There were no IT champions.

## 4.2. Within-case analysis: evolution of I.S. application portfolio

For within-case analysis, we traced changes in the IS application portfolio of each of the nine firms over three diferent time periods. As Table 1 shows, strategic factors especially applicable to developing countries include Infrastructure and Government Policies. Based on these two factors, we considered three time periods for analysis. Each period had diferent levels of infrastructure availability and government regulation. In Period 1, prior to 1991, i.e., ‘‘Before Liberalization’’, the economy was regulated and the business environment was characterized by the absence of free market competition. IT was expensive, telecommunications infrastructure was poor and government controlled, and modern computing and communication technologies were not widely available or afordable. During Period 2 (1991–1997), i.e., ‘‘During Liberal ization’’. successive industries were opened up to foreign investment and competition. The deregulation of the telecommunications industry resulted in large inflows of foreign technology and investment, and networking and telecommunications capabilities developed rapidly. The domestic IT industry also grew, partly because of greater availability of hardware and software and partly because of impending Y2K-related problems. IT became more afordable and available for Indian companies. In Period 3, after 1997, i.e., ‘‘After Liberalization’’, there were further developments in telecommunications infrastructure, Internet access, broadband technologies, cellular technology, and wire less local loop technologies. More industries were deregulated. The availability of IT hardware and software increased further, and costs decreased. The Indian IT industr emerged as one of the world’s largest providers of IT services and back ofice support operations.

Table 6 describes the evolution of the IS application portfolio of each of the nine firms, during these three periods.

1. FIN: FIN was established in 1992, and functioned from the beginning, in a deregulated and competitive environment. Further, its strategic stance was proactive. It was an ‘‘Innovative IS User’’ during the period of study, and had extensively deployed IS. The availability of IT having increased considerably after 1992, it was able to leapfrog right away into a strategic user of IT.

2. HOS: HOS was one of India’s most well known companies. In Period 1, its IS deployment and assimilation was high, even in the absence of any competitive or environmental pressures. This was partly because of the nature of its organizational factors and partly because it had the financial resources to acquire IT from abroad. During Periods 2 and 3, faced with competitive pressures and with access to more advanced IT, its application portfolio also evolved.

3. CND: was a multinational company in the manufacturing sector, and was one of India’s most well known companies. It had access to information about current applications used in developed countries partly through its parent company, and partly through its top management. Even though a large fraction of its employees was semi-skilled, IS assimilation was high because of organizational factors (particularly the high involvement and knowledge of IS professionals and the presence of functional IT champions). Like HOS, it used successively more sophisticated applications in the three periods, mirroring developments in the developed nations.

4. HYD: HYD changed from being a Reluctant IS User in Period 1 to an Enlightened IS User in Period 2 and Period 3. The company had operated in a monopoly market during Period 1. Even though its processes were ineficient and its inventory high, top management did not want to invest in IT. The company could not sustain its position after deregulation in Period 2, and profits and market share decreased drastically. According to the CEO, ‘‘We realized that if we had to reduce costs and decrease cycle time and inventories, we needed better IT. We recruited a new IS manager, in 1993.’’ This person was inducted into the senior management cadre. He hired more IS professionals. The size, visibility, responsibilities, competence and importance of the IS function increased sharply. Applications deployed during Period 1 included payroll and accounting transaction processing systems. In Period 2, a materials management application was developed in-house. This integrated data from diferent functions related to materials management. Since materials management was one of the critical functions at HYD, the company’s dependence on computers increased. However, in Period 3, the CEO stepped down to make way for a member of the family, whose approach to IT was not as positive. No new applications were deployed in Period 3.

5. COUR: COUR was a large public sector firm, with significant transaction processing requirements. It did not have any competition in Period 1. During this period, it was a Reluctant IS User and only basic payroll and accounting processes were computerized. During Period 2, there were government directives for computerization, as well as a change in top management. A senior manager told us, ‘‘Starting from 1991 the top management had begun developing some ideas about areas where IT could be applied and tried to make available some resources. Earlier, we did not even have a separate department or function for IT. Now we have a group headed by a senior manager’’. The company deployed core operational applications in Period 2. Due to the very large size of the organization and its spread across the country, these were assimilated and further difused during Period 3.

## 5. Discussion

Our interpretation of the results suggests some key observations about the three types of organizations.

Three kinds of firms were likely to be Innovative IS Users: well known multinationals that existed prior to 1991, premier Indian organizations, or organizations established after 1991, especially in banking and financial services, consulting, IT services and retail sectors. The multinationals had access to technology and resources (including the IT mindsets and the relevant management approaches) of their parent companies from developed nations. The premier Indian firms had access to good managerial talent and were leaders in their industry. IS assimilation and application portfolio evolution in these two kinds of organizations paralleled that of organizations in the developed countries. Organizations that were established after 1991, operated from the beginning in a competitive and increasingly unregulated economy. They were likely to be stafed by professionally qualified people, often IT literate and with business degrees. In a large number of cases, their top managements consisted of second generation entrepreneurs who had a relatively stronger global orientation than their predecessors. Moreover, given the availability of hardware, software and telecommunications infrastructure, such organizations were able to leapfrog<sup>7</sup> into deploying sophisticated IT and strategic applications from the very beginning. We observed that there needed to be a strong relationship between the top management and the IS leadership to guide and manage IS assimilation. It was also important to encourage and direct line managers and end users towards IT innovation.

Enlightened IS Users were likely to be of two kinds: private sector firms which existed prior to 1991 and public sector firms that were among the earliest to be computerized by the government. The private sector firms had functioned in markets marked by an absence of competition before 1991. After liberalization, they were able to quickly adapt to the changed business conditions – greater competition, requirements for better customer service and more operational eficiency – and implement and assimilate suitable IS applications. The public sector firms were primarily in the railways and the banking sectors. The computerization process was carried out with the help of ‘‘programs’’ (Earl, 1989), where IS departments were set up and existing staf was retrained to carry out IS related functions such as data entry, maintenance and report generation. Enlightened IS Users secured necessary resources and used standard technology. We observed that top management in these organizations was not inclined towards achieving IT leadership in the industry. Therefore it was important to develop and retain IS capabilities, in order to quickly play catch-up with industry leaders. Surveillance of industry trends was also critical. Enlightened IS Users risked becoming Reluctant IS Users if they failed to adequately survey important IS applications in their industry or if top management stopped supporting resource allocation towards IS adoption

Reluctant IS Users were typically of two kinds: small and medium private sector entrepreneurial firms which produced low technology products, and had operated in local markets before 1991, and large public sector firms where IS deployment ‘‘programs’’ were dificult to implement because of organizational barriers like size, employee unions, complexity of operations and management indiference. The private sector firms were often ‘‘first’’ generational family owned businesses. They had absolutely none or very little exposure to IT. The public sector firms included federal and state-government owned manufacturers of technology equipment. They supplied to assured government markets and had not invested in process modernization over the years. These firms were not part of large scale government computerization programs and were largely left to themselves to deploy IS. Reluctant IS Users used basic applications for immediate cost reduction in transaction oriented processes, but did not attempt to proactively extend computerization to other processes. They did not have any long term plans for investments in IT and lacked suficient support for the development of new applications. Our observations of the characteristics of Reluctant IS Users suggest that could leapfrog into Innovative IS Users without going through the Enlightened IS User stage. This could happen if they formed joint ventures with foreign firms for example, and consequently experienced a sharp change in their organizational focus towards IS, along with changes in the top management and in the IS function.

The study identifies strategic and organizational imperatives that have influenced IS assimilation and evolution in Indian organizations. It describes contrasting approaches that Indian firms have adopted towards implementation and assimilation of IS. The results have refined and added to findings from earlier research in a number of ways. First, existing literature, for the most part, has studied the efect of diferent strategic and organizational factors in isolation. In the Indian context, environmental and competitive changes due to economic liberalization have afected organizations that vary widely in terms of size, ownership, age and nature of workforce. Many of these organizations operated for a number of years in a protected business environment, with little exposure to competition. Therefore, while it is important to understand the efect of individual factors, it is also necessary to consider the organizational and strategic aspects together in an integrated manner. As the findings show, it is not the separate efects of individual factors, but the combined efects that influence IS assimilation and determine the application portfolio. For example, while literature suggests that environmental uncertainty and increased competition lead to IS deployment and assimilation, the Enlightened IS Users and Reluctant IS Users approaches showed that it was not necessary for all organizations faced with these conditions to be the first to deploy IS. Further, as the Enlightened IS User approach shows, an organization would not necessarily be an IT leader in the industry, even if the IS department was technically competent and proactive about supporting and educating top managers and end users.

Second, while the Indian software industry is well known for its competencies in developing IT services and providing IT based of-shoring support, these co-exist with low IS assimilation and difusion in many firms, as shown by the Reluctant IS Users approach. At the same time, there are firms which have been able to leapfrog into strategic uses of IS and have deployed sophisticated IS application portfolios, as the Innovative IS Users approach shows.

Third, although factors such as top management support have afected IS assimilation in developed nations also, this study brings out findings unique to the Indian context. The direct involvement of top management in implementation of new IS projects through membership in project committees emerged as an important aspect of top management support. Also, given that IT hardware and software became afordable and easily available only recently, many firms had not historically considered IT to be a strategic resource. Hence the extent to which top management was inclined towards IS leadership in the industry and was willing to allocate resources to it, was critical. The role of IS professionals has also come out in greater detail. IS professionals facilitated assimilation by supporting IT champions, educating and influencing end users and forming close relationships with the top management. Further, as in the case of Enlightened IS Users, IS professionals were able to drive IS assimilation eforts even in the absence of enthusiasm from top management, by influencing other functional departments and end users. Given low IT awareness and literacy among end users and managers in many firms, it was possible for the IS department to have a more prominent role in educating them, and in directing and influencing IS assimilation.

Fourth, most mature Indian organizations, until recently, did not have access to the latest information technologies, due to their high cost and restrictive government policies. This, combined with relatively low levels of competition, resulted in centralized, batch processing computing environments. IS was not a business priority in such environments and the IS function was not important. Dialog between the CIO and CEO, or between IS managers and functional end users, was almost non-existent. After liberalization, the availability of IT increased, as did the intensity of competition. Although IS deployment and assimilation have also increased as a result, many organizations still lack the mindset for using IS strategically. They also do not have the required management paradigms and IS capabilities for managing IT environments that reflect an increased importance of IS. On the other hand, multinationals (because of prior exposure to technology and managerial practices from their western counterparts) and the newer Indian organizations (because of the changed environment and greater availability of technology) did not face these problems to the same extent. Our findings illustrate these aspects, which are characteristic of the Indian context, and are also found to a certain extent in other developing countries.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper presents an integrated and first – level analysis of strategic and organizational imperatives that have influenced the assimilation and evolution of IS in Indian organizations. First, in terms of academic contribution, it identifies important aspects relevant to the Indian context, such as the role of IS professionals, leapfrogging, and government-led IT implementation programs. Previous studies (Saxena and Sahay, 2000; e.g.) have found diferences in the extent to which Indian firms have exploited and used IT. They have found that most organizations have adopted primarily a ‘‘transaction processing’’ and fragmented stance and have separately computerized functions such as finance, accounts, purchasing and inventory. Few have used integrated and process-oriented applications of a strategic nature. This paper identifies the strategic and organizational factors relevant to the Indian context, which have caused such variations. It further explains how these factors have played out diferently in diferent kinds of organizations and have resulted in contrasting levels of IS assimilation.

Second, in terms of managerial strategies, the findings can be used by firms to identify factors important to their business situation, take stock of these, and change their approach towards IS assimilation. For instance, organizations may analyze the strategic and organizational factors specific to their context. They could then frame appropriate policies for managing their IS assimilation and evolution, particularly in terms of changing certain relevant organizational factors. They could also plan for transition to a diferent approach by identifying the necessary changes.

However, the findings need to be considered in the light of certain limitations. First, the data was primarily collected from retrospective interviews and therefore might be subject to constraints of respondent recall. Second, the single country research setting necessitates caution in extending the findings to other developing nations. Third, the limited number of firms and industries represented in the study restricts the generalizability of the findings.

In terms of possibilities for future research, this study poses important questions. For instance, are certain factors, approaches and transitions more prevalent in particular industries? With increased availability of hardware and software, and with advances in telecommunications infrastructure, has leapfrogging been more prevalent in certain indus tries and types of organizations? Longitudinal case studies conducted in real time in other countries that are currently at diferent stages of economic liberalization, could answer some of these questions and build on the findings reported in this paper. Also, studies focused on specific industries would provide industry specific perspectives on IS assimila tion in developing country environments.

To conclude, this paper examines IS assimilation in Indian organizations in the context of strategic and organizational imperatives. India is similar to many developing societies in Asia, Africa, South America and Eastern Europe, in terms of telecommunications and transport related infrastructure, large internal markets, as well as the entry of foreign investment and international competition in recent years. Also, the availability of modern information technologies has been a catalyst for corporate modernization in these countries, similar to India. We believe that our findings can be used as a starting point for understanding and analyzing the opportunities, imperatives and contexts for IS assimilation in these nations too.
