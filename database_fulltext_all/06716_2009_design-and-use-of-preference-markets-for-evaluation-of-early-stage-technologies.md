Abstr ac t: In the work presented here, we develop and apply preference markets in evaluating early stage technology. Partnering with a Fortune 5 company, we developed and implemented two internal preference markets (field experiments). In both cases, nonmonetary (play money) incentives were utilized, but one market provided additional nonmonetary (play money) incentives. Working with the partner company, our investigation started with seven emerging technologies and expanded to a total of 17 emerging technologies. Our results suggest that even a simple form of additional nonmonetary play money incentive yielded greater price convergence, increased spread across final market prices, and greater consistency with a costly expert panel that was set up by the partner company. Based on the outcomes of our analyses, the partner company is investing in developing extended applications of preference markets as a potentially scalable approach for dealing with its ongoing and expanding strategic identification of promising emerging technologies.

Key wor ds and p hr ases: convergence, incentives, information market, market performance, preference aggregation, preference ranking market, technology evaluation.

In a gl obal ec onomy with intense c omp etition, companies can face tremendous pressure to grow their businesses and to sustain that growth. In pursuing growth goals, companies are becoming more innovation driven, seeking to exploit new ideas (products and technologies) and turn those ideas into marketable realities. One particular focus has been the identification of high-potential emerging technologies. By emerging technologies, we mean very early stage innovations that often are not yet linked to a specific product or product line.<sup>1</sup> For example, various forms of global positioning system (GPS) sensor technology emerged before the linkages to specific products that now range from driving direction support devices to truck and trailer tracking devices to teenage monitoring devices. Biometric recognition technology emerged prior to the emergence of specific products such as iris scan devices and fingerprint scanner devices that incorporated variations of this technology. Companies seek competitive advantage by developing methods to evaluate emerging technologies earlier and more effectively than their competitors. But predicting the eventual business or profit impact from emerging technologies is a difficult problem. Further, at least in the short run, there is no direct means to determine whether or not a prediction is accurate.<sup>2</sup> Examples include identifying consumer preferences on new ideas [28, 54], new product designs [14, 15], or emerging technologies where the true outcome on alternatives is unlikely to be fully revealed. The decision choices in these problem contexts (i.e., to pursue a few, or perhaps one, “top”-ranked alternative from available alternatives options) pose very important continuing challenges to many firms [13, 26, 52].

In dealing with early stage technology evaluation, companies have not yet found a satisfactory technique that is scalable and can incorporate feedback and learning. In the research presented here, we worked with a major Fortune 5 partner company to investigate the usefulness of preference markets to improve on its current process for assessing emerging technologies to determine which, if any, should be chosen for further action. Such actions could range from adopting the technology within an existing commercialization activity, to utilizing the technology in developing a new commercial product or improved industrial process, to completely acquiring and commercializing the technology itself. The nature of our partnering company is such that early stage emerging technology evaluation can involve thousands of innovations and advances across areas such as information technology, specialized materials, pollution abatement, diagnostic enhancement, and energy conversion. The company’s expanding scope combined with ever-increasing rate of technology innovation has brought out the limitations of the company’s traditional technology evaluation techniques, including surveys, focus groups, and use-case ideation.

Consider the process that is currently in place at the company. An individual (employee of the company) titled as the emerging technology leader for a specific business line “decides” what technologies enter the evaluation phase while those not chosen for evaluation remain in the “technology database.” The evaluation phase includes an in-depth analysis by a company-selected panel of experts. In discussions with the company, we jointly identified three concerns with the existing process:

1. opportunities are likely missed because only a few emerging technologies from the technology universe were selected for further evaluation;

2. quite often the technology leader identified a high-potential emerging technology but had no panel of experts available in the appropriate pool capable of evaluating the technology; and

3. the emerging technology leader acknowledged that the existing process is not flexible, engagement of panel participants is a constant struggle, and panel participants do not interact and learn from each other during the evaluation/ ranking process.

We agreed to explore whether an internal preference market (I-PREF) could tap the collective wisdom of company employees and provide consensus views on the emerging technologies.

The company looked to our analysis and results as key indicators of whether it would invest in expanded analysis of the use of I-PREFS in larger problem settings where the company would seek to pare large candidate sets (their indication was \~100 at a time) of emerging technologies down to a manageable set (\~3 to 5) of promising alternatives. In our investigation, the determination that there is “business value” in the I-PREF approach translates into whether our Fortune 5 partner would find the outcomes of our preference markets to hold sufficient business promise to warrant additional investment and development. The use of I-PREFS had the goal of reducing a large set of emerging technologies down to a set of perhaps two to five that would then receive enhanced evaluation using approaches including panels of experts, focus groups, and mathematical models.

Our analysis is based on two internal preference markets structured and run for the Fortune 5 company. The two markets are identical except that one of them provides an additional nonmonetary (play money) incentive to participants. In our I-PREFS, participants buy and sell emerging technology stocks based on their assessment of the technology’s future business value. During the operation of the market, market bids, offers, and transactions provide feedback and potential learning experiences to participants. We develop and analyze a variety of metrics to better understand the dynamic interactions in the market, including trader’s participation, their influence on other traders and the market, and security convergence. We study how nonmonetary trader incentives influence user participation, dynamic preference aggregation, convergence of security prices, and market performance. The market with added (nonmonetary) incentives outperforms the other market (without added nonmonetary incentives) in increased participation, greater price convergence, increased price spread across different technologies, and greater ranking consistency with a panel of experts the company assembled to assess a subset (six) of the emerging technologies. The impact of additional nonmonetary incentives was important to our partner company for two important reasons: (1) company policy strictly barred monetary payments to employees, and (2) the effectiveness of additional nonmonetary incentives provided positive support for the expanded use of preference markets in the company’s strategic decision making. As detailed below, the results of our investigation, paired with the company’s assessment of these outcomes and the company’s decision to invest further in preference market use, suggest potential for I-PREFS.

## Foundations of I-PREFS

As noted earl ier , c omp anies have c ontinued to use ad hoc qualitative approaches in attempting to analyze emerging technologies. Techniques have included surveys, focus groups, critical success factors, scoring models, and auctions [12, 19, 34, 40, 53]. There are also a few streams of quantitative research models focused on optimal resource allocation given a small portfolio of new technologies [23, 29]. Benaroch [7] and Brennan and Trigeorgis [10] proposed extending the financial options valuation model to real projects in order to capture the dynamic nature and broad flexibilities (options) available to decision makers. It is also possible (e.g., [1]) to integrate both qualitative and quantitative approaches for evaluating and selecting competing alternatives. Dahan et al. [15] emphasize that, while these approaches may provide some useful inputs for managers, they suffer when the set of alternative choices is large and the uncertainty (or risk) associated with each alternative is high. Dahan et al. [15] use preference markets but focus on specific features of a final consumer product—a smart phone. LaComb and colleagues [27, 28] illustrate the use of preference markets in the area of idea generation and research topic identification. A recent working paper [42] centers on event prediction, but incorporates an analysis of the use of I-PREF incentives and the final price orderings. Research in this area is still at an early stage, but we do note two specific points from these earlier papers. First, when the preference orderings sought are over concepts or ideas rather than specific end products, the I-PREFS are run much longer (weeks rather than hours; see [16, 27, 28, 44, 45]). Second, the initial evidence in Slamka et al. [42] supports the usefulness of I-PREF mechanisms. Here, we strive to add to the I-PREF body of research.

In discussions with our partner company, we identified I-PREFS as a potential alternative that can induce participants to reveal/update their preferences, efficiently aggregate these preferences, and provide consolidated preference rankings (measured by security prices) on emerging technologies. The development of I-PREFS draws from a variety of disciplines, including economics, finance, and information systems. We briefly discuss four foundation areas and specific literature related to our work: (1) learning under rational expectations, (2) efficient markets, (3) experimental economics, and (4) wisdom of crowds.

## Learning Under Rational Expectations

Recent research has provided evidence that consumers’ preferences for technologybased products tend to change over time [48, 58]. One foundation theory incorporating dynamic learning is the “rational expectation” theory [30, 32]. Under rational expectation theory, market participants are assumed to be rational in observing, learning, and adapting. They learn from the experience and patterns of economic activities and adapt appropriately to changes in economic circumstances. If traders in I-PREFS exhibit rational expectations patterns, this suggests that firms can aggregate observed trader preferences for use in the company’s decision making.

## Efficient Markets

Fama’s [18] market theory argues that, in an efficient market, all information available is reflected in the prices of the market. Thus, in our analysis, an efficient preference market implies that all the information on participants’ preferences on the technologybased products can be aggregated into the market prices. Von H ayek’s [56] hypothesis says that a competitive market is the most efficient pricing mechanism to aggregate dispersed information of market participants. Since I-PREFS are designed to provide an environment of competitive bids and offers, this mechanism is capable of providing efficient market outcomes.

## Experimental Economics

While I-PREFS can be designed to follow most of the key principles of controlled laboratory experiments [31, 36, 37, 43, 49, 55], there are two common exceptions. First, in various environments, including that of our partner company involved in this study, monetary rewards are not permissible. We utilize play money and analyze the preference market operations and outcomes in two markets differentiated by the “incentives” provided, one with additional play money incentives and one control market with no such additional incentives [41]. In addition, since performance may not be immediately and directly measurable in a preference market, Smith’s performance-based reward criteria may not be feasible. Thus we investigate and compare the operation of two markets under differing non-performance-based play money incentives (see the discussion in [47]).

## Wisdom of Crowds

Sociology provides the underlying concept termed the “wisdom of crowds” [51]. The applicability of this concept is important as a basis for relying upon the results of I-PREFS. In particular, the “wisdom of crowds” concept argues that aggregation of individual information across a group, even if biased, leads to more accurate predictions and better decisions than those made by any single or small set of individuals. Arakji and Lang [3], applying economic analysis, also demonstrated the benefits of producer–consumer collaboration for innovation and product development in the video game industry. Prediction markets are cited as the most common application for “wisdom of crowds” [51] and “arguably, the most efficient financial market” [39, p. 21]. However, the “wisdom of crowds” emphasizes the need for verifiable predictions that typically will not be available immediately (or possibly ever) in I-PREFS.

In sum, if we knew that the conditions of rational expectations theory, experimental economics, and the “wisdom of crowds” all held in our I-PREFS, our task and our analysis would be quite simple. Given the issues with monetary rewards and with direct determination of performance, we must ask how the accuracy, how the value, and how the usefulness of a preference market can be demonstrated.<sup>3</sup>

## Market Design and Implementation

As noted in the intr oduc tion, we expl or e whether preference market (I-PREF) is a viable approach that could effectively tap the collective knowledge (preferences) of individuals and provide consensus views on emerging technologies whose true outcome (success or not) may not be fully revealed, at least in the short run. In the I-PREF market setting, we utilize the price of a security to represent the degree of aggregated preference for a technology. Measures of price convergence and price separation are used in assessing the performance of these markets. As explained in the next section, price convergence provides an indication of agreement/consensus on preference or value of a technology. Similarly, all other things equal, price separa tion suggests clearer differential rankings than closely aligned prices across different technologies.

The design of the preference markets involved a cooperative process with our partner company. But the company required certain constraints for the I-PREFS. First, because of intellectual property and business secret concerns, the company required that participants must be current employees. Second, company policy prohibited the use of cash payments or monetary rewards as a means to solicit employee viewpoints. Below, we describe the structure and operations of our preference markets in terms of incentive design, pool of participants, choice of securities, starting portfolio, information design, and communication.

Our first major decision choice in structuring the preference markets was the design of a proper incentive mechanism (see discussions in [21, 22, 36, 43, 57]) and the medium of exchange for participants buying/selling securities. Since monetary rewards were not possible in our case because of a strict company policy, we utilized “play money” incentives. While a single market would allow us to observe trading activity, price changes, and price convergence, it would not provide us any benchmark for comparison. Thus we set up two parallel markets. The two markets differ only in the structure of the incentive design. In one market (termed the “additional-incentive market”), traders have the opportunity to earn up to two awards of additional investment capital. One award was based on a participant’s activity in days 1–3 and the other on activity in days 7–9. Participants who were active on two of the three specified days and who completed at least three trades in each specified period were awarded \$1,000 in play money. The awards represented 10 percent of the initial “cash” endowment (see below), an amount that we felt would incentivize participation but not high enough to “incite” participation (that is, make participation a dominant force over preference revelation).<sup>4</sup> The other market (termed the “control market”) did not provide any such additional incentives.

We note that the play money had no cash-in value or trade-in value to purchase prizes. During the duration of the market, the play money provided a performance tracking measure. The “value” of the top ten portfolios was listed each day on each trading market site so individual participants could compare their performance. Thus, there was some recognition of top performers and, we hoped, a seriousness and intensity from the competitive nature of the market. As detailed more fully below, the outcomes from the additional incentive market suggest the motivating capability of play money in our specific setting.

The participant pool in any market should have critical mass, diversity, and a mix of knowledge. The company identified a total of 720 individuals from a wide array of business units as participant candidates. We contacted all 720 individuals via an invitation e-mail (sent out by the company’s emerging technology leader) with a brief introduction and the objective of the markets. Of these 720, 129 agreed to participate. We randomly assigned participants into the two markets, providing an initial split of 64–65 per experiment (control market versus additional-incentive market). Of these, 61 actually participated in the control market and all 65 in the additional-incentive market. These numbers meet the “20 to 30” standards suggested in Chen and Plott [11] and Klarriech [25]. Traders in one market were not informed about the existence of the other market. Given that the participants come from more than a dozen functions across several business groupings (many more individual businesses), the participant set appears consistent with the diverse participant pool and knowledge level suggestions in Klarriech [25] and Sunstein [50].

We determined the number of technologies to be traded based on a desire to keep the market interesting and yet manageable for participants (see [15]). Limits on the number of securities for a market are also derived from the concepts of bounded rationality, respondent fatigue, and simple time constraints. There is no absolute rule of thumb on what is the optimal number of securities in a market. LaComb et al., noting that they “did not limit the number of ideas submitted to the market,” nevertheless argue that there is evidence that “thin markets are less effective than thick markets” [28, p. 255]. Chen and Plott [11] were successful with a ratio of traders to assets of 5:2 while Kirchner [24] utilized approximately a 5.5:1 ratio. Discussions with our partner company indicated its desire to consider some 17 emerging technologies. Given that each market has about 65 participants, we had a trader to security ratio of just under 4:1.

Each market included trading in the same set of 17 securities in both markets. On opening day, the market contained seven emerging technology stocks. In an attempt to keep the market interesting and to reflect the frequent injection of new information and technologies into the emerging market landscape, the market was structured to have one new emerging technology initial public offering (IPO) each business day of the market. The two markets ran from March 27, 2007, through April 11, 2007, and were accessible to the participants 24/7 through the company’s intranet. Given that the markets focused on emerging technologies rather than end products, we used a market run length paralleling that of LaComb et al. [28] rather than the very short period used in Dahan et al. [15] in analyzing consumer product features. In total, the market included 17 emerging technologies, 7 at market initiation and 10 entering as IPOs. The emerging technologies represented in the securities were chosen with the goal of comprising an orthogonal set—that is, they are not competing technologies. An example of an emerging technology is a “nano body-sensoring device” capable of providing real-time physiological monitoring. While this emerging technology exists, it is not an end product. Rather, the technology might be incorporated in clothing or in a wearable device. Such a device could provide detailed information of significant importance for world-class athletics, military, or police.

When running a play money market without a market maker, it is suggested that at the start of the market, participants need to be seeded with initial investment capital, a portfolio of securities, or a combination of the two in order to start trading. There is debate on the composition of the starting portfolios and the initial cash (play money) endowment. In general, the amount of initial cash to be seeded should be predicated on the number of securities in the market, providing sufficient initial market liquidity, yet not so much as to encourage excessive speculative trading behavior. Dahan et al. [15] suggest that an amount of initial cash endowment of approximately 25 percent of the trader’s overall starting portfolio is effective.

In our experiments, we started all participants with 100 shares of each of the seven technologies (\$35,000 = 100 shares × 7 stocks × \$50 initial value of a share) and \$10,000 worth of “play money” to invest in the stocks of technologies they felt had the largest potential. For each new technology that was IPO’d (one per day) during the course of the market, each participant also automatically received 100 shares of that technology’s stock.

The I-PREFS were deployed on a software platform from Consensus Point (www. consensuspoint.com). Consensus Point implements the continuous double auction trading mechanism in which buyers and sellers continually place trading offers. A transaction occurs as soon as a buy offer is greater than (or equal to) a sell offer. At any given instance of time, there is no overlap between the highest buy offer and the lowest sell offer. Detailed instructions and appropriate training were provided to the participants. In addition to trading emerging technology stocks, the participants were also able to view historical information about the technology’s performance. In the Appendix, we provide several screenshots of the computerized market, including ones that were part of participant training and reference (1), real-time order book (2 and 3), and completed trades (4). There was no market maker and a transaction occurred when a participant accepted a bid or an offer.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

## Participation Incentives

In r el ated el ec tr onic auc tions mark ets, Bapna et al. [5, 6] demonstrated that different auction mechanisms and their associated incentives cause different bidding behavior and participation levels. In I-PREFS, design of a proper incentive mechanism is similarly a key issue. Wolfers and Zitzewitz [57] suggest that markets are powerful only if they provide proper incentives to motivate trading. Active trading allows traders to reveal more about their preferences, interact with other traders, and learn from the market that provides aggregate information. It is well known from the literature that real money rewards help induce reliable results (see [21, 22, 36, 43]).

As stated earlier, monetary rewards were not possible in our case because of a strict company policy. However, the effectiveness of nonmonetary incentives was a necessary, but not sufficient, condition in the determination of whether the company would decide to make further use of preference markets in evaluating emerging technologies. If a preference market generates little trading volume and little price movement, then the company is unable to draw conclusions about trader consensus and emerging technology rankings. Several research studies (e.g., [9, 41, 46, 57]) suggested that play money may serve to motivate participants. The argument is that play money rewards serve as recognition of accomplishments in pitting one’s mental acuity and judgment against those of other participants. From a strictly monetary viewpoint, an economist might voice the concern that if there is no performance-based dollar payout, then there is no consequence for performance or nonperformance. From our viewpoint, however, the issue is whether the play money incentive provides the perception of a reward in the sense that participants respond in ways to obtain more of the reward, albeit “an illusion of money” reward. This discussion leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Participation levels (trading activities) are higher in the presence of additional play money incentives than in the absence of such incentives.

## Dynamic Interaction

## Trader Preference Revision

One of the advantages of I-PREFS over traditional approaches is that they allow real-time market interactions among participants. Each participant is able to observe individual bids and offers together with the aggregate behavior of other participants. Individuals can learn and choose to adapt or not adapt, to revise or not revise their own preferences and market behavior. While we have no way to know with certainty a trader’s true preference (value) for a specific security, market observations do provide a means for us to estimate the preference (willingness to buy or sell) range reflected by a trader’s actions. Before trader i makes a trade, we have no information on the upper or lower bounds of the trader’s true preference. When we observe trader i buying security j for the first time (say in the mth market transaction) at a price $p _ { i j } ^ { ( m ) } = \$ 4$ , under the assumptions of rational behavior and truthful revelation, we have \$a as an estimate of the lower bound of trader i’s preference value for security j—that is, $V _ { _ { i j } } ^ { } = [ a , \nu _ { _ { i j } } ^ { u } ]$ . Similarly, when we observe trader i selling security j for the first time in the nth market transaction at ${ q _ { i j } ^ { ( n ) } = \ S b }$ , we can estimate trader i’s preference range as $V _ { _ { i j } } = [ a , b ]$ . For example, if we observe a trader first bought a security at \$30, and then sold the same security at \$40, we can estimate the trader’s preference for this security to lie in that range [29, 41]. By observing trader interaction activities in the market, we can determine whether or not traders change their preferences and, if so, in what directions and possible patterns. In the following, we define several concepts to describe the dynamic interaction process we track and analyze.

As an individual trader observes the operation of the preference market, the trader may or may not alter individual preferences. A trade by individual i is termed a “revision” trade if the trade provides us with new information on trader i’s preference—that is, a revised upper or lower bound for that trader’s true preference. Similarly, a “nonrevision” trade provides no new information, no new estimate of the upper or lower bound for the trader’s true preference range. If traders only learn from the preference market, then a “revision” trade reflects the impact of market information on trader i. A “revision” occurs for a trader when a trade indicates that the trader has fundamentally altered his or her preference. Suppose after the mth transaction, our interval estimate of trader i’s preference on security j is $V _ { _ { i j } } = [ a , b ]$ . If in the next transaction trader i makes on security j, trader i buys security j at a price that is “much higher” than a, or sells j at a price “much lower” than b, we term this “revision behavior” for trader i and refer to the buy or sell as a “revision.” If this next transaction does not meet the revision criteria, then we term the transaction a “nonrevision.”

## Trader’s Influence on Others

Trader i’s activities (bids or offers) may also have effects on other traders or on the overall market values. For example, observing a large transaction in a market or a transaction by an influential trader (e.g., Warren Buffett) may provide a signal or “call for action” to other traders. In such cases, the trade can impact or “influence” the subsequent actions of other traders who might alter their preferences. Trades that have an enduring effect on other traders are of particular interest. We classify a trade by individual i for security j as being “influential” if (1) the trade meets the condition to be classified as a revision trade, and (2) the trade appears to influence the market in the sense that a specific number of subsequent trades also meet (or exceed) the same criteria to be classified as “revision” trades. Thus, if the mth trade meets the “revision” trade criteria (see above) and the subsequent number of trades (say, 3) by individuals other than i also meet the same revision criterion as the mth trade by i, then this mth trade is classified as an influential trade of level 3. If these conditions are not met, then the mth trade is classified as a noninfluential trade. This concept of influential incorporates the requirement that the mth trade be a revision trade for individual i and that the next trades by other individuals also be revision trades in the same direction and to the same or greater extent as the mth trade.

Based on the above, we propose the following hypotheses regarding dynamic interaction process in the markets:

Hypothesis 2a: In the presence of additional play money trading incentives, there is a greater presence of “revision” transactions than when such incentives are absent.

Hypothesis 2b: In the presence of additional play money trading incentives, there is a greater presence of “influential” transactions than when such incentives are absent.

Together these hypotheses investigate issues of concern to our partner company. If during the running of an I-PREF, there is little individual revision of preferences, then it would seem preferable to just seek individual preferences rather than incur the cost and time of designing and running a preference market. In comparing the two market forms, the company was very interested in analyzing whether, in the added incentive market, the market feedback was followed by greater preference revision and, eventually, convergence. If the second hypothesis in this set is accepted, this indicates that, in the added incentive market, there is a greater presence of certain individual preference revisions affecting the market. With greater presence of such effects, the firm has more potential for identifying individuals whose knowledge (revealed in their preferences) helped fashion the market. For the company, such ability to impact the market marks such an individual as a candidate for a possible expanded role in the company regarding the evaluation of emerging technologies.

## Price Convergence and Market Outcome

I-PREFS are designed to aggregate information on topics of interest. The convergence (or divergence) of the price of a security has significant implications on the usefulness of outcomes in guiding business decisions. Price convergence indicates that market traders reached a consensus on the likelihood of success of the emerging technology, while nonconvergence indicates the absence of consensus. With additional play money incentives, we anticipate that traders will participate more and are likely to interact/ learn from the market so as to quickly reach a consensus. Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a: In the presence of additional play money trading incentives, prices converge faster than when such incentives are absent.

When the actual outcome of an event can be observed, it is relatively easy to measure the prediction accuracy of the market. In fact, many studies (e.g., [11, 35, 46, 57]) have documented that markets produce very accurate predictions in a variety of contexts. Moreover, manipulative attempts in these markets do not seem to reduce the accuracy of the prediction as markets are robust enough to self-correct themselves [20]. However, when the market outcomes are preference rankings that cannot be compared in the short run to actual event outcomes, what ways might exist to directly measure the performance of such a market?

When we chose what technologies to be evaluated in the markets, we purposely included six specific technologies that had very recently been evaluated and ranked using the company’s current evaluation process involving a panel of experts. By including these six technologies, we are able to benchmark the outcome (in terms of preference rankings of emerging technologies) from I-PREFS against those obtained by the group of technology experts (see also [15] for examples of such comparison in new product design settings). The consistency of the rank ordering of technologies at the market close is of significant interest to the company because this would potentially affect its strategic decision choices in terms of what technologies it would choose to move forward. This leads us to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3b: The market outcomes from a preference-ranking market are consistent with the assessment of an expert panel.

To test Hypotheses 2a and 2b, we need to first operationalize “revision” and “influential.” We utilize two measures for “revision.” Consider trader i who at time t completes a trade on security j at price $p _ { i j t } .$ Under the “revision-1” definition, trader i’s next transaction on security j (if there is one) is a revision if that transaction occurs at a price at least 5 percent different from ${ { p } _ { i j t } }$ . Under definition “revision-2,” a revision occurs if the subsequent price is not equal to $p _ { i j t }$ . We performed a Wilcoxon test comparing the two markets for each of the two definitions of “revision.” Under both definitions, traders revised their bids/offers more frequently in the presence of play money incentives than in the control market where the extra incentive is absent. While the results are significant at an α level of approximately 10.4 percent under “revision-1,” the differences are significant at the level of 1.3 percent for “revision-2.” This is consistent with Hypothesis 2a.

To test Hypothesis 3a, we need to define measures on price convergence. We utilized two alternative types of convergence measures as follows:

1. z% Convergence—price convergence occurs at time t for security j if t is the earliest time where every transaction on security j after time t occurs at a price within z% of the price of j at time t, and

2. x\$ Convergence—price convergence occurs at time t for security j if t is the earliest time where every transaction on security j after time t occurs at a price within x dollars (x\$) of the price of j at time t.

The z% Convergence is similar to a stock pricing range which is viewed as “normal fluctuation.” Various stock analyst services provide such ranges and identify the boundaries as “breakout prices.” StockCharts.com offers a detailed illustration of support and resistance prices, making reference to establishment of the boundaries at ±1/8th of a certain price. We chose a ±13 percent (approximately 1/8th) for z% Convergence. For x\$ Convergence, we report the results for a value of \$4 (that is, for 4\$ Convergence). We tried a variety of dollar values and, though the results were similar, the use of \$4 provided the cleanest breaks between convergence and nonconvergence.

Tables 4 and 5 summarize the results for convergence (numbers in parentheses refer to just those securities that converged in both markets). In the additional incentive market, prices of 14 of the total of 17 stocks converge while prices for 13 of the 17 converge in the control market. In our comparisons (Table 4), we used normalized times (time period normalized to 1—time period for a stock j runs from the day where j is introduced into the market until the final day of the market). The average normalized time to convergence is 36.4 (that is, approximately 36.4 percent of the trading period elapsed) for the additional incentive market compared to an average normalized time of 43.0 for the control market. When comparing only stocks whose prices converge in both markets (11 of 17 stocks), the normalized time to convergence is 41.0 for the additional incentive market compared to 43.7 for the control market. While these results are consistent with Hypothesis 3a, mean comparison tests (assuming unequal variances) did not yield statistically significant differences.

Although we initially focused on comparing the speed of price convergence in the two markets, our analysis yielded additional interesting insights. As discussed earlier, the purpose of the preference ranking market was to provide information on relative preferences for emerging technologies. The market owner was seeking information to help identify and select “top performance” technologies from among the set of 17 considered. We offer two suggestions when considering the market outcomes. First, if the price for a security does not converge, this indicates that the final price has not yet reached “reliability.” In other words, market volatility suggests that we should not draw inferences regarding technologies whose price has not converged during the trading period. Second, the more closely the convergent prices are bunched, the less information is provided to the market owner; that is, separation in convergent prices provides larger differences in the signal to the market owner about the relative preferences for the different technologies.

Whether comparisons are based on all stocks whose prices converge in either market or on those that converge in both markets, the standard deviation of the convergent prices was approximately twice as high in the additional incentive market than in the control market (25.4 versus 11.9 and 27.9 versus 12.4). This indicates that the additional incentive market provides much better delineation in terms of technology rankings.

The results for 4\$ Convergence are reported in Table 5 (again, numbers in parentheses refer to those obtained for securities whose prices converged in both markets). In the additional incentive market, prices of 12 of the 17 stocks converge, while prices for 11 of the 17 converge in the control market. When comparing all stocks that converge in either market (Table 5), the normalized time to convergence is 52.5 for the incentive market compared to 67.9 for the control market. When comparing only stocks whose prices converge in both markets (9 of 17 stocks), the normalized time to convergence is 57.1 for the incentive market compared to 70.6 for the control market. As with the z% Convergence, this evidence is also consistent with Hypothesis 3a. Here, however, we did find statistically significant differences, albeit at only the 0.1 level, when comparing all stocks converging. When comparing only those converging in both markets, we did not find statistically significant differences. We note that the four mean comparison tests (two each for 4\$ and 13%) yielded only one statistically significant outcome and that at a 0.1 level. We have discussed this issue with our partner company and suggested carefully monitoring outcomes as the thickness (number of technologies analyzed) of the markets analyzed increases.

We hypothesized that the market outcomes from a preference market which is aimed to aggregate public’s preference ranking should be consistent with the ranking evaluation of an expert panel. Our analyses up to this point have focused on comparing behaviors and security convergences across the two markets. We now turn to benchmarking the outcomes from the two markets by comparing them to the rankings for six of the technologies previously obtained from an expert panel. This panel was set up separately by the Fortune 5 company—we were not involved. The rankings from this panel were made available to us by the company for comparison purposes with the preference market outcomes. The methodology involved in the panel assessment combines both surveys and brainstorming sessions. Each of the six technologies was scored on a set of questions designed using Porter’s “5 forces model” [38] and then further evaluated along two dimensions—usability and appropriateness of the technology. Table 6 provides the three different rankings for the six early stage emerging technologies. One ranking is from the expert panel while the other two rankings are based on the final market prices from the two I-PREFS. When a cell is boldface, this indicates that the final price was a convergent price in the market. All other values indicate that the price was not a convergent price in the indicated market. Four of the prices are convergent prices in both markets while two are final (nonconvergent) prices. The additional incentive market had one tie, for which we assigned the average of the two ordered rank values (e.g., (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5).

It can be seen from Table 6 that the two markets yielded quite different outcomes in terms of the ranking of technologies. In addition, the additional incentive market rankings track the expert panel rankings much more closely than is the case for the control market rankings. We also performed a correlation analysis for the rankings of technologies among the three sources. The correlation between the rankings of the additional incentive market and the expert panel is 0.899 while that between the control market and the expert panel is –0.143. Hence, Hypothesis 3b is supported for the additional incentive market. While this evidence is hardly strong enough to establish any sort of causal relationship that appropriate participant incentives increase market accuracy, the results do provide us encouraging indications for use of the added incentive approach (even in the simple form of extra play money).

## Conclusions

Infor mation mark ets [2] have been establ ished for some time and empirical evidence indicates that such markets have yielded quite accurate predictions. In some situations, however, the information of interest is preferences and preference mapping. This paper has focused on the development and use of preference markets where a Fortune 5 company sought preference details and rankings on early stage emerging technologies. Our I-PREF was structured as a stock market with participants trading virtual securities. One major difference between I-PREFS and traditional information markets is that there is typically no easily measurable (at least in the near term) outcome against which the market results can be compared. In our case, however, the market owner (the Fortune 5 company) had previously compiled the rankings (using surveys and focus groups) on six of the technologies by a panel of experts the company had carefully selected. This provided a benchmark for us to consider the performance of the preference market.

Although an initial study, our analysis was structured and completed with a Fortune 5 partner company for which early stage emerging technology evaluation and selection are critically important recurring problems. The contributions include (1) a real-world application for a Fortune 5 company; (2) the development and consideration of two new metrics for price convergence, two new metrics for participant bid/offer revisions, and four new metrics for participant influential bids/offers; and (3) the capability to compare the preference market outcomes to the standards of a company-selected panel of experts. Perhaps most important, our partner company viewed the study results as sufficient to warrant expanded investigation and use of I-PREFS in their early stage emerging technology screening.

This study has several limitations. We consider only one very specific form of play money incentive. Given the initial encouraging results, our future research will investigate the impact of sliding incentive scales. The early stage emerging technologies evaluated were selected by the company rather than through a stratified approach where we might carefully analyze the relationships between technology characteristics and characteristics of the preference outcomes and participant behaviors. In addition, our initial markets considered just 17 early stage emerging technologies. While I-PREFS can be easily scaled up to handle significantly more technologies, we must carefully analyze how well the markets function as the scale increases and whether there are identifiable points where I-PREFS struggle or fail.

As noted earlier, this study is a first step in studying the usefulness of I-PREFS for early stage technology selection and evaluation. There are many issues that remain to be examined. For example, in addition to alternative incentive mechanisms (including sliding scales), we can examine what other design properties influence market performance and whether market participants consistently reveal their true preferences. Finally, a new form of online (voting) community is shaping up in several large companies (such as Dell and Starbucks) that utilize the collective knowledge of customers to rank new ideas and improve products/services. Although not as structured or controlled as I-PREFS, one can investigate whether these communities provide management with useful information. Further issues include whether such information provides a useful supplement to the information provided by I-PREFS. As companies face continuing decision problems in this arena, they seek tools and processes to assist them in making more successful choices. Our initial work suggests that I-PREFS may help in these endeavors.
