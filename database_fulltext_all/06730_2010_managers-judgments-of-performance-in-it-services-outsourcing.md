Abstr act: An important task for managers in information technology (IT) service settings is the judgment of service performance. The complex and intangible nature of IT services, however, renders this task especially difficult. We use a sample of 85 outsourced software development projects to test for the presence of the “input bias,” which is defined as the systematic misuse of nondiagnostic input information in forming managerial judgments of outcomes. The service outcome we examine is process performance. The diagnostic inputs are given by objective performance metrics based on the final cost and duration of completed projects, whereas the nondiagnostic inputs are risk anticipations formed by managers prior to the start of the project. We find strong evidence of the input bias, which leads managers’ subjective assessments to diverge considerably from objective outcomes, and that it is moderated by contract type. Our study contributes to better service management by improving our understanding of managers’ judgments of service performance and how these judgments are formed.

Key wor ds and phr ases: decision-making biases, field studies, input bias, IT services outsourcing, judgment, regression analysis, service science.

Subjectiv e assessments ar e commonly used in a variety of settings to provide information about outcomes that are difficult or expensive to measure objectively, such as job satisfaction [66], employee performance [9], auditors’ performance [22], service quality [20], and teaching ratings [14]. Similarly, in many service settings such as information technology (IT) outsourcing engagements, the complex, intangible, and heterogeneous nature of the (software) service necessitates the use of managerial judgments of performance because objective decision criteria may not be easily available in a reasonable manner [8, 41]. As a result, judgments of service performance are routinely elicited from managers under the assumption that managerial estimates of performance are consistent with objective outcome criteria. These judgments then often form fundamental inputs into organizational functions such as providing project status updates to senior executives [57] and informing decisions on resource allocation, budget revision, and staff hiring, among others [59]. Given the ubiquitous use of subjective assessments in judging outcomes, recent work in the services operations area has focused on the development of behavioral scales that can be used to more appropriately elicit performance judgments from managers on different aspects of service provisioning [20, 53, 55].

The use of managerial judgments of service outcomes, while no doubt useful and important, also raises the significant issue of bias inherent in judgment tasks. The extensive work on cognitive biases in the psychology literature and existing laboratory evidence commonly point to the inadequacies in subjects’ abilities to form accurate judgments [21]. Thus, before we continue to use managerial judgments in IT service tasks, it is useful to evaluate how the judgment process of managers in the field is influenced by commonly occurring factors in service settings. Among several factors cited in previous works as contributing to biases in subjective judgments, we focus specifically on the effects of input information. As a determinant of outcome assessments, the nature of inputs used in the judgment process can decisively influence the decision maker’s judgment. Specifically, we focus on examining the role of diagnostic and nondiagnostic input information on performance judgments. We formally define diagnostic inputs as the set of data that a decision maker should predicate his or her judgments on because it helps discriminate between outcomes of interest. These inputs therefore form the most appropriate and normative information to be used in the judgment task [44, 51]. Nondiagnostic information, by contrast, includes information that may be available to the decision maker but does not represent the most accurate, timely, or suitable input into the judgment task.<sup>1</sup> This may be because the information is irrelevant for the task at hand as it does not differentiate between outcomes. Alternatively, the input information is unsuitable or no longer current due to the availability of more comprehensive or accurate information at the time of judgment. In particular, our objective in this paper then is to understand how subjective assessments of performance are made in IT service contexts and, more specifically, the extent to which potentially nondiagnostic information may influence managerial judgments even in the presence of diagnostic information.

One such judgment bias, the “input bias,” is particularly relevant to managerial judgments of the performance of service projects. Input bias is defined as the systematic misuse of input information in making judgments of outcomes [11]. Subjects in experimental settings have been observed to rely on irrelevant or nondiagnostic input factors in making outcome judgments even though the relationship between the input and the resulting outcome was ambiguous. The input bias therefore produces judg ments that may be inconsistent with objective outcomes. Chinander and Schweitzer [11] refer to such effects as nonnormative because normative decision theory maintains that judgments should be influenced only by the most diagnostic information available at the time of judgment (see also [44]).

The existing evidence on the impact and influence of nondiagnostic inputs on judgments is mainly derived from laboratory settings and based on relatively simplistic judgment tasks. In contrast, we investigate the validity of managerial judgments in the field, thereby helping add realism and external validity to the findings. Our study is set within the domain of IT outsourcing services, which is considered one of the leading avenues for research in services operations [12, 54]. While “IT outsourcing” encompasses several different strands of economic activity [15], we focus on software development outsourcing (SDO), which is defined more formally in the next section. SDO projects possess several of the features associated with complex service-provisioning contexts [49] that make the assessment of many outcomes especially difficult. Further, because input measures are more easily manipulated or misrepresented in such complex settings, the input bias is likely to affect IT managerial judgments and lead to consequences that may be severe and costly for both vendor and client firms. For example, the judgment of outcomes such as software/service quality and customer satisfaction are crucial for the vendor and may affect its decisions related to continued interactions with clients and future contracting with other parties. Thus, identifying the effects of judgment biases in these tasks is critical if we are to better manage IT services engagements and provide managers with the tools to recognize and possibly account for the effects of such biases.

In one of the first studies to examine the input bias in the SDO domain, we identify and select an SDO service outcome that is relatively amenable to objective measurement— namely, process performance. Process performance is defined as the extent to which the development team is able to manage costs and duration in a project [50, 53, 65] and is commonly associated with size-adjusted total cost and duration metrics on a project. The central strategy we use in identifying the input bias in this paper is to contrast objective measures of process performance with subjective managerial judgments of process performance obtained from managers of recently completed software projects. We argue that the objective indicators of process performance available to managers at the end of the project form the most diagnostic information to the judgment of process performance while other data available to the manager or formed during contracting (ex ante) are nondiagnostic. Our set of nondiagnostic inputs is given by managers’ ex ante anticipations of three risk factors studied extensively in the information systems (IS) literature—requirements instability [16, 29], technological complexity [50, 56], and lack of skilled personnel [6, 18]. Given the salience and potential negative effects of these risks, project managers are likely to form and maintain ex ante anticipations of them at the contracting stage. While some of these risks may materialize during the development of the project, the anticipations are rendered nondiagnostic at the time of judgment of performance (postcompletion of the project), because the effects of the risks are reflected accurately in diagnostic inputs such as objective process performance data. To the extent that managers’ judgments of process performance continue to be affected by nondiagnostic input factors even in the presence of diagnostic input data, managers’ judgments are influenced by the input bias.

We further investigate whether the input bias is moderated by the salience of the input project risks described above. We do so by studying the effect of contract type used to govern the project. If the input bias were indeed driven by the salience of nondiagnostic input information, we could expect to observe a reduction (increase) in the bias for managerial judgments provided on projects governed by contracts that lower (raise) the exposure to risk faced by the outsourcing vendor firm.

We test our hypotheses using data obtained from a set of 85 completed SDO projects undertaken by an IT outsourcing vendor for multiple clients. Our analysis uncovers strong evidence for the input bias in the field. We find that while managers use diagnostic information in forming performance judgments, they are concomitantly prone to the use of nondiagnostic information that could lead to systematic bias in their judgments of performance. We also observe that this effect is moderated by contract type. Our analysis thus suggests that while subjective assessment schemes may be valuable as a methodology to elicit performance judgments, their use should be combined with recognition of the presence of managerial biases. The presence of the input bias in a relatively simple judgment task of process performance, where objective counterparts are more readily available, suggests that biases may be more prevalent for other outcomes, such as quality and customer satisfaction, that are less easily mapped to objective metrics.

Our results provide several implications for service science. As firms increasingly turn to services and service-oriented approaches to maintain agility and flexibility in their operations, managers must learn to grapple with new complexities surrounding the melding of information, technology, and strategy [5]. This new focus also brings to the fore the crucial need for better decision making, with the ability to obtain and use unbiased outcome judgments especially critical for the management of IT services. In particular, our study contributes to the literature in IT services and decision making along three distinct dimensions. First, ours is one of the earliest papers in IS to examine the extent to which subjective managerial judgments of performance in IT service settings are influenced by diagnostic and nondiagnostic input information. Second, our study provides external validity to the input bias, previously examined under controlled settings, using managerial judgments of performance obtained from the field under a reasonable facsimile of an experimental setup. Employing a combination of survey and archival data, we show that the input bias is not a phenomenon limited to the lab, and exists among professionals with several years of task experience even for relatively simple outcome measures. Third, the context in which we study the input bias is representative of many technology-enabled service operations that face requirements, technological, and human resource–related risks. The results we obtain may therefore have important ramifications in the broader domain of IT services and operations.

## Research Context

## Software Development Outsourcing

Softwar e dev elopment outsour cing is a hig hly technical and complex undertaking, often consisting of several interconnected and interleaved tasks, including software research, detailed design, code development, testing, documentation, and support [60]. Often, these projects are contracted out to a service provider (vendor) on a turnkey basis and include significant software development and software service components (such as maintenance of existing software or reengineering parts of an existing application) [25]. While software outsourcing provides firms with multiple strategic benefits, it remains a high-stakes undertaking for the firms involved, with a majority of projects failing to meet cost, schedule, or quality expectations [63].

Our focus within the domain of SDO projects lies in understanding how vendor managers’ subjective judgments of performance may be influenced by diagnostic and nondiagnostic input factors. Relevant to our study are certain characteristics of the context that make managerial judgment especially challenging. First, the final offerings of SDO projects are intangible, typically combining software and value-added services. The service, rather than the product, focus of the outsourcing industry complicates the judgment of performance by making the relationship between inputs and outputs more ambiguous [49]. Second, SDO projects tend to be customized based on client requirements, rendering the final product heterogeneous and harder to compare across different service experiences. Third, SDO projects are characterized by numerous risks such as high requirements volatility, fast-paced technology change, and resource uncertainties that complicate the achievement of predetermined cost and schedule objectives [41]. These factors are likely to challenge even seasoned vendor managers in accurately separating the effects of nondiagnostic inputs from diagnostic inputs when assessing service outcomes. Thus, as a starting point for this research, we focus our analysis on service outcomes that are relatively easier to assess from a subjective viewpoint while still maintaining the complexity and ambiguity that surround managers in their daily tasks. We do so by eliciting managers’ judgments of process performance of recently completed SDO projects.

While prior literature that addresses the role of biases in the IT outsourcing context is sparse, there exists some research in the domain of software development and a larger body of work in decision support systems (DSS) that address similar questions. Our interest here is in software development as outsourced services and the input bias, both of which are relatively recently researched in the academic literature. Thus, we focus our review on the study of biases and judgment in the domain most relevant to our current work—software development—and refer the interested reader to Eom [17] for a review of DSS research.

Keil et al. [37] and Snow and Keil [60] study managers’ reporting of the intermediate status of software projects and find that biases such as framing, selective perception, and illusion of control influence managers’ escalation of commitment to failing software projects. Other researchers have examined the impact of irrelevant and misleading information in estimating development effort (e.g., [34]) and schedules (e.g., [43]). The majority of this work in software development has focused on examining the quality of software project managers’ estimates of the effects of probabilistic risks on outcomes prior to the start of a project (see [39] for a review). In contrast, we study vendor project managers’ assessments of completed project performance and identify multiple input factors that may cause subjective judgments to vary from objective estimates.

## Timeline of Managerial Judgments

Before we proceed to define the key constructs in our study, we describe the sequence of managerial judgments as it pertains to our research (see Figure 1). Once the vendor has procured an SDO project with a client, and prior to the start of the project, the responsible vendor business unit manager may form judgments of the risks he or she expects to challenge the project during its life cycle. We refer to these a priori assessments as anticipated risk factors. The contract used to govern the project further affects the salience of project risks and may therefore be expected to influence the extent to which managers judge risks to be detrimental to project performance. Finally, on completion of the project and termination of the contractual obligation, final cost and duration data are available to the managers. At this point, vendor managers provide their subjective assessment of the performance of the completed project.

Following our primary interest in studying the input bias, we define factors that are likely to influence managers’ subjective performance judgments by serving as inputs into managers’ judgment processes. The first set of inputs is given by objective process performance metrics that are available postcompletion of the project. A second set of inputs includes managers’ a priori anticipations of the risks they expect to face during the project life cycle. Finally, we consider contract type as a moderator of the perceived salience of anticipated project risks in managers’ judgments.

## Process Performance

Process performance in an SDO project is defined as the vendor’s demonstrated ability to develop and deliver the contracted outputs within a given budget and time frame. It is, therefore, an outcome measure of the extent to which the software development process in a project is efficiently optimized around development cost and duration [29, 40]. Thus, there are two objective project metrics that are most often associated with a measure of process performance [29]. The first is the total cost of the development of the project. The second is given by the duration of the project—that is, the calendar days between the start and the end of the project. These objective metrics are then normalized by the size of the project to obtain size-adjusted measures of process performance. Projects with lower unit costs and shorter unit development times, in general, denote higher efficiency or process performance [53, 65]. We subsequently define subjective performance (SP) as the vendor project managers’ judgment of the extent to which a project was completed with control over cost and duration figures, while adhering to preferred process standards.<sup>2</sup> The above-described two measures of objective performance (OP) form critical inputs into managers’ judgments of process performance (or SP) elicited upon completion of the project and availability of OP.

## Anticipated Risk Factors in SDO Projects

Prior research in software outsourcing has identified several salient risks in the life cycle of an SDO project. We identify three such risks—requirements instability, technological complexity, and lack of trained personnel for the project—which were chosen for several reasons. First, this set of risks encompasses several key challenges known to plague software projects, and these risks have been demonstrated in extant literature to affect project costs and schedules in outsourced projects [6, 53]. Second, these risks occur commonly enough in SDO projects, thereby increasing the likelihood that managers form and retain early expectations or a priori anticipations of the extent to which the project will be affected by these risks. Finally, using these three risks helps ensure that there is adequate variance and coverage in our sample. We then obtain managers’ a priori anticipations of the above-defined risks, formed at the time of contracting and prior to the start of the project, and use them as the second set of inputs in our analyses of managers’ judgments of process performance. We note that this set of input risks is by no means exhaustive; other sources of information may be used in the model as well. Nevertheless, the three risks form a good starting point for the study of input bias in an IT service context. We describe each in greater detail next.

## Requirements Instability

Requirements instability is defined as the ambiguity associated with the process of converting vague and changing client needs into the final software output [24]. In the context of software outsourcing, where business relationships are defined between a client firm and its geographically remote vendor partner, there is a greater need for constant developer–user interaction [48]. Frequent design changes, interspersed with back-and-forth negotiations, increase the requirements volatility associated with a project. Inadequate understanding of the business requirements may cause the vendor team to revisit the drawing board several times. These problems often leave the vendor manager unable to correctly identify the project’s input requirements in terms of resources and employees and also the processes needed to convert specifications into outputs [8]. The materialization of this risk factor has been shown to strongly affect project costs and duration in existing research [16, 29, 38]. Anticipated requirements instability therefore refers to the extent to which the manager estimated that the project would suffer from this pathology during the development life cycle of the project.

## Technological Complexity

Most vendors’ project portfolio typically includes a mix of maintenance and reengineering (or porting) types of projects as well as a varied set of complex applications development and management and product implementation tasks [10]. As SDO vendors, firms also need to maintain expertise in the technology and business infrastructure that makes software development for multiple clients viable, such as systems integration capabilities, network infrastructure management, and IT consulting skills. While the presence of many clients and different projects allows vendors to achieve economies of scale and scope, this double-edged sword also necessitates that the vendor teams be proficient in the use of new sophisticated technologies as and when needed for software development. Juggling diverse projects requires the vendor firm to be technologically familiar and competent to provide services across a wide range of platforms and domains [3]. The more technologically sophisticated the required hardware and software infrastructure for the project, the greater are the risks of technical problems developing during the life cycle of the project [50, 56, 58]. Materialization of such technology-related difficulties has been shown to adversely influence project costs and duration in prior software development work [8, 53]. Anticipated technological complexity risk therefore measures the extent to which the manager estimated that the technological environment would be a source of problems during the project.

## Lack of Trained Personnel

Insufficient availability of skilled and trained employees for the project is often a significant problem in most IT outsourcing arrangements [41]. The presence of knowledgeable and capable team members is vital in SDO and has been shown to enhance performance [18]. Thus, a key factor in the management of software projects is the ability of the manager to handle delays in the hiring and assimilation of staff [57]. Lack of expertise has been classified as a source of software development risk in prior studies [7], and the persistent nature of employee turnover in the software industry makes this phenomenon crucially important [2]. Anticipated trained personnel risk refers to the extent to which the manager estimated that the software development process would suffer from challenges related to the inadequate availability of skilled team members and expertise.

## Contract Type

Finally, we consider the effect of contract type, which may systematically affect the vendor’s level of risk exposure in a project. In contrast to in-house development, outsourcing relationships employ a contractual mechanism to create appropriate incentive alignment between the client and the vendor. The contract serves as an ex ante instrument to apportion and manage development risks between contracting parties in an outsourcing project [4, 36]. Contracts used in outsourced software development are broadly classified as either fixed price or time and materials [4]. In a fixed price contract, the vendor bears the majority of the risk, whereas in a time and materials contract, it is the client that bears the risk of cost and schedule overruns. Because the vendor’s risk exposure in a project is contingent on the contract type chosen, it is not surprising that past work has demonstrated the impact of contract type on project outcomes [24]. In our study, we similarly expect that contract type would moderate the salience of anticipated risks in managers’ judgments of performance.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Our r esear ch model, depicted in Figure 2, provides a suitable map for testing the presence of the input bias in managers’ performance judgments of SDO projects. When assessing process performance after the project has been completed, it is reasonable to expect that managers would use the objective cost and duration metrics, along with project size information, to form their judgments. Size-adjusted cost and duration figures comprehensively take into account the effects of materialized risks and benefits and represent the most diagnostic information on project performance available to managers at the time of providing judgments. Because this input information is both available to the vendor project managers and accurate, we hypothesize that these diagnostic inputs form an appropriate basis for managers’ perceptions of process performance. As a baseline, we present our first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (Diagnostic Inputs Hypothesis): Managerial judgment of project process performance is influenced by diagnostic inputs such as size-adjusted project cost and duration.

We describe the input bias in greater detail next and derive our main research hypotheses.

## Input Bias

In many domains, the effects of input factors are critical and determine the outcomes achieved. For example, the nature of the raw materials used to manufacture finished goods will determine the quality of the finished product. In other instances, while the quality of inputs has an effect on outcomes, the association may not be direct or consistent. Consider the use of research and development (R&D) expenditures as a measure of the innovativeness of the firm. Although R&D spending is a critical input, greater spending does not unambiguously imply greater innovativeness. The disassociation of outcomes to inputs is further aggravated in cases where the assessment of the effects of inputs themselves is inaccurate.

Chinander and Schweitzer [11] demonstrate that laboratory subjects are prone to automatically, often erroneously, associate more input with better quality, leading to input bias. In four experimental studies, the authors found that subjects were strongly inclined to treat information such as greater time spent in preparing a presentation and higher cost of machinery used to prepare fudge as being correlated with better quality of presentation and taste of fudge, respectively. However, the former two pieces of information were irrelevant to the quality of outcomes in their respective scenarios. Surprisingly, subjects tended to associate irrelevant inputs with higher quality even when they were aware that such relationships were untenable, and other more relevant input information was available. In their study, Chinander and Schweitzer [11] found that subjects continued to perceive a positive association between time spent (cost of machinery) and outcome quality, even after viewing the presentation (tasting the fudge), which is the more relevant input. In light of these observations, Chinander and Schweitzer define input bias as the systematic misuse of input information in judgment tasks.

In other research using real auditors, judgments of fraud and material misstatements were found to be different when made in the presence of both diagnostic and nondiagnostic information than when only diagnostic information was present [27, 31]. The underlying reasoning provided for such an effect suggests that managers who use only diagnostic information relied on similarity-based inference judgments whereby they assessed the outcome of the current case based on the outcomes of other cases with a similar endowment of diagnostic inputs [27]. The presence of nondiagnostic inputs then served to reduce this similarity, leading to dissimilar judgments to the first case.

Although input bias has been documented in the lab, it has not been studied in a nonexperimental setting. Yet, since managers in organizational settings routinely form and utilize subjective assessments in their day-to-day tasks, there is significant value in extending prior lab-based work to the field. The results from a study such as ours would potentially help shed light on the extent and nature of the input bias faced by managers and on factors that aggravate or mitigate the bias. Several characteristics prevalent in the field, however, make this a challenging task. An important consideration is that whereas diagnostic and nondiagnostic inputs can be separated and their effects individually studied in the laboratory, managers in the field are typically subject to the aggregate effects of the two types of inputs. Identification of the input bias in the field therefore necessitates that we first obtain subjective judgments for which there exist diagnostic inputs that are relatively separable from nondiagnostic inputs. Second, the diagnostic inputs must be available (known) to the manager or decision maker at the time of providing judgments. In such cases, we would expect (rational) managers to base their judgments on the set of inputs that are most diagnostic of the outcome they are subjectively assessing. The effect of the input bias would be discerned when managers are observed to also rely on information present in the nondiagnostic inputs.

Keeping these considerations in mind, we devise the following test for the input bias. In the present study, managerial judgments of process performance are elicited after the project is completed. As discussed in the Diagnostic Inputs Hypothesis (H1), the OP metrics—size-adjusted cost and duration—are diagnostic inputs that accurately represent the process performance of the project. These metrics essentially reflect the extent to which project management practices were successful in managing the risks faced during the life cycle of the project. The negative effects caused by the materialization of the risks we study (requirements instability, technological complexity, and inadequacy of trained personnel), if any, are thus more appropriately reflected in the OP measures. By contrast, the ex ante anticipations of the same risk factors have limited relevance to the manager’s judgments because they are, at best, noisy indicators of the extent of actual risk materialization in the project. The anticipated risks therefore provide no new information for the judgment task and are rendered nondiagnostic of a project’s process performance in the presence of diagnostic OP metrics. Within our field setting, in the presence of the OP metrics, the input risk factors should be discounted by project managers, and the judgments of process performance should be based on OP to a significant extent. The input bias is thus identified when managerial assessments of completed project performance continue to be affected by the nondiagnostic inputs, even in the presence of diagnostic OP metrics. In expectation of the input bias, we propose that

Hypothesis 2 (Anticipated Input Risk Factors Hypothesis): Managerial judgment of process performance is influenced by the anticipated input risk factors even in the presence of more diagnostic objective process performance (OP) metrics.

## Effect of Contract Type on Input Bias

Thus far, we have argued for the presence of input bias arising from the influence of anticipated risk factors serving as inputs to managers’ judgments of process performance. We now examine a condition under which the input bias may be moderated—the level of vendor accountability in the project. Prior research has found that in an attempt to provide judgments that are defensible and justifiable, decision makers in more “accountable” situations were likely to fall prey to cognitive biases. One reason for this finding is that accountability increased subjects’ motivation, but not their ability, to process more information (e.g., [27, 42, 62]). Subjects’ attempts at complex thinking therefore increased the size and salience of the nondiagnostic information considered and subsequently led to biased assessments. This overemphasis on nondiagnostic inputs under accountability leads to different judgments than when only diagnostic inputs are used to judge outcomes [27, 62]. Following these prior findings, Chinander and Schweitzer [11] suggest that the input bias may also be exacerbated under scenarios of greater accountability.

In SDO projects, the contract type used to govern the project is a key determinant of the level of accountability related to project outcomes. Through risk-sharing arrangements, the contract changes the risk exposure, and therefore the accountability, that a vendor manager faces in a project. Under a fixed price contract, the materialization of risks, and resulting cost and duration overruns, if any, directly influence the vendor’s efficiency and profitability [18], thereby increasing the level of accountability faced by the manager. On the other hand, the risk protection accorded by the time and materials contract reduces the vendor’s exposure to the negative consequences of risks, thereby lowering, on the margin, the extent to which vendor managers are held accountable for outcomes on the project. To be clear, we do not argue that time and materials projects impose no accountability; rather, because vendor margins are more protected under time and materials contracts, they represent less accountable regimes than most fixed price contracts [36]. We therefore expect that managers of fixed price contracts are likely to exhibit stronger effects of the input bias, by assigning higher weights to the negative effects of anticipated risks, than managers of time and materials contracts. We hypothesize that

Hypothesis 3 (Impact of Nondiagnostic Inputs Hypothesis): The impact of nondiagnostic inputs on managerial judgment of project process performance will be greater under fixed price contracts than under time and materials contracts.

## Control Variables

In evaluating the effects of the materialized risks and contract type on judgments of performance, we specify four relevant control variables consistent with prior research. First, project type is used to indicate whether the project involved new software development or reworking of an existing application (such as reengineering and maintenance on existing applications). Prior research has shown that differences in project type translate into differences in exposure to uncertainties and, thus, varying levels of required effort, cost, and cycle time [24]. Second, we control for team size, which has been shown in software teams to be an important determinant of the firm’s ability to achieve process performance objectives [19, 40]. We also control for the nature of the vendor–client relationship. The vendor’s prior experience with the client measured as the number of previous projects completed for the same client is expected to have an impact on project performance [18]. For instance, when the vendor has completed several projects for a client, the vendor may be well positioned to take advantage of existing knowledge and reuse code modules to work faster and achieve higher process performance. Our last control accounts for the extent of financial risk the SDO project poses to the vendor. Projects that involve larger financial risk could lead the vendor to handle risks differently, using more sophisticated practices. Finally, we use total effort on the project as a proxy for project size, consistent with prior literature on SDO projects [24], and normalize both total costs and duration by this variable in order to contextualize OP and allow reasonable comparisons across projects.

## Research Methodology

## Research Site and Design

The data for this paper wer e collected from a leading SDO vendor headquartered in India. The firm employed around 5,000 people at the time of data collection. Its primary area of expertise is software development and maintenance of business systems. A random sample of 120 projects from all the projects completed by the firm over a two-year period (approximately 400) was identified for data collection, and relevant nonmissing data were available for 85 projects. Our sampling frame of a single vendor enables us to control for firm-level heterogeneity and allows us to focus attention on the impact of project-level inputs on managerial judgment. Our sample size, though small, is comparable to existing work that examines outsourced software projects using field studies [18, 29, 50].

Our data were obtained using a combination of questionnaire and archival records. The questionnaire served as our primary data collection instrument and was developed after discussions with the senior management at the vendor firm. The questionnaire was then pretested at the research site by members of the firm’s quality assurance group before it was administered to the project personnel. For the purpose of our analysis, primary information was required on two sets of inputs. We needed information on the anticipated risk factors that were identified during the project contracting stage and the performance judgments of completed projects. The data were collected from managers in the best position to provide this information. In the SDO context, it is often the case that technical and managerial aspects of the project are managed by different persons. The technical lead on the project is usually located at the development center, while the business manager typically handles client relationships from the marketing offices located closer to the clients. Although project information is often shared between these two entities, technical perceptions of risks and uncertainties originate at the team level while business performance issues are handled by the business manager. Therefore, in our study, we collected data on the input risk factors from the project manager, while the process performance judgments were provided by the business unit manager responsible for the client relationship. Additionally, objective data on project-level metrics such as effort, duration, costs, team size, project type, and contract type were collected from the company databases.

To replicate, to the extent possible, a quasi-experimental methodology, we took the following steps during data collection. First, we attempted to reduce the impact of recall bias by collecting data on a set of projects that had been completed within the last six months at the time of data collection. Second, in order to avoid “demand effects,” we did not explicitly provide the business managers with financial or operational data on the project they reported on.<sup>3</sup> Our assumption that managers would possess the necessary information on critical cost and schedule metrics on projects they managed was later verified during debriefing, where managers reported being conversant with these objective numbers and were able to reproduce them with high levels of accuracy. This is not surprising since prior research in software cost estimation has shown that business managers retain detailed information on their projects [47, 64].

Third, in order to avoid the common method bias, we independently obtained data on anticipated project risks from the project manager and performance judgments from the business unit managers, under the assumption that input risk factor perceptions were commonly shared by the project managers and business unit managers. Making this necessary assumption allows us to collect data from two managers closely involved with creating risk estimates (project manager) and assessing project performance (business unit manager). Prior research on software development outsourcing projects suggests that this may be valid because the project and business managers work closely on the project and share information extensively [19, 34]. On the contrary, if the level of anticipated risks diverged considerably between business unit managers and project managers, it would strengthen the case for the input bias because project managers’ risk perceptions would then be even less diagnostic to business managers judgment task in our study. Finally, during debriefing, we uncovered a high degree of agreement between the risk perceptions produced by the two sets of managers, suggesting that our assumption was reasonable.

The three items used to measure SP reflect the extent to which the completed project showed control over project costs and duration and adhered to control and audit standards [50]. We address three qualifications to this measure. First, the questionnaire items address managers’ level of control over cost and duration rather than directly measuring process performance. This ensures that managers factor in the effects of both materialization of risks and risk management in their judgment of how they fared in controlling process performance outcomes such as project costs and duration, in the same manner that OP metrics implicitly do. We assume that better control over relevant cost and duration figures also indicates better process outcomes for the project; indeed, the literature on organizational controls attests to this relationship [13, 23]. Second, our objective is not to devise an exact subjective counterpoint to normalized cost and duration data for a project; rather, we seek a subjective measure of process performance that can be used to elicit managerial judgments for which the normalized cost and duration data would serve as the most diagnostic information. Finally, while the construct from the literature uses three questionnaire items, including one pertaining to adherence to audit standards, it is plausible that alternative conceptualizations of SP may show different results. We explore these alternative conceptualizations in more detail in the Results and Discussion section.

Below, we examine the psychometric properties of the multi-item constructs of anticipated risks and SP. From a factor analysis with varimax rotation, we find that the factor structure displays good construct validity as shown in Table 2 [28]. The resulting four-factor structure explains over 75 percent of the underlying variance in the items. The items for risks loaded well on the hypothesized factors with loadings of 0.68 and above, while the cross loadings were less than 0.30, indicating that our measures had sufficient discriminant validity as well [28]. The risk factors obtained Cronbach’s alphas of 0.77 and above, thereby establishing their reliability. In the factor corresponding to SP, one questionnaire item pertaining to the adherence to audit and control standards had a loading of 0.58. As a result, the reliability of the construct is 0.67; although marginally lower than the recommended 0.70 threshold, it falls within the acceptable level of 0.60 [28]. Factors were then computed for each construct by standardizing the sum of the constituent items.<sup>4</sup>

## Data Analysis

The summary statistics and correlations among the measures are provided in Table 3. The average project in our data set required 1,005 person-days of effort and was completed in 342 days and cost INR 11.6 million. As expected, larger project size (in terms of effort) is also associated with larger project costs and duration. We note that the three anticipated risk factors are correlated with each other, with the highest co-occurrence observed between technological complexity and inadequacy of skilled personnel. However, managers’ estimates of anticipated risks are not significantly different across contract and project types. As argued earlier, we find that the risk factors are highly negatively correlated with SP but not significantly correlated with OP. Because the risks we study are expectations during contracting, it is possible that many of these do not materialize during development and, therefore, there is limited correlation with the objective process performance on the project. More importantly, this first suggests that project managers’ responses to questionnaire items on anticipated risks were not influenced by the final objective performance on the project or outcome bias. Second, this indicates that managers were not mistakenly reporting on materialized risks instead of their ex ante anticipations. Indeed, if this were the case, it would undermine the argument that these input factors are nondiagnostic. That the correlations between cost/duration (OP) and each of the risks—RI, TC, and TP—were insignificant increases our confidence in the validity of the anticipated risk measures, and provides some evidence that they are not confounded with materialized risks. We discuss these further in the Limitations section. Finally, contract type is not correlated with either SP or costs and duration, suggesting that fixed price and time and materials contracts were equally likely to perform well for this vendor, and be judged so by its managers.

We perform preliminary diagnostic checks on our data to test for any violations of the ordinary least squares (OLS) model that would bias our regression estimates [25]. We verified that the residuals in our model conform to the distributional assumptions of normality. Given the modest sample size, we conducted outlier analyses and failed to find any influential observations. Multicollinearity is potentially a problem with regression analysis containing interaction terms, especially if the sample is small. However, none of the condition indices for the variables were greater than the standard cutoff value of 15. The interaction terms used in Equation (1) were created using centered variables. Finally, heteroskedasticity, if present, would cause a downward bias in the OLS standard errors while still producing unbiased coefficients, thereby casting doubt on the significance levels obtained. The Breusch–Pagan test applied to the independent variables in the model is not rejected $( \chi ^ { 2 } \left( 1 3 \right) = 6 . 2 3 , p > 0 . 1 0 )$ , while the same test applied to the fitted values of the subjective performance measure is rejected $( \chi ^ { 2 } \left( 1 \right) = 7 . 1 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This test suggests the presence of heteroskedasticity that causes the error variances in the model to be linearly related to the fitted values. We ran robust regressions using White’s heteroskedasticity-consistent variance estimator to address this issue.

## Effect of Control Variables

The results in M1 indicate that team size is weakly significant in M1b and M1c, suggesting that projects involving larger teams were assessed as performing better, ceteris paribus. Project type is strongly significant in models M1a, M1b, and M1c, indicating that rework (reengineering and maintenance) projects (coded 1) were associated with lower subjective performance than new development projects (coded 0). Prior experience with the client positively influenced SP, while the effect of financial risk was insignificant.

## Testing the Effect of Diagnostic and Nondiagnostic Inputs

We first discuss the effects of diagnostic OP measures on managerial performance judgments (SP). The Diagnostic Inputs Hypothesis (H1) is tested by examining the coefficients of OP measures. The effects of both normalized cost and duration variables on SP are negative and significant in models M1b and M1c, indicating that projects with greater size-adjusted costs and longer size-adjusted duration are judged by vendor managers as having performed worse, all else being equal. This is consistent with our expectation that managers’ process performance judgments are influenced by diagnostic OP metrics, thereby supporting Hypothesis 1. This result provides us with significant face validity and allows us to progress to the actual test of the input bias. Next we examine the impact of the nondiagnostic inputs—that is, the anticipated risk factors. The regression coefficients for RI, TC, and TP show consistency in sign and significance across models M1b and M1c, indicating that anticipated risks have significant negative effects on SP. Moreover, the nondiagnostic inputs continue to influence managerial judgment even when more objective and diagnostic information is available, thereby providing strong support for the input bias Hypothesis 2 (the Anticipated Input Risk Factors Hypothesis).

## Moderating Effects of Contract Type

Moderation effects are tested through the change in $R ^ { 2 }$ upon the addition of interaction terms to the direct effects model. Contract type enters our model in M1a, but does not have a direct effect on SP, and continues to be insignificant in M1c with somewhat marginal significance in M1b. Of greater interest to us are the coefficients of the interaction terms, $\mathrm { R I ^ { * } }$ C ontract, $\mathrm { T C } ^ { \ast }$ C ontract, and $\mathrm { T P ^ { * } }$ C ontract. In M1c, the change in $R ^ { 2 }$ upon addition of the block of interaction variables is found to be significant $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and all three interaction terms between the nondiagnostic inputs and contract type are statistically significant. Broadly, this suggests that while contract type did not result in differences in managerial judgments of process performance, it moderated managers’ judgments of the effects of anticipated project risks. Our results show that the effects of anticipated technological complexity and inadequacy of trained personnel are more negative on SP under fixed price contracts, which is consistent with the Impact of Nondiagnostic Inputs Hypothesis (H3). The effect of requirements instability is, however, in the opposite direction, with stronger negative effects on SP found in time and materials than in fixed price contracts. Thus, Hypothesis 3 is only partially supported.

The finding related to requirements instability (RI \* C ontract) runs counter to the hypothesized direction. One possible explanation can be attributed to the dynamics of time and materials contracts. In time and materials contracts, because the client is willing to bear a larger share of the development risk, there is greater control exercised by the client on the development process [18, 41]. A direct result of these dynamics is that requirements tend to change more often based on the client’s business needs. Common wisdom in SDO, as reported by practitioners [46] and researchers [18, 36], also indicates that time and materials contracts tend to be associated with projects characterized by higher requirements uncertainty. Therefore, our result suggests that the fluctuation of requirements assumes primacy in the minds of project managers in time and materials projects and overwhelms the relatively lower accountability that vendor managers face in such contracts. While this reasoning is preliminary, future work is required to clearly establish why this may be the case.

In summary, model M1c shows significant support for the input bias arguments in managerial assessments of completed project performance. We further show that the influence of the input bias is moderated by the level of accountability introduced by contract type. Because a field study does not accord us the perfect experimental setup possible in a lab, it is incumbent upon us to test our models for possible violations to both econometric assumptions and measurement issues that may affect the underlying result. We explore these issues in some detail using the following tests to establish the robustness of our results.

## Accounting for Violations to Independence

A possible concern that arises in models M1a, M1b, and M1c is the potential violation to the independence of observations that might exist in our data because project managers often oversee more than one project. We consider additionally complex models to account and test for this violation. If vendor business managers who provided us with subjective performance judgments managed more than one project in our sample, their responses to SP may not be independent. On further examination, we found that the data on SP for the 85 projects in our sample were provided by 27 vendor business managers. A simple methodology to account for this nonindependence of observations from the same manager is to use a pooled OLS model with cluster standard errors, which is reported as model M2 in Table 4. The cluster standard errors are robust to correlations among observations (SP) of the same manager across projects and provide more conservative standard errors. As observed in M2, due to the inflated standard errors, the interaction of contract and TC reduces in significance while the other results remain consistent. A more rigorous way to capture manager effects is to postulate fixed and random effects models and to estimate these models while describing their assumptions in detail. We describe these tests and their results in more detail in Appendix A and note here that the results are largely consistent with models M1 and M2 in Table 4.

## Accounting for Differences in the Correspondence Between SP and OP

In model M3, we address the possible discrepancy that may arise given that we measure OP in absolute terms while SP is measured conceptually as deviations from expected costs, durations, and audit standards. While this measurement scheme should not invalidate our arguments for the diagnosticity of OP or the lack of diagnosticity of the input risk factors, it is useful to check if the input bias is observed once the OP measures are converted into deviations from expected duration/cost. In order to perform this analysis, we develop a two-stage model as follows.

First, we estimate expected cost and duration for projects using an auxiliary regression where OP measures are regressed on several factors shown in prior work to be useful in predicting the project’s final cost and duration. In several cases, these estimated numbers affect contract negotiations and serve as the baseline against which managers judge the project’s ultimate success. While SDO projects are inherently complex and rife with uncertainties, especially in the early stages of the project, a large part of the software development literature has been concerned with developing tools to obtain better ex ante estimates of project effort, cost, and schedules (see [35] for a recent review). This stream of work finds that a relatively small set of factors is typically used (and is able) to estimate the effort required on a project. Moreover, this literature also suggests that organization-specific variables should be taken into account in making these cost and effort estimates [1, 45]. Mukhopadhyay et al. [47] and Vicinanza et al. [64] found that an analogical strategy based on estimating effort by calibrating against similar previously managed projects produced the most accurate results. Further, following Rubin [55], who suggests an approach to the estimating task using business terms, we regress OP on factors such as prior experience with client, financial risk, stringency of acceptance criteria, vendor’s domain experience, schedule pressure, and strictness of contractual conditions. These factors are typically known to managers in reasonable measure during contracting and can be used to obtain estimates for expected cost and duration metrics.

Once the auxiliary regressions are estimated, we use the regression residuals as our new OP measures. Because they represent differences between the estimated and actual cost and duration for the project, they capture the over- or underachievement of expected OP targets and are arguably a more consistent counterpart to the SP measures. In the second stage, we include these estimated cost and duration residuals as OP regressors instead of the size-adjusted cost and duration metrics. These results are shown in Table 4 as model M3. The consistency of findings across models M1, M2, and M3 offers strong evidence for the input bias that is robust to different specifications of the objective performance metrics.

## Robustness to Alternate Measures of SP and OP

Another potential issue that we face with our SP measure pertains to the relatively low factor loading that emerged from the third questionnaire item pertaining to adherence to control and audit standards. In this section, we examine the robustness of our main results in Table 4 using alternate measures of SP. Our current measure is referred to as the three-item SP. We repeat our empirical analysis with two alternative measures of SP. First, we remove the third questionnaire item and use a two-item definition of SP. Second, we use single-item SP measures for cost and duration and reestimate our models using the corresponding objective cost and duration figures. The details of these tests as well as their results are provided in Appendix B and show that the results for the input bias appear consistently across all three conceptualizations of SP. Finally, it is possible that normalizing cost and duration by project size may introduce error in the regressions if managers’ responses are based on absolute outcomes. We therefore reestimate our models using nonnormalized OP measures and include project effort as a control variable. These results, also presented in Appendix B, are again consistent with the results shown in Table 4. Overall, the additional analyses yield robust support for the main results presented in the paper.

## Discussion

While we focus on the input bias in this paper, behavioral decision theory has an extensive literature on the different forms of biases exhibited in judgment tasks. It is therefore appropriate here to discuss how our findings compare to relevant other biases documented in existing experimental literature regarding the effects of irrelevant input factors. Nisbett et al. [51] documented the dilution effect and showed that the predictive power of diagnostic (or relevant) information can be reduced or diluted by the addition of nondiagnostic (or irrelevant) information, leading to regressive or less extreme judgment. In our study, we observe that the effects of diagnostic information are fully absorbed by the nondiagnostic information (namely, M2) in only one scenario. In all other cases, both OP and materialized risks continue to remain significant in determining SP. Further, the resulting subjective performance assessments of process performance in our work are more negatively biased in comparison to the actual performance on the project as measured by objective measures. Therefore, more research is necessary to understand what factors determine whether nondiagnostic inputs lead to strengthening or regressive biases.

Another related effect—the elastic justification bias—has been observed by Hsee [32, 33], who found that subjects in laboratory judgment tasks relied heavily on unjustifiable factors even in the presence of more justifiable factors. Justifiable factors are those that the decision maker believes should be taken into consideration, whereas unjustifiable factors are those that the decision maker wants to consider but knows he or she should not [32]. Hsee found that the effect of the unjustifiable factors was to veer the effects of the justifiable factors in its direction when there was ambiguity or elasticity in the latter. In our study, the results similarly indicate that managers making judgments are influenced by anticipated risk factors (which, in Hsee’s terms, are unjustifiable). Because we did not ask our managers if they considered the cost and duration figures to be sufficiently diagnostic, we cannot strictly make the claim that we observed the elastic justification bias. However, our results indicate a striking similarity with what would be observed if it were the case that vendor project managers considered the link between OP and SP to be ambiguous, and therefore considered the effect of OP to be more “elastic” in the direction of the risks RI, TC, and TP.

## Conclusion

Decision mak er s attempting to mak e judg ments have been found to fall prey to a wide array of cognitive biases and errors [7, 30, 61]. The evidence accumulated thus far on human judgment suggests that

we are too quick to draw conclusions about others, too slow to change our minds, excessively confident in our prediction, and prone to give too much weight to irrelevant cues (such as sunk costs) and too little weight to relevant ones (such as opportunity costs). [61, p. 1]

Much of this evidence has been accumulated from experimental studies using laboratory subjects. Our study diverges in its use of a field setting to study the influence of cognitive biases in managerial judgments.

As reported at the beginning of this study, managers in IT service contexts such as outsourcing are often called upon to provide estimates of project outcomes at various stages of the project life cycle. The especially complex nature of SDO contexts betokens the presence of input bias and points to the need to assess the extent to which managers’ subjective judgments of performance are unduly influenced by nondiagnostic inputs, thereby rendering them diverged from objective outcomes. Such biases in managerial judgments can have especially dire consequences when they are used to inform other decisions in the firm.

Our study provides significant support for the presence of the input bias in an IT services field setting. We find that managers are influenced by nondiagnostic inputs such as anticipated risks in assessing performance of SDO projects, even upon their completion when more diagnostic information is available. Our findings are robust to alternate modeling assumptions regarding manager-specific unobserved variables and heteroskedasticity. We also continue to observe the input bias using alternate measures of OP and SP. Thus, we establish that even professionals with ample training in their fields, and performing routine tasks, display cognitive biases when evaluating process performance in complex domains.

## Implications

Our findings provide a number of implications for managers and researchers. First, a key implication is that before subjective performance judgments are used in lieu of objective measures for tasks in IT service settings, it is necessary to consider the possible sources of bias. It is imperative for managers to be aware that their judgments of performance for complex tasks, regardless of their training and experience, are prone to the input bias. Managers should specifically pay close attention to the inputs they use to form their performance judgments and be sensitive to the distinction between diagnostic and less diagnostic inputs to the task at hand. Although it may not be possible to remove the impact of judgment biases such as the input bias, being sensitive to their occurrence can help mitigate their negative effects in organizational settings.

Second, our study reveals conditions under which the input bias is likely to be exacerbated. We set out with the expectation that all three anticipated risks would be more salient for fixed price projects than time and materials projects. Yet we find this to be the case only for risks associated with the use of extra resources and time to learn new technologies and experiment with more efficient, but possibly unfamiliar, systems (TC) and to hire and train new project employees when faced with unexpected turnover (TP). Unlike TC and TP, which primarily originate at the vendor, risks related to changing and volatile requirements (RI) are client driven, resulting in the vendor’s expecting greater loss of control over the process. Thus, we conclude that perhaps it is a combination of accountability and source of the risk (vendor-side versus client-side) that determines the direction in which the input bias is exacerbated. This suggests that managers in conditions of greater accountability or less control over the origin of risks should exercise caution in making performance judgments; these relationships need to be further tested through controlled experiments or field studies.

A third implication is relevant for both managers and researchers. Our findings suggest that it would be appropriate to suitably weight managerial performance judgments when objective data are either not available or easily accessible. For managers, we recommend the use of relevant objective data (when available) to gauge the veracity of managerial judgments. Further, triangulation of important outcome judgments using multiple sources may be a preferred procedure for using subjective judgments, particularly in critical organizational activities. For researchers, it is necessary to consider the possibility that existing research that uses judgments as proxies for objective performance without correcting for such biases in their dependent variables could show spurious results. It may therefore be necessary to replicate some of these studies using econometric methods that can account for any such biases, such as the possible use of instrumental variables.

## Limitations

We believe that our study adds robustness and external validity to lab findings related to the input bias by using a richer real-world context. Nevertheless, the field sets some limits on what we can accomplish relative to the lab. First, our sample size of 85 projects is relatively small. However, the additional depth in data available through a field study as well as the generalizability of such context-driven analysis compensates for the sample size. Second, unlike in lab experiments, we cannot control for the effects of all possible exogenous variables not captured in our analysis and we cannot claim that the distribution of these omitted variables is truly random in our sample. While it is possible that many other variables contribute to the managers’ perceptions of subjective performance over and above the variables we control for, we attempted to mitigate several confounding factors by being mindful of recall bias, demand effects, common method issues, and conducting robustness checks, as described in the paper.

Third, some of our data are perceptual and therefore open to misinterpretation. One such concern could be that managers, when asked about anticipated (ex ante) risks, are instead providing estimates of actual risk materialization (ex post). This could occur due to miscommunication, wherein managers assume that the survey questions pertain to ex post rather than ex ante factors. However, discussions with managers after data collection established that they understood the ex ante nature of the reported risk factors. A second possibility is that the ex ante responses were “contaminated” by the actual materialization of risks during the project—that is, ex ante responses are highly correlated with ex post materialization. Our data, however, suggest that this is unlikely. Table 3 shows that the correlations between the input risk factors and OP are weak and insignificant, which would not be true if the managers were providing responses based on materialized risks (these would then significantly correlate with OP). This indicates that managers were indeed reporting on ex ante risk perceptions. In addition, even if the ex ante responses were contaminated by ex post perceptions, this still does not invalidate the input bias argument because in the presence of OP, which comprehensively accounts for the effects of materialized risk factors, the use of materialized risks again in performance judgments would still represent the input bias. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this is a limitation of our work brought about by the exigencies of the field and should be taken into account when evaluating our results.

A fourth limitation, mentioned earlier, arises from the need to avoid demand effects. We do not explicitly provide the business unit managers with diagnostic OP information. However, our assumption that managers would possess the necessary information on critical cost and schedule metrics on projects they managed was verified during debriefing. Finally, our models remain silent on the extent of materialized risks and the management of risks because we were unable to obtain these measures. While this information would have provided a richer picture, its absence does not invalidate our findings of the input bias. Because we contrast OP and SP for the same project, the risk management is the same across both measures. Furthermore, given that all the projects included in our sample come from a single vendor that remained certified as capability maturity model (CMM) level 5 during the life cycle of the projects, it is likely that the vendor employed consistent and clearly defined methodologies in managing the underlying software development process. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the level of risk management is controlled for in our setting, thereby allowing attribution of differences in OP to the extent of risk materialization across the projects.

## Future Research

Our work in this paper points to several avenues for future research. First, we study the impact of the input bias using a small set of input factors, selected for their salience in the SDO context. We do not claim to be exhaustive in our selection of risks in this paper and expect that other salient input factors would induce similar biases in managerial judgments in IT service settings. We leave it to future research to possibly test these effects in more detail. Second, we examine one specific measure of performance in this study. Arguably, process performance is easier for managers to assess directly compared to “softer” measures such as quality performance. Yet we find significant evidence of the input bias in our study, suggesting that the bias may be even more problematic when judging other outcomes. It would therefore be important to study how cognitive biases lead to inconsistencies in managerial judgment across other critical organizational outcomes of interest.

Third, econometric and statistical techniques could be developed to enable managers and researchers to account and control for possible biases. The input bias is, by definition, considered to be “hardwired”; that is, it is a structural bias that cannot be removed by mere exertion of additional effort by the decision maker performing the judgment task [11, 59]. Therefore, merely making managers aware of such biases may not lead to better judgment [67]. Future research is needed to devise methods and procedures by which the effects of such biases may be reduced. This would enable managers to use “de-biased” performance judgments, especially when objective numbers are hard to find. There are possible econometric methods that can be used to tease out the “true” performance judgments based on our work. For example, the use of instrumental variables for performance judgments, which are, by definition, uncorrelated with the input factors, can help mitigate the effects of the input bias. Alternatively, archival information on objective and subjective performance data on the same set of projects or service interactions (such as the data we present here) can be used to formulate statistically valid (perhaps vendor-specific) models of managerial judgment that can then be used going forward. It is our hope that this study will help spark more experimental and field-based work in similar areas. Such work would address some of the open research areas that we identified above and extend the study of behavioral decision theory within IS.

## Appendix A: Assessing the Manager Effects

We examine whether manag er effects may influence our results regarding the impact of nondiagnostic inputs on SP. In order to test for these effects in a more systematic manner, we conduct additional analyses to compare a manager fixed effects and a manager random effects model. The managers in our study managed 1 to 12 projects, for an average of 3.15 projects per manager. This is potentially problematic if (relatively time-invariant) differences in managers’ bounded rationality or other idiosyncratic factors result in manager-specific effects that systematically affect judgments of project performance. We therefore control for this effect using a set of 26 managerial dummies in the regression analysis as per the model shown in Equation (A1):

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{SP} _ {i j} = \beta_ {1} + \left[ \beta_ {2 i j} \text {Controls} \right] + \left[ \beta_ {3 i j} \mathrm{OP} \right] + \left[ \beta_ {4 i j} \mathrm{RI} + \beta_ {5 i j} \mathrm{TC} + \beta_ {6 i j} \mathrm{TP} + \beta_ {7 i j} \text {Contract} \right] \\ \quad + \left[ \beta_ {8 i j} (\mathrm{RI*Contract}) + \beta_ {9 i j} (\mathrm{TC*Contract}) + \beta_ {1 0 i j} (\mathrm{TP*Contract}) \right] + \varepsilon_ {i j}, \end{array}\tag{A1}
$$

where i indexes managers and j indexes projects; unobservables are disaggregated into a manager-level effect captured by a set of dummies and project-level independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) errors.

A fixed effect model takes into account information about the partitioned structure of the error term. The choice of fixed versus random effects to model manager heterogeneity depends on the relationship between unobserved manager-specific variables absorbed in the dummies $( \gamma )$ and the project-level covariates (α) in the model. A fixed effects model treats the two as correlated $( E ( \gamma , \alpha ) \neq 0 )$ , whereas a random effects model assumes independence [2]. Results from both fixed and random effects estimates for the SP model are presented in Table A 1. Note the reduction in sample size from 85 to 73 due to the presence of 12 managers who managed only one project in our data set. We find that the manager fixed effects are not significant $( F ( 1 4 , 4 5 ) = 0 . 8 9 , p = 0 . 5 7 )$ , the random effects model reduces to a pooled OLS model (error variance attributed to manager effects is not different from zero), and the Hausman test is not rejected in our model $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 1 3 ) = 7 . 9 0 , p = 0 . 8 5 )$ ). As a result, the random effects model with cluster robust standard errors (also reported as model M2 in Table 4) is deemed appropriate. The variance estimator in this model assumes independence across managers and is robust to arbitrary within-cluster correlation and heteroskedasticity [52, 68].

In summary, the results in Table 4 and Table A 1 are consistent and show that accounting for manager effects in our regression analysis does not change the evidence for the presence of the input bias. While the decreased power associated with the fixed effects specification renders more independent variables insignificant, it still provides significant results for the effect of the nondiagnostic input factors on SP. The dependent variable used in Table A1 is the three-item SP measure; however, similar analysis performed with the two-item SP measure as well as single-item measures provide similar results. These results, not reported here, are available from the authors on request.
