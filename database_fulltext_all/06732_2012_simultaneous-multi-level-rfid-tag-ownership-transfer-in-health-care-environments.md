## a b s t r a c t

Authentication of Radio Frequency Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (RFID) tags takes several forms depending on the dictates of the considered scenario. For example, mutual authentication protocols are used in scenarios where both tag and reader need to authenticate each other. We consider and develop ownership transfer protocols for three scenarios in the health care domain that have not been addressed in extant literature. RFID-tagged items in a health care supply chain experience different levels of ownerships, often simultaneously, throughout their lifetime. We consider a scenario related to ownership of RFID tags in a health care supply chain, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the seamless incorporation by a third party logistics provider. Another scenario considered is where tags simultaneously possess a temporary and a permanent key for communicating with a temporary (e.g., rental customer) and a permanent (e.g., owner) user respectively. The third scenario considered is where there are several levels of ownership (e.g., primary owner, rental owner, sub-rental owner). We provide example cases in the health care domain where such scenarios are observed. We then present brief security analyses of the developed protocols. © 2012 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

As Radio Frequency Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (RFID) tags become ubiquitous in a wide variety of application domains with disparate idiosyncratic characteristics, the need for authentication protocols that are appropriate for these scenarios is urgent. RFID authentication has been a very active area for researchers during the past decade. While a majority of authentication protocols proposed to date are either oneway or mutual authentication (e.g., [11]) of a single tag and a single reader, there exist scenarios that warrant protocols with different characteristics. For example, the Yoking Proof [5] protocol and its variants are appropriate for scenarios that necessitate the simultaneous presence of two tags. Given the prevalence of ownership transfer in RFID tag applications, there have also been several protocols that explicitly deal with such issues for various related scenarios such as one-owner/one-tag and multiple-owner/multi-tag (e.g., [6,7]).

Item-level information can be generated by the use of any of the several auto-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation technologies such as bar codes including their two-dimensional variation, data matrix, RFID tags, among others. However, an issue with a majority of the auto-identi<sup>fi</sup>cation technologies is that these cannot be easily incorporated in an automated system. Moreover, when the environment is dynamic and there is a need to continually update the stored information with minimal use of other resources, RFID tags clearly emerge as a winner.

RFID systems are increasingly being used in distributed environments where there is a need to embed item-level information on the item of interest to improve access to necessary (item-level) information when and where they are needed regardless of network connectivity and related issues. Although there are protocols that consider multiple-ownership transfer where the ownership is either shared or otherwise (i.e., strict ownership transfer), to our knowledge, there exist none that address multi-level ownership of RFID-tagged items where ownership at different levels have different access privileges. There is clearly an increasing trend toward automation in health care institutions to reduce medical errors and cost as well as to increase the general ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of processes in these environments. When RFID tags are used to facilitate automation in health care environments where entities change ownership over time, there is a need to develop protocols that seamlessly accomplish context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c ownership transfer. We consider three different scenarios that involve multi-level ownership of RFID tags.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst case we consider is that of third party logistics providers and their role in temporary ownership transfer of items in a distributed health care supply chain environment. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider the simultaneous RF access of an RFID-tagged item by the item's owner and the (temporary) owner (here, the third party logistics provider) through the use of two keys — a main key for the owner and a sub-key for the third party logistics provider.

We then consider an authentication scenario that calls for a permanent primary key and a temporary sub-key where the former remains constant for the life of the RFID tag and the latter is updated periodically as the situation dictates. An example where this scenario may be appropriate is any rental facility (e.g., bicycle, car, video, hotel room) where the owner of the RFID-tagged item has access to the primary key of the item and the rental customer has access to the sub-key of the item. Another example of this scenario is the hospital bottled gas inventory management case discussed in Meiller et al. [9]. In the bottled gas case, the hospitals in France considered in [9] rent the <sup>fi</sup>lled medical gas bottles. Once the content of these bottles are used, the bottles are returned to the provider. The hospitals pay for the content of these bottles (i.e., medical gas) as well as a rent on the bottles themselves. In this scenario, the provider of these bottled gas is the primary owner of these bottles and the hospital is a renter. Clearly, as long as the owner remains the same, there is most likely no need for the owner to update the primary key. However, the sub-key needs to be updated every time a new rental event occurs for the item of interest.

Lastly, we consider a scenario where simultaneous multi-level ownerships have decreasing levels of authority and ownership at lower levels in the hierarchy. An example of this scenario is where an RFID-tagged item has one primary owner. This owner rents the item to a rental-owner. This rental-owner again rents the item to another rental-owner. The complexity of this scenario arises from the fact that an end to the ownership of the tag at any level above the lowest level would automatically nullify the ownership at every level below. Again, an example of this structure in health care applications is presented in Meiller et al. [9]. The scenario involves ancillaries that are used for implantation and extraction of prosthetic devices in humans. These ancillaries are shared (rented) among hospitals — in a sense, these are rented from one hospital to another beginning with their primary owner. The different hospitals renting such an ancillary would be a node in this hierarchy of ownership.

We develop ownership transfer protocols for these three scenarios. The proposed ownership transfer protocols address these scenarios comprising simultaneous multi-level RFID tag ownerships with varying levels of access to the functionalities of the RFID tag. We <sup>fi</sup>rst present the scenario and then present the proposed protocol. We provide a brief security analysis to check for some common vulnerabilities that are present in related protocols.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: The considered scenario, the sequence of steps for ownership transfer in this scenario, as well as the proposed protocol following these steps are presented in the next three sections. Section 5 concludes the paper with a brief discussion.

## 2. Varying ownership levels in health care supply chains

We <sup>fi</sup>rst present the considered scenario, followed by the sequence of events in the modeled scenario, and then the set of notations used in the proposed protocol. We then present and discuss the proposed protocol.

## 2.1. The scenario

We consider a scenario (Fig. 1) in a health care supply chain where an RFID-tagged item changes ownership

Along the way, the RFID-tagged item also needs to have the capability to communicate with third party logistics provider(s). Whereas the former can readily be handled by generic single-tag single-owner ownership transfer protocols from extant literature, to our knowledge, existing protocols do not address the scenario that interleaves the former with the latter. There exist at least four main entities/ players in this system: current and next owners of the RFID tag (and, therefore, the RFID-tagged entity), the RFID tag, and the third party logistics provider. We consider the use of only passive tags, thereby adhering only to lightweight cryptography to develop the proposed ownership transfer/sharing protocol. Current state-of-theart on RFID (cryptography) authentication protocols for basic passive RFID tags necessitates the use of symmetric-key cryptography. The choice of symmetric-key cryptography renders it extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, to perform ownership transfer without a trusted third party (TTP). Therefore, we include a TTP as an entity that participates in the proposed protocol.

The following are some of the characteristics of this system:

1. The (current and next) owner and the tag share a common secret K (the main key)

2. The third party logistics (TPL) provider i and the tag share a common secret k (the sub-key).

3. The owner can communicate with the tag only with the knowledge of both K (the main key) and k (the sub-key).

4. The TPL provider, on the other hand, can communicate with the tag using just the sub-key.

5. The owner has ‘super user’ power and can modify the settings including the sub-key in the tag, whereas the TPL provider is not allowed to change any of the keys (i.e., the main- and/or the sub-keys).

## 2.2. The event sequence

We assume that ownership transfer occurs when an item is transported from one physical location to another. The ownership transfer process itself can occur at the origin or the destination locations or even somewhere in-between. Regardless, there are two main dynamic at play here: ownership transfer of the item from current to new owner and the granting of communication privilege/permission (i.e., shared secret sub-key) to the TPL provider so that the TPL provider can communicate directly with the tag. We assume the presence of a TTP when ownership transfer occurs as well as when the TPL provider obtains and surrenders the sub-key. Since the TPL provider obtains the sub-key at the origin of this trip-segment, we assume the presence of a TTP at the origin point. Similarly, since the TPL provider surrenders the sub-key at the destination of this trip-segment, we assume the presence of a TTP at the destination point.

There are a few variations to this scenario depending on where (i.e., origin, destination, somewhere in-between) the primary (i.e., between current and next owner) ownership transfer occurs (Figs. 2–4). Regardless, given that the two owners need not simultaneously be at the same physical location, ownership transfer can be accomplished by the two TTPs (one at the origin and the other at the destination points) independent of when it occurs since all that is necessary are the physical proximity of the two (current and next) owners to either or both of the two TTPs and the existence of a (secure or unsecure) communication channel between the two TTPs. We also assume the presence of several (at least two as discussed above) TTPs and that these TTPs are all identical and are able to communicate among one another to pass necessary messages through secure channels. I.e., for our purposes, even though we consider two ‘separate’ TTPs, they both can be considered asingle entity.

The following is the sequence of events that occur in the modeled scenario:

1. The current owner possesses (or, obtains from the TTP) the main key (i.e., K) to the item of interest.

2. The tag, current owner, and the (if any) TPL provider obtain sub-key for the item at the origin location.

3. When appropriate, the TPL provider transports the item from origin to destination location.

4. The new owner obtains the main key from TTP.

5. The new owner and tag obtain the updated sub-key from the TTP.

We enforce the need for any owner to have knowledge of both main- and sub‐keys for communicating with the tag. The composite key thus necessary can be represented as K⊕k , where k is the item's sub-key during the ith period that may or may not involve a TPL provider. When the two (i.e., current and next) owners are physically at different locations, the TPL provider is assumed to transport the item between the two owners. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a period as one when either ownership transfer occurs or the item is transported to another physical location, or both. The beginning of a new period triggers item's sub-key update.

## 2.4. The protocol

The proposed protocol (Fig. 5) can be used for (1) generation and transfer of the main key whenever ownership transfer occurs and (2) generation and transfer of the sub-key whenever either ownership transfer or the use of a new TPL provider is involved. I.e., the main- and sub-keys are updated whenever ownership transfer occurs and only the sub-key is updated whenever the RFID-tagged item is transported by a TPL provider. The main key is associated with the owner whereas the sub-key is associated with the TPL provider. The messages between any two entities as presented in Fig. 5 occur through over-the-air channel that is not assumed to be secure. The entities participating in the protocol are assumed to be in close proximity to one another — at the least, any pair of communicating entities are expected to be in close physical proximity to each other.

The protocol includes several shared secret keys between pairs of entities. The shared secret keys between the current and next owners and TTP are respectively s and s′. The shared secret sub-keys between the current and next owners and TTP are respectively $k _ { i - 1 }$ and $k _ { i \cdot }$ This sub-key is also shared with the current (if any) TPL provider. The shared main keys between the current and next owners and TTP are respectively K and K′. The shared secret key between the TPL provider and the TTP is $k _ { L } .$ Finally, the shared secret key between the tag and the TTP is $k _ { T } .$ Each of the entities generate fresh nonce, which is a random number that is used only once, when initiating a ‘loop.’ The set of nonce include o, o′, l, t, and p respectively for the current owner, next owner, TPL provider, tag, and TTP.

The messages in the protocol form four loops: the <sup>fi</sup>rst loop between current owner and TTP is used by the current owner to inform the TTP of the change in status of an RFID-tagged item — i.e., this tagged item either changes ownership or goes through a TPL provider or both. The second loop between the TTP and the next owner is for the TTP to generate a new main key and inform the new owner of the same. However, when the owner remains the same but there is a TPL provider involved, the TTP informs the new owner and the new owner generates a new sub-key for the tag. The third and fourth loops are for the TTP to inform the TPL provider and tag respectively of the new sub-key for the tag.

## 2.5. Security analysis

We brie<sup>fl</sup>y consider a few security violations that can arise in the presented context and evaluate the proposed protocol.

• Denial of Service (DoS)/desynchronization attack: Denial of Service (DoS) attacks can arise due to several reasons including desynchronization between tag and reader and can lead to disconnect between tag and reader. When this occurs, the tag (or, reader) will not be able to communicate with the reader (or, tag). In the proposed protocol, there is very little chance for desynchronization to occur since when the shared keys are updated, the entity updating the key (e.g., TTP) ensures that all parties involved (e.g., Owner, TPL provider, tag) are aware of this update through timed message loops.

• Forward Security: Forward security is necessary to maintain the integrity of the system and is especially critical in systems where messages are exchanged over the air. Forward security implies that even if an adversary copies every message that has been exchanged between two entities, the adversary cannot decipher any of them even though the secret keys at a later point in time are known to the adversary. This protocol does not guarantee forward security since none of the messages are encrypted using one-way hash functions. It is possible to address this issue through the use of one-way hash functions to encrypt messages throughout the protocol. However, that could lead to increased computational load on the entities, especially the tag, which is bound by extreme resource constraints. The messages sent between the TTP, Owner , $\mathrm { O w n e r } _ { j + 1 } ,$ and TPL provider can all be encrypted using one-way hash functions (instead of the encryption function used) to alleviate this problem.

• Replay attack: Replay attack occurs when an adversary passively observes communication among entities and copies those messages for later use. At some later point in time, the adversary strategically replays some of these messages, which are accepted as valid by the receiving entity. Because most messages that could be replayed in the proposed protocol include freshly generated nonce, replaying any of these will not work (i.e., will not be validated by the receiving entity).

• Impersonation attack: Impersonation attack occurs when an adversary is able to completely impersonate an entity to all other entities that participate in the protocol. In the proposed protocol, an adversary cannot impersonate any of the entities from the perspective of any other entity due to the use of freshly generated nonce in each of the three (and the fourth optional) loops. It is also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for an adversary to obtain the shared secrets $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s , s ^ { \prime } , k _ { i - 1 } , k _ { i } , K , K ^ { \prime } , k _ { T } , k _ { L } )$

We now verify the correctness of the assumptions with respect to message source as well as the beliefs of the sender and recipient of messages using GNY logic [3]. In GNY logic, the principals are not assumed to be trustworthy and redundancy is always explicitly present in encrypted messages. GNY logic distinguishes between what a principal can possess and what it can believe in. It enables expressing different trust levels and implicit conditions behind protocol steps. GNY logic ensures that all entities learn what they should learn and what the entities learn are indeed true. We proceed by including the individual messages in the protocol followed by inherent explicit assumptions and then the goals. We then present proofs of these goals using GNY logic.

## 3. Simultaneous temporary and permanent ownership of RFID tags

As in the previous section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present the considered scenario, followed by the sequence of events in the modeled scenario (Fig. 6). We then present and discuss the proposed protocol.

## 3.1. The scenario

Consider a scenario where a rental transaction occurs (Fig. 7). Here, the owner (hereafter referred to as Reader) of the RFID-tagged item has primary access to the tag.

The following are some of the generic characteristics of this system:

1. The owner and the tag share a common secret $K$ (the main key).

2. The renting customer i and the tag share a common secret $k _ { i }$ (the sub-key).

3. Upon renting the item, customer i changes the key $k _ { i }$ to $k ^ { \prime } { } _ { i } .$ . The owner does not know this $k ^ { \prime } { } _ { i } .$

4. The Reader can communicate with the tag only with the knowledge of K (the main key) and k or $k ^ { \prime } { } _ { i }$ (the sub-key).

5. The customer, on the other hand, can communicate with the tag using just the sub-key.

6. The Reader has ‘super user’ power and can modify the settings, et c. in the tag, whereas the only modi<sup>fi</sup>cation the customer is allowed to do is change the sub-key.

## 3.2. The event sequence

The following is the sequence of events that occur at the rental facility when a rental event occurs:

1. When the previous $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \{ i - 1 \} \mathrm { t h } )$ customer returns the item, the TTP immediately changes the sub-key to k and informs the Reader of the same.

2. Now, the Reader and the TTP wait for the arrival of a new customer.

3. When the new (i.e., ith) customer rents this item, the process begins with the TTP.

(a) The new customer informs the TTP of interest in renting the item.

(b) The TTP then transfers the secret keys (the one that would enable secure communication between the TTP and the customer and the one for secure communication between the customer and Reader).

4. The customer requests Reader for information on the item's tag (i.e., sub-key of the item of interest).

5. The Reader transfers the current sub-key $( \mathrm { i } . \mathsf { e } . , k _ { i } )$ to customer after completing necessary transaction procedure (e.g., rental payment).

6. Customer now proceeds to update the key (from k to $k ^ { \prime } { } _ { i } )$ with the help of the trusted third party (TTP).

7. Procedure to update the key:

(a) Customer i requests the TTP to change the key.

(b) The TTP authenticates the customer and then updates the key. (c) The TTP then informs both the tag and the customer of this updated key.

8. Customer is now able to communicate with the tagged item.

9. If customer loses the reader (i.e., its means to communicate with tag, Reader, and TTP), the TTP has all the information that is necessary to create a new reader with an updated key.

## 3.3. The protocol

The proposed protocol (Fig. 8) can be used for authentication of an RFID-tagged item, which possesses both a permanent and a temporary key. The temporary key is modi<sup>fi</sup>ed when a new customer needs to communicate with the tag. The permanent key is assumed to be permanent for the lifetime of any given tag. We assume the presence of a (momentary) secure channel between the customer and TTP only for the TTP to transmit the customer-Reader and customer-TTP shared keys to the customer. The messages between any two entities as presented in Fig. 8 occur through an over-the-air channel that is not assumed to be secure. The entities participating in the protocol are assumed to be in close proximity to one another — at the least, any pair of communicating entities are expected to be in close proximity to each other. The communication between tag and owner are not a part of this protocol and are therefore omitted.

## 3.4. Security analysis

We brie<sup>fl</sup>y consider a few security violations that can arise in the presented context and evaluate the proposed protocol. These are structurally very similar to those considered for the previous protocol.

• Denial of Service (DoS)/desynchronization attack: Denial of Service (DoS) attacks can arise due to several reasons including desynchronization between tag and reader and can lead to disconnect between tag and reader. When this occurs, the tag (or, reader) will not be able to communicate with the reader (or, tag). In the proposed protocol, there is very little chance for desynchronization to occur since when the shared keys are updated, the entity updating the key (e.g., TTP) ensures that all parties involved (e.g., customer, tag) are aware of this update through timed message loops.

• Forward Security: Forward security is necessary to maintain the integrity of the system and is especially critical in systems where messages are exchanged over the air. Forward security implies that even if an adversary copies every message that has been exchanged between two entities, the adversary cannot decipher any of them even though the secret keys at a later point in time are known to the adversary. This protocol does not guarantee forward security since none of the messages are encrypted using one-way hash functions. It is possible to address this issue through the use of one-way hash functions to encrypt messages throughout the protocol. However, that could lead to increased computational load on the entities, especiallythe tag, which is bound by extreme resource constraints. The messages sent between the customer, Reader, and TTP can all be encrypted using one-way hash functions (instead of the encryption function used) to alleviate this problem.

• Replay attack: same as that for the previous protocol.

• Impersonation attack: Impersonation attack occurs when an adversary is able to completely impersonate an entity to all other entities that participate in the protocol. In the proposed protocol, an adversary cannot impersonate any of the entities to another due to the use of freshly generated nonce in each of the three loops. It is also dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for an adversary to obtain the shared secrets $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , k _ { i } , k ^ { \prime } _ { i } , k ^ { \prime \prime } _ { i } , r _ { a } )$

## 4. Hierarchical ownership levels

As with the other two cases, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present the considered scenario (Fig. 9), followed by the sequence of events in the modeled scenario. We then present and discuss the proposed protocol.

## 4.1. The scenario

This scenario comprises a hierarchy of ownerships of the same RFID-tagged item (Fig. 10). For example, consider an item X with one primary owner. This primary owner rents the item to a renter. The renter in turn could (sub-)rent the item to another renter. Eventually, the entities that rent a given item at any given point in time could form a hierarchy that is tree-structured with a single (i.e., one sub-owner) branch or several (i.e., several sub-owners) branches originating at each node. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne ownership of a renter loosely here as that of possessing necessary permission to access that item. The peculiarity of this scenario is that when an intermediate node (i.e., renter) loses ownership of an item, every node below this node in the “ownership tree” lose their ownership as well.

The following are some of the characteristics of this system:

1. The primary (main) key for the owner never changes unless there is a change in the primary owner of the tag. We do not consider this scenario in this paper since any extant ownership protocol can be used to accomplish this.

2. Whenever ownership transfer occurs (ownership transfer in this scenario is said to occur when the item has a new renter or when a renter ceases to have access to the item), a sub-key is updated.

3. We model the keys such that an RFID-tagged item's main key (say, K ) is a composite of the main key for the owner or renter one level up in the hierarchy (say, $K _ { i _ { 1 } }$ and a sub-key (k )).

![](/api/attachments/5763RNDC/fulltext/images/99757a013a236fea34a540f11fc605991b30f7488c96e63b017c1311c8fa94d2.jpg)  
Fig. 9. A schematic of hierarchical ownership levels of RFID tags.

![](/api/attachments/5763RNDC/fulltext/images/4421806200c565bf597de409c1135ef2ef3ed4a0b4e598cd165409bb02fba21a.jpg)  
Fig. 10. Hierarchical ownership levels.

4. The main key at any level is known only to the renter at that level (or owner, if there's no renter) and the TTP.

5. The sub-key is known only to the TTP.

6. The main key of an item at any level (below the root-node in the hierarchy) is, therefore, a composite of the main key of the (primary) owner and the sub-keys of renters until (and including) the level of interest when no cancelation of rental agreement has occurred for this item.

## 4.2. The event sequence

At time zero, we assume that an item is owned by only one entity (i.e., the primary owner). Multiple‐ownership is handled similarly whereby the same main key (K) is shared among all the owners of this RFID-tagged item. When this RFID-tagged item is rented, the TTP generates a sub-key $\left( k _ { 1 } \right)$ and XORs the main key of the owner with this sub-key to generate the main key (K ) for the renter (i.e.,

$K _ { 1 } \gets K \oplus k _ { 1 } )$ . This main key is then communicated to the renter and the tag. The (primary) owner is now unable to communicate with the tag.

Similarly, when this renter (sub-)rents this RFID-tagged item to another renter, the TTP generates yet another sub-key $\left( k _ { 2 } \right)$ and XORs the main key of the <sup>fi</sup>rst-level renter $( \mathrm { i . e . , } K _ { 1 } )$ to generate the new main key $\left( K _ { 2 } \right)$ for the second-level renter $( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , \ K _ { 2 }  K _ { 1 } \oplus k _ { 2 } )$ The TTP communicates this new key to the second-level renter and the tag. Again, neither the primary owner nor the <sup>fi</sup>rst-level renter would be able to communicate with the tag. This process continues as long as none of the renters at a higher level cancels its rental agreement. When that happens (say, the (i+1)th-level renter cancels the rental agreement), the TTP changes the sub-key (k ) and communicates the new main key $\left( K _ { i } \gets K _ { i - 1 } \oplus k _ { i } \right)$ to the tag and the ith‐level renter. Now, the ith‐level renter is able to communicate with the tag.

## 4.3. The protocol

The proposed protocol (Fig. 11) can be used for generation and transfer of the main key whenever ownership transfer (granting or revoking) occurs. The main key is associated with the owner or renter whereas the sub-key is associated with the renter's level in the hierarchy. As in the other two protocols presented earlier, the messages between any two entities as presented in Fig. 11 occur through over-the-air channel that is not assumed to be secure. The entities participating in the protocol are assumed to be in close proximity to one another — at the least, any pair of communicating entities are expected to be in close physical proximity to each other.

This protocol assumes the existence of shared secret key between several pairs of entities. The shared secret keys between the main owner and TTP is s and the shared secret key between the TTP and a level renter is s . The shared secret key between the TTP and the tag is $k _ { T } .$ The shared key between the primary owner and the tag is K. The shared key between a level renter and the tag is K . As in other protocols presented earlier in this paper, each of the entities generate fresh nonce when initiating a protocol ‘loop.’ The set of nonce generated and used in the protocol include r, o, t, and p respectively for the renter, owner, tag, and TTP.

The messages in the protocol form three loops: the loop between owner and TTP is used by the TTP to inform the owner of either (1) renting of the RFID-tagged item by a level renter or (2) termination of rental by a level renter. The loop between the TTP and Renter is (1) for the TTP to generate a new main key and inform the new renter of the same when this level renter rents the RFID-tagged item or (2) for the TTP to generate a new main key and inform the renter at level when renter at $\mathsf { l e v e l } _ { i + 1 }$ terminates its rental contract. The remaining loop is for the TTP to inform the tag when a new renter rents this RFID-tagged item or an existing renter terminates its rental contract on this tagged item.

The protocol begins when a renter rents the RFID-tagged item. The TTP generates a fresh nonce (p) and a fresh sub-key $\left( k _ { 1 } \right)$ and encrypts its message to the new renter using its shared key (s ) and sends the message to the renter $( p , f _ { s _ { i } \oplus p } ( K _ { i } ) )$ . The purpose of this loop is to inform the renter of the tag's main key. In response, the renter acknowledges by generating a fresh nonce (r) and the message $( r \oplus s _ { i } , f _ { r \oplus p } ( K _ { i } \oplus s _ { i } ) )$ to the TTP. To prevent trivial DoS attacks, the TTP waits for a predetermined amount of time to receive acknowledgement from the renter. When this does not happen, the TTP generates a fresh nonce (say, p′) and re-starts the process. This is repeated until successful completion of the loop.

When the response does not materialize, the TTP generates a fresh nonce and repeats its message to the tag after incorporating this nonce.

The remaining loop is used to inform the owner of a change in key. Here, since the main key (for the owner) does not change, this loop is instantiated only when there is a change in access for the owner (i.e., when renter rents the tag from the owner or when renter terminates ownership of the item). The message structure between the TTP and owner are very similar to those between the TTP and tag or TTP and renter. As with the other two loops, the TTP waits for acknowledgement from the owner and repeats the loops with a fresh nonce when acknowledgement is not received within a pre-speci<sup>fi</sup>ed amount of time.

Every loop in the protocol is timed to ensure that all initiating messages reach their destination and the acknowledgement messages reach their destination.

The security analysis for this protocol is omitted since it is very similar in structure to those for the other two protocols presented earlier in this paper.

## 5. Discussion

We began by considering two scenarios that necessitate the presence of a pair of keys for each RFID tag — a permanent one for the owner of this item and a temporary one for each temporary ‘owner’ (e.g., renter, third party logistics provider) of this item. We proposed authentication protocols for these scenarios that involve five entities — the current owner, the next owner, the third party logistics provider, a trusted third party (TTP), and the tagged item — and four entities (the (permanent) owner, the (temporary) renter, a trusted third party (TTP), and the tagged item) respectively. Except for the initial transfer of shared secret keys from the TTP to the customer/renter in one of the protocols, all communication in these two protocols occur over the air where the communication channel is not assumed to be secure. We used lightweight symmetric cryptography to develop this protocol and discussed some of its security properties.

The use of a new sub-key every time a new customer or third party logistics provider gains access to the RFID tag ensures maintenance of a certain degree of privacy and security of the RFID-tagged item. This also prevents (both physical and RF) unauthorized access of the RFID-tagged item by anyone, including adversaries. The separation of the composite key pair into main- and sub-keys facilitates maintaining and granting different security access levels/permissions to the RFID-tagged items. Communication with the RFID-tagged item can therefore be seamlessly accomplished both by the owner with the knowledge of the permanent main key and the user or TPL provider with the knowledge of the temporary sub-key. We also assume that, to gain access to higher level privileges the owner needs access to both the main- and the sub-keys.

While it is possible, in principle, to develop secure authentication protocols, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to ensure protection from relay attacks. Relay attacks occur when adversaries simply relay messages between a honest reader and a honest tag with or without the knowledge of either party (e.g., [1,2,4,8]). The dif<sup>fi</sup>culty with relay attacks lies in the absence of cryptographic manipulations by the adversary. The proposed protocols, therefore, are not immune to relay attacks. This is not a major drawback given that a majority, if not all, of extant authentication protocols are not immune to relay attacks. Even those protocols that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally purport to address relay attacks, the degree of otect immunity is not 100% (e.g., [10] and the references therein).

We considered the use of only the temporary sub-key in the proposed protocol for the rental scenario. In the considered context, the permanent key is used between the owner and the RFID-tagged item. We did not present nor discuss this part of the authentication protocol. Nevertheless, the presented example scenario and the proposed protocol highlight the importance of the existence of a pair of keys in each tag in the considered context.

For the scenario with TPL provider, we considered the possibility of simultaneous update of both main- and sub‐keys of the tag. Whereas the main-key can be updated without updating the sub-key (i.e., when a change of tag ownership occurs), we assumed that the sub-key is not updated without related update of the main-key. The proposed protocol only considered (main- and/or sub-) key update. We did not present protocols where the owners as well as other entities communicate with the RFID-tagged item. The presented example scenario and the proposed protocol highlight the importance of the existence of a pair of keys in each tag in the considered context.

Finally, we considered the scenario where ownership gets transferred in a hierarchical fashion. The requirement in this scenario is that cancel of ownership at an intermediate level dictates cascade cancel of ownership at all levels below in the tree-based hierarchy.

The protocols developed only accomplish ownership transfer in the considered scenarios. While these tags are implicitly authenticated in the process, it might be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient to use a separate authentication protocol to authenticate these tags once ownership transfer is accomplished. Another reason for a separate authentication protocol is the requirement for the presence of a TTP for ownership transfer. It is challenging to develop ownership transfer protocols using symmetric key cryptography that do not involve a TTP. However, a TTP may not be expected to be present under all authentication circumstances. In the absence of a TTP, ownership transfer is just ownership sharing as in all existing ownership ‘transfer’ protocols using symmetric key cryptography (e.g., [6,7] and the references therein).

We do not explicitly consider the possibility of multiple readers simultaneously communicating with the tags. However, race conditions are not an issue in two of the three protocols since each entity either sends and receives a message in response or receives and sends a message in response in these protocols. In the scenario considered in Section 3, the Customer is associated with three messages with the TTP. Even here, the Customer initiates the ‘loop’ and expects a certain type of response from the TTP and its response to the TTP is based on this response. The probability of race conditions occurring here is, therefore, zero. A complement of this is the simultaneous presence of multiple ownership changing tags in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of the Reader. This is not an issue since the Reader communicates individually with each of the tags with their speci<sup>fi</sup>c secret keys.

The three scenarios we considered are, to our knowledge, novel to RFID authentication and ownership transfer protocol literature. The common theme among them is the fact that there exists implicit or explicit simultaneous ownership of an item by several entities. Moreover, these simultaneous ownerships vary in degree across different owning entities. We developed protocols that seamlessly accomplish ownership transfers in these scenarios. As RFID authentication and ownership transfer protocols are continually developed and improved, there is a need to identify scenarios that have not yet been studied for protocol development purposes. Although a majority of extant published protocols target one-owner/one-tag/one-way-authentication scenarios, other scenarios are no less important in terms of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and these scenarios demand attention from researchers. Given the explosion of interest in RFID-tagging entities in a wide variety of applications, there is an urgency in this endeavor. We sincerely hope that this paper motivates researchers in this area to consider unexplored scenarios and to target their efforts in addressing existing void.
