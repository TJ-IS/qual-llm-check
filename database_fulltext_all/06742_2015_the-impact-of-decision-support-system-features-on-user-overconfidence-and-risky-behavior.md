# The impact of decision support system features on user overconfidence and risky behavior

Chi-Wen Chen<sup>1</sup> and Marios Koufaris<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York, New York, U.S.A

Correspondence: Marios Koufaris, Department of Statistics and Computer Information Systems, Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York, One Bernard Baruch Way, B11-220, New York, NY 10010, U.S.A. Tel: 1-646-312-3373;

E-mail: Marios.Koufaris@baruch.cuny.edu

## Abstract

There is considerable research on how Decision Support Systems (DSSs) enable users to make better decisions. However, there is less focus on the possibility that some of their features may introduce biases and encourage suboptimal user behavior. We report on an experimental study that examines three DSS features that are generally considered beneficial to the user: the degree of choice the system provides to the user, the presence of competition among concurrent users, and the use of training to increase system familiarity. We hypothesize that the three DSS features may increase risky behavior (measured as the amount invested in a stock investment task with random outcomes) and overconfidence, conceptualized as the illusion of control, the phenomenon whereby people believe their chances of success at a task are greater than would be warranted by objective analysis. Our results confirm the effects of the three DSS features on risky behavior but only degree of choice impacts overconfidence. Moreover, overconfidence does not appear to mediate the impact of the DSS features on risky behavior. Finally, we hypothesize and confirm that, controlling for the effect of actual performance, overconfidence increases user satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome.

Keywords: decision support systems; overconfidence; risky behavior; competition; choice; system familiarity

## Introduction

Human decision making is fraught with biases and illusions. A large body of research has demonstrated the many ways in which humans are irrational and easily biased when making decisions (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Langer, 1975). In the case of technology-assisted decision making, prior research has shown that the system itself can in<sup>fl</sup>uence users by either helping them or leading them to irrational or suboptimal decisions (Arnott, 2006; Bhandari et al, 2008; Lee et al, 2008). For example, users may engage in coping strategies that can result in biased decisions (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Tarafdar et al, 2007) or exhibit overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence because of miscalibration (Kasper, 1996). At the same time, systems can be designed to help users avoid decision-making biases, such as the con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias (Huang et al, 2012) or the representativeness bias (Lim & Benbasat, 1997). As managerial decision making is rapidly becoming more data driven, and therefore more technology dependent, understanding the way system features can either exacerbate or ameliorate human biases and errors is critical.

There is a large number of research studies in Information Systems (IS) that have examined technology-aided decision making and the role of decision support system (DSS) features on decision-making behavior (e.g., Todd & Benbasat, 1999; Barkhi, 2002; Lilien et al, 2004; Kamis et al, 2008). Among them, a minority of studies has included human biases and illusions as part of the research models being examined (Paradice & Courtney Jr., 1986; Kottemann et al, 1994; Kahai et al, 1998). In this study, we augment that research tradition by looking at how speci<sup>fi</sup>c DSS features, that have traditionally been considered bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the users, may in fact lead to an increase in risky behavior.

For the purposes of this study, we have designed an experiment in which we isolate and test the impact on risky behavior of three speci<sup>fi</sup>c system features: (1) the degree of choice provided by the system to the user, (2) whether the system enables competition among users, and (3) whether the system allows the users to increase their familiarity with it through training. We concentrate on these three features because they all concern elements of user behavior that, as we explain in the next section, are often associated with skills, something that prior research has shown can increase overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior (Langer, 1975; Lejarraga et al, 2010; Zollo & Reuer, 2010). In addition, they are common features in many types of DSSs where they are often assumed to have a positive impact on user decisions.

We set up a business-related decision-making task (investing in company stock) in such a way that the users know and understand that the task outcome is random, in order to remove the effect of task-related ability or expertise. This experimental design allows us to measure risky behavior as the amount of money subjects invest, that is, the more money they invest on the random-outcome task the riskier their behavior. In order to further elucidate the impact of the DSS features on risky behavior, we propose and test overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence as the potential mediating psychological mechanism. Finally, we test whether the subjects’ overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence increases their satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome regardless of their actual gains in the task.

## Theoretical foundation

## Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior in decision making

In this study, we propose and empirically test that certain DSS features can lead to an increase in risky behavior, possibly because of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence by the users (McKenzie et al, 2008; Tan et al, 2012). Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence has been widely studied in different contexts and by different disciplines. In our study, we have chosen to focus on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c conceptualization of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, known as the illusion of control (IOC). The IOC has been extensively studied in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of psychology and has been shown to apply in a variety of situations from gambling to virtual reality simulations for treating fear of heights (Thompson, 1999). In her seminal paper on the subject, Langer (1975) de<sup>fi</sup>ned IOC as ‘an expectancy of a personal success probability inappropriately higher than the objective probability would warrant’ (p. 313). In other words, IOC is an instance of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence whereby people believe their chances of success at a task are greater than would be warranted by objective analysis (Kottemann et al, 1994).

The cognitive process underlying the IOC is composed of two components: the belief that one can increase the probability of succeeding in a task and the belief that the probability of success, having been increased, is greater than it really is (Langer, 1975). More importantly, the IOC has been theorized to lead to risky behavior, since the person’s in<sup>fl</sup>ated belief of being in control corresponds to a de<sup>fl</sup>ated estimation of risk. In fact, most research on IOC in Psychology has never tested the actual beliefs of the subjects but instead used risky behavior, often in some form of gambling, as a proxy for IOC, thus confounding the two (Presson & Benassi, 1996). For the purposes of our study, we separate the two constructs. We conceptualize and measure overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence as IOC (and, for the purposes of this paper, use the two terms interchangeably) but we measure risky behavior separately, as the amount invested in the experimental task.

Prior experimental research has shown that IOC and risky behavior can happen in situations where causal connections between action and outcomes are not clearly established, such as complex tasks and ambiguous settings (Lejarraga et al, 2010; Zollo & Reuer, 2010). In such situations, people fall under the IOC because they confuse luck for skill (Langer, 1975). This confusion is likely to happen when tasks with random outcomes exhibit characteristics of skill-based tasks. In fact, though prior research has shown that many factors, such as prior success at the task (Langer, 1975) or a non-depressed mood (Alloy & Abramson, 1979), can increase IOC and subsequent risky behavior, the most consistent predictors of IOC have been skill-related factors, and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the degree of choice allowed by the task, the presence of competition, and task familiarity. The reason is that people often make the false assumption that skills are associated with more success in the presence of these three factors, even in random-outcome tasks: (1) when a random-outcome task provides individuals with more choices in the actions they can take, they assume that better skills will allow them to make better choices, (2) when a random-outcome task allows competition between people, they assume that skills will allow them to outperform their competitors, and (3) as people perform a random-outcome task more often and become more familiar with it or the task interface, they assume that they are improving their skills through training.

For our study, we focus on these three skill-related factors for two main reasons. First, they have been shown to be some of the strongest antecedents of risky behavior and IOC (Langer, 1975). Second, all three factors can manifest themselves as common DSS features: a DSS can be designed to increase or decrease the degree of choice available to the user, to enable or disable competition among users, and to provide training in order to increase user familiarity with the system. Furthermore, in our study we use a stock investment task for which the association between these three factors and skills is very relevant. Stock investment is a highly unpredictable and volatile task, and without the use of highly specialized and voluminous information, it is a task with random outcomes, as we discuss later in the paper. However, it is also generally considered a task that can be completed more successfully with the application of the right skills.

## Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior in IS research

Traditionally, perceived control (or perceived behavioral control) has been considered a positive outcome of DSS design in IS research. Prior studies have examined the system, task, user, and environmental factors that can increase a user’s sense of control when using a DSS (e.g., Jiang & Benbasat, 2004; Elie-Dit-Cosaque et al, 2011). However, as we just discussed, perceived control may not be a positive outcome in situations where it is not warranted but is instead illusory and leads to risky behavior.

There have been some studies in IS that have looked into the potential for overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior among users of different systems. Kasper (1996) proposed the theory of DSS for user calibration. This theory posits that user calibration (i.e., the difference between decision con-<sup>fi</sup>dence and decision quality) depends on the design of a DSS. When the DSS can be designed in a way that it effectively supports the user’s symbolic representation in problem solving, users can exhibit perfect calibration. In other words, user calibration occurs when the symbols and actions of the DSS are parallel to those of users’ natural system of symbolic representation. In a later experimental study partly based on this theory, Park et al (2013) showed that investors who used a stock message board exhibited evidence of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation bias, resulting in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky investment behavior. A separate study showed that users of a database who were allowed to reuse SQL queries exhibited overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in the correctness of the query results (Allen & Parsons, 2010).

A few studies have speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examined the IOC in DSS use. A small number of papers was published in the 1990s, describing experimental studies of users of DSSs performing very speci<sup>fi</sup>c tasks, such as production scheduling (Kottemann et al, 1994), loan application approval (Marsden & Mathiyalakan, 1997), and spreadsheet-based business modeling (Kahai et al, 1998). The authors examined the factors that could contribute to increased IOC among users. They found that factors related to speci<sup>fi</sup>c DSS features, such as performing a what-if analysis or familiarity with model execution and the decision situation, increased IOC among users.

In a more recent study, Rhee et al (2005) found that users generally exhibit increased biases in their perceptions of controllability with information security threats. In an experimental study, Mathiyalakan (2008) found that the use of a market-based experimentation environment resulted in the reduction of IOC among users of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c group DSS. In a study of another speci<sup>fi</sup>c technological tool, Dudezert & Leidner (2011) found that use of knowledge maps (kmaps) for visual representation of knowledge can increase two speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of illusion: illusion of knowledge control and illusion of executive control. Their study was a qualitative case-based analysis of users in three French companies and it did not empirically examine the antecedents or impact of these speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of illusion. Finally, in an intriguing study, Vetter et al (2011) tested the impact of different types of knowledge on IT managers’ overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, which was partially measured as IOC. What they found was that subjective knowledge, that is, a subject’s self-perceptions and self-reports of knowledge, was positively related to IOC.

Overall, we believe that question on the potential negative impact of certain DSS features on user behavior deserves additional attention. Given how much business managers today rely on the use of DSSs to aid their decision making, further research is necessary to understand how the design of such systems can lead to risky or suboptimal decisions. In addition, if certain DSS features do increase risky behavior, it is equally important to understand why.

## Research model and hypotheses

The <sup>fi</sup>rst DSS feature we examine is the degree of choice that the system provides to the user. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne choice as the set of tools, data, and actions that a user is given access to by a DSS. For example, a DSS may be designed to automatically eliminate certain choice alternatives before the user can make his or her choice. A DSS may also be designed to select certain alternatives for the user based on a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned model. It may also constrain the decisionmaking process with limited data analysis tools or it may expand the choices through the use of data aggregation and evaluation tools.

There is, in fact, considerable research on DSS restrictiveness and its impact on user behavior and performance (Silver, 1990, 1991). Prior studies have found that less restrictive systems may be more effective in problemsolving situations (Nakatsu & Benbasat, 2006). For example, a recent study has found that while restrictiveness reduced unnecessary adjustments, it deterred desirable adjustments and also encouraged over-large adjustments so that accuracy was damaged (Goodwin et al, 2011). However, studies have also shown that system restrictiveness may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. This can be true in certain cases, such as when the system is prescriptive or it provides effective guidance that reduces cognitive load (Silver, 1991). In our study, we propose that lowering a system’s restrictiveness can have the negative effect of increasing risky behavior. Research outside the <sup>fi</sup>eld of DSS research has also shown that increasing the degree of choice in a task, that is, reducing the restrictiveness of the task environment, can result in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, IOC, and risky behavior (Langer, 1975; Henry, 1994).

One explanation on the role of choice in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior focuses on the perception of control one has over a situation or a task. Limited choice restricts perceived control, while increased choice encourages perceptions of control, as demonstrated in studies based on the theory of psychological reactance (Worchel & Brehm, 1971; Worchel, 1974). According to this theory, reactance will be aroused in a situation in which people know that they have choices but are forced to accept a certain one or in which they <sup>fi</sup>nd that certain choices are eliminated. An unwillingness to accept the limited choice may threaten free behavior, decreasing the expectation of control. With the failure to control, people with high levels of reactance may generate negative emotions, such as frustration (Worchel, 1974). On the other hand, when the level of choice is increased, expectations of control can also rise, leading to overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior. Therefore, we expect that when the degree of choice is increased by a DSS, it will result in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (IOC) and riskier behavior (amount of money invested in the task) by the users.

H1a: The degree of choice provided by a DSS is positively related to overconfidence by its users.

H1b: The degree of choice provided by a DSS is positively related to risky behavior by its users.

A DSS can be designed to provide opportunities for the users to become more familiar with the system. For example, a DSS can feature training sessions to enable the users to learn how to use the system’s features. There is a long tradition of research in IS on user training and its impact on user acceptance and use of the technology as well as user performance (e.g., Igbaria et al, 1997; Gallivan et al, 2003; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010). Though training is generally considered bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for users, some prior research has shown that familiarity created by experience accumulation may have a negative effect by creating asymmetric increases in two beliefs: competence and con-<sup>fi</sup>dence (Heimeriks, 2010). When competence overtakes con<sup>fi</sup>dence, the repetition of activity will lead to a positive outcome in the sense that people will correctly understand the causal linkages between decisions/actions and performance outcomes (Zollo, 2009). Conversely, as familiarity with a system grows, people tend to feel increasingly con<sup>fi</sup>dent about their own competencies. If con<sup>fi</sup>dence outweighs one’s competencies or develops faster than competence as experience accumulates, overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence may occur, causing a false perception of ability (Zollo, 2009). Such a misperception generates misunderstanding about the causal links between decisions/ actions and outcomes. The mismatch between con<sup>fi</sup>dence and experience is also the basis of the theory of DSS user calibration, which we discussed previously (Kasper, 1996).

In the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld, there has been a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of research on continuous usage of a system, past the point of initial acceptance (Barr & Sharda, 1997; Kahai et al, 1998; McLain & Aldag, 2009). However, none of that research, as far as we know, has looked at the possible negative impact of increased familiarity with the system. On the basis of our discussion above, we expect that a DSS that allows users to increase their familiarity with the system through training may lead to an increase in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior.

H2a: Familiarity with the DSS is positively related to overconfidence by its users.

H2b: Familiarity with the DSS is positively related to risky behavior by its users.

Prior research has shown that competition can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial by increasing employee participation and intrinsic motivation (Huberman et al, 2004; Kosfeld & Neckermann, 2011). However, other research has also shown that the presence of competition, a skill-associated factor, in random-outcome tasks can increase risky behavior because of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (Langer, 1975; Camerer & Lovallo, 1999). Once again, this is consistent with the theory of user calibration for DSS users that we discussed previously (Kasper, 1996). The same phenomenon has been called the better-than-average effect, which occurs when people incorrectly estimate how well they have performed on a task (Hoorens, 1993). This effect may take place because, on one hand, people tend to ignore others’ performance and focus only on their own, and on the other hand, because people have better information about themselves than they do about others (Camerer & Lovallo, 1999). As a result, this phenomenon is very common in different situations. After all, evaluating one’s own ability is a human fundamental desire, and people prefer to be better than others (Festinger, 1954). Consequently, subjective and biased judgment of others and incorrect self-assessments can lead to incorrect con<sup>fi</sup>dence estimates of the accuracy of people’s own actions.

The better-than-average effect has been found consistently in a large variety of contexts (Kruger & Dunning, 1999; Ehrlinger & Dunning, 2003). There are two major categories in which the tendency for people to perceive their skills as being above average may occur: (1) when people have to evaluate their own ability and (2) when people have to make a decision. The <sup>fi</sup>rst category focuses on individual ability. The overestimation of one’s own ability has been demonstrated in different situations, such as drivers overestimating their ability to drive, people incorrectly perceiving how well they conveyed their feelings, doctors inaccurately estimating their level of knowledge of illnesses, and nurses erroneously evaluating their life support skills (Marteau et al, 1989).

The second category focuses on the decision makers who tend to bolster or exaggerate the attractiveness of an option they chose. In other words, decision makers do not know whether they made the correct decision, but they tend to believe their decision is better than others decisions. Several studies have found that this effect frequently takes place in different decision tasks, including the decision to <sup>fi</sup>nd a new <sup>fi</sup>rm (Cooper et al, 1988), acquire a company (Hayward & Hambrick, 1997), enter an existing market (Zajac & Bazerman, 1991), or make an investment (Malmendier & Tate, 2005).

Overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence is a common result for people who exhibit the better-than-average effect (Camerer & Lovallo, 1999; Malmendier & Tate, 2005), and competition plays an important role in causing this effect (Moore & Cain, 2007). The presence of competition can create an environment where people want to evaluate how well they have performed and compare their performance with that of others (Moore & Cain, 2007). Such an environment may foster an illusion that people are better than others and can eventually win, resulting in risky behavior and inaccurate self-assessments of performance. For instance, stock market traders tend to engage in large amounts of stock trading because they believe they are the best and most likely to succeed (Odean, 1998). We, therefore, expect that when a DSS is designed to allow for competition between users, those users will be more likely to exhibit overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and engage in risky behavior.

H3a: The presence of competition in a DSS is positively related to overconfidence by its users.

H3b: The presence of competition in a DSS is positively related to risky behavior by its users.

User satisfaction with the process and outcomes of using a system is an important factor in the success of organizational systems, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally as a strong determinant of user intention to continue or discontinue use of a system (e.g., Bhattacherjee, 2001; DeLone, 2003; Sun, 2013). Therefore, the question of what variables can increase that satisfaction is an important one. In the case of our study, it is reasonable to expect that how well subjects perform in the stock investment task will in<sup>fl</sup>uence how satis<sup>fi</sup>ed they are with their decisionmaking process and outcome. Subjects who make investment choices that result in gains should exhibit higher levels of overall satisfaction than those whose investments result in losses. However, we also believe that overall satisfaction will be directly affected by how con<sup>fi</sup>dent the subjects were when they made their investment choices, regardless of how those choices turn out. In other words, subjects who exhibited high levels of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, or IOC, should also exhibit higher levels of satisfaction after they are shown the result of their investment even if that result is a loss. We base this hypothesis on the theory of cognitive dissonance, which states that the inconsistency between beliefs and outcomes may result in discomfort and that this discomfort motivates people to mitigate a dissonant state by altering their beliefs to match outcomes so that the consistency between beliefs and outcomes can be achieved. As a result, regardless of how the inconsistency occurs, people will <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to alter their beliefs and in turn make themselves feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed (Sweeney et al, 1996).

In our case, we expect that if an individual made an investment decision that resulted in a loss but he or she exhibited strong overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence at the time the decision was made, they will change their level of perceived satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome in order to align with how con<sup>fi</sup>dent they felt before the outcome was revealed. There has been some indication in prior research that this is true, where it was shown that higher expectations of success or performance were associated with higher levels of satisfaction (Kahai et al, 1998). Therefore, we hypothesize that even after taking into consideration the impact of the investment task’s gains or losses, the level of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, measured as IOC from when the decision was made (and before the outcome was revealed) will also have a positive impact on overall satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome.

H4: Controlling for actual performance, overconfidence will be positively related with the overall satisfaction of DSS users with the decision-making process and outcome.

As we already discussed, overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence has been theorized to lead to risky behavior, because of the in<sup>fl</sup>ated belief of being in control leading to a de<sup>fl</sup>ated estimation of risk (Langer, 1975). However, this connection has been assumed but not empirically tested in most research on IOC in Psychology. In fact, risky behavior has been used as a proxy for overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence beliefs, confounding the two (Presson & Benassi, 1996). In our study, we have separated the two variables and hypothesized that the three DSS features we examined will have a separate impact on overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior. Given the lack of prior empirical research to support the link between them, we propose but do not hypothesize that overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence will mediate the impact of the three DSS features on risky behavior. In other words, overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence will be the underlying psychological mechanism that leads to risky behavior in the presence of the three DSS features.

Finally, the impact of prior knowledge on the IOC and overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in general has been shown to vary in prior research. Some studies have shown that prior experience and expertise have no impact on overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, in that experts and novices display the same amount of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (Shepherd et al, 2003). Others have shown that prior experience-based knowledge can in fact lead to an increase in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment and forecasting decisions (McKenzie et al, 2008; Van De Venter & Michayluk, 2008; Vetter et al, 2011). Given the con<sup>fl</sup>icting results in prior research, we do not hypothesize on the impact of prior experience with stock investments (the task in our study) on IOC. However, we do measure it and control for it as a covariate in our data analysis. The full research model can be seen in Figure 1.

## Study design

For our experiment, we employed a 2×2×2 full factorial design with the following three factors that may be enabled through speci<sup>fi</sup>c DSS features: high degree of choice vs low degree of choice, presence of competition vs no competition, high system familiarity vs low system familiarity. A total of 782 subjects participated in the experiment, which took place in a computer lab. The subjects were undergraduate business students at a major Northeastern U.S. University and they participated in the experiment for class credit. The subjects were told that they would be taking part in an investment task.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst screen of the experimental interface was common for all subjects. In order to reinforce the prior knowledge that the stock investment task they were about to engage in was essentially a random-outcome task, we presented the subjects with a short description of the random walk hypothesis which states that no prior knowledge can help predict the movement of a stock price in the market. We also provided them with a brief mention of alternative hypotheses that state that some limited predictions may be possible only if certain information is available, such as ‘the changes in stock prices in the prior <sup>fi</sup>ve years, earnings announcements over the prior six months, or market volatility’. None of that information would be supplied to the subjects in the experiment. The subjects were also informed that ‘there is no single accepted theory on how stock prices evolve in markets’. For the complete instructions, please see Appendix A.

Subjects were then given an endowment of US\$10,000 and were presented with information on 10 companies: the year the company was founded, the year of its IPO, its industry/sector, approximate number of employees, general location of its headquarters (e.g., Southern United States), the gender of its CEO, and its stock price on 1 March 2005. Subjects were told that these were real companies whose names were masked with pseudonyms (e.g., ManuCo). We chose 2005 for the stock price data because it was a year without extreme changes in the general economy and because it was enough years in the past that the subjects could not think that they could identify the companies behind the pseudonyms. That way, only the information presented in the experimental interface would be relevant to their decisions.

The subjects were informed that they would be allowed to invest some or all of their \$10,000 on a single company stock by buying at least one share. The system would then show them the price of the stock they invested in 1 month later (on 1 April 2005) and their earnings or losses would be calculated. Their goal would be to maximize their earnings. They were also told that the 10 companies had been chosen so that at least one of them would show a stock price increase on 1 April 2005. This eliminated the possibility that maximization of earnings could be achieved by investing as little as possible or nothing at all (which would be the rational case if subjects believed that all 10 stock prices would drop a month later).

Even though the subjects were told that the companies were real, they were in fact <sup>fi</sup>ctional, as were all the facts presented about them. All information was kept constant throughout all conditions. In order to make sure that large variations in stock price or change in stock price did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the subjects’ investment decisions, we used the following procedure to set them:

1. A ‘seed’ price of \$75 was used for each company stock. Using a random number generator we then created a random integer between 50 and 50 that we added to the seed price of each company. We then randomly generated another number between 0 and 0.99, which we added to the price. In this way, we created the 10 initial stock prices that ranged from \$36.63 to \$106.60.

2. In order to create the stock price changes, we assumed a market trend of 2.4%. For each stock, we applied a random factor variation with an approximate normal distribution to calculate the stock change. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used the NORM.INV function in Excel with a randomly generated probability, a 2.4% mean, and a− 6% standard deviation. As a result, the percentage change in stock price for the 10 stocks ranged from 8.58% to 6.23%.

The experimental factors were then designed as follows:

1. High degree of choice: The subjects were free to choose the company out of the 10 choices in which they would invest by buying shares of its stock. They were also free to buy as many shares as they wanted (up to \$10,000) with a minimum of one.

2. Low degree of choice: The subjects were told that the system would pick a stock for them out of the 10 choices to invest in. They would only be able to invest in that stock but they could buy as many shares as they wanted (up to \$10,000) with a minimum of one. In order to maintain consistency with the high degree of choice condition, where the subjects were essentially making a random, uninformed choice, we did not claim that the system would pick the best stock for them or that it used any algorithm to select a stock. That way, the subjects would not feel that they were at an advantage by having the system pick a better stock for them while still believing that the system could select a stock whose price would increase.

3. Presence of competition: The subjects were informed that they would be competing with three other subjects who were participating in the experiment at the same time. All four would be making their investment choices synchronously and their choices would be visible to each other through the system interface, though no communication between competitors was possible. In reality, the three other subjects were simulated and their investment choices were kept constant for all subjects who were participating in this condition.

4. No competition: The subjects were participating in the study by themselves and were not informed of the choices of any other subject in the study.

5. High system familiarity: Before making their stock investment choice, subjects were given four practice rounds where they used the exact same interface but with <sup>fi</sup>ctional companies (different ones than those used in the actual task) and different data for each <sup>fi</sup>ctional company. The subjects were explicitly told that the companies used in the information sessions were <sup>fi</sup>ctional so that they would not use their performance in the practice sessions as a predictor of prices in the actual stock investment task.

6. Low system familiarity: The subjects made their stock investment decisions immediately, without any practice sessions. The subjects were entirely unfamiliar with the system when they were asked to complete the investment task.

A screenshot of the web-based DSS for the stock investment task can be seen in Figure 2.

The DSS allowed the subjects to sort all the information by any column. It also automatically calculated the amount they were investing as soon as they entered a number of shares. In addition, it noti<sup>fi</sup>ed them of errors, such as buying shares in multiple companies, buying more shares than they could afford, or entering negative or nonnumerical values. We expressly kept the design and functionality of the DSS minimal. By doing so we were sacri<sup>fi</sup>- cing some generalizability, since current actual DSSs are much more complex than what we designed. However, our goal was not to test the impact of real DSSs but to isolate the effect of each of the three DSS features. A more realistic DSS, with its added complexity, would have introduced too many confounding variables and would not have allowed us to isolate the impact of each of the three features.

Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the eight possible conditions. After they made their stock investment decisions but before they were shown the results of their investment, subjects were requested to answer a brief questionnaire. We asked them for their prediction of where the price of the stock they invested in would be in 1 April 2005, followed by a belief measure of overcon<sup>fi</sup>- dence. The 3-item scale, which was adapted from prior IOC research (Kahai et al, 1998), measured their con<sup>fi</sup>- dence in their investment decision, their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their prediction of the stock price in which they invested, and how likely they thought their investment decision would maximize their earnings. Given that the task has a random outcome, a purely rational subject would express no con<sup>fi</sup>dence at all in their investment decision. Therefore, we are able to consider the 3-item scale as a measure of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (and not simply con<sup>fi</sup>dence).

## Data analysis

A total of 782 subjects took part in the experiment. We removed 34 data points that had signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly incomplete responses or were extreme outliers, resulting in a sample size of 748. Even though we told the subjects that at least one stock’s price would rise in a month, so that all subjects would try to choose one such stock, a small number of subjects reported that they invested in stock that they believed would go down in price. This was either because they did not understand the instructions or because they did not believe them. Overall, 85 subjects predicted that the stock they invested would go down in price, so we removed them, resulting in a <sup>fi</sup>nal sample size of 663.

The sample demographics were as follows: 51% were male and 48.4% female (0.6% did not report gender), 64.4% were 21 years old or younger, and 55.8% of them had no prior investment experience while only 0.5% said they had extensive prior investment experience. The full breakdown of the sample can be seen in Table 1.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst conducted a principal components analysis of the two variables of IOC and satisfaction. Since we expected the two factors to be correlated, we used a direct oblimin rotation on the two multi-item scales of satisfaction and IOC in order to con<sup>fi</sup>rm their convergent and discriminant validity and reliability. As seen in Table 2, the factor analysis resulted in two distinct factors and the items for each scale loaded heavily on their respective factors, con<sup>fi</sup>rming convergent and discriminant validities for both. Cronbach’s α values were also above 0.70 for both scales, which con<sup>fi</sup>rmed their reliability. Harman’s single-factor test revealed no critical problems with common method bias; the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor accounted for 50% of the variance but given that there were only two variables measured, we considered that acceptable (Podsakoff et al, 2003).

In addition, both constructs met the necessary conditions for being treated as re<sup>fl</sup>ective (Petter et al, 2007). First, the direction of causality is from the construct to its items for both IOC and satisfaction. Second, the items for each construct are unidimensional, that is, they measure the same thing, and are easily interchangeable. Third, the antecedents and consequences of the items for each construct are expected to be the same. Finally, the items for each construct are highly correlated with each other. Therefore, we averaged the scores of the three items for each of the two variables of satisfaction and IOC.

Given the experimental nature of our study, ANCOVA (or MANCOVA) is generally considered an appropriate and robust analysis method (Pedhazur and Schmelkin, 1991). To determine if we should use MANCOVA, we <sup>fi</sup>rst examined the correlation between overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior. The two variables showed a bivariate Pearson correlation of 0.188 at P<0.01 which, though signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, is considered small (Cohen, 1988). Therefore, we tested H1a through H3b using two full factorial ANCOVAs for overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (IOC) and risky behavior (amount invested) with the three experimental conditions as the independent variables, as well as prior experience with stock investments and gender as the covariates.

## Discussion

In this paper, we report the results of an experimental study that examined the possible negative impact of three DSS features that are traditionally considered bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the user: (1) the degree of choice provided to the user, (2) the presence of competition among users, and (3) system familiarity through training, on the users’ risky behavior. We also examined whether the underlying mechanism for this phenomenon is user overcon<sup>fi</sup>- dence, measured as IOC. In addition, we looked at the impact of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence on user satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome. We used a stock investment task and a tightly controlled experimental DSS for our study.

First, we found that all three DSS features can increase risky behavior in users. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally we found that designing a DSS so that it provides more choice, introduces competition among concurrent users, or increases system familiarity through training increased the amount invested in our random-outcome stock investment task. Furthermore, we found that this effect is additive. In other words, each of these factors impacted risky behavior independently of the other two. The actual differences in amounts invested were dramatic. As seen in Figure 3, subjects in the high degree of choice, presence of competition, and high system familiarity condition invested an average of \$8476, while those in the low degree of choice, no competition, and low system familiarity invested an average of \$2753, which corresponds to a 208% increase. All other conditions fell somewhere between those two extreme amounts. This result veri<sup>fi</sup>ed what we had predicted: keeping everything else constant, certain DSS features can induce users into riskier behavior.

Our results on the role of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence were less conclusive. We found that a DSS that provides more choice to the user can increase the user’s overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, that is, higher con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their investment decisions and greater perceived accuracy in their stock price predictions. However, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the hypotheses that presence of competition and increased system familiarity would have a similar effect. This was surprising, especially given the strong results on risky behavior. We also found no indication that overcon<sup>fi</sup>- dence mediates the impact of the DSS features on risky behavior.

## Theoretical implications

Our study has several important implications for theory, especially when it comes to potentially negative effects of DSS features on user decisions. It is a topic that we believe deserves additional attention, since in the extensive literature on systems and the impact of their design on user behavior (e.g., Todd & Benbasat, 1999; Davidson, 2002; Lilien et al, 2004; Kamis et al, 2008; Mohan & Jain, 2008), there has been less attention paid on how speci<sup>fi</sup>c system features can have unintentionally detrimental effects, such as introducing biases, on the decisions made by the users.

First, prior research based on the theory of DSS restrictiveness (Silver, 1990) has examined how system designers can increase or decrease the set of activities or information available to the user in order to improve decision making. For example, a DSS system could prevent the use of more complex models where their use might be damaging or it could require users to process a statistical forecast before being allowed to make decisions. Prior research has examined both sides. On one hand, decreasing restrictiveness by designing DSSs that generate multiple and appropriate options for decision makers can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial (e.g., Fazlollahi & Vahidov, 2001; Nakatsu & Benbasat, 2006). On the other hand, increasing restrictiveness may be necessary when designing a DSS that is more prescriptive in nature, especially in situations where the user requires more guidance and more aid in processing information (e.g., Wheeler & Valacich, 1996; Looney & Hardin, 2009). Our data indicate an additional reason why restrictiveness may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. On the basis of our results, increasing restrictiveness by limiting the number of choices that a DSS makes available to a decision maker may drastically reduce risky behavior and overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, and vice versa. While the traditional argument for restrictiveness is prescriptive, that is, a restrictive DSS helps guide the decision maker toward a wanted behavior, our study provides a proscriptive argument, that is, a restrictive DSS can prevent the decision maker from engaging in unwanted behavior.

Second, the use of system-enabled communication and competition has been found in prior research to improve user performance (e.g., Lam, 1997; Hilmer & Dennis, 2000). The idea is that by introducing competition, users can be motivated to perform to the best of their abilities in order to win. This is also one of the main underlying arguments behind the introduction of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques in the workplace (Kapp, 2012; Hamari, 2013). Our study provides some indication that system-enabled competition may sometimes be detrimental. Our results showed that users engaged in riskier behavior when they believed they were competing with other users than when they thought they were working alone. However, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd the same result for overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, indicating that the reason for the increase in risky behavior may be unrelated to the user’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence. One possibility may be the impact of competition on intrinsic motivation. Prior research has shown that competition can decrease intrinsic motivation (Reeve & Deci, 1996; Deci et al, 1999), which may reduce the anticipated impact of failure. In other words, if the users engage in the decision-making task with lower anticipated intrinsic rewards (such as personal ful<sup>fi</sup>llment, learning, or approval), they may not care if their decision has a higher chance of failure, thereby engaging in riskier behavior. Though somewhat counterintuitive (we tend to think of competition as a motivator), our results serve as a con<sup>fi</sup>rmation of prior research on competition’s negative impact on intrinsic rewards and provide a demonstration of its subsequent behavioral impact.

Finally, there is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant research stream on the importance of training and improving familiarity with a system for improved user performance (e.g., Cronan & Douglas, 1990; Sniezek et al, 2002). System familiarity can lead to better integration of a system with a user’s work (Saga & Zmud, 1993), reduce perceived dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in using the technology (Thompson et al, 1994), and contribute to better decisions made by the users (Sniezek et al, 2002; Lee et al, 2008). Our study, however, indicates that there may be an unintended negative consequence of increased system familiarity through training. Our subjects engaged in riskier behavior when they had a chance to practice with the system than when they used it for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time ever. An obvious explanation would be that the increased familiarity with the system results in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence because of a user miscalibration effect (Kasper, 1996). However, our results did not show an increase in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence. We believe that an alternative explanation is desensitization to risk. There is prior research that shows that prior exposure to high-risk situations without negative consequences can increase risky behavior (Bernile et al, 2014). In our study, our subjects performed four training sessions where any losses they incurred had no impact on their subsequent performance in the actual task. It is possible that this desensitized them to the level of risk involved, resulting in riskier behavior. If this is true, and further studies are necessary to con<sup>fi</sup>rm it, then it would provide reasons to be cautious in providing simulated training sessions to users where the impact of their actions during training is inconsequential.

The lack of tangible consequences for bad decisions may in fact be an explanation for the impact of the presence of competition on risky behavior but the lack of impact on overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence. The money our subjects were investing was not real but imaginary, with no real losses when they made bad investment decisions. As a result, when the DSS introduced competition, subjects may have been more likely to engage in risky behavior, not because they felt more con<sup>fi</sup>dent but because the task became a lossless game. The more they invested, the more likely they were to beat the competitors if they made the right investment choice, while losing money was ok because it was imaginary. Further research is necessary to determine if this is indeed the case, but the possibility hints at a larger issue. DSSs can act as a barrier between the decision makers and the real world effects of their decisions. They can reduce real-life problems into abstract numbers and <sup>fi</sup>gures. This creates a distance between the decision makers and the impact of their decisions, potentially rendering that impact less tangible in their minds. This can potentially encourage them to engage in riskier behavior with negative consequences.

Our study also contributes to the literature stream centered on the theory of DSS user calibration (Kasper, 1996). The theory posits that the design of a DSS can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between the user’s objective decision quality and their subjective con<sup>fi</sup>dence in that decision. In some cases, a DSS can result in miscalibration of the two, resulting in users who exhibit overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and who make unnecessarily risky decisions (Kasper, 1996). When the DSS can be designed in a way that it effectively supports the user’s symbolic representation in problem solving, users can exhibit perfect calibration (Beemer & Gregg, 2013). In other words, user calibration occurs when the symbols and actions of the DSS, speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally expressiveness, visibility, and inquirability, are parallel to those of users’ natural system of symbolic representation. Our study contributes to the calibration literature in two ways. First, we empirically examine the impact of three speci<sup>fi</sup>c DSS features, as opposed to system symbols. Second, we focus on pre-decision overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence as a possible explanation for the link between a DSS’s features and risky behavior. Given that we only found support for the degree of choice provided by the DSS, the impact of system familiarity and competition remains an open question.

Our study focused on the users’ overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, but other variables may play a role in risky behavior. There is a wealth of research in IS on the role of emotions in usersystem interaction (e.g., Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Anderson & Agarwal, 2011). Typically, studies have looked at the role of emotions, such as enjoyment and <sup>fl</sup>ow (Koufaris, 2002) or technostress and anxiety (Thatcher & Perrewe, 2002; Tarafdar et al, 2007), in user adoption or use of technology. The lack of support in our study for the role of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence as a mediator between the three DSS features and risky behavior may mean that the underlying mechanism is based on other emotions that we did not measure. For example, in the high system familiarity condition, the intrinsic enjoyment of using the system and engaging in the task may lead to addictive behavior. When people participate in an activity where gain and loss are involved, such as gambling, they will have higher levels of repeated engagement that could lead to addictive behavior that is increasingly riskier (Ladouceur, 2004; Ng & Wiemer-Hastings, 2005). In the high familiarity condition of our study, subjects engage with the task repeatedly, albeit only four times, which might lead to a mild form of addiction that is independent of their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their ability to pick the right stock. In other words, with each iteration of the task, they invest more money to increase the enjoyment or thrill they get from it. This may be exacerbated when competition is also thrown in the mix.

Our study also contributes to the theoretical discussion on the tension between positive and negative outcomes of giving users more control, whether real or perceived. Need for control is inherent in human nature. Under uncertain situations, people want to gain control over all possible things to lessen perceived risk and increase the possibility of success (Rhee et al, 2005). In other words, having control brings a sense of safety for people to minimize the possible threats, lead to speci<sup>fi</sup>c desired outcomes, and thereby enhance satisfaction. Hence, prior studies on IOC have found that even in a situation in which the outcomes are uncontrollable (e.g., rolling dice), satisfaction may always exist if people gain control. Our results on the impact of IOC on satisfaction with the decision-making process and outcome con<sup>fi</sup>rm that. Furthermore, people with high IOC also have higher motivation and persistence (goal striving) as well as better mental health because of a positive attitude toward sustaining life (Fenton O’Creevy et al, 2003). Of course, an in<sup>fl</sup>ated sense of control can also have negative consequences. For example, the IOC may blur the distinction between chance and skill, cause insensitivity to feedback, impede learning, and encourage greater risk taking as some of our own results have shown (Langer, 1975; Burger, 1986; Dixon, 2000).

## Practical implications

Our results highlight some important issues that should be of concern to companies, designers of DSSs, and their users. They are especially troublesome given the task we used: investing in company stock. Since the majority of investment decisions or other resource allocation decisions are made using some form of DSS, our results should act as a warning to the designers of those systems to be aware of the potential biases that certain system features can introduce in the users’ minds. In addition, system users and their managers should be aware that their decisions may have been biased based on the level of choice, competition, and familiarity the DSS provided them with.

For example, organizations may design their DSSs to encourage competition. This is especially relevant today, with the proliferation of gami<sup>fi</sup>cation techniques in the workplace. Systems are designed to provide both transparency of performance and status rewards based on that performance. The intention is to motivate employees, such as sales managers, equity traders, and franchise owners, to maximize their efforts and quality of work. However, as our results have shown, embedding competition in the system may increase risky behavior by users, with possible negative effects on organizational performance. Companies that introduce competition should also provide mechanisms that counter risky behavior, such as implementing triggers that automatically warn users when they are being overly risky.

Our results also have implications for how companies deal with both training in and prolonged use of systems by employees. Our study used a system that was new to our subjects in order to show how increased familiarity through training impacted risky behavior. On the basis of our results, we would advise companies to be careful of the potential negative impact of simulated training sessions. Since such simulations do not have real consequences, employees may be desensitized to the potential risks or outcomes of their actions. As a result, when they start actually working with the system, they may engage in risky behavior because of underestimating risk. This may also be a problem with prolonged use of a system, where employees become overly familiar with the systems they use, to the extent that the consequences of their actions when using them become abstract and intangible. Paradoxically, this may call for additional intermittent training in how decisions made when using the system can impact the organization as a whole.

Many companies may not choose the features of the DSSs they install and provide to their employees or they may have limited options. For example they may have to embed competition in the system, because of the nature of their activities. Given the potential negative impact on user behavior that our results have shown, we would advise companies to take proactive steps, especially when introducing a new DSS. One possible way may be to educate employees and users about the potential negative impact on their behavior by demonstrating decision-making biases in training sessions. Another may be to monitor employee behavior and when excessive risk taking is detected, to interrupt it either by limiting the amount of risk-taking possible over a threshold, or by using a reward system for behavior that is not excessively risky.

## Limitations

There are some important limitations to our study. First, being an experimental study, our results have limited generalizability. This is especially true given the simplicity of the DSS we designed and used in the study. However, this simplicity was intentional and key to answering our research questions. We wanted to isolate and test the impact of three system features: degree of choice, presence of competition, and system familiarity through training. In order to be able to exclude any alternative explanations for our results, we needed to make sure we used a system that allowed us to manipulate only those three features. Any real DSS would provide too many opportunities for subjects to use it differently and it would have introduced too many confounding explanations for the variance in our dependent variables. Having said that, we do concede that the generalizability of our results is limited. What we can claim is that any DSS that introduces one of the three features, through any change in its interface or functionality, could result in increased risky behavior by its users in certain tasks that resemble the task in our study. We encourage further research using more realistic and more complex DSSs. The increased functionality and the ability to perform additional analyses before making decisions could exacerbate the phenomenon of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and risky behavior, though empirical research is necessary to con<sup>fi</sup>rm this.

Second, the subjects were investing with imaginary money and their performance was not tied to any compensation. This limited the realism of the situation for them and lowered the stakes for risky behavior. Prior research in experimental economics has demonstrated that monetary rewards can decrease the variance around the predicted outcome (Smith & Walker, 1993). However, given that the differences between our experimental conditions were large and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we believe that this limitation was not detrimental to our results. It is reasonable to expect that if subjects did not care about the outcome of their investment task, because there was no monetary reward, then we would not have observed those differences. It is also important to note that the subjects were rewarded with course credit for their participation, albeit uniformly and not dependent upon performance.

Third, we manipulated system familiarity by asking subjects to engage in four practice rounds using the experimental DSS. One may argue that four practice rounds are not enough to increase familiarity. We would, however, note the distinction between familiarity with the task and familiarity with the system. In our study, we agree that familiarity with the task may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciently increased after four practice rounds, especially given that prior investment experience varies among our subjects. However, we do not hypothesize on the impact of familiarity with the task but of familiarity with the system. We would argue that since the subjects in the low familiarity condition have never seen the experimental DSS before, they will be, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, less familiar than the subjects in the high familiarity condition, who bene<sup>fi</sup>t from four practice rounds. Even if that increase in familiarity is small, we still see that it can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase risky behavior by the users. Nevertheless, the small number of practice rounds may be one explanation for the lack of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in our results.

Fourth, our subjects are undergraduate students and not actual investors. The majority of them lack speci<sup>fi</sup>c domain expertise, that is, prior investment experience. In our study, however, our main focus is not the impact of domain expertise on behavior but the impact of system features on behavior. Our study is consistent with a long tradition in IS research that has employed undergraduate students as surrogates to test performance when using a system (e.g., Speier & Morris, 2003; Allen & Parsons, 2010; Adipat et al, 2011). Having said that, we do agree that domain expertise can have an impact on our subjects’ behavior and we acknowledge that our subjects’ domain expertise varies (as our data descriptive statistics show in Table 1). This is why we have included domain expertise (measured as prior experience in investments) as a covariate in all of our analyses. Our results show that prior experience does matter, but even when we account for it, the system features we study have a large impact on risky behavior (and less so on overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence). This is consistent with prior research that has shown that the impact of prior knowledge or domain expertise on overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence can vary. Some studies have shown that experts and novices display the same amount of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (Shepherd et al, 2003). Others have shown that prior experience-based knowledge can increase overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence in <sup>fi</sup>nancial investment and forecasting decisions (McKenzie et al, 2008; Van De Venter & Michayluk, 2008; Vetter et al, 2011). Future research could use experienced investors as subjects to con<sup>fi</sup>rm our results in a more realistic setting. It could also alter the task from an investment task to another resource allocation task where the outcome is still largely random. For example, a budget allocation task for managers of multiple teams could test our model in a different but still realistic context.

The random walk hypothesis is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial theory that states that stock market prices evolve according to a random walk, which means that they increase or decrease randomly. According to this theory, past stock prices are of no value in forecasting future prices because past, current, and future prices merely re<sup>fl</sup>ect market responses to information that comes into the market at random. In short, price movements are no more predictable than the pattern of the walk of a drunk.

There are also other theories that state that stock prices may be predicted to some degree, based on knowing speci<sup>fi</sup>c information from the past, such as the changes in stock prices in the prior 5 years, earnings announcements over the prior 6 months, or market volatility. However, there is no single accepted theory on how stock prices evolve in markets.
