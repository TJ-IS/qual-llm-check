# An Empirical Examination of the Influence of Biased Personalized Product Recommendations on Consumers’ Decision Making Outcomes

## ABSTRACT

To assist consumers in product search and selection while shopping online, many e-commerce retailers have implemented web-based product recommendation agents (PRAs). However, consumers are empowered to the extent that the PRAs provide true personalization by recommending products based solely on, and thus best representing, consumers’ preferences. This study constructs and empirically tests a theoretical model that examines how biased recommendations from PRAs influence consumers decision quality and decision effort. The results of an online experiment show that consumers are extremely vulnerable to biased personalized recommendations from online PRAs. In addition, our results extend prior research by identifying perceived personalization as a critical mechanism driving the influence of biased PRA on consumers’ decision quality and decision effort. This study fills a void in the literature and calls attention to an insidious form of manipulation made possible by innovative technologies supporting e-commerce.

Keywords: Product Recommendation Agent, Personalization, Bias, Decision Making, Manipulative Practices

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Today’s digital marketplaces offer consumers great convenience, immense product choice, and large amounts of product-related information. However, as a result of the cognitive constraints of human information processing, finding products that satisfy consumers’ needs and/or interests is not an easy task. Many online stores have implemented technological tools to assist consumers in product search and type of web personalization technology that provides individual consumers with product recomm ndations based on their product-related needs and preferences expressed (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007, 2014, 2015). PRAs have the potential to help consumers make effective purchase decisions (Haubl & Trifts, 2000; Maes, 1994) and reduce consumers’ uncertainty about buying products online (Hong & Pavlou, 2014).

Personalized recommendations is an e-commerce feature that is highly valued by consumers and also help online companies create brand loyalty (Limelight, 2010). This explains the phenomenal increase in the adoption of PRAs by online merchants in various dustries (e.g., Amazon, eBay, Apple) (Chau, Ho, Ho, & Yao, 2013). Despite the potential for PRAs to aid consumers in decision making, consumers are empowered to the extent that the PRAs provide true personalization by recommending products based solely on and thus best represent consumers’ preferences (Hill, King, & Cohen, 1996; King & Hill, 1994). Just as a salesperson may simultaneously serve two principals (i.e., the client and the employing company) (Cupach & Carson, 2002; Kurland, 1996), a PRA may also be designed to serve the interest of its users (i.e., consumers) as well as that of its provider, creating opportunities for competing loyalties (Cupach & Carson, 2002). The objectives and economic incentives of the PRA providers (e.g., online merchants) are not always necessarily aligned with those of the consumers (Murray & Haubl, 2008). To balance their competing goals of aiding consumers in decision making and generating more business opportunities (Allen & Wu, 2010; Chau et al., 2013), online merchants may implement PRAs to provide recommendations that are not solely preference-matched to benefit consumers but instead are biased toward their own interests (Chau et al., 2013; Xiao & Benbasat, 2011, 2015). This may result in recommendations for products from certain vendors and/or with certain characteristics (e.g., high profitmargin products, soon-to-be discontinued products) so as to attain higher-than-usual profits (in terms of mark-ups, commissions, referral fees, etc.) (Kurland, 1991; Oakes, 1990) or reduce losses.

There have already been reported incidents of online companies (e.g., Amazon, Hotel.de) using PRAs to provide biased recommendations to consumers (Allen & Wu, 2010; Riemer & Lehrke, 2009; Wingfield & Pereira, 2002). Prior academic research has also observed that PRAs have the potential to Curley, & Zhang, 2013; Aksoy & Bloom, 2001; Cosley, Lam, Albert, Konstan, & Riedl, 2003; Haubl & Murray, 2003; Haubl & Murray, 2006). However, to our knowledge, there has been limited understanding of the underlying mechanisms by which biased recommendations influence consumers’ decision making outcomes in online buying, which is a gap the current study aims to fill.

Drawing on the theoretical perspective of personalization, this study examines how biased personalized product recommendations from PRAs influence co umers’ decision quality and decision effort when shopping online. It further explores the role pe ceived personalization plays in mediating the impact of biased PRAs on consumer decision making. Specifically, this study compares the relative impact of biased personalized recommendations with that of biased non-personalized recommendations (based on false claim of popularity) on consumers’ decision quality and decision effort. We posit that the availability of a PRA on a website may enhance a consumer’s belief that due to personalization (be it true or not) her product search would be more effective and efficient. If the biased personalized recommendations do not result in improved objective decision quality for the consumer, while at the same time these recommendations lead the consumer to believe that her product choice is an optimal one, leading to her continued reliance on the PRA, then unscrupulous online merchants will have a powerful means with which they can influence consumer decision making towards the merchants’ own benefits. With its focus on undesirable consequences of PRA use, this study answers the call for more academic research into the dark side of information technology (IT) (Tarafdar, Gupta, & Turel, 2015).

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the study’s theoretical foundation and develops the hypotheses. The research method and results of hypothesis testing are reported in sections 3 and 4. And the paper concludes with a discussion of the results, limitations, and contributions of the study and suggests areas for future research.

## 2. THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS AND HYPOTHESES DEVELOPMENT

This section first reviews the theoretic perspective of personalization, which lays the theoretical foundation for this study. It then presents our research model and introduces associated hypotheses.

## 2.1 Theoretical Perspective of Personalization

While the idea of tailoring products and services to consumers’ needs existed long before the inception of the Internet (T. Li & Unger, 2012), advances in Internet technologies have made it possible to effectively offer personalized products and services on rander scale. Personalization is defined as the ‘‘use of information about a particular user that provides tailored or personalized services for the user’’ (Serino, Furner, & Smatt, 2005, p. 1). Using a consumer’s demographics, shopping history, and preference information (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007), websites with PRAs can generate recommendations that are personalized to her needs.

Prior research reveals that personalized recommendations have significant power in influencing consumers’ decision making (Tam & Ho, 2005, 2006). They are more accepted by consumers than nonpersonalized recommendations (i.e., those generated without consideration of consumers’ personal preferences) (Senecal & Nantel, 2004), lead to more clicks than random suggestions (De Pechpeyrou, 2009), and result in higher user ratings when compared to random recommendations (Panniello, Ardito, & Petruzzelli, 2017). In mobile commerce, they lead to a significant increase in items viewed/sold and overall sales (Jannach & Hegelich, 2009).

The power of personalized recommendations in influencing consumer decision making may be explained by the high level of perceived personalization, defined as the extent to which a consumer believes that a personalization system understands and represents her personal preferences (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). Komiak and Benbasat (2006) found that a PRA perceived by consumers to better understand their product related preferences and needs (i.e., is more personalized) enhanced their perception of the consensus between their own decision making and that of the PRA, and encouraged them to accept the recommendation of the PRA. Liang, Li, and Turban (2009) noted that personalized recommendations induced intimate feelings (consisting of caring, commitment, c munication, comfort, and trust) in consumers, which in turn enhanced consumers’ attitude toward the recommendations.

Perceived personalization by a PRA can also positively shape consumers’ attitudes toward the well as Komiak and Benbasat (2006), found that perceived personalization significantly increased systems. Kumar and Benbasat (2006) showed that the provision of personalized recommendations increased both the perceived usefulness and social presence of the e-commerce website. A longitudinal experiment by de Pechpeyrou (2009) also showed that consumers’ attitude toward personalization positively influenced their attitude toward the website implementing personalized merchandising. Li (2016) further noted that it is perceived personalization, rather than actual personalization, that explained message effectiveness.

Personalization is essentially system-driven (Sundar & Marathe, 2010), as consumers typically do not have high control over the actual inputs requested by the PRA (e.g., consumer preferences specifically considered by the PRA), its processes (e.g., algorithm for generating recommendations), or output (e.g., list of recommendations presented). This creates ambiguity regarding the veracity of the personalization: i.e., whether the recommendations provided by the personalization system are indeed generated solely on the basis of the consumers’ preferences.

Extant research on personalization systems have been largely focused on their consumer benefits (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007, 2011, 2015). The current study addresses this gap in literature by examining the impact on consumer decision making of a personalization system (PRA, to be more specific) designed to bias consumers into choosing products that they would not otherwise have considered without using such system. By unravelling a potential mechanism, namely perceived personalization, that underlies the impact of biased PRAs, it adds to the limited body of research on the dynamics and effects of PRA bias, a type of strategic manipulation performed on the generation of information (Xiao & Benbasat, 2011).

## 2.2.1 Type of Recommendations

This study compares consumers’ decision making outcomes when they receive (1) biased nonpersonalized recommendations, or (2) biased personalized product recommendations from a PRA. We are particularly interested in examining how perceived personalization underlies the effect of biased PRAs on consumer judgment and decision making.

In the “biased non-personalized recommendations” condition, a sub-set of available products that are promoted or “pushed” by the online store<sup>2</sup> are displayed before other, non-promoted products on the e-commerce website, with the false claim that the products are sorted in best-selling (or popular) order.

In contrast, in the “biased personalized recommendations” condition, a sub-set of available products promoted or “pushed” by the online store are recommended by a PRA to consumers and displayed on the e-commerce website before other non-promoted products<sup>3</sup>. Within both sub-sets (i.e., promoted and non-promoted), products are arranged in descending order of how well they fit customers’ needs obtained in the PRA’s preference-elicitation process (see section 3.1 and Table 1 for full details).

## 2.2.2 Consumer Decision Making Outcomes

In line with the taxonomy of decision effectiveness proposed by Lilien et al. (2004) and following the practice of prior research assessing consumers’ decision performance (Tan, Teo, & Benbasat, 2010), we focus on both subjective and objective assessment of consumers’ decision quality and decision effort (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007, 2014). Specifically, the dependent variables of our investigation are: 1) choice confidence, defined as the extent to which a consumer feels confident in the quality of product choice she has made at an e-commerce website; 2) product choice, which refers to the actual product selection made at the e-commerce website and measured as whether or not the consumer chooses a promoted product (i.e., a product from the sub-set of products promoted by the online store); 3) perceived cognitive effort, defined as a consumer’s assessment of the cost associated with evaluating and choosing products at the ecommerce website; and 4) extent of product search, an objective measure of effort, which refers to the total number of products a consumer examines at the e-commerce website prior to making her choice.

## 2.3 Hypothesis Development

Findings from previous behavioral research reveal that consumers are “cognitive misers” who aim to exert as little cognitive effort as possible when retrieving and processing information (Costley & Brucks, 1992). To reduce the cognitive processing effort, consumers are known to rely on the wisdom of the crowd when making purchase decisions (e.g., Chen & Xie, 2008; Jiménez & Mendoza, 2013; Park, Lee, & Han, 2007). And just as personalized recommendations can be biased to influence consumers product choices, non-personalized recommendations (generally based on sales volume or consumer

ratings) can also be manipulated to mislead consumers. However, we expect that the impact of biased non-personalized recommendations on consumers’ decision quality and decision effort will not be as pronounced as that of biased personalized recommendations. After all, products liked by others, no matter how good they are, may not be perceived by all consumers as fitting their own product-related needs. The fact that the PRA elicits customers’ personal preferences (see Appendix) adds to the impression that it is making an honest effort to understand and represent consumers’ needs. This elevated perception of PRA personalization will increase consumers’ identification with and trust in PRAs (Kramer, Hanna, Su, & Wei, 2001). Furthermore, in a website providing biased personalized recommendations, products are organized such that a consumer can easily observe and ascertain (via intra-list comparison) that a product higher on the recommendation list more closely reflects her preferences (unless the consumer engages in extensive search by examining products appearing in latter pages, in which case better-fitting products may be located). The enhanced perception of personalization not only increases the likelihood that consumers will choose from among the promoted produc recommended by the PRA (thus leading to sub-optimal product choices) but also elevates consumers’ confidence in their product choices.

To exert as little cognitive processing effort as possible, consumers may selectively ignore certain aids (e.g., in the form of product recom mendations) at e-commerce websites, consumers have been found to rely mostly on the efforts of the decision aids rather than on their own effort in engaging in a thorough decision making process (Xiao & Benbasat, 2007, 2014). In the context of our study, while both types of biased recommendations are expected to lead to less extensive product search and reduced cognitive effort, consumers receiving biased personalized recommendations may nevertheless be “fooled” into believing that, in addition to making their product searching and selection easier, the PRA does recommend products that best fit their expressed preferences. The heightened level of personalization perceived by consumers may also motivate them to further reduce their own search effort and rely on the PRA’s recommendations when making purchase decisions. ## 3. RESEARCH METHOD

An online experiment is conducted to test the effects of different types of recommendations on consumer decision making.

The independent variable, type of recommendations, consists of two levels: Biased personalized recommendations and biased non-personalized recommendations.

## 3.3 Sample

Participants for this study were 59 e-commerce shoppers recruited from a North American panel maintained by a marketing research firm, which emailed invitations to participate in the study to its members. The firm also provided subjects with a point-based incentive (redeemable for various prizes) for their participation in the study.

## 3.4 Experimental Task and Procedures

All participants were randomly assigned to one of the two experimental groups. They were told that a limited selection of digital cameras was currently on sale at an online camera store. They were asked to visit the online camera store, explore the alternatives, and choose a digital camera as a gift for a friend, Pat. They were provided with Pat’s camera-related preferences (see Appendix) and asked to use them to evaluate the PRA’s product recommendations<sup>7,8</sup>. Participants were informed that, at the end of the task session, they would be asked a few questions about their shopping experience at the e-commerce website. To motivate participants to take the shopping task seriously, participants were informed before the experiment that the top 25% performers in justifying product choices (i.e., how the products chosen satisfy Pat’s preferences) would be entered into a draw to get one of twelve \$50 gift certificates.

## 4.2.4 Mediation Analyses

Mediation analysis was conducted to explore the potential role perceived personalization plays in mediating the influence of type of recommendations on consumers’ decision making outcomes. In particular, we aimed to confirm our conjecture that a high level of personalization perceived by participants contributes to their increased likelihood to choose promoted products (indicating suboptimal purchase decision), enhanced choice confidence, reduced product search, and lowered perception of cognitive effort.

The causal steps approach to testing mediation popularized by Baron and Kenny (1986), which focuses on examining the individual paths in a mediation model, has been heavily criticized on multiple grounds (such as low statistical power and underestimated Type I error) (see Hayes, 2009; Zhao, Lynch Jr, & Chen, 2010). In this study, consistent with modern practice in testing indirect influence advocated by Mackinnon, Preacher, Hayes and others (e.g., Hayes, 2009; MacKinnon, Lockwood, Hoffman, West, & Sheets, 2002; Preacher & Hayes, 2004), we adopted the more rigorous and powerful bootstrapping method (using Process for SPSS 2.16.3 by Andrew Hayes, with 5000 samples) to assess the mediating role of perceived personalization. Bootstrapping, which is a nonparametric resampling technique, has two advantages over Baron and Kenny’s (1986) approach (Vance, Benjamin Lowry, & Eggett, 2015): First, indirect effect is measured directly in the bootstrapping method, rather than merely inferred to exist through a sequence of tests. Second, the bootstrapping method imposes no assumption of the normality of the dataset, and thus is recommended for studies with small sample size (as in the case of our study).

The results of our analysis (see Table 9) reveal that perceived personalization significantly carries the influence of the independent variable, type of recommendations (with biased non-personalized recommendations coded as “0” and biased personalized recommendations coded as “1”), on all the dependent variables (with the exception of extent of product search). ## 5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

This section summarizes the major findings of this study, presents its contributions to research and practice, and discusses its limitations.

## 5.1 Discussion of Findings

Examining how biased web-based product recommendations (particularly biased personalized recommendations from online PRAs) influence consumers’ decision making outcomes, this study validates the importance of perceived personalization as an important underlying mechanism driving such influence.

Our results show that type of recommendations exerts significant influence on consumers decision making outcomes. More specifically, while consumers receiving biased personalized

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

recommendations are more inclined to choose sub-optimal products (an indication of lower objective decision quality) when compared to those receiving biased non-personalized recommendations, they will also paradoxically perceive enhanced effectiveness and efficiency of their own decision making, as demonstrated by higher choice confidence, less extensive product search, and lower perceived cognitive effort. In addition, our results reveal that an important mechanism underlying the influence of type of recommendations on consumers’ actual product choice, confidence in their product choice, and perceived cognitive effort is their perception that the recommendations received are personalized to their individual preferences and needs. An unexpected finding is the non-significant effect of perceived personalization in mediating the impact of type of recommendations on consumers’ extent of product search. This suggests that, while biased personalized recommendations influence consumers’ product search, they do so directly rather than through the operation of perceived personalization.

Additional analysis of our data shows that, 73% of the products viewed by consumers receiving biased non-personalized recommendations are promoted products (with 27% being dominant products), suggesting that some consumers in this condition have ventured beyond the first eight pages (on which only promoted products are listed) to check on additional alternatives. In contrast, 93% of products viewed by consumers provided with biased personalized recommendations condition are promoted products, indicating a very high level of confidence in the products the PRA recommended. Furthermore, consumers receiving biased personalized recommendations have significantly lower perception of the website acting contrary to their best interests<sup>13</sup> compared to those receiving biased non-personalized recommendations (M = 2.255 vs. 2.879, t = 2.211, $\mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 5 )$ . Insomuch as biased personalized recommendations lead to impairment in actual (i.e., objective) decision quality (indicated by consumers’ choice of sub-optimal products), enhancement in choice confidence (i.e., perceived decision quality), and lack of awareness of the bias, these findings reveal the serious risk consumers face when shopping with biased PRAs.

## 5.2 Contributions to Research and Practice

This study makes significant contributions to research in several ways. Overall, it is the first to our knowledge to illustrate how effecting perceived personalization in the context of PRAs could be used as a tactic to increase the acceptance of deceptive recommendations. The findings of this study provide strong evidence for the power of biased personalized recommendations in influencing consumers’ decision making outcomes. By validating the role perceived personalization plays in facilitating such influence, this study has also unraveled the mechanism by which biased personalized recommendations function. More specifically, the results of this study show that biased personalized recommendations using PRAs, which result in sub-optimal product choice, nevertheless create the illusion of improved decision quality (an important predictor of consumers’ intention to shop at an e-commerce website) as indicated by customers’ increased choice confidence. This, when coupled with the finding that the influence of biased personalized recommendations on consumer decision making can be explained by the high level of perceived personalization, accentuates the danger of the insidious form of manipulation made possible by e-commerce personalization technologies. To our knowledge, this is the first study that draws on the theoretical perspective of personalization to shed light on consumer vulnerability to biased PRA recommendations in e-commerce product websites. Via an empirical investigation of potential danger of personalization systems in e-commerce, this study contributes to a unique perspective on IS research into the dark side of IT (e.g., Biros, George, & Zmud, 2002; Grazioli & Jarvenpaa, 2000, 2003; W. Hong & Thong, 2013; Oh, Agrawal, & Rao, 2013; Xiao & Benbasat, 2011, 2015).

The findings demonstrate that biased personalized recommendations are particularly powerful in influencing consumer decision making at the expense of consumers. It is thus important for government agencies, industry leaders, and consumer organizations to not only promote consumers’ awareness of this novel manipulative practice made possible by e-commerce technologies but also educate consumers (via informative brochures or training courses, online and/or offline) on strategies to address and reduce such risks (Xiao & Benbasat, 2011). Additionally, given consumers’ vulnerability to biased recommendations from online PRAs, effective interventions (such as, contextualized warning messages) need to be devised to help consumers detect such bias more easily (Xiao & Benbasat, 2015).

Furthermore, it is imperative for industry leaders to establish guidelines of good business practices online (Xiao & Benbasat, 2011). For instance, online companies may be required to provide an explanation of the PRA’s (or any personalized recommendation systems) reasoning logic (Wang & recommended and an explanation of how that score is calculated, and/or provide functionalities allowing consumers to sort/search/compare all products by their choice of feature. Strong, enforceable selfregulation and self-policing by industry members are critical to addressing the growing problem of ecommerce manipulation, as unethical merchant behavior affects not only consumers but also honest businesses online. After all, if consumers are reluctant to make purchases online for fear of being swindled, online businesses as a whole suffer from loss in sales and reputation.

## 5.3 Limitations and Future Research

This study has some limitations that in turn open up a number of interesting avenues for future research. First, this study focuses on transaction-driven, content-based personalization systems (which offer personalized recommendations based on consumer preferences) (Ho, Davern, & Tam, 2008). Future research may explore the influence of other types of online personalization systems (see Ho et al., 2008). Second, the study examines only one way by which online PRAs provide biased product recommendations to consumers (i.e., recommending promoted products over products that best fit consumers’ preferences), leaving other potential types of manipulation techniques (see Xiao & Benbasat, 2011) open for investigation. Third, the experimental websites in this study have more products (96 in total) and more product pages (16 pages in total) than those in many other e-commerce studies (e.g., Haubl & Murray, 2003; M.-X. Li, Tan, Teo, & Wei, 2010). Nevertheless, this is done to simulate consumers’ experience at real e-commerce websites, where they typically encounter hundreds of

alternatives in a product category. In addition, participants in our study needed to wade through 8 pages of promoted products to reach a dominant product. While we designed Pat’s profile such that products best matching Pat’s preferences were the dominant products, rather than the promoted products, we acknowledge the possibility of some participants finding (and ultimately choosing) a promoted product that matches their brand preferences perfectly and other camera-related needs closely. Future studies can explore whether (and how) consumers’ vulnerability to biased recommendations is influenced by the number of product alternatives and their placement in product pages at e-commerce websites. Fourth, our finding reveals nonsignificant indirect effect of type of recommendations on participants’ extent of product search via perceived personalization, which is contrary to what is expected and warrants additional investigation in future research into mechanisms (e.g., conscientiousness) driving participants product search behavior. Further, while all but one of our hypotheses are supported by data, future research can explore other potential mediating or moderating variables that influence the impact of type increase the power of hypothesis tests).

## 5.4 Conclusion

Drawing on the theoretical perspective of personalization, this study constructs and tests a research model that examines how consumers’ decision making outcomes are influenced by biased product recommendations when shopping online. The results of this study have not only confirmed the effect of biased personalized recommendations on consumers’ decision quality and decision effort, but they have also extended prior research by identifying perceived personalization as the underlying mechanism explaining the influence of biased PRAs. With enriched understanding of consumer vulnerability to biased web-based product recommendations (a shadowy but very real phenomenon), this study fills a void in the literature and calls attention to the unsavory side of e-commerce.

## An Empirical Examination of the Influence of Biased Personalized Product Recommendations on Consumers’ Decision Making Outcomes

## Highlights

 Consumers are extremely vulnerable to biased web-based product recommendations, whether they are personalized or non-personalized.

 Consumers receiving biased recommendations are not aware of the bias.

While biased personalized recommendations lead to impairment in actual (i.e., objective) decision quality, they result in enhanced choice confidence (i.e., perceived decision quality) as well as reduced decision effort (both subjective and objective).

 Perceived personalization is a critical mechanism driving the influence of biased recommendations on consumers’ decision making outcomes
