# DESIGNING DIGITAL MARKET OFFERINGS: HOW DIGITAL VENTURES NAVIGATE THE TENSION BETWEEN GENERATIVE DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY AND THE CURRENT ENVIRONMENT<sup>1</sup>

Julian Lehmann KIN Center for Digital Innovation, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam Amsterdam, THE NETHERLANDS {j.c.lehmann@vu.nl}

Jan Recker Hamburg Business School, University of Hamburg Hamburg, GERMANY {jan.christof.recker@uni-hamburg.de}

Youngjin Yoo Weatherhead School of Management, Case Western University Cleveland, OH, U.S.A. {yxy23@case.edu}

Christoph Rosenkranz Cologne Institute for Information Systems, University of Cologne Cologne, GERMANY {rosenkranz@wiso.uni-koeln.de}

Digital ventures must navigate a key tension as they design new digital market offerings—that is, products or services that are embodied in digital technologies or enabled by them. On the one hand, digital ventures pursue a vision that builds on what might be possible through the generative potential that digital technology offers; on the other hand, they face an environment in the here and now, with existing customer preferences, extant regulations, and legacy technology. Taking a designing view, we trace how six independent digital ventures in the German financial services industry dealt with this tension as they created their digital market offerings. Our findings suggest that digital ventures enact three designing mechanisms to resolve the tension: bounding the technology scope, transposing through digital objects, and probing the solution space. Through these mechanisms, digital ventures construct a buffer—one that has functional, material, and temporal dimensions—between the vision they gradually realize through their market offering and the here-and-now conditions of the environment that digital ventures enter.

Keywords: Digital entrepreneurship, designing, tension, digital market offering, case study

## Introduction

The introduction of a new market offering marks a milestone for an entrepreneurial venture that is essential for its survival and growth (Schoonhoven et al., 1990). Digital ventures— professionally funded and privately owned young firms (Garg & Eisenhardt, 2017) that bring about digital market offerings—are no exception.

However, the introduction of digital market offerings—that is, the purposeful configuration of digital technology components to create value—present digital ventures with a dilemma (Smith & Lewis, 2011): Digital offerings leverage generative digital technology components that provide the ventures seemingly unbounded possibilities for recombination, rapid scaling, and continuous innovation (von Briel et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021; Nambisan, 2017), but digital ventures must situate their offerings in an existing environment with extant customer preferences, regulations, incumbent firms, and legacy technology (Ansari et al., 2016; Hargadon & Douglas, 2001).<sup>2</sup>

How do digital ventures deal with this dilemma? Research provides only partial answers. While studies on digital innovation consider generativity to be a source of combinatorial innovation and explain how generativity can be leveraged (Eaton et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2017; Jarvenpaa & Standaert, 2018), they often do not consider the environment in which digital ventures must operate. As a result, the literature creates an unrealistic expectation that digital ventures can—and should—pursue the generativity of digital technology however they see fit.

We find this premise to be problematic: Pursuing generativity at all costs may trigger adversarial reactions from competitors, leave customers and complementors overwhelmed, or render market offerings incompatible with the prevalent industry structure. Therefore, neglecting the environment into which digital ventures enter distorts our understanding of how generativity manifests and how it impacts value creation.

To understand how digital ventures resolve this dilemma, we adopt a designing view (Berglund et al., 2020; Boland & Collopy, 2004; Sarasvathy et al., 2008; Simon, 1996). Designing recognizes that digital market offerings are artifacts (Simon, 1996)—man-made, purposefully configured digital objects (Faulkner & Runde, 2019)— designed by digital ventures in relation to the environment they enter but also with the goal of changing existing situations into preferred ones (Berglund et al., 2020; Simon, 1996). The designing view draws attention to the key tension that constitutes the dilemma that digital ventures face: As these ventures construct their offerings, should they focus on current environmental conditions or explore the use of generative digital technology that will address future needs? A designing view facilitates analysis of the concrete actions through which digital ventures navigate this dilemma (Buchanan, 2015; Yoo et al., 2006). The research question we ask is: How do digital ventures navigate the tension between digital technology generativity and the existing environment in the design of digital market offerings?

We conducted a grounded, inductive, multiple-case study of six digital fintech ventures operating in the Rhine-Main area of the German financial services industry, a global top-ten entrepreneurial ecosystem (Startup Genome, 2018). Over three years, we observed the concrete actions that these six ventures took to create and introduce their digital market offerings. All six ventures were caught between the generativity promise of emergent digital technologies, such as artificial intelligence or distributed ledger technology, and the salient conditions of the existing environment, such as legacy banking systems, high levels of regulation, and dependence on incumbent organizations. Based on our case analysis, we develop new theory on how digital ventures deal with these opposing forces.

Our study makes several contributions to the literature. First, we offer the literature on digital innovation a theoretical model that outlines three designing mechanisms—bounding the technology scope, transposing through digital objects, and probing the solution space—through which digital ventures navigate the tension they face. These mechanisms resolve specific aspects of the tension, such as uncertainty about digital technologies, connecting with legacy systems, and dealing with extant regulation, which allows digital ventures to place their market offerings in the environment. Departing from the prevailing notion that digital ventures are largely unbounded (Autio et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2021), our study shows that digital ventures constrain the generativity of their market offerings so they can attend to the needs and requirements of the environment they are entering.

Second, our model suggests that, through these mechanisms, digital ventures construct the functional, temporal, and material dimensions of a buffer between their market offerings and the existing environment. Buffering allows digital ventures to demarcate a space that they can occupy with their market offerings without having to rush their design. In contrast to the popular narrative that digital ventures operate at high speed (Nambisan et al., 2017), we find that digital ventures use the buffer to pause and reflect and defer the elaboration of their offerings.

Third, we show that designing offers a way to appreciate the concrete actions that digital ventures take to construct a digital market offering. Thus, we contribute to a more grounded, practical view of what digital ventures do (Huang et al., 2021; Nambisan, 2017), focusing on how digital ventures appropriate digital technology, manage relationships with other actors, and gradually materialize the future they envision.

## Background

## Digital Ventures and Their Use of Generative Digital Technology

Digital ventures create new market offerings by (re)combining various digital technology components (Huang et al., 2021). Because these market offerings build on digital technology, they have traits that distinguish them from nondigital market offerings (Autio et al., 2018; von Briel et al., 2018a; Nambisan, 2017), including that they are reprogrammable, open, and malleable, all of which allow digital ventures to continually (re)design their market offerings.

The creation and introduction of market offerings are milestones in digital ventures’ lifecycles that determine their prospects of survival and growth (Huang et al., 2017, 2021). However, the literature does not pay adequate attention to how digital market offerings are created, as scholars usually assume that, because of digital technology’s inherent flexibility and versatility, digital ventures can easily change direction if necessary (von Briel et al., 2018b; von Briel et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021; Nambisan, 2017). As such, while some entrepreneurship researchers have shown interest in related concepts such as pitches (Ciuchta et al., 2017), landing pages (Camuffo et al., 2020), or business models (McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2020), how the market offering itself is designed remains under-researched.

The absence of substantive discussions of digital market offerings is problematic because the creation and introduction of digital market offerings lead to a key dilemma that digital ventures must navigate. The dilemma emerges from a tension between leveraging the generative potential of digital technology and addressing the here-and-now environment in terms of factors such as customer expectations, legacy technology, regulations, and market expectations.

Undoubtedly, the generative potential of digital technology opens a host of opportunities for digital ventures to create novel market offerings. These opportunities emerge because digital technology (e.g., blockchain, artificial intelligence, process automation) is agnostic to products and industries (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010, 2012) and can be adapted to ever newer uses (Huang et al., 2021; Yoo et al., 2010; Zittrain, 2008). Therefore, digital ventures can create novel market offerings by combining digital technology components in ways that may have not been intended or even imagined by the technology’s inventor (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Nambisan, 2017). For instance, Google’s TensorFlow machine learning technology has been used for various purposes, from detecting cancerous tissue to natural language processing. Although each of these uses built on the same core technology components, the components were rearranged to perform new functions in new settings.

Notwithstanding the generative potential that is vested in digital technology, new ventures must also arrange their market offerings so they can operate in the environments they enter (Ansari et al., 2016; Garud et al., 2022; Hargadon & Douglas, 2001). The introduction of a novel market offering may disturb the existing environment and, as a result, evoke resistance and adversarial reactions and introduce incompatibilities (Snihur et al., 2018). Adding to these difficulties, novel market offerings typically depend on complementary resources that are often controlled by incumbents (Ansari et al., 2016; Teece, 1986); thus, entrepreneurial ventures must consider how their market offerings can fit into an environment, even if their goal is to change it.

As a consequence, entrepreneurial ventures often seek to align the design of their offerings, at least temporarily, with current conditions (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001) in order to gain a foothold in a market by, for example, establishing strategic partnerships (Teece, 1986, 2018), embedding familiar cues (e.g., features or visual elements) in their offering (Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2020), co-opting incumbent organizations (Ansari et al., 2016; Hannah & Eisenhardt, 2018; Santos & Eisenhardt, 2009), or adopting established frames (Garud et al., 2022; Gurses & Ozcan, 2015).

Thus, a key challenge for digital ventures lies in navigating this tension between leveraging the generative potential of digital technology and accommodating the existing environment. This tension reflects the dilemma that organizations face between engaging in learning or performing (Smith & Lewis, 2011)—that is, between developing capabilities for the future and building success in the present (Andriopoulos and Lewis, 2009; Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). The unrestricted pursuit of digital technology’s generative potential might be successful in the future but can backfire if it makes the new offering incompatible with the existing environment, the install-base of legacy technology, or current regulations. On the other hand, aligning too closely with the existing environment may limit digital ventures’ ability to make full use of their digital technology’s generative potential, thus reducing their chances of survival.

Understanding how digital ventures navigate this tension requires understanding how they transform generative digital technology into context-specific digital offerings. However, existing work (Table 1) provides few answers on how to do so.

While digital innovation research analyzes the properties of digital technology that give rise to generativity (Tilson et al., 2010; Yoo, 2010; Yoo et al., 2010; Zittrain, 2008), asks how generativity can be nurtured (Andersen & Ingram Bogusz, 2019; Eaton et al., 2015; Jarvenpaa & Standaert, 2018), or examines the consequences of generativity (Boland et al., 2007; Lyytinen et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2012), it leaves unexplored how to embed generative digital technology into novel market offerings. To address this lacuna, we analyze how digital ventures deal with the environment by designing their market offerings in particular ways.

## Designing Digital Market Offerings

Designing has emerged as a way to understand how human actors (e.g., entrepreneurs, managers, engineers) devise artifacts to change existing situations in preferred ways (Simon, 1996). Artifacts are man-made, purposefully created objects that consist of an inner environment, an outer environment,<sup>3</sup> and the interface between the two (Simon, 1996). The inner environment represents the working of the artifact itself, that is, its structure and organization of interconnected components, modules, and subsystems, with varying degrees of independence between them (Baldwin and Clark, 2000; Simon, 1996). These components equip an artifact with specific functions. The outer environment captures the surroundings in which an artifact is set to operate, such as natural laws, market mechanisms, and other conditions that govern the artifact’s behavior. The interface connects an artifact’s inner and outer environments such that the artifact can fulfill a specific purpose (Simon, 1996). Artifacts achieve their goals through the functional relations they establish between the inner and outer environments. Designers can adapt the inner environment of their artifacts to the conditions of the outer environment, just as their artifacts can shape the outer environment to establish favorable conditions for the inner environment. Therefore, designing is a recursive process in which any action is the basis for subsequent action (Simon, 1996) that makes an artifact more useful.

Scholars from several disciplines apply design to study a broad range of artifacts (Rai et al., 2017), including information systems (Boland, 1978; Hevner et al., 2004; Necco et al., 1987), organizations (Garud et al., 2008; Sarasvathy et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2006), culture (Buchanan, 2015), products (Ulrich, 1995; Verganti, 2011), and new ventures (Berglund et al., 2020; Cohen et al., 2019). Practitioners are also interested in designing—e.g., in the form of design thinking and other tools that support the development of new offerings (Brown, 2008; Liedtka, 2018).

Our study builds on designing to examine “where the rubber meets the road,” that is, what concrete actions digital ventures take to address the dilemma between generativity and the current environment. We draw on two streams of design research: managing as designing and entrepreneurship as a science of the artificial (Table 2).

First, management scholars who embrace a designing view are interested in how managers approach and solve wicked problems (Boland, 2004; Buchanan, 1992). This stream of research portrays managers as reflective agents that devise courses of action by bringing about artifacts like novel products, services, and organizational designs, while being sensible about environmental conditions (e.g., constraints) and how the artifacts fit within them (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Garud et al., 2008; Weick, 1993). Departing from the traditional decision-making logic that emphasizes choosing from a set of alternative courses of action, the designing view portrays managers as problem solvers who devise new courses of action to change the world that surrounds them for the better (Boland et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2006). Doing so requires that managers question the assumptions that underlie extant designs and depart from established paths (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Buchanan, 2015; Garud et al., 2008).

Second, scholars who view entrepreneurship as a science of the artificial are interested in how entrepreneurial ventures deal with inherently uncertain environments (Sarasvathy et al., 2008). Here, researchers argue that entrepreneurial ventures enact an effectual decision-making logic (Sarasvathy, 2001) to gradually reduce uncertainty. Rather than having clear ends in mind, entrepreneurial ventures begin from existing means to accumulate commitment to certain courses of action and converge on specific goals (Reymen et al., 2015; Sarasvathy, 2003). These actions are typically codetermined by the environment (Wiltbank et al., 2006); however, while acknowledging the constraints the environment imposes, it is viewed as mutable (Sarasvathy et al., 2008). As such, entrepreneurial ventures shape the environment (Sarasvathy & Dew, 2005) by creating artifacts, be they material (e.g., minimum viable products, business plans) or abstract (e.g., new venture ideas, opportunities) (Berglund et al., 2020). Viewing entrepreneurship as a science of the artificial embraces the concrete actions through which entrepreneurial ventures fabricate artifacts like digital market offerings by attending to how these ventures construct their inner and outer environments, but without assuming full forethought on the side of the venture. Artifacts propel the formation of entrepreneurial ventures by resolving sources of uncertainty (Berglund et al., 2020).

We build on key concepts from both of these streams of design research to determine how digital ventures navigate the tension they face when they design their offerings. First, we acknowledge that digital market offerings are artifacts created to implement a long-term vision that defines a set of goals that a digital venture wants to achieve (Berglund et al., 2020; Boland et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2006). The vision captures both what is—how things currently are—as well as what might be, how things ought to be through the design of a digital market offering (Simon, 1996). In this way, the vision circumscribes a venture’s use of digital technology. While final goals are not clear from the outset and evolve over time (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Garud et al., 2008), digital ventures design their market offerings with the goal of changing a given situation into one that is preferred (Sarasvathy et al., 2008). Thus, the designing view emphasizes the specific actions through which digital ventures envision and bring about a digital market offering (Boland et al., 2008; Sarasvathy, 2003).

As a second key concept, we argue that to understand how digital ventures deal with the tension they face, we must attend to both the architecture of their market offerings and the environment for which they are designed (Berglund et al., 2020; Sarasvathy et al., 2008). In digital market offerings, the inner environment refers to particular configurations of the digital technology components that constitute the offering. These components are potentially generative and should be arranged in such a way that they implement a set of desired functions (Yoo et al., 2010). The outer environment captures the existing conditions into which digital ventures seek to introduce their offerings (Boland, 2004; Sarasvathy et al., 2008). It subsumes technical conditions like existing digital infrastructures, legacy technology, available digital technology (Tilson et al., 2010; Yoo, 2010; Yoo et al., 2010), and social conditions like customer preferences, the power of incumbent organizations, and regulations (Gurses & Ozcan, 2015; Hargadon & Douglas, 2001; Santos & Eisenhardt, 2009).

## Method

We used a form of grounded theorizing (Gioia et al., 2013; Strauss & Corbin, 1998), drawing on qualitative data from multiple cases (Eisenhardt, 1989; Locke, 2007). We chose the German financial services industry as our setting because the financial services industry has been at the forefront of emergent digital technologies since the 1970s, introducing well-known technologies like automated teller machines, online banking, and cashless payments (Ang & Cummings, 1997). The German financial services sector has also recently seen a surge of new digital ventures, labeled the “fintech revolution” (Gomber et al., 2018; The Economist, 2015), which has offered a unique opportunity to observe how digital ventures deal with the existing environment when they design their market offerings. Research on fintech ventures in the IS discipline does not typically pursue this focus (Gomber et al., 2018), instead focusing largely on phenomena like the emergence of novel industry segments like payments and settlement (Kazan et al., 2018; Scott et al., 2017), particular uses of digital technologies in the financial services industry like blockchain and machine learning (Fu et al., 2021; Hendershott et al., 2021), and mapping the fintech landscape (Gozman et al., 2018). Our study complements this stream of research. In addition, the German financial services industry features pronounced and salient characteristics of the environment. For instance, the industry has high levels of regulation (Danker, 2016) and legacy banking technology (Haeberlin, 2018; Keyes, 1998).

We used three sampling criteria for case selection: (1) The digital ventures had to be active in at least one of four established financial services segments—saving and investment, alternative lending, technology, and payments (Ancri, 2016); (2) the ventures had to be young, independent, professional firms (Garg & Eisenhardt, 2017) to ensure they pursued their own visions; and (3) the ventures still had to be active when we concluded our data collection in 2020.

We selected two ventures, referred to hereafter as RoboCo and SavingCo, that were active in saving and investment; two ventures, WorkflowCo and LendingCo, that were in alternative lending; and two ventures, PortfolioCo and MiddleCo, that were in financial technology. These six digital ventures varied in terms of organizational structure, processes, and culture, but environmental conditions in terms of their infrastructure, regulatory, and competitive environments, among others, were comparable. With these choices made, we traced how the six ventures navigated the tension between the potential generativity of digital technology and the environment as they designed their digital market offerings from vision to launch and postlaunch. We focused on the decisions these digital ventures made and the actions they undertook as they tried to evaluate the generative potential of the digital technology components available to them and dealt with the conditions from the environment they entered. Our analysis identified the specific mechanisms through which they navigated the tension when they designed their digital market offerings. We provide a detailed account of our data collection, coding, and analysis procedures in Appendix A. Table 3 summarizes our data sources.

We relied on a variety of firm-level data sources, including formal and informal interviews, firm documents, archival data, and real-time on-site observations. We complemented this data with informal industry-level data about the German fintech landscape, particularly through meetings with Clevel executives and attendance at industry-level events like community hubs, workshops, pitches, and forums. We initially used an interview protocol for formal interviews (Appendix B) but relied on probing questions as data collection progressed.

To analyze the data, we followed established guidelines for grounded theorizing and qualitative, inductive research (Charmaz, 2006; Miles et al., 2014). We started by crafting thick descriptions for each case (Denzin, 2001) and then relied on iterative sequences of open, axial, and selective coding to generate emergent themes and higher-level concepts (Charmaz, 2006; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). We organized our analysis around design events as critical incidents (Van de Ven & Poole, 1990). A design event refers to any action or sequence of actions that is related to the creation and introduction of a digital market offering.

## How the Digital Ventures Designed Their Digital Market Offerings

Table 4 presents an overview of the six digital ventures and their digital market offerings. While each venture designed its market offerings through a unique sequence of design events, we noticed consistent patterns across the cases and over time, particularly how all six ventures struggled to confront three broad challenges that characterized the tension they faced:

The first challenge was specifying the particular purposes for which digital technologies would be used as part of the market offerings so they fit the environment they entered. The digital ventures demarcated the boundaries within which their digital technologies would be employed so they would be compatible with the prevalent social and technical conditions. For example, a common challenge was deciding how and to what extent existing business practices (e.g., risk analysis) could be automated through digital technologies like artificial intelligence and blockchain. This challenge relates to how the ventures structured the outer environment to define the goals of their digital technology use and how these goals could be achieved.

The second challenge involved placing their market offerings in relation to existing legacy infrastructures and technology and managing relationships with incumbent firms. For example, several ventures collaborated with incumbent firms that helped them comply with regulatory requirements. These collaborations often involved substantial difficulties related to data exchange and facilitating cross-organizational processes. The ventures’ offerings had to take actions to ensure they could operate in the current financial services industry. This challenge relates to how the ventures designed their offerings as interfaces between the inner and outer environments.

The third challenge was deciding which digital functionalities should be included in their market offerings and how to allocate them to specific components of their offerings. For example, a common challenge was deciding which technology stacks to use. This common challenge concerns how the ventures determined the structures of their digital market offerings’ inner environments.

Our analysis identified contingent causal patterns in how the digital ventures dealt with the tension they all faced. The ventures engaged in similar actions in responding to triggering conditions to achieve similar outcomes. We conceptualized these conditions, actions, and outcomes as three primary designing mechanisms (Table 5): (1) bounding the technology scope, (2) transposing through digital objects, and (3) probing the solution space. These three designing mechanisms serve as motors of empirically observable design events—not as deterministic forces but as contingent causal forces (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Sayer, 2000). ## Three Designing Mechanisms

## Bounding the Technology Scope

The digital ventures all began their journeys with an abstract vision of what they wanted to offer (e.g., LendingCo’s new AI-powered financing solution for small and medium-sized enterprises—SMEs). However, they struggled to translate their abstract visions, which had been inspired by the generative power of digital technology, into specific digital market offerings. A key challenge for them was to define the exact purpose for which they would use digital technology as part of their market offerings in the financial services industry so that it would not be rejected by customers or regulators. For instance, under German legislation, using digital technologies like blockchain or machine learning for purposes like automated trading and issuing loans has wideranging regulatory implications that make such offerings potentially costly and risky. Accordingly, the digital ventures we studied were concerned that their intended use of digital technology might render their offerings unfit for the financial services industry because of either stakeholder perceptions or regulatory concerns.

To deal with this challenge, the digital ventures evaluated existing business practices and market offerings to define the functional boundaries of their market offerings—that is, the specific use case(s) for digital technology. In doing so, the digital ventures ensured that their use of digital technology was tailored to specific, current problems in the financial services industry. We refer to this mechanism as bounding the technology scope, which captures how digital ventures sharpen the purpose for their use of digital technology in their market offerings by delineating the contour of the functional boundary of their digital market offerings. Through bounding the technology scope, digital ventures define what digital technology they employ to redesign particular elements of existing offerings and business practices. For example, some ventures looked at existing offerings, asking what an improved, digital version would look like. Others identified bottlenecks in existing business processes and considered how digital technology could be used to resolve the bottleneck. Thus, the digital ventures constrained the generativity of the digital technology they employed, by applying it only to specific use cases or by excluding potential use cases for the chosen technology, to translate their vision into a contour of the functional boundary that fit the outer environment they intended to enter (e.g., a specific industry segment). Table 6 provides examples of these activities.

For example, PortfolioCo had the broad vision of improving private banks’ wealth-management practices, but struggled to define what they could offer to realize that vision not only because there were many forms of digital technology that could be used, such as artificial intelligence or distributed ledger, but also because most private banks were hesitant to change their existing wealth-management practices to exclude a personal advisor for key customer accounts. PortfolioCo examined current private banking practices through repeated interactions with incumbent banks in the form of workshops and interviews to determine precisely what and which digital technologies could be employed to improve which elements of wealth management:

We don’t look for technologies and then for a business case. We don’t say, “okay, here’s blockchain. What now? We can’t think of anything, but it’s great, so let’s try it out.” We don't do that. We implemented a blockchain mechanism two years ago, when no one knew blockchain. Our case was not blockchain; our case was that there’s a bank that falsified 20,000 advisory protocols. We needed a technology where the advisor thinks he’s signing a PDF but, basically, he’s on the blockchain. At that time, it wasn’t called blockchain; it was a procedure that made signatures unforgeable. It didn’t matter that we used blockchain. (CSO of PortfolioCo)

PortfolioCo focused on elements (to which the CEO referred as “bricks”) of private banking practices that could be improved by wrapping them in digital technologies like blockchain and process automation. At the same time, other elements were left untouched because PortfolioCo was concerned that private banks’ employees (i.e., client advisors) would resist. Reflecting on this approach, the CEO noted:

If Edison had said, “In a hundred years, there will be nuclear power,” people would not have understood because they did not even know what electricity was, so it’s incredibly difficult to find the right message. Instead, we ask, “What problems do you have today?” Then we’ll discuss how we could improve these problems through technology. We try to break bricks out of the bank so they won't have to do it at the bank anymore; they just get a user interface to work with. A good example is a bank that does investment proposals with Excel, which takes a lot of manpower. Then you say, “I’ll take away from you this whole process of investment proposal creation,” and give you a website for that. You just have to convince them to break the bank. (CEO of PortfolioCo)

## Transposing through Digital Objects

The second key challenge for the digital ventures was to ensure that their digital market offerings not only fit the particular contours of functional boundaries but could also operate in the environment. The digital ventures we studied understood that their digital market offerings would have to operate within a complex web of legacy technology, processes and workflows, and regulatory requirements. The extant conditions of the financial services industry posed the risk that the digital ventures’ digital market offerings would not be able to operate in this environment. While bounding the technology scope helped the ventures ensure that their use of digital technology would fit both their vision and the environment, it became clear that their offerings would also have to be designed to operate, that is function, in the environment.

To address this challenge, the digital ventures designed dedicated digital objects to render their market offerings compatible with the social and technical conditions of the existing environment. These digital objects acted as material buffers between their new offerings and the outer environment that made them compatible. Designing these objects ensured that the digital market offering could be arranged within the outer environment (e.g., through dedicated interfaces with traditional legacy technology) or could connect the outer environment to the offering (e.g., through an online tool that determined customers’ risk aversion and attracted a subset of users who were likely to see automated advisory as a valid alternative to traditional private banking). Ultimately, these efforts placed the digital market offering within its outer environment in such a way that resolved operational incompatibilities between the digital market offerings and the existing conditions of the financial services industry (e.g., the inability to exchange data with incumbents or the use of cloud architectures when regulatory requirements required a server room).

We refer to this mechanism as transposing through digital objects, which denotes how digital ventures ensured their market offerings could operate in the environment they entered. In transposing through digital objects, the digital ventures create an interfacing layer around their digital market offerings so they can operate with legacy technology. These objects serve as material boundary markers that ensure that the offerings can connect to incumbent technology and processes where needed. Table 7 provides examples of this mechanism.

Consider the case of RoboCo, which set out to design an automated advisory offering for affluent individuals with the vision of providing investment opportunities that were previously available only to the very wealthy. The firm faced two major challenges: First, it had to establish automated cross-organizational processes with a custodian bank’s legacy systems for account management, trading, and settlement. This requirement meant that RoboCo had to design dedicated digital objects in the form of interfaces for data exchange and process automation.

## Probing the Solution Space

The digital ventures also had to find a way to deal with environmental, technical, and regulatory uncertainties while translating their vision into a concrete set of core functionalities in their market offering. For example, we learned that some of the ventures were uncertain about how using distributed ledger or machine learning technology would implicate regulatory legislation, how accurate such algorithms would be during live financial transactions, or how customers would react to such technologies in an offering.

Given the uncertainty they faced, the ventures perceived a quick and early commitment to a specific set of core functionalities as risky, as they saw revising decisions, once made, as costly and potentially detrimental to their reputation. In response, the ventures left their solution space—the set of available options in the constitution of market offerings through configurations of digital technology components—unrefined by launching only preliminary sketches of core functionality early on. The ventures made assumptions about how envisioned functionalities could be designed without fully implementing them, and thus launched incomplete versions of the intended functionality. Elaboration of core functionalities was deferred until later, when additional data showed them how functionalities could be implemented.

Most of the ventures we studied designed initial versions of digital technology components, such as algorithms for rebalancing and risk assessment, in such a way that they mimicked but did not actually realize the core functionality. These initial sketches involved substantial manual work, but they helped the ventures learn from users’ and other stakeholders’ reactions what expedient ways of implementing the core functionality might be. Thus, early versions of the market offerings probed potential reactions in the outer environment, rather than requiring the firm to commit to a complete and fully functional offering. We refer to this mechanism as probing solution space, which captures how digital ventures test possible configurations of digital components by launching unrefined sketches of functionality before committing to a specific configuration. Probing the solution space allows digital ventures to defer the commitment to a particular configuration until later to see how the envisioned functionality could be implemented while leaving more design options available for their solution. Therefore, this mechanism was critical to the firms’ ability to adjust direction if needed; thus, it was akin to creating a temporal buffer. Table 8 provides examples of this mechanism.

For example, LendingCo envisioned a new risk model that would enable them to provide SMEs with short-term working capital loans more profitably than existing offerings. LendingCo’s model had to work much faster than existing processes, where loan processing could take up to three months. To do so, the firm developed a new risk-assessment algorithm that was based on machine learning technology, but it did not know what parameters to use in the algorithm for an optimal analysis. It launched a web application as an offering but relied on a common, general-risk model at this stage without using the sophisticated machine learning functionality:

We started rudimentary at the very beginning with just a front end. Everything you had behind the facade was an entirely manual process. We had a little front end and we had an idea, and we financed the first loans from our own equity. Then, after the second or third customer, we focused on what was the first pain point of technology. We found that what was the first thing we needed was an online loan application line, very rudimentary, so the request was no longer sent by email but went through a platform with user interfaces and so on. It was still only front-end. Then, in the next step, we said now let’s start by adding customer data, everything that is available—Federal Gazette and all external data that we could use in our back end. (COO of LendingCo)

By launching the web application, LendingCo became operational immediately. Processing the first loan applications allowed the firm to gain a deeper understanding of the relevant parameters, and feedback from their growing customer base told them how to build an algorithm that would automate large parts of the risk analysis. In particular, LendingCo worked to automate the pre-assessment stage, which was used to filter out unfit loan requests at an early stage. By the time we stopped collecting data, LendingCo was still keeping parts of the analysis manual, as they still lacked sufficient amounts of data to train the model and automate the analysis. As the CRO explained:

In this asset class, there is a higher risk of fraud and deceit. You can’t beat that with classic bank forensics models. … We have the issue that we do not have enough data to be able to make a clear, unambiguous assessment; we simply do not have enough defaults yet, which is good, but it hinders the development because you cannot grow the population. Nobody collects data like we do, so you simply have to go step by step, calibrate, and improve on the go. Of course, we are not disregarding classic credit analysis because what was good 100 years ago is still good today, but there are factors that help you to recognize certain things better. If you look at complex parameters with several factors that appear somewhere as a flag, you only see them in the big picture.

## Interactions between the Mechanisms

The three designing mechanisms we identified interacted with each other, jointly acting as a buffer that creates a space between the constraining forces of the present environment and the generative potential of digital technology in which the digital ventures could place their offerings. The three mechanisms allow digital ventures to buffer along functional, material, and temporal dimensions, thereby minimizing a digital offering’s blunt exposure to the tension between the environment and generative digital technology. The digital ventures navigated this tension by continuously enacting all three mechanisms. Table 9 provides examples of the interactions between the mechanisms.

Three types of interactions were salient. First, as the digital ventures translate their visions into digital market offerings, bounding the technology scope triggers transposing through digital objects in that digital ventures must situate their functional boundary within a particular set of environmental conditions. Conversely, transposing through digital objects conditions the bounding of the technology scope by revealing a set of conditions in the environment the ventures must accommodate in defining how digital technology will be used in their market offerings. Thus, transposing outlines to digital ventures’ digital technology use must be bound.

Second, bounding the technology scope prescribes feasible functionality that can become part of the solution space, as it helps define what functionality is admissible in the market offering and how the functionality should be implemented. At the same time, by deferring the commitment to how exactly a new functionality will be implemented, probing the solution space stimulates digital ventures to bound the technology scope; that is, probing the solution space yields a set of preferred digital technology components, which leads digital ventures to reflect on how they can use them as part of a digital market offering.

Third, transposing through digital objects necessitates probing the solution space; that is, it enables digital ventures to assess the viability of (at least partially sketched) functionality, considering the environment in which it will operate. Thus, transposing plays an important role in validating the key assumptions about the market offering’s solution space on which digital ventures base the design of key functionality.

## Discussion

## A Model of Designing Digital Market Offerings

We set out to explore how digital ventures deal with the tension between the generative potential of digital technology and the conditions of the existing environment. Figure 3 proposes a new theoretical model to summarize the insights we gained through our study. The model suggests that digital ventures deal with this tension through three design mechanisms: bounding the technology scope, transposing through digital objects, and probing the solution space. These three designing mechanisms allow digital ventures to translate their vision gradually into concrete digital market offerings while ensuring that they fit into and can operate in the existing environment. Enacting the three designing mechanisms yields a buffer between the here-andnow environment and the market offering that consists of a functional, a material, and a temporal dimension so digital ventures can deal with the tension they face.

By bounding the technology scope, the digital venture translates its vision into a preliminary functional boundary for its market offering, thereby elaborating the functional dimension of a buffer around the market offering. This step ensures that the functional boundary of the digital market offering fits into the environment for which it is designed. The functional dimension of the buffer forms a preliminary boundary that helps the digital venture ascertain whether its digital market offering fits into the given environment. Its absence may render a brittle boundary between the offering and the environment such that the digital market offering is unfit for the environment, halting any further designing. Thus, the functional buffer acts as a “shock absorber” (Berente et al., 2016).

Second, by transposing through digital objects, the digital venture clarifies how its digital market offering is situated in the outer environment and the relationships it maintains to incumbents and legacy technology. Digital ventures thereby elaborate the material dimension of the buffer and couple (Baldwin & Clark, 2000; Orton & Weick, 1990) the market offering loosely with the existing conditions in its outer environment. This dimension of the buffer enables digital ventures to explore various configurations of digital components that can be implemented with the underlying core digital technology, while ensuring a connection with the existing technological infrastructure; it allows digital ventures to evaluate whether a particular configuration of digital components that constitutes a functional boundary of the market offering can be situated in an existing technology infrastructure with legacy technology components.

Finally, by probing the solution space, the digital venture controls the pace by which it launches new functionality and the underlying digital technology components. The venture constructs the temporal dimension of a buffer between what it envisions and the environment it faces. With this dimension of the buffer in place, digital ventures do not have to rush the development and placement of digital technology components into their market offerings. It allows them to pause, learn about, and improve the nascent digital technology with which they started because the digital components that form the inner environment of a digital market offering are inherently malleable (Garud et al., 2008; Yoo, 2010). Pausing to reflect ensures that the ventures consider the regulatory, technological, and market uncertainty that is inherent to using digital components (Autio et al., 2018; McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2020; Nambisan, 2017). With a temporal buffer, digital ventures can experiment with a functional boundary, a set of core functionalities, and their interfaces with the existing environment without fully committing to a particular configuration of digital components.

Taken together, our theoretical model highlights how the three designing mechanisms allow digital ventures to navigate the tension between generative digital technology and the existing environment. These designing mechanisms create a buffer that helps digital ventures to concretize their vision iteratively by continuously and loosely coupling the vision—fueled by the generative power of digital technology—with the current state of the environment in which the offering must somehow exist (Yoo et al., 2006). The three designing mechanisms allow these ventures to create, introduce, and sustain digital market offerings that are perpetually in the making (Garud et al., 2008; Yoo et al., 2010).

A digital market offering consists of a functional boundary and a corresponding configuration of digital technology components that situate the offering in a particular environment. Digital market offerings may begin with incomplete sketches of a plausible functional boundary as a contour of the offering. The functional boundary shapes and is shaped by the detailed configuration of the offering’s digital components, some of which must interface with legacy technology.

Therefore, designing digital market offerings is a continuous, iterative push and pull between different, possibly generative, forward-looking configurations of digital components and the need to extract functionality from a configuration that fits into the current environment. Thus, the functional boundary of the digital market offering remains contested as digital ventures try to bring their vision into reality.

In a way, the buffer that the three designing mechanisms produce resembles the idea of modular designs (Baldwin & Clark, 2000). With a modular design, designers can absorb uncertainties by generating options through mixing and matching modules and subsystems. The flexibility afforded by modularity allows designers to create a design that is robust and resilient to changing requirements. In this sense, design rules (Baldwin & Clark, 2000) offer a structural recipe for evolving a system like an offering (Simon, 1996). In contrast, the buffer from the three designing mechanisms we identified forms part of a processual recipe to deal with the uncertainty that digital ventures face. The buffer that digital ventures create is not intended to create opportunities to spawn multiple variants of the same digital offering but to create multiple options for pursuing various paths during the designing process. The end point of such a designing process could be a solution with no options and with minimal modularity. However, during the process, digital ventures use a buffer to ensure maximum flexibility until they reach their destination.

## Contributions

Our study makes three key contributions to the literature. First, our study contributes to the literature on digital entrepreneurship by offering three mechanisms with which to resolve aspects of the tension that digital ventures face in designing market offerings. The mechanism of bounding the technology scope allows digital ventures to tame the generativity of digital technology so it can be employed in a digital market offering to resolve problems and bottlenecks in the environment, the mechanism of transposing through digital objects resolves potential incompatibilities with the environment that digital ventures enter, and the mechanism of probing the solution space configures digital technology components to realize desired functionality. Research on digital ventures thus far neither considers the environment nor how the tension can be resolved (von Briel et al., 2018b; Nambisan, 2017). Ours is the first concrete account of what digital entrepreneurs do as they create new market offerings, given that entrepreneurs do not create new ventures out of thin air (Kimjeon & Davidsson, 2022). Our focus on what aspiring entrepreneurs do (Shepherd et al., 2021) allows us to develop a more grounded view of how entrepreneurs reconcile a vision of the future with the present, and how they construct and experiment with digital technologies as part of their ventures’ journeys (Berglund et al., 2020; Garud et al., 2018). Our model clarifies how certain aspects of digital technologies shape entrepreneurial opportunities, decisions, actions, and the outcomes that are the market offerings (Huang et al., 2021; Nambisan, 2017).

Second, our study contributes to the designing literature by proposing the notion of a buffer as a tool that designers use to deal with the tension they face as they try to leverage the generative power of digital technology while attending to the here-and-now environment their offerings will enter. We found that the buffer allows digital ventures to connect their (often grand) ambitions with the reality of the present environment. The ability to buffer existing conditions and generative digital technology by enacting three designing mechanisms is a novel insight into how digital ventures change existing situations into preferred ones (Boland & Collopy, 2004; Buchanan, 1992; Simon, 1996). This insight also clarifies how digital ventures shape the inner and outer environments (Baldwin & Clark, 2000), along with the interface between the two (Sarasvathy et al., 2008). A buffer provides a way to leverage such an interface. More importantly, the interface is not a sharply defined, discrete line but a fluid space that resides between the inner and outer environment. Collectively, these insights add to our designing vocabulary (Boland & Collopy, 2004) and further our understanding of how designers deal with the tension between their vision and the environment (Weick, 2004).

Finally, our study adds to knowledge on managing and entrepreneurship as designing by highlighting the relevance of design for the understanding of how digital ventures appropriate digital technology, manage relationships with incumbents, and implement novel functionality to navigate the tension between the environment they enter and the generative potential of the digital technologies they intend to use in their offerings. The three mechanisms they use and their interactions serve as contingent causal power in producing a digital market offering: As the digital market offering becomes increasingly concrete, digital ventures reevaluate and update their vision and continually enact the mechanisms.

## Theoretical Implications

## The Constrained Generativity of Digital Market Offerings

Our findings on how digital ventures deal with the generative power of digital technology contrast how generativity is presently understood in the literature. The existing work suggests that digital technology spurs unbounded generativity in digital ventures (Autio et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2017; Jarvenpaa & Standaert, 2018). A central assumption is that generativity is desirable because it allows digital ventures to gain economies of scale and scope (Huang et al., 2021; Yoo, 2010).

However, we found that digital ventures constrain the generativity of their market offerings so they can introduce them into a particular environment by bounding the scope of the technology in terms of how they construct the buffer’s functional dimension. Constraining generativity allows digital ventures to converge on feasible ways of using digital technology while avoiding incompatibilities and adverse reactions from market incumbents and regulators (Autio et al., 2018; Snihur et al., 2018). Such constrained generativity becomes the basis of building more operational variety later (Jarvenpaa & Standaert, 2018), which is consistent with the role that narrowly specified technical standards of the internet (Zittrain, 2008), platform ecosystems (Eaton et al., 2015), and broadband mobile (Yoo et al., 2005) have played in spawning generative overall systems.

## Controlling the Speed of Digital Ventures

Our study also draws attention to the speed (Huang et al., 2017) at which digital ventures move. Moving fast is considered essential for digital ventures if they are to gain a dominant competitive position and maintain momentum. Hypotheses like first-mover advantages (Huang et al., 2021) and winner-take-all markets (Lee et al., 2006) suggest that digital ventures must race to bring their offerings to market and turn profitable before the money runs out (Shepherd & Gruber, 2021).

Our study develops a more nuanced perspective because we find that digital ventures do not move at a uniformly fast pace. Of course, moving quickly to occupy a dominant market position is critical. For instance, launching a web front end to enable initial transactions with select customers plays an important role in obtaining customer feedback and validating assumptions (McDonald & Eisenhardt, 2020). However, many challenges that digital ventures face require more mindful development. Rushing the development of core technology may be problematic in several ways, as it might invoke regulatory complications or incompatibilities with legacy technology or antagonize the incumbents on which digital ventures depend to make their market offerings work (Ansari et al., 2016; Snihur et al., 2018). We find that digital ventures use buffering to control the speed at which they develop and launch novel functionality. While this insight is largely consistent with other work that suggests revising today’s “good enough” components of modular systems later as an effective way to deal with uncertainty (Baldwin & Clark, 2000), a novel aspect of our finding lies in the mechanisms’ ability to help digital ventures maintain flexibility during the process of designing.

Functional buffering allows digital ventures to scaffold the design of novel functionality; they can launch sketches of intended functionality quickly and without much risk. These sketches provide digital ventures with design options so that they can collect information for the development before they commit to a specific solution. For instance, they may initially use these sketches to generate trace data (Huang et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2012) so that they can learn how users interact with the offering or to generate data for training algorithms. They can also use these sketches to evaluate options using A/B tests and to obtain feedback. Material buffering helps digital ventures reduce errors, which are often costly and can threaten a digital venture’s often fragile reputation, such as when issues of fit between the offering and its environment arise.

## Implications for Practitioners

Our study also has several implications for our understanding of practice-related concepts like rapid prototyping, minimal viable products, and lean startup (Blank, 2013; Ries, 2011), some of which are at some odds with current entrepreneurship theory (Berglund et al., 2020; Shepherd & Gruber, 2021). First, our study develops a more nuanced understanding of these concepts. A core assumption of lean startup is that new ventures evolve through continuous, fast-paced iterations of prototyping, collecting data, and learning. If a new venture finds itself on a right track, it perseveres, and difficulties can be overcome by pivoting. Our study suggests that iterations are likely to unfold at varying speeds, meaning that digital ventures might suspend the full elaboration of their market offerings and the decision concerning whether to persevere or pivot until they have a better understanding of the implications of either route. This insight is akin to finding a product-market fit (Ries, 2011).

Second, practitioner concepts typically do not allude to the particular environmental conditions in which digital ventures must operate. However, the digital ventures we studied embraced their environment and acted in response to it. Much like fundamental differences in prototyping and software design can be traced to cultural elements like existing structures and tacit norms (Schrage, 1996), the design of a digital market offering does not occur in a lab but in a broader setting in which environmental conditions shape how designing is carried out (Winograd, 1995). These insights suggest that practitioners should focus their design efforts not only on their market offerings and target customers but also on their relationships with incumbents, partners, and complementors to ensure their offerings create value in the environments they enter.

## Limitations

We highlight two main limitations that stem from our choices of setting, conceptual lens, and analysis strategy. First, our theoretical model is grounded in the context of the financial services industry as our empirical setting. We derived the mechanisms in the context of a mature, regulated industry with largely known customer needs, demands, and technologies. We expect to observe the same mechanisms in other industries with similar characteristics (i.e., high levels of regulation, competition, and legacy technology), such as healthcare, insurance, and pharmaceuticals. In contrast, nascent industries present entrepreneurial ventures with challenges related to the formation of industry structures. Therefore, future attempts to compare digital ventures across industries with differing characteristics (e.g., less regulation, different patterns of demand) will be useful in determining the generalizability of our proposed designing mechanisms.

Second, we focused on the early days of digital ventures when uncertainty about the environment and how their vision can be realized is high. As ventures mature and grow, other issues become important as well. For instance, research indicates that rapid growth requires more formal ways to organize designing digital market offerings (Huang et al., 2017, 2021). Future research that contrasts our model with mature and growing digital ventures may help to refine our insights and provide an opportunity to explore the use of buffers by digital ventures in response to changing environments.

## Conclusion

No digital venture exists in a vacuum. Each venture operates in a space delineated by its vision of the world it is entering. To understand how new digital ventures’ ideas are made real, researchers must focus on how they design their digital market offerings as we continue research on the importance of digital entrepreneurship to business and society.

## Research Procedures

## Data Collection

Our primary data source was 18 semi-structured formal interviews and 13 informal interviews with key members of six case organizations: cofounders, CEOs, CTOs, CSOs, board members, lead developers, and other organization members involved in key decisions. The interviews covered topics about the ventures’ founding contexts, the constitution of their market offerings, the evolution of the firms and their offerings, and the role of digital technology in these processes. Formal interviews lasted between 36 and 60 minutes, averaging 47 minutes, and were captured in 235 single-spaced pages of transcribed interviews. Appendix B presents the initial interview guide, which we complemented with more probing questions as the study progressed. Where possible, we also relied on observation at the firms’ meetings, workshops, and presentations. We spent approximately ten hours observing five such events with three of the ventures.

We also collected archival data that captured events, actions, and decisions related to the ventures’ journeys from founding to August 2020. Archival sources included firm blogs, IPO documents, pitch slide decks, social media posts, press releases, financial statements, term sheets, published interviews with founders and senior executives, and audio and video material. The archival data allowed us to contextualize, triangulate, and extend the insights we generated from interviews and provided insights into how the ventures designed their market offerings. For instance, roundtable discussions in which employees discussed collaborations with incumbent firms, release notes, or sales pitches fo potential customers were included because they helped to clarify how the ventures positioned their market offerings in the financial services industry. However, information on such events as office relocations or social media posts about team-building events were not included. Archival data amounted to 1,274 pages of written material and 969 minutes of audio and video material.

Finally, we drew on several informal sources of data (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Fiske, 2004) to generate data at the industry level. We regularly attended industry events to inform ourselves about recent sectoral and technological developments. In addition, one of us became a member of a leading “fintech community and coworking space” (https://techquartier.com) and spent considerable time working next to and engaging in discussions with several fintech venture founders and employees (>60 hours). We also engaged in repeated discussions with and conducted site visits at a leading fintech firm builder, FinLeap (https://www.finleap.com/), whose business model is to launch and scale new fintech ventures. Finally, we maintained regular discussions with several industry experts, including biweekly calls and meetings with two C-level executives at leading private banks over more than 24 months (>100 hours), to inform our unfolding data collection and subsequent analysis and learn about their views on emergent ventures and forms of collaboration with new technology providers

## Data Coding and Analysis

A stylized visualization of our data coding and analysis is provided in Figure 2 (Gioia et al., 2013). We involved an outside member, a domain expert from the financial services industry who was familiar with all six ventures, in the early stages of data coding and analysis to ensure our interpretations and analyses were credible and plausible. The industry expert was involved for about 30 months during the early stages of analysis.

We started by repeatedly going through the interviews and the other materials we had collected to get a holistic understanding of each venture, the industry context in which it operated, and its market offering (Yin, 2009). To ensure we built a shared and holistic understanding of each case in our team, we developed thick case descriptions that detailed each venture’s founding context (i.e., who are the founders? Why was it founded? With what ambition?), its market offering (i.e., who are customers? What problem does the offering address? What is the core digital technology?), and the history of the venture (i.e., how did the venture and market offering evolve?).

As part of the descriptions of each venture, we constructed a chronology of design events that captures how the venture translated its vision into a digital market offering (Charmaz, 2006; Van de Ven & Poole, 1990). We defined a design event as any action or sequence of actions that contributed to the implementation of the digital market offering. In this sense, decisions for or against a technology stack, the launch of a prototype or new functionality, and relationships with third-party organizations to serve customers collaboratively all qualified as design events, while raising funds, appointing board members, and signing new customers did not. This chronology developed from the raw data for each venture a detailed account of the design events, links, and patterns between various types of events, as well as emerging themes, that contributed to implementing the digital market offering

With this chronology of design events, we undertook two more phases of analyses that unfolded in logical, but not necessarily temporal, sequence. We started by deriving explanations for the occurrence of and relationships between the design events we captured. To assist with this step, we borrowed notions from the designing literature (Berglund et al., 2020, Boland & Collopy, 2004, Sarasvathy et al., 2008, Simon, 1996), particularly artifacts (i.e., digital market offerings) as a realization of a vision, where artifacts exist at an interface between the inner environment and the outer environment. With this vocabulary, we coded design events based on whether they structured the outer environment, the inner environment, or both. For instance, we coded design events that specified a (set of) problem(s) that were prevalent in the financial services industry that a digital venture attempted to address with a new digital market offering as structuring the outer environment. In contrast, we coded design events that specified the technology stacks to be used as structuring the inner environment. We coded design events like the creation of interfaces with incumbent firms to establish cross-organizational workflows as structuring both environments simultaneously.

Organizing design events depending on whether they structured inner, outer, or both environments allowed us to compare design events and derive commonalities between them. For instance, in comparing design events that structured the outer environment, we noted that some of these events revolved around developing a deep understanding of problems in the financial services industry that the ventures wanted to solve with a new market offering, whereas other design events ruled out the use of certain technologies because the ventures feared these technologies would differentiate them too much. We captured such similarities under categories that subsumed similar design events. To sharpen the categories, we performed a cross-case analysis (Miles et al., 2014). For instance, while most of the ventures addressed various segments and customer groups in the financial services industry, defining the purpose for which digital technology would be used in the market offering was a common concern. We ended with two or three categories for each set of design events (i.e., those that structured the outer environment, the inner environment, or both).

Having established our core categories for the design events, we then focused on identifying relationships between categories of design events to clarify how digital ventures designed their market offerings as an interface between the inner and outer environments. For instance, we noted that ruling out the use of digital technologies like blockchain often took place after the ventures identified and understood problems in the financial services industry that they wanted to resolve. In elaborating on such relationships, we aggregated the categories into three mechanisms: bounding the technology scope, transposing through digital objects, and probing the solution space. In line with other mechanism-based theorizing (Henfridsson & Yoo, 2014; Huang et al., 2017), we use the term mechanisms as an analytical concept to capture the processes that underlie the relationships between design events (i.e., causes) and the ventures’ digital market offerings (i.e., effects) (Gross, 2009). That is, we used the mechanisms to group design events and, ultimately, higher-level categories to explain the underlying sequence of design events and how they produce digital market offerings (Hedström & Ylikoski, 2010). We derived for each mechanism triggering circumstances (the circumstances under which the mechanism is enacted), actions (the activities the ventures performed that underpin each mechanism), and outcomes (how each mechanism contributes to developing a digital market offering) (Table 3).

In a final step, we analyzed the interactions (“theorizing the arrows”) (Langley, 1999) between the mechanisms to determine how the ventures used them to implement their digital market offerings. This part of the analysis, we investigated when and why digital ventures traversed from one mechanism to another mechanism. For instance, we noted that digital ventures often enacted transposing through digital object logically after they engaged in probing the solution space. That is, the ventures often devised new digital objects, such as APIs, when they had just developed a set of new functionalities to test whether they would work in the financial services industry. Following this logic, we identified and labeled the interactions between each of the mechanisms. For example, we noticed that probing the solution space yielded a set of digital technology components with certain functionalities, which then had to be made familiar with a certain segment of the financial services industry. Hence, we concluded that probing the solution space generates options for transposing.

To establish the plausibility and credibility of our data analysis, we corroborated the insights our analysis generated by comparing our mechanisms with other digital ventures in the financial services industry that we knew, and by engaging key industry experts. We attended fintech community events like startup competitions to validate our emergent theorization of how digital ventures position digital market offerings against the narratives of the digital ventures in attendance.

We also discussed our analysis with executives at the FinLeap firm builder to contrast our insights to their approach to building and scaling fintech ventures. Finally, we regularly exposed our theorizing progress and outcomes to industry experts who were familiar with the ventures we studied and the banking sector as a whole. We conducted regular face-to-face meetings and conference calls to discuss our theorizing (e.g., in the form of tables, diagrams, “research pitches,” written analyses) over two years. When challenged, we explained our interpretations and revised them where necessary. For instance, these exchanges helped us to understand the regulatory requirements that affected some of the ventures’ decisions related to bounding the technology scope—that is, the purpose for which they used digital technology as part of their market offerings.

## Initial Interview Protocol

## Introduction

• Welcome, and thank you very much for your time.

• Project description: A scientific study of the role of digital technologies in startups

• Aim of the interview: Understand how your startup has evolved, what role digital technologies have played, and your perspective on digital technologies.

• Time frame: 60 minutes.

• Confidentiality: If possible, the interview will be recorded for transcription and analysis. All content will be kept confidential, and recordings and transcriptions will be anonymized.

• Do you have any questions before we start?

## Background and context

• Could you please describe your startup? What is the startup called and what does it do? Why was the company founded?

• Who were the founders? What training do the team members have? Who else was involved in founding the company? How did the financing take place?

• What kind of product/service does your startup offer? How does the product/service offered work today?

• How is the product/service structured? IT components? What is the company's business model? What is the central value proposition? Who are the target customers?

## Interview partner background

• What is your current role? What are your tasks? Has your role changed over time? If so, why?

• What points of contact do you have with digital technologies? What do you understand digital technologies to be? How and for what purpose do you personally use digital technologies in your professional environment?

## Development of the startup

• Could you describe, in as much detail as possible, how the startup has evolved and how you have used digital technologies?

• How has the startup changed over time? What has caused these changes?

• What was the situation before and after the change? What did the team look like and how did it change over time? Which digita technologies were used before and after the change and how were they used?

• Could you describe, in as much detail as possible, your product and how you developed it?

• How has the product changed over time? What did the idea look like at the beginning? How did it change? Where did the idea come from?

• Were there times where several ideas were pursued at the same time? If so, why? What were these ideas?

• What problems and challenges arose during the development of your company? What did you do to counter the problems? How did you use digital technologies to counter the problems?

• Were there any other events that had a significant impact on the company’s development?

## Miscellaneous

• Did we forget something you'd like to say? Are there any other events or circumstances worth mentioning? Who else could we talk to to learn more, and can you help us get in touch?
