# Understanding the impact of business cases on IT investment decisions: An analysis of municipal e-government projects

Egon Berghout <sup>a,</sup>\*, Chee-Wee Tan <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Innovation Management & Strategy, Faculty of Economics and Business, University of Groningen, The Netherlands <sup>b</sup> Department of IT Management, Copenhagen Business School, Denmark

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 18 June 2012 Received in revised form 1 July 2013 Accepted 24 July 2013 Available online 1 August 2013

Keywords: Business case Decision making Information systems Information management IT assessment IT evaluation Economies of scale

## A B S T R A C T

This study synthesizes the extant literature to derive an integrative developmental framework for IT business cases that can be applied to diagnose the feasibility of technological investments. We then construct a theoretical model that postulates the impact of IT business case elements on the initial cost estimates of technological investments. Subsequently, our theoretical model is subjected to empirical validation through content analysis of IT business cases developed for municipal e-government projects. Findings indicate that the richness of the richness of business cases translates to more initial costs being identified in technological investments, thereby conserving resources for the organization through informed investment decisions.

## 1. Introduction

While IT investments consume 20–40% of organizations’ overall IT expenditures [55], the failure rates of such investments are infamous. The ‘CHAOS’ report, released by the Standish Group [70], indicated that 44% of IT projects exceeded their initial budget estimates while 24% were abandoned prior to completion or never delivered. Keil et al. [34] also painted a bleak picture by confirming that escalation occurs in 38% of IT projects with 82% of these exceeding their original budgets (see also [19,58]).

Fortune and White [23], in a review of the extant literature on project management, identified the development of business cases as a key criterion for project success. The same can be said for IT investments. Kappelman et al.’s [32] survey of 55 project managers revealed that the absence of a business case constitutes an ‘early warning sign’ of failed IT projects (see [80]). Ward et al. [81] further alleged that the richness of business cases bolsters the success of IT investments by offering a complete breakdown of cost elements that informs investment decisions (see also [20,57,62,76,84]). Indeed, Whittaker [83] demonstrated that incomplete business cases erode the success of IT investments by culminating in budget overruns due to a priori cost underestimation.

Business cases assess multiple dimensions of IT projects, including the technology being deployed, its impact on targeted users and its implications for corporate strategies and business processes. Business cases hence determine the success of IT investments by empowering organizations to: (1) undertake informed decisions regarding IT projects; (2) monitor the progress of invested projects; and (3) evaluate project outcomes upon completion [4,62,81]. Business cases should lead to improvements in project governance and, in turn, decrease the failure rates of IT investments [80]. Yet, despite the importance of business cases, empirical investigations of business case development methodologies remain scant.

This study therefore contributes to an in-depth understanding of business case development for IT projects by deriving the set of constituent elements that facilitate an informative and purposeful approach to investment decisions. Specifically, we endeavor to answer the following research question: ‘‘What are the constituent elements of IT business cases, and do these elements add value to IT investment decisions?’’ In answering the aforementioned research question, we contribute to the extant literature in two ways. First, we identify constituent elements that support the development of rich business cases for IT investments. Second, we determine the extent to which these business case elements inform IT investment decisions.

We begin by synthesizing the extant literature on project evaluation to elicit and consolidate the constituent elements of IT business cases. Next, we construct a theoretical model that postulates the impact of these constituent elements on IT investment decisions, specifically with respect to cost estimations. This model is then subjected to empirical validation through a content analysis that examines (1) the business cases of comparable e-government projects for municipalities and (2) their impact on the initial cost estimates of these projects. Given the paucity of studies exploring the value of IT business cases, the results of this investigation make a novel contribution to theory by (1) deriving a framework for developing IT business cases that delineates constituent case elements, and; (2) determining the marginal value of each case element in informing investment decisions.

This paper comprises four sections, including Section 1. In Section 2, we compare and contrast contemporary business case development methodologies to advance an integrative framework for developing IT business cases. Based on this framework, a theoretical model and testable hypotheses are developed to predict the impact of different business case elements on the initial cost estimates of IT investments. Next, Section 3 elaborates on the data collection and analytical procedures that were employed to test the applicability of our proposed developmental framework and validate the hypotheses in our theoretical model. Finally, Section 4 discusses our empirical findings and their implications for both theory and practice. This section also highlights the limitations of our study and suggests potential avenues for future research.

## 2. Theoretical foundation

As organizations struggle to realize the benefits of IT investments, repeated calls for a comprehensive and robust framework to evaluate the feasibility of such investments have been made by both scholars and practitioners alike (e.g., [56,70,81,84]. Yet, as noted by Melville et al. [48], the extant literature on IT evaluation is fragmented, as past studies have advocated a variety of evaluation criteria. These myriad evaluation criteria in turn, can be broadly classified into two schools of thought, namely the Economic Approach and the Socio-Technical Approach. Whereas the Economic Approachseeks to quantify the costs and benefits of IT investments in terms of financial metrics [14,43], the Social-Technical Approach regards IT investments as a form of organizational intervention that embodies both social and technical elements of change [71,77]. Although the economic approach has dominated prior research in IT evaluation [24], it suffers from two distinct drawbacks: (1) an overemphasis on potential revenue growth rather than cost displacement [21] and, (2) long-standing challenges faced in cost–benefit analysis given the tangible and intangible impacts of technologies [12,22]. Indeed, there is growing evidence to suggest that the socio-technical approach circumvents the limitations of the economic approach by taking into account both quantifiable and non-quantifiable aspects of IT investments [36,39,41,47]. For instance, Ward et al.’s [82] survey of 100 European organizations concluded that organizations with greater success in IT investments are often those subscribing to a broader definition of benefits such as innovation and enhanced cooperation with trading partners (see also [72]). In contrast, less successful organizations tend to restrict their evaluation of IT investment benefits to those associated with business performance and efficiency improvements [82].

A review of published articles on IT project evaluation, which have appeared in leading journals<sup>1</sup> for the last five years (i.e., 2007–2012), painted an identical picture (see Appendix B). While a fraction of these studies persisted in applying an economic approach to the evaluation of IT projects [1,16,29,35,49,67, 68,74,78], the bulk of them tend to rely on evaluation criteria that recognize both quantifiable and non-quantifiable aspects of IT projects [3,5,7–9,13,18,26,28,36,38,40,42,47,54,60,69,73,79,85]. Consistent with an increasing emphasis on the socio-technical approach to IT evaluation, the development of business cases has emerged as a standard practice among organizations when undertaking IT investments [81]. As defined by Matejka and Cosse´ [46], a business case is a ‘‘description of a situation or sequence of events confronting an individual, a set of individuals, or an organization [and] includes a detailed account of the events leading to the point in time at which the case concludes’’. When applied to IT investments, business cases capture the rationale behind IT projects and in so doing, safeguard against resource wastages by ensuring that project outcomes satisfy important business motives [10,11,15]. Ward et al. [81] thus claimed that the development of business cases is an essential step in IT evaluation because it (1) aids in the prioritization of competing investment options based on resource constraints; (2) facilitates the formulation of concrete plans to realize benefits from investments; (3) elicits commitment from stakeholders to attain projected investment benefits; and (4) creates a formal basis for reviewing the eventual outcomes of these investments. Nevertheless, while scholars have generally acknowledged to the value of business cases in bolstering the success of IT investments (e.g., [20,32,57,62,76,80–84]), few studies clarify how such business cases can be developed comprehensively. This study therefore contributes to the literature by synthesizing contemporary business case methodologies to derive a developmental framework for business cases that allows organizations to systematically reflect on the viability of IT investments.

## 2.1. Business case methodologies: an overview

Although research on IT evaluation is gaining momentum, studies on business cases remain scarce. To solicit business case methodologies from the extant literature, we began with a keyword search of 500,000 periodicals and 12 million books from the EBSCO database, which includes Business Source Premier and Econlit, yielding 254 articles in the process. A similar search conducted via Google (Scholar) generated an additional 10 articles, resulting in a total of 264 articles for review. Going through the abstracts of these 264 articles, we arrived at 89 articles that (directly or indirectly) touch on the evaluation of IT investments. Crosschecking the author(s) and references of these 89 articles, we added another 30 articles to the pool, resulting in a final sample of 119 articles for analysis. Appendix A provides a schematic overview of the aforementioned literature review process.

Further analysis of these 119 articles reveals that a majority relates to IT justification in general, and we can only uncover eight prominent business case methodologies for evaluating IT investments. From these eight methodologies, we endeavor to elicit and consolidate elements that constitute an integrated developmental framework for IT business cases. Such an approach precludes the omission of business case elements that are paramount to the evaluation of IT investments. In subsequent sections, we introduce and compare these eight business case methodologies in constructing our developmental framework.

## 2.1.1. Barnes’s business case methodology

Barnes [4] defined a business case as ‘‘an internal proposal that is submitted to management for a decision’’ (p. 12). A welldeveloped business case must therefore provide answers to four fundamental questions: the extent to which (1) an organization is in need of the projected benefits from an IT investment; (2) the organization possesses the capability to realize those projected benefits; (3) the investment is risky in nature; and (4) projected benefits are being attained at optimum cost [4]. To provide answers to the aforementioned questions, Barnes [4] recommended that the development of a business case should incorporate seven key dimensions as summarized in Table 1.

## 2.1.2. Remenyi’s business case methodology

Remenyi [62] defined a business case as a ‘‘justification for pursuing a course of action in organizational context to meet stated organizational objectives or goals’’ (p. 6). In this sense, a well-developed business case stresses an alignment between the strategic targets of an organization and its intended IT investment. According to Remenyi [62], such business cases typically comprise five key dimensions as summarized in Table 2.

## 2.1.3. Cresswell et al.’s business case methodology

As argued by Cresswell et al. [17], a business case represents ‘‘a well-reasoned argument that attempts to convince the audience of the benefits of investment while educating them about the changes, costs and risks that will be part of the effort’’ (p. 25). Consequently, Cresswell et al. [17] put advance six business case guidelines that touch on areas that are likely to be of interest to organizations undertaking IT investments (see Table 3).

## 2.1.4. Schmidt’s business case methodology

Schmidt [65] maintained that the basic objective of a business case is to shape the decision making process by bringing to light various organizational concerns related to the intended IT investment. Because IT investments denote intervention strategies undertaken by organizations to improve existing business conditions, Schmidt [65] proposed five checkpoints for business cases that attest to the effectiveness of the intervention. These checkpoints denote key aspects of IT investments that distinguish a baseline ‘As-Is’ scenario from projected ‘To-Be’ scenarios for organizations (see Table 4).

In June 2008, the Australian Government initiated a program to develop business case guidelines for governmental organizations embarking on IT investment projects exceeding AUD \$10 million.

Based on the format introduced by the Australian Government [2], a business case supplies valuable information on the costs, benefits and risks associated with an intended IT investment and consists of eight key dimensions as summarized in Table 5.

## 2.1.6. Frisk’s business case methodology

Scrutinizing the way IT investments are evaluated within nonprofit organizations, Frisk [24] observed that business cases are too often interpreted as financial instruments rather than as value propositions regarding intended investments. Frisk [24] described the business case as a formative and pluralistic evaluation method that reflects decision makers’ projections of how the intended IT investment culminates in improved efficiency (i.e., doing things right) and effectiveness (i.e., doing the right things) for the organization. As value propositions, Frisk [24] claimed that a business case should include the eight dimensions summarized in Table 6.

## 2.1.7. Gambles’ business case methodology

Gambles [25] defined a business case as ‘‘a recommendation to decision makers to take a particular course of action for the organization, supported by an analysis of its benefits, costs and risks compared to the realistic alternatives, with an explanation of how it can best be implemented’’ (p. 1). Gambles [25] introduced five business case dimensions that assist decision makers in planning and directing IT investments (see Table 7).

## 2.2. Theoretical model of the impact of business case constituents on initial cost estimates of IT investments

Exceeding initial budget projections remains an elusive and persistent challenge confronting organizations when undertaking IT investments [34,70,86]. Whenever IT investments exceed their initial budget projections, organizations may be compelled to make hasty decisions with respect to project continuation and/or cancelation [51], thereby leading to situations of unwarranted escalation or premature abandonment. Indeed, as observed by El Emam and Koru [19] in a longitudinal survey of IT project managers, going over budget has been cited as one of the main reasons behind project cancelations (see also [1,16,29]). These sentiments were echoed by Procaccino et al. [59], who noted that disappointment with IT projects is more often caused by expectations established at the outset than events that occur afterwards (see also [81]). A specification of cost estimates before embarking on IT investments would therefore reduce if not eliminate the problem of budget overruns and conserve organizational resources that could otherwise be wasted on unwarranted escalation or premature abandonment decisions. Consequently, this study examines the impact of business case elements on cost estimates for IT investments such that investment decisions can be undertaken in an informed manner.

Remenyi [62] contended that the richness of business cases is pivotal to the evaluation of IT investments. As maintained by Money et al. [50], the development of a business case constitutes an extensive and structured approach to evaluating the intended IT investment in its multi-dimensional facets. That is, a business case combines both financial (e.g., cost estimations and productivity improvements) and non-financial (e.g., stakeholder involvement and strategic alignment) aspects of IT investments into a blueprint that charts out the best course of action for organizations [65]. Conceivably, the more elements addressed in a business case, the more likely it is able to identify investment costs upfront and prevent hidden costs from surfacing much later on in the project. We therefore hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. The number of elements in a business case is positively related to the initial cost estimates of an IT investment.

Yetton et al. [86] alleged that the organizational, technical and project characteristics of an IT investment determine of its performance (see also [63,64]). As stated by Yetton et al. [86], the organizational characteristics of an IT investment govern management’s commitment to and support for the investment and include factors such as well-articulated business objectives and proper communication. Conversely, technical characteristics concern the technological novelty of an IT investment and as argued by Yetton et al. [86], may influence investment performance whenever the investment revolves around the implementation of new and innovative technologies. Finally, project characteristics are predictive of the performance of an IT investment because they reflect the extent to which conditions conducive to project success are present: these conditions include sufficient budgets and the involvement of stakeholders with the required knowledge and technical expertise [86]. Consistent with Yetton et al.’s [86] IT project performance model, we classify the nine business case elements in our developmental framework into categories of organizational, technological and project constituents to further postulate the effects of these business case elements on the cost estimates of IT investments.

## 2.2.1. Organizational constituents

A business case should clearly describe the current situation and targeted business objectives to be realized via the intended IT investment [81]. Larson and Gobeli [37] contended that higher chances of success are often derived from investments with welldefined business objectives. Byrd et al. [15] also demonstrated that higher returns from IT investments are likely to result from better business-IT alignment (see also [27,60,61]). In contrast, weak alignment between business and technology will mislead organizations into delaying or even abandoning IT investments, that are competitively necessary [52]. In addition, Neirotti and Paolucci [52] recommended that organizations consolidate IT investments (whenever possible) to: (1) remove redundant business requirements; (2) initiate innovation; and (3) avoid delays in the exploitation of emerging technologies. As one of the critical success factors behind successful IT projects, consolidation can reduce maintenance costs from legacy systems and allow the channeling of resources to other urgent forms of IT investment [52]. From the above, we can thus surmise that ‘Business Case Objectives’, ‘Benefits Appraisal’ and ‘Consolidation’ in a business case serve as Organiza tional Constituents that govern how organizations can go about realizing their targeted business objectives through IT investments. ## 2.2.2. Technological constituents

In examining unsuccessful IT projects across organizations with of varying sizes and situated within different industries, Whittaker [83] found that the adoption of novel (or unproven) technologies with poorly specified requirements (see also [7,8,28,67,68,79]) and the selection of vendors that are incapable of completing projects in a timely fashion and according to predetermined specifications represent key factors contributing to project failures (see also [23]). Similar conclusions were reached by El Emam and Koru [19], who observed that investing in unproven technologies is a primary reason behind project cancelations, especially when it culminates in ever-changing technological requirements and project scope. Because a thorough analysis of ‘Technological Requirements’ and ‘Supplier Options’ translates into higher investment costs due to the accommodation of a larger set of technical considerations, we hypothesize that the greater the number of Technological Constituents (i.e., elements concerning the acquisition of appropriate technological solutions) being addressed in a business case, the higher the cost estimates of IT investments:

Hypothesis 3. The number of technological constituents in a business case is positively related to the initial costs estimates of an IT investment.

## 2.2.3. Project constituents

Another oft-cited reason for IT project failures is inadequate risk management [53,73,83]. Poor risk analysis and management results in serious budget overruns due to a priori cost underestimation [83]. By improving the ability to identify and manage risks associated with IT investments, organizations can remain vigilant against deviations from expected outcomes and avoid project failures [31,85]. Apart from risk assessment, other reasons for IT project failures – as uncovered through the work of Whittaker [83] and subsequently confirmed by Fortune and White [23] as critical determinants of the success of IT investments – include flawed cost estimations, insufficient project planning and governance and a lack of stakeholder involvement and support (see also [18,38,42,47]). Together, these aforementioned elements (i.e., project planning and governance, cost estimates, risk assessment and stakeholders) capture project-specific concerns for IT investments [6]. We therefore hypothesize that the greater the number of Project Constituents addressed in a business case, the higher the cost estimates of IT investments due to a more complete analysis of issues relevant to the project at hand:

Hypothesis 4. The number of project constituents in a business case is positively related to the initial costs estimates of an IT investment.

## 3. Methodology

Our theoretical model is validated in two stages. In the first stage, content analysis was performed to: (1) elicit case elements from the business cases of 38 Dutch municipal e-government projects in accordance with our framework; and (2) investigate the extent to which different case elements are addressed in IT project planning for Dutch municipalities. We then analyzed the impact of the existence of different case elements on the initial cost estimates of these e-government projects in the second stage.

## 3.1. Data sample

Due to stipulations of the national e-government stimulation program, all municipalities in the Netherlands must undertake IT investments in compliance with new legislation regarding the delivery of public e-services. In the process, the municipalities are encouraged to develop business cases to outline the planning behind their proposed e-government projects and justify cost estimations, although they are not obliged to do so. The contextual similarity among the municipalities’ e-government projects presents a unique opportunity to compare and contrast the impact of business cases on technological investments without introducing unnecessary confounds due to project-level differences.

## 3.2. 1st stage – content analysis of e-government business cases

For each business case, a content analysis was performed by two of the authors to determine whether it contains one or more of the case elements in our proposed developmental framework. Table 12 summarizes the content analysis schema utilized for this investigation.

As seen in Table 12, our content analysis schema is constructed from synthesizing previous characterizations of business case elements to guarantee internal validity and reliability in how each e-government business case is interpreted and analyzed. It should be further noted that the characterization of each of the nine business case elements remains fairly broad to ensure that empirical findings are not impacted by contextual variations among different e-government projects. For instance, we do not specify the type of costs that should be estimated for an egovernment project because different municipalities might have different cost considerations. Rather, what is of interest is whether each municipality has undertaken proper cost estimations in evaluating the feasibility of its e-government project. For the same reason, each business case element is scored in a dichotomous fashion: for each of the nine business case elements, a ‘1<sup>0</sup> is awarded if the element is found to be present in an e-government business case and ‘0<sup>0</sup> otherwise.

Fig. 2 depicts an overview of the presence of various case elements, in descending order of frequency, as extracted from the e-government business cases of the 38 municipalities in our dataset.

Insights can be gleaned from Fig. 2 concerning the development of IT business cases for Dutch municipalities. The ‘Business Case Objectives’ element can be found in the business cases of all 38 municipalities, thus implying consistency in the manner in which municipalities state the purpose of their e-government project, the business goals to be attained and the key decisions to be made.

‘Cost Appraisal’ is a core element of business cases [62,65]. While a standardized budget model is employed by 31 municipalities to estimate costs for their e-government projects, the level of detail from the remaining 7 municipalities is insufficient to arrive at proper cost estimates.

‘Project Planning & Governance’ is another prominent element found in the business cases, with 31 municipalities offering an elaborate planning and governance structure for their e-government projects. Conversely, the planning and governance structure for the e-government projects of the other 7 municipalities is too general to be informative.

The ‘Benefits Appraisal’ element is found in the business cases of 27 municipalities. For these 27 municipalities, the business case contains precise guidelines for their e-government projects by explicating: (1) the exact goals to be attained and (2) the specific benefits to be realized from the investment.

Though ‘Stakeholders’ are the direct beneficiaries of the egovernment projects, only 21 municipalities explicitly identified the stakeholder groups to be consulted and devised ways to comply with their service expectations.

‘Technological Requirements’ are found in the business cases of 20 municipalities. The remaining 18 municipalities opted to relegate the elicitation of technological requirements to their project vendors.

Although ‘Risk Assessment’ is necessary to preempt problems and secure project success, only 19 municipalities gave a thorough appraisal of risk in their business cases. From the content analysis, it appears that the other half of the municipalities do not perform risk assessments in favor of relying on the expertise of consultancies and/or the experiences of other municipalities to cope with impending problems.

The ‘Supplier Options’ element concerns the evaluation of potential vendors that may be awarded the e-government projects. Yet, only 6 municipalities perform this activity whereas the remaining 32 municipalities chose to partner with familiar vendors: vendors with which the municipalities have collaborated in the past.

‘Consolidation’ is least represented in the e-government business cases, with only 4 municipalities concluding with a section where decisions are evaluated and formal agreement is established among decision makers.

## 4. Discussion

This study argues that business cases are an indispensable tool for improving the success of IT investments. Because managers are typically constrained by the amount of resources they can devote to the evaluation of IT investments, there is value to be gained from an in-depth appreciation of the relevance of business cases in driving the cost estimates of such investments. A well-developed business case for an IT project conserves resources in two ways. First, it channels resources to the evaluation of core elements that are critical to project success. Second, it brings to light key indicators that alert managers to economically unviable and/or failing projects.

Synthesizing the extant literature, we uncovered nine constituent elements of business cases that are pertinent to the evaluation of IT investments. Building on the work of Yetton et al. [86], these elements were then grouped into three theoretically distinct categories (i.e., organizational constituents, technological constituents and project constituents), each reflecting a higher-order consideration in IT project management. When combined, the nine constituent elements together with their respective higher-order categories denote an integrative framework to guide the development of business cases for IT projects. We then construct a theoretical model that postulates the impact of these business case elements on the initial cost estimates of IT investments. An empirical validation of our theoretical model based on a content analysis of e-government business cases from 38 Dutch municipalities bears implications for both theory and practice.

## 4.1. Implications for theory and practice

Our content analysis reveals that seven of the nine constituent elements in our proposed developmental framework can be found in the e-government business cases of at least half (or 50%) of the 38 municipalities. These elements include ‘Business Case Objectives’, ‘Cost Appraisal’, ‘Project Planning and Governance’, ‘Benefits Appraisal’, ‘Stakeholders’, ‘Technological Requirements’ and ‘Risk Assessment’. Our study is one of the first to corroborate previous scholarly claims, in that these seven business case elements truly determine of whether an IT investment may be worthwhile (e.g., [2,4,12,20,30,62,65,66,75,84]). As our developmental framework reflects the majority of case elements elicited from the egovernment business cases (see Figure 2), we can infer that it is both rich and robust in capturing aspects of IT projects that are crucial to their success.

‘Supplier Options’ is found in only 6 of the e-government business cases, as many municipalities choose to delegate their egovernment projects to ‘tried’ suppliers. Although such suppliers may prove to be more trustworthy in their ability to deliver on project requirements, a failure to broaden one’s search for alternate suppliers represents a missed opportunity to re-evaluate existing vendor–client arrangements and identify avenues for reducing investment costs [4,7,8]. Moreover, ‘Consolidation’ is found in 4 of the e-government business cases. While the inclusion of this element could be construed a repetitive and redundant summary of the other eight elements, this is not the case. ‘Consolidation’ is a conclusive overview of an IT project developed by obtaining stakeholders’ formal agreement on essential business case elements such as benefits, costs and milestones. ‘Consolidation’ hence secures stakeholders’ commitments to an IT project and forms the basis for its evaluation upon completion [81]. For the aforementioned reasons, we would caution against the exclusion of ‘Supplier Options’ and ‘Consolidation’ despite their absence from most of the e-government business cases.

Our empirical findings indicate a significant, positive relationship between the number of business case elements and the ‘Initial Cost per Citizen’ for the e-government projects. This implies that the inclusion of additional business case elements from our developmental framework translates to improved cost estimates for IT projects. Conceivably, it is advisable to develop detailed business cases because they will safeguard against overoptimism on the part of decision makers and provide a more precise picture of the costs to be incurred (see also [23,83]), both of which have been cited by Nelson [53] as leading causes of failed IT projects.

We further discovered that different categories of business case elements have distinct impacts on the initial cost estimates of IT projects. Of the three categories (i.e., organizational constituents, technological constituents and project constituents), business case elements associated with project governance (i.e., ‘Project Planning and Governance’, ‘Cost Appraisal’, ‘Risk’ and ‘Stakeholders’) exert the strongest influence on the initial cost estimates. Conversely, organizational and technological business case elements do not affect initial cost estimates. Such an observation, while unexpected, is not entirely surprising. Based on meta-review of 99 IT projects, Nelson [53] advanced a series of ‘best practices’ for IT projects that coincide with business case elements associated with project governance: (1) avoid poor cost estimation; (2) avoid ineffective stakeholder management; (3) avoid insufficient risk management; and (4) avoid inadequate planning and quality assurance.

From the post hoc analysis, we demonstrate that none of the nine business case elements impacts the initial cost estimates of IT projects on its own. This indicates that an optimal mix of business case elements is required to arrive at improved initial cost estimates for IT projects. That is, our findings hint at the possibility of the existence of synergistic effects among business case elements: the collective consideration of certain elements yields improved initial cost estimates for IT projects. Though ‘Project Planning and Governance’, ‘Cost Appraisal’, ‘Risk Assessment’ and ‘Stakeholders’ have little influence on initial cost estimates separately, their combination in a business case provides more realistic cost estimates for e-government projects. For instance, the recognition of the various stakeholders involved in an IT investment is less informative than the identification of the risks associated with their involvement.

Finally, our investigation corroborates prior claims regarding the value of the socio-technical approach in that strict financial evaluations of IT projects could be insufficient (e.g., [24]). Financial evaluations are likely helpful as constraints for IT projects; illustrating the extent to which the project is affordable. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to supply empirical evidence that not only testifies to the usefulness of developing business cases for IT projects, but also alludes to the importance of rich business cases in identifying project costs.

## 4.2. Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. Despite extensive efforts to locate existing literature on development methodologies for IT business cases, we acknowledge that our proposed developmental framework is neither definitive nor exhaustive. However, as the nine business case elements in our developmental framework have received broad representation in past studies, we are convinced that the inclusion of these elements should constitute a solid business case for IT projects. Moreover, our content analysis fails to uncover new business case elements not already embodied in our framework.

Furthermore, it is possible that certain business case elements in our proposed developmental framework are covered in internal documents that are circulated within the municipality and but are not publicly accessible. That is, our content analysis may not fully reflect how the nine business case elements were addressed in the e-government projects of certain municipalities. Additionally, the analysis of e-government projects would reduce the generalizability of our empirical findings. Conclusions based on this investigation must therefore be interpreted with caution in that our findings would most likely be applicable to IT projects with similar characteristics.

As seen in Table 13, the business case elements in our proposed developmental framework explain a fraction of the variance in initial cost estimates of the e-government projects. We thus suspect that other factors could have influenced these estimates. For example, the experience and expertise of the business case developer may have affected his/her judgment during cost estimation. Future studies might wish to incorporate other contextual or developer-specific variables that may be pertinent to the evaluation of IT projects.

## 4.3. Recommendations

There are a few promising lines of research suggested by this study. First, additional analyses involving the longitudinal effects of business cases on IT projects may be warranted. Because our dataset is a cross-sectional sample of business cases from comparable e-government projects, it offers common grounds for analyzing the decisional value of business case elements. Nevertheless, the dataset does not include longitudinal data pertaining to the performance of these e-government projects. We recommend that future research conduct a post-mortem evaluation of the e-government projects to compare and contrast the extent to which the richness of business cases contributes to discrepancies between initial cost estimates and final project costs.

Furthermore, it would be valuable to verify whether the business case elements embodied in our proposed developmental framework can be applied to IT projects in areas other than egovernment services. Applying the developmental framework beyond e-government business cases can serve as a basis for its further validation and refinement. Consequently, exploring the extent to which the developmental framework can be leveraged to develop business cases for IT projects with characteristics that are distinguishable from e-government services.constitutes a worthwhile future endeavor.

Finally, this study indicates that the richness of a business case stems from having a structured approach to the evaluation of an IT project. A well-developed business case can therefore act as a platform for scoping an IT project and configuring its requirements such that it enhances the likelihood of project success [33,45,65]. Future studies can build on our findings by examining different configurations of the nine business case elements to decipher the synergistic effects that exist among them.

## 4.4. Conclusion

In summary, this study makes a strong case for the development of business cases as a precursor to IT project implementation. Our research not only provides a greater appreciation for the role of business cases in IT projects, but also indicates the benefits to be gleaned from the development of rich business cases. In other words, while the development of rich business cases consume substantial resources, organizations venturing into unfamiliar IT projects will be rewarded in kind from a thorough understanding of the business worthiness of these projects. Additionally, we offer valuable lessons for both current and future e-government projects: projects with incomplete business cases or questionable cost estimations should reconsider a review of their business cases or face the risk of running into problems further down the road. Although there are ample studies on economic approaches to the evaluation of IT investment, socio-technical approaches to such evaluations remain few and far between in the extant literature, especially with respect to the growing prominence of business cases. We are not aware of any previous empirical investigations of the impact on business case methodologies on the evaluation of IT investments beyond a single case study. This paper therefore offers unique empirical evidence attesting to the importance of developing rich business cases that are consistent with our proposed developmental framework of constituent elements when undertaking IT investments.
