# Research Note

# Social Interactions and the “Digital Divide”: Explaining Variations in Internet Use

Ritu Agarwal

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, ragarwal@rhsmith.umd.edu

Animesh Animesh

Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Quebec H3A 1G5, Canada, animesh.animesh@mcgill.ca

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, kprasad@rhsmith.umd.edu

There are large geographic variations, as well as variations across ethnic and racial lines. Prior research suggests that individual, household, and regional differences are responsible for this disparity. We argue for an alternative explanation: Individual choice is subject to social influence (“peer effects”) that emanates from geographic proximity; this influence is the cause of the excess variation. We test this assertion with empirical analysis of a data set compiled from a number of sources. We find, first, that widespread Internet use among people who live in proximity has a direct effect on an individual’s propensity to go online. Using data on residential segregation, we test the proposition that the Internet usage patterns of people who live in more ethnically isolated regions will more closely resemble usage patterns of their ethnic group. Finally, we examine the moderating impact of housing density and directly measured social interactions on the relationship between Internet use and peer effects. Results are consistent across analyses and provide strong evidence of peer effects, suggesting that individua Internet use is influenced by local patterns of usage. Implications for public policy and the diffusion of the Internet are discussed.

Key words: social interactions; digital divide; peer effects; instrumental variables; fixed effects; residential isolation; Internet use

## 1. Introduction

This paper is motivated by the observation of large variations in Internet use across regions of the United States. Patterns of use are of interest for several reasons. They allow us, first, to develop an understanding of the process by which use of new technologies spreads in a population. For firms engaged in electronic commerce, this is of interest for strategy along several dimensions (pricing, rebates, product range, advertising, etc.). Who buys online is likely to be related to who goes online. Internet use, because of the immense value of the Web in education, is also of interest for public policy. Policy makers, and others worried about the digital divide, are likely to be concerned about who does not go online, and why. As we might expect, and as studies confirm (Kraut et al. 1999, U.S. Department of Commerce 2002), wealthy, young, and better educated people are more likely to be online. However, even after accounting for a variety of individual and regional characteristics considerable geographical variation remains. This raises the possibility that observed patterns of Internet use are a social phenomenon, best understood in terms of the way individuals interact with one another.

A Department of Commerce report (2002, p. 78) acknowledges as much:

Generally, the adoption of a technology does not take place uniformly across the entire economy or the entire population    If a person’s family, friends, and broader community are Internet users, there would be increased incentive for them to go online. On the other hand, if few of a person’s family, friends, or community were online, there would be less of an incentive to go online. In looking at the relationship of home Internet use to variables such as income, education, race, or Hispanic origin, we may be picking up in part the probability of family, friends, and community to be online.

Despite acknowledging its existence, however, the report and other literature that we are aware of does not investigate the role of social influences emanating from an individual’s community on Internet use in any depth. We redress this important gap in our understanding of spatial Internet usage patterns.

Our paper builds on and extends Goolsbee and Klenow’s (2002) study about the effect of social interactions of computer adoption to the domain of Internet adoption.<sup>1</sup> We test the hypothesis that individual choices with respect to the use of the Internet are subject to a social influence that is grounded in geography and that explains variations over and above those accounted for by individual and regional characteristics. Consider, for instance, a region with lots of wealthy, young, and well educated people. These people will be more likely to go online. In the presence of social influence even others in the region (who do not have the traits that predict Internet use, but live in proximity to people who do) will be more likely to go online. The influence can be a consequence of learning from others or of a local network externality (for instance if the primary use of the Web is to send email to friends) or simply pressure to conform (not to appear to be “behind the times”).

While it seems plausible that the spread of Internet use relies on contact and the direct influence that people’s choices have on those of others, the effect can be difficult to detect in the data for at least two reasons. Spurious effects may be detected if people with a prior propensity to use the Internet (a common unobserved trait) also choose to live in the same region, or if people in some regions face lower costs and barriers to Internet use (an unobserved regional characteristic). In either case, we would observe a correlation between an individual’s Internet use with that of people living around him, but for reasons entirely unrelated to social influence.<sup>2</sup> Manski (1993) emphasizes this difficulty of distinguishing between endogenous effects (where individual choices are influenced directly by those of peers), and correlated effects (where decisions of individuals in a group are similar because of common unobserved characteristics or a shared local environment). What is required is a strategy that makes a causal argument while avoiding the pitfalls outlined above. Several approaches have been developed in the literature, and are used here.<sup>3</sup>

Our empirical analysis is based on a matched data set compiled from four sources. The first is a data set on patterns of Internet use collected by the U.S. Census Bureau as part of its Current Population Survey (CPS) in October 2003 that includes a supplemental set of questions related to households’ use of computers and the Internet. The probability sample consists of approximately 56,000 households. The geographic location of a household is determined on the basis of an indicator for the Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA) in which a household resides.<sup>4</sup> We compute peer effects using two different time periods: one based on the CPS October 2003 data set, and one on the CPS November 2001 data set, to create a lagged peer effect variable. We further use the 2001 and 2003 data sets to create regional characteristics that are used as instrumental variables to predict peer effects.

We augment the 2003 CPS data set with data from Federal Communications Commission (FCC) on the number of high-speed Internet service providers. As a further test for the presence of peer effects, we extend the CPS data set with Census data on segregation and isolation of racial and ethnic communities within an MSA. This is used to establish the presence of neighborhood influence on individual choice using an approach that does not require peer use as an explanatory variable. Finally, we match the extended CPS data set with data from the Social Capital Community Benchmark Survey that provides variables reflecting the intensity of an individual’s social interactions within a community.<sup>5</sup> In this survey, respondents were asked a number of direct questions about their social interaction (e.g., the number of times they had friends over at home, visited relatives, etc.). Results are consistent across analyses and provide strong evidence for the existence of peer effects, suggesting that individual Internet use is strongly influenced by local patterns of usage.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We first review literature from economics and information systems (IS) that has studied peer effects, as well as research focused on Internet use patterns. Then we describe our data and econometric models used for estimation, and present our results. The paper concludes with a discussion of the implications of our findings.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Prior Research

## 2.1. Peer Effects and Social Influence

Peer effects are said to be present if the likelihood that a particular action will be used depends directly on the incidence of the action within some reference group. Theoretically, three types of mechanisms have been implicated in producing such effects. First, an individual’s choice could depend on those of others if social learning is occurring (e.g., Duflo and Saez 2002, Munshi 2004). Consider, for instance, the decision to use some software. Individuals may learn about the effectiveness of this software for performing specific tasks from others who have started using it. Their adoption decision would then depend on those of others, and widespread adoption is likely to speed up the pace of learning. Second, group decisions matter when there is pressure to conform to the behavior of the reference group. Such pressures could be behavioral in nature, e.g., if people who are different are shunned. Third, pressures may also be grounded in network externalities that make the cost of a decision smaller when it is widespread. For instance, if some software is widely used by colleagues, users may incur a higher cost when using incompatible software. Network externalities exist whenever an individual’s value of using some technology (or behavior) increases with prevalence of use within some reference group. Although the reference group is often taken to be the full population (e.g., the classic Rohlfs 1974 model, and the numerous diffusion studies that use installed base as an explanatory variable), it can be defined in alternative ways (for instance, Goolsbee and Klenow 2002 take the reference group to be city of residence; Duflo and Saez 2002 define it as departmental colleagues, Munshi 2004 focuses on a village or a district; and education studies typically treat classmates as the reference group). If spatial proximity affects the value of use, then it is appropriate to define the reference group in geographical terms.

The study of such social influences on individual decisions has a long history in several disciplines, and has recently been making significant inroads into economic modeling and econometric estimation as well. What distinguishes the economics literature is the theoretical grounding in equilibrium models, which has become the basis for identification of causal effects in empirical models (Brock and Durlauf 2001a, b). An early classic model in economics with social interactions is Schelling’s (1971) model of residential segregation. Other phenomena that people have sought to explain using social interactions include racial inequality (Loury 1977), variation in crime rates (Glaeser et al. 1996), uniformity in agricultural contracts (Young and Burke 2001), stock market crashes (Schiller 2000), patterns of unemployment (Topa 2001), and the adoption and diffusion of new technologies (Goolsbee and Klenow 2002). It is perhaps natural that an important stream of this literature focuses on education and classroom effects (for recent contributions see Hoxby 2000; Angrist and Lang 2004). The central questions of interest here are whether educational achievement is influenced by the composition of peer groups, and what econometric strategies successfully distinguish peer influences from spurious effects.

A second stream of related work may be found in the information technology (IT) adoption and use literature, where social influences on individual use constitute a key theoretical construct in several models that have been used to predict workplace behavior (see Venkatesh et al. 2003) as well as home PC use (Venkatesh and Brown 2001, Brown and Venkatesh 2005). For instance, models based on the theory of reasoned action, (e.g., Taylor and Todd 1995) include the construct of a “subjective norm” to capture the influence perceived by an individual from important others and examine its effects on the use of a new IT system. In a home setting, social referents have variously been characterized as friends and family, secondary sources (e.g., television and newspapers), workplace sources (Brown and Venkatesh 2005), and family and household media exposure (Shih and Venkatesh 2004). In general, this stream of research operationalizes social influence as perceived by the focal individual, rather than on the basis of the observed actions of referent others.

In contrast, other research uses a social information processing (SIP) lens (e.g., Rice et al. 1990, Schmitz and Fulk 1991) to isolate the impact of others’ actions on one’s own. Work by Rice and Aydin (1991) finds that social information processing influences individual innovation decisions over and above the traditional sources of influence such as individual use of the system. Schmitz and Fulk (1991) examined SIP in the context of a social network comprised of an individual, her supervisor, and five close communication partners. They found that close workers’ frequency of use as well as their perceptions of usefulness about email significantly influenced the target actor’s frequency of use and perceptions, and supervisor frequency of use influenced individual use.

Rice and Aydin (1991) investigated social influence in the context of an integrated health system. They posited that the effects of social information would be more potent as one moves from spatial proximity (e.g., physical colocation in the workplace), to positional proximity (occupying similar roles within a network), to relational proximity (based on the intensity with which individuals interact directly or indirectly). Empirical results supported this hypothesis, and further indicated that the two major sources of social influence were those individuals with whom they communicated most frequently, and the individual’s supervisor. The authors note that it is important for scholars who develop models of social influence to better articulate the relevant “local reference group.” Others similarly find that social information plays a significant role in technology-related behaviors and attitudes, and that ties between individuals facilitate the flow of social information (Burkhardt 1994).

In summary, multiple literatures point to the existence of social forces and peer effects in individual decisions and behavior. Such effects have been observed in a wide range of social settings (e.g., in the home, in classrooms, and in the workplace) using varied conceptualizations of reference groups. Our interest is in explaining geographical variation in Internet use. Accordingly, we use the spatial distribution of individuals to define their reference group. Among prior research, this is most closely related to notion of spatial proximity expressed by Rice et al. Next we review research that has examined specific factors that affect Internet use in the home.

## 2.2. Explaining Internet Adoption and Use

Understanding geographical patterns of Internet use is an important question, and has naturally attracted considerable attention. Research in this domain can be categorized into two broad areas: (1) factors affecting Internet adoption (Katz and Aspden 1997, Hindman 2000, Loges and Jung 2001, van Dijk and Hacker 2003), and (2) factors affecting frequency (Teo et al. 1999, Emmanouilides and Hammond 2000) and specific type of Internet usage i.e., e-banking, e-communication, e-library etc. (Hoffman et al. 1996, Liao et al. 1999, Shah et al. 2001, Teo 2001, Hong et al. 2002, Siu-Cheung and Lu 2004). Our work is most closely related to the first stream that has investigated issues related to the “digital divide” i.e., the factors that distinguish “haves” and “have-nots.”

Prior literature has focused primarily on individual specific demographic variables such as age, income, education, gender, and the like. For example, in one of the earliest studies on the digital divide, Katz and Aspden (1997) documented the existence of a digital divide within the USA. Based on a nationwide survey conducted in 1995, they found that Internet users are generally wealthier and more highly educated, and Blacks and Hispanics are relatively less likely to use the Internet. Bucy (2000), based on statewide survey data from two states (Indiana and North Carolina), suggests that income, age, education, and family structure are important determinants of Internet use. He found that older respondents, single mothers, and members of lower socioeconomic groups are disadvantaged in terms of Internet use. Hindman (2000), Mills and Whitacre (2003), Loges and Jung (2001), van Dijk and Hacker (2003), and other researchers also report similar results. Studies of home PC adoption yield comparable findings: Shih and Venkatesh (2004) found education and income to be significant determinants of different types of home PC use, while Brown and Venkatesh (2005) posited and confirmed more complex moderating effects of household demographic characteristics such as income and number of children on relationships between belief structures and home PC adoption intentions.

Supplementing the work on individual characteristics, some researchers have also studied the impact of demographic characteristics of the region where the individual resides on that individual’s likelihood of adopting the Internet. Hindman (2000) used national survey data to compare the effects of household and residential area attributes (i.e., whether a household lives in a metropolitan or nonmetropolitan area) on home Internet use and found that an individual’s income, age, and education predict the use of IT more strongly than geographical location. Based on this, Hindman suggests that though structural barriers between rural and urban areas limit the extent to which citizens of nonmetropolitan communities adopt and use IT, the place of residence is a much smaller constraint than individual specific demographic variables such as income, age, and education. Mills and Whitacre (2003), likewise, argue that metropolitan versus nonmetropolitan areas may exhibit differences in Internet usage because of the differences in the availability and quality of household Internet connectivity. However, like Hindman (2000), their findings suggest that area level differences (because of infrastructure and technology access) are not the major factor in facilitating or inhibiting Internet usage. Rather, household attributes, especially, income and education, account for 63% of the digital divide between urban and rural areas.

Using a qualitative approach, Stanley (2003) highlights the role of psychosocial barriers to computer usage and suggests that there is a complex relationship between ethnicity, identity, and attitudes toward computers. A dominant theme that emerges from her case studies is that the social networks around an individual are important influences on the decision to use computers. For example, one of the subjects in the study explained that none of her friends use computers and thus computers were not part of her everyday life. Katz and Aspden (1997) also emphasize the role of peer social and work networks in stimulating the interest of the focal individual in the Internet and providing users with support. Niles and Hanson (2003, p. 36) likewise argue that physical infrastructure may not be the only factor affecting Internet use. They note:

Conceptualizing access to online information as a function of uneven penetration of physical infrastructure encourages one to see physical space and physical access as the primary constraints on access in cyberspace; if you lack proximity to a telephone network, electricity etc., you’re offline. This sort of analysis denies the possibility that one’s on-the-ground social relations also create opportunities for and constraints on virtual accessibility.

Findings from a study by Wellman et al. (2001) provide further support for the assertion that peer effects are an important determinant of Internet use: They found that the Internet is especially used to maintain active social ties to existing friends. Furthermore, their data indicated that people contact friends who live physically close more frequently (as much as three times more often) than they contact friends who live at a distance, and that frequent use of email is associated with frequent face-to face and telephone interactions. Detailed studies of the diffusion of the

Internet at a national level also point to the existence of such “spatial” or neighborhood effects (Fong et al. 2001). Finally, in a study most closely related to ours, Goolsbee and Klenow (2002) examined peer effects on the diffusion of home computers and found strong evidence that they play an important role in explaining the household computer ownership decision.

## 2.3. Why Does Geography Matter?

As the literature reviewed above suggests, individual, household, regional, and spatial characteristics influence Internet use at home. There are several reasons to assert that geography plays a key role in constraining or facilitating Internet adoption. In addition to the physical infrastructure, the production of Internet content and “grounded geographic contexts and the grounded social relations that are unevenly distributed across those contexts” may be the forces that lead to region specific variations in Internet adoption (Niles and Hanson 2003, p. 37). Accessing localized information has been found to be an important motive for using the Internet (Kraut et al. 1996) and both the volume as well as value of localized content (such as local classifieds, bus schedules, information about social services) produced can be expected to increase as the proportion of Internet users in the locality increases because of the presence of local network externalities. Another local network externality may be found in the context of local recruitment. It has been observed that rather than using the Internet to broaden their employee search in terms of geography, recruiters prefer to use the Internet to hire employees who live nearby (Niles and Hanson 2003). These local level network externalities may stimulate peer effects and at the same time be driven by peer effects. For example, Kraut et al. (1996) note that people might visit local sites frequently because they are more heavily advertised in local areas and/or are more frequently recommended by friends and family living locally. Given the presence of such local network externalities, arguably the effects of social factors will be relevant and observable within a geographical region.

Finally, the presence of geographical peer effects is supported by studies that specifically focus on the influence of social factors in explaining the digital divide. Reviewing prior work (Dowling 2001, Stanley

2003), Warschauer (2003) observes that the challenges involved in purchasing, setting up, and learning to use a computer can be overwhelming if one’s social network does not include computer users. He further reports that the communication of benefits of the Internet from members of a localized social network (such as neighbors) was instrumental in making people adopt computers and the Internet, and that this effect was much stronger than providing computers, Internet access, and training for free. Likewise, Miniaci and Parisi (2005) demonstrate that peer effects at the household level are important in determining computer abilities. Such effects are also likely be manifested within a larger “social” circle, beyond the household.

## 3. Methodology and Results

In general, geographical proximity yields greater social contact because individuals living closer together spatially are more likely to “run into each other” at local events, schools, grocery stores, etc. Consider, for instance, baseball games held in two regions at opposing ends of the Internet use spectrum. In one region 90% of all residents are regular Internet users, whereas in the other region only 40% are. At the former game it is relatively more likely that the person sitting next to you will be a regular Internet user. If you have not been using the Internet, casual interaction in which the topic of the Internet comes up may influence you to begin use. How do we isolate the extent to which the choices made by such “peers” affect individual action? The estimation of peer effects can be accomplished by using either analytical approaches that do not require the direct measurement of social interactions but instead use the proportion of “peers” who are Internet users as the key explanatory variable, or by using variables that more directly assess the level of contact between individuals in the same geographic location.

We use a variety of analytical strategies to test for the presence of peer effects. In one set of analyses we treat interaction as an emergent and implied rather than observed factor, and simply suggest that “interaction” is greater with those you live in closer proximity to than with those who live farther away. Here our goal is to examine if such geographical proximity to individuals who have adopted the Internet plays a role in explaining an individual’s decision to adopt the Internet, after controlling for other drivers of adoption such as individual, household, and regional characteristics. If we observe such an effect, we can infer the existence of a geographical peer influence. As discussed in the Introduction, the principal challenge here is the possibility of finding spurious effects. A variation of this approach is based on the plausible assumption that people interact with those around them, but are more likely to interact with people in their own community. A testable implication is that people who live in more segregated or isolated communities are more likely to adopt the behaviors of their community.

An alternative approach to testing for the presence of peer effects is to regress individual choice on the level of interaction they have with other Internet users in their community, with appropriate controls. To the best of our knowledge, a direct measurement of social interactions for each individual on a nationwide basis has not been attempted. However, there are alternative ways of capturing the level of contact between members of a geographic community. The 2000 Social Capital Benchmark Survey was an ambitious attempt in this direction, and we use this data to further test for the robustness of our findings. We begin with a discussion of the data.

## 3.1. Data and Measures

The CPS conducted by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) in October 2003 together with the Computer Use Supplement provides the primary data set for this study. In addition to the supplemental questions related to the Internet and computers, the basic CPS questions provide data on labor activity and personal characteristics such as age, sex, race, marital status, veteran status, household relationship, educational background, and Hispanic origin. The universe for this survey consists of all persons in the civilian noninstitutional population of the United States living in households. The CPS uses a multistage probability sample where the United States is divided into 2,007 geographic areas; the areas are further grouped into 754 strata, and one geographic area is selected from each stratum. The CPS data also provides an indicator for the MSA in which a household resides. The 1999 classification contains 280 MSAs; our data set contains 221 and is hence, broadly representative of all metropolitan areas in the United States.

The probability sample consists of 156,941 individuals from approximately 56,000 households. A respondent in each household, not necessarily the household head, was asked to give proxy answers for other household members. After eliminating households who were not assigned an MSA code (i.e., either living in a rural area or missing) and those who did not respond to the Internet access and other relevant questions, we are left with a usable sample of 45,212 individuals from 21,352 households.

The specific individual characteristics included in the analysis are those typically found in the studies of regional adoption of technologies reviewed earlier (e.g., Goolsbee and Klenow 2002) and of variations in Internet use (e.g., Hindman 2000, Loges and Jung 2001, Mills and Whitacre 2003, van Dijk and Hacker 2003), namely, sex, age, income, education, race, and ethnicity. In addition, because the presence of children at home has been associated with increased use of technology (Bucy 2000, McLaren and Zappalà 2002), we include a variable that captures the number of children in the household under 18 years old. The dependent variable is I\_USER, which records the response to the following question: “Do you connect to the Internet at home?” A response of “Yes” is coded one, while a “No” response is coded zero. In the sample 70% of the subjects responded “Yes.”

Individuals’ peer or reference group within which they are more likely to interact with each other is identified as those who live in the same MSA. The key peer choice variable we construct is PEER\_MSA, computed as the proportion of individuals in the reference group who go online. We construct the PEER\_MSA variable for two different time periods: In addition to peer effects based on the CPS October 2003 data set, we use the CPS November 2001 data set to create a lagged peer effects variable. The two data sets are similar in the questionnaire and sampling strategy used. The November 2001 data set contains data on 110,691 individuals from 39,818 households.

Cost of access is likely to be an important determinant of home Internet use and we need to control for this. Because of the unavailability of reliable data on price, we use a proxy for price. We augment the 2003 CPS data set with data from the FCC on the number of high-speed Internet service providers (ISPs) as a measure of the cost of access. More competition should lead to lower prices (which may take the form of rebates and discounts at the start of service). The FCC biannually releases data about the number of companies providing high-speed service in geographical areas identified by ZIP code. We use data released in June 30, 2002<sup>6</sup> as a proxy of competition in the ISP market within an MSA. The FCC data contains information about 42,383 ZIP codes located in 262 MSAs. We mapped the ZIP codes to MSAs using a commercial database available from ZipInfo.com. The competition data for an MSA is created by averaging the number of high-speed ISPs in all the ZIP codes within it.

We supplement the CPS data set with data reflecting the residential housing pattern within an MSA. The Housing and Household Economic Statistics Division of the U.S. Census Bureau provides indices of housing patterns (also called segregation indices) for MSAs based on decennial census data. We use the isolation index based on 2000 census data to capture the interaction between the individuals within an MSA. The isolation index is computed from detailed data on residential segregation and isolation of racial and ethnic groups at the census block level. It reflects the extent to which minority members are exposed only to one another and is computed as the minority-weighted average of the minority proportion in each area.<sup>7</sup>

Finally, we conduct analyses to examine the moderating effect of the level of social interaction that exists within an MSA on the relationship between PEER\_MSA and I\_USER. Data on informal social interactions was obtained from the nationwide 2000 Social Capital Benchmark Survey conducted by the Saguaro Seminar at the John F. Kennedy School of Government, Harvard University. The goal of this survey was to quantitatively assess the existence of social capital within U.S. communities, and it includes variables related to social trust, racial trust, civic participation, etc., assessed across 29,233 individuals in 309 MSAs. The restricted version of this data set provides a variable related to informal social interactions (labeled SCHMOOZ) within an MSA. The housing density data was obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau, Census 2000 Summary File 1 through the American FactFinder system<sup>8</sup> and measures the density per square mile of land area. Data definitions are provided in Table 1; Table 2 presents summary statistics. Variable intercorrelations are in the online appendix.<sup>9</sup>

## 3.2. Peer Effects Analysis and Results

Empirically, isolating and estimating peer effects involves some subtle complications. As a first approach to this estimation, we may consider the regression of individual choices on some average of choices made by people in proximity to each individual, i.e., a peer choice variable. There are many important sources of difficulty with this approach. First, if people’s choices depend on those of others, the peer choice variable becomes endogenous.<sup>10</sup> Furthermore, if there is any difference in Internet use across regions, for whatever reason, we would expect to find a positive correlation between individual and group use. To establish a causal link we look for an external variable that affects causes (here peer choice), but does not affect the causal relationship (i.e., an individual’s choice either directly or via a third variable).<sup>11</sup> If there is such an external variable, then its variation induces variation in the purported cause (peer choice), and we may estimate its effect on individual use. This is the core idea underlying the instrumental variables (IV) approach to establishing causal relationships.

The IV approach also allows us to overcome some other difficulties noted in the Introduction, such as the possibility of sorting or self-selection whereby people who are more likely to go online also choose to live in the same areas (e.g., major metropolitan areas). In other words, we have an instance of “birds of a feather flocking together.” A similar problem arises when people living in some regions may face similar institutional constraints or higher costs/barriers to Internet use. In either case we would obtain a correlation between individual and group choice even when peer effects are absent, the source of the difficulty being missing variables (individual or regional characteristics). Two standard modeling choices, when faced with bias caused by missing variables, are IV and fixed effects.

The logic behind the IV strategy is as follows. We focus only on the portion of the variation in the peer choice variable that is explained by instruments. To select instruments, we locate variables that have an important effect on this variable, but no direct effect on individual Internet use. If peer choices indeed have a causal effect on individual Internet use, we can identify it by estimating the effect on individual Internet use of the changes induced in peer choice by such variables. For instance, the number of children in a household or the age of the respondent is likely to affect Internet use. After controlling for respondent age or number of children in the household, there is no compelling reason why the number of children in the peer group (or the average age of the group) should have a direct effect on Internet use. Likewise, a geographic location with a high concentration of educated individuals (i.e., high mean education) is likely to have high Internet use (i.e., a high peer choice). However, after controlling for one’s own education the fact that the region has high mean education is unlikely to directly affect an individual’s propensity to go online. If at all, this variable will operate through the peer choice. Therefore, these could plausibly be good instruments. In general, the instruments in an IV analysis should be correlated with the endogenous independent variable and should not have a direct impact on the dependent variable (nor affect it via some third variable). A good instrument overcomes the missing variable problem because it is uncorrelated with the error term (and hence missing variables).<sup>12</sup>

We use the two-stage IV Probit model with maximum likelihood estimation.<sup>13</sup> From the first stage, we obtain the value of PEER\_MSA predicted by the instruments, which is then used to explain Internet use in the second stage equation. We use mean Income, mean Education, mean Age, and proportion of families with children in an MSA as instruments to predict the proportion of Internet users in an MSA (i.e., peer effects in an MSA).<sup>14</sup> As suggested earlier, because we include controls for own income, education, age, and number of children living at home, the average number of children in the region should have no further explanatory power and are plausibly good instruments. We conducted statistical tests to confirm the validity and relevance of the instruments. Because the IV Probit procedures that are available do not provide these test statistics, following (Brueckner and Largey 2008) we confirm this in the context of an IV linear probability model. The marginal effects computed from the linear model are virtually identical to the IV Probit estimates, therefore these results should be informative. The corresponding coefficients of the instruments in the first stage regression are also significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . The Anderson likelihood ratio statistic suggests that the instruments are relevant (Chi-sq(4) <sub>=</sub> 1720661; p < 001 and Chi-sq(4) <sub>=</sub> 1901180; $p < 0 . 0 1$ for peer effects based on 2001 and 2003, respectively). Furthermore, the Hansen J statistic confirms that the instruments are valid (Chi-sq(3) <sub>=</sub> 362; p <sub>=</sub> 031; Chi-sq(3) <sub>=</sub> 323; p <sub>=</sub> 036 for peer effects based on the 2001 and 2003 data, respectively).<sup>15</sup>

To account for the interdependence of individual responses within the households, we use regression with cluster variance estimation (Wooldridge 2002). This technique allows observations to be independent across households (clusters), but not necessarily within households, and corrects for the interrelated standard errors stemming from the observations from multiple individuals within a household. The CPS data set assigns a weight to each individual in the sample. The weight, which is the inverse of the probability of the person being in the sample, is a rough measure of the number of actual persons that the sample person represents. We use this weight in our IV Probit analysis to more accurately reflect U.S. population characteristics. However, we find that the results are not sensitive to the use of the weight. Results of the IV Probit using lagged peer effects data from 2001 and cross-sectional peer effects data from 2003 are shown in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. We find that the first-stage F statistic is highly significant and much higher than the minimum value of 10, alleviating weak instrument concerns (Staiger and Stock 1997). The first stage R-square (partial) values are 0.31 and 0.34 for analysis using 2001 and 2003 peer usage, respectively.<sup>16</sup> Variance inflation factors across all models range from 1.18 to 3.37, suggesting that the estimates obtained are not biased because of multicollinearity.

The core assertion that peer effects based on geographic proximity are present in Internet use is supported by the results: We obtain a positive and significant effect of the peer choice variables, PEER\_ MSA\_2003 and PEER\_MSA\_2001. The marginal effect coefficients are 0.33 and 0.23 using the 2001 and 2003 peer usage, respectively, and are significantly larger than any of the other marginal effects, such as those for race or ethnicity. Note that the measured effect of early (i.e., 2001) usage is somewhat larger, suggesting that there might be a lag before others’ Internet use influences one’s own.

We also obtain interesting results for the effect of cost of access on Internet use. Recall that our proxy for price is ISPCOMP, which is the number of firms providing high speed Internet access in a region. In Tables 3 and 4, ISPCOMP has a significant positive sign, indicating that greater competition makes it more likely that an individual living in the region will go online. Finally, as a robustness check, we recomputed the estimates when the sample is restricted to MSAs with either very low or very high rates of Internet use (more than one, and more than two, standard deviations away from the mean). Our results remain qualitatively unchanged.

All of the other effects are consistent with what has been previously determined in the literature. Asians, Blacks, and Hispanics are less likely to be online. Income and education are positively related with Internet use, while age is negatively related. Men are somewhat less likely to go online, and the presence of children in the household makes Internet use more likely. Finally, household size is negatively related with Internet use.

Our fixed effects approach to causation and missing variables follows a logic similar to the IV approach. Here, we examine only the variation induced in the peer choice variable by the passage of time, thereby accounting for missing variables that differ across cases (here, regions) but are constant in time. Similarly, this removes the effects of variables that vary across time but are fixed across cases. In our data set we have multiple observations within households, which are possibly not independent. To account for this together with the nonindependence of observations from the same region, we estimate a hierarchical (i.e., mixed/multilevel) linear model as well. Our results will be seen to be consistent across this entire spectrum of models.

A possible concern with the IV analysis is that the model does not adequately control for affects that vary by MSA or by time. For instance, MSAs may differ based on the presence of “high tech” firms. Alternatively, there is the possibility of a uniform increase in Internet use (or a uniform decrease in price) over time. We conduct a fixed effects analysis as an additional robustness check. We combine the CPS data sets from 2001 and 2003. ## 3.3. Community Isolation and Internet Use

Communities exhibit considerable variation in the overall level of interaction that exists within their populations (Massey and Denton 1988) as well as the specific individuals that comprise the social network of a focal person. Such variation has frequently between attributed to race and ethnicity. Indeed, as discussed earlier, race and ethnicity have been identified as important influences on individual behavior and choices in a variety of domains, including Internet use (Katz and Aspden 1997). The degree of isolation of a minority group assesses the extent to which it interacts only with other members of its own group (Massey and Denton 1988,), i.e., in isolated communities, individuals are unlikely to interact with others that do not belong to the community. We examine whether the isolation index amplifies the effects of race and ethnicity on Internet usage. Specifically, we ask whether an individual who belongs to a segment of the population where Internet use is less common becomes even less likely to use the Internet if he/she lives in a location with a higher isolation index. Our conjecture, based on the theoretical expectation of peer effects, is that greater isolation within a community where Internet use is not prevalent will decrease the likelihood of going online. The role of language and gender differences in Internet use within different communities comes to mind as possible contributory factors. On both these counts African Americans resemble the majority population whereas Hispanics and Asians do not.

## 3.4. Community Interaction and Internet Use

Finally, it is conceptually feasible that in certain locations Internet use is high but the level of social interactions (by which we mean the level of contact with friends, relatives, and neighbors) is limited. Again, consistent with the logic underlying the expectation of peer effects, we would anticipate that the use of the Internet by one’s peers in this instance would be less related to one’s own adoption behavior. Conversely, social interactions among community members could be intense in the presence of high Internet penetration within the community; here we would expect the social contact to amplify an individual’s likelihood of adoption. In other words, a positive interaction between the intensity of individuals’ social interactions and the proportion of their geographic peers who have adopted the Internet is theoretically consistent with and provides further evidence for the presence of peer effects. We use two measures of social interactions. First, we proxy social interactions using housing density within an MSA obtained from U.S. Census 2000 using American FactFinder. Second, we use data on direct social interactions within geographic communities from the 2000 Social Capital Benchmark Survey. In each case our key variable is the interaction between the social interaction measure and the peer choice variable.

As noted, we use two measures of social interaction. We obtain the measure for MSA level social interaction (SCHMOOZ) from the 2000 Social Capital Benchmark Survey, and the MSA housing density (HOUSING\_DENSITY) from U.S. Census 2000 using the American FactFinder system. ## 3.5. Limitations

Prior to discussing the implications of our findings, we acknowledge the limitations of this research. First, although our focus was the use of the Internet from home, the notion of “use” can have many nuanced extensions such as the nature of use, extent of use, etc. Detailed data on the nature of use (such as that available in the data collected by the Pew Internet Project (Madden 2006)) could be used to further supplement our analyses. Second, as we noted earlier, finer grained analyses for smaller geographical regions could be useful to provide additional insight into regional peer effects and their range of influence. Nonetheless, the size and coverage of the data sets used in our work offers a unique opportunity to study the phenomenon on a nationally representative sample. Finally, social interactions are implied, but not directly measured, in our study at the individual level (recall that we aggregate social interactions to the MSA level). Of course, such analyses are pragmatically infeasible on a nationwide basis, but can certainly be performed on smaller communities.

## 4. Discussion and Conclusion

The principal motivation for this research was a desire to understand Internet usage patterns in the United States. There are fairly large variations in Internet use, and our particular hypothesis was that peer effects based on spatial proximity are critical in explaining why this is so. From a methodological perspective, this is the first study that we are aware of that has examined geographical variation in Internet use by using an instrumental variables specification. While others (e.g., Shih and Venkatesh 2004, Brown and Venkatesh 2005) have examined the determinants of the adoption of computers in the home, no study we are aware of has established the presence of peer effects in Internet use arising from the behavior of others in the same region. After using a strategy that controls for the effects of individual and regional characteristics, we find a strong influence for such peer effects. Additional analyses using community isolation data, housing density measures, and directly assessed social interactions provide further support for the presence of peer effects.

This finding is important for a number of reasons. In the presence of peer effects, individuals spatially located in the proximity of people with a propensity to go online will also end up going online. Those who are surrounded by people who choose not to go online will be less likely to do so. As a result, differences in use will tend to get accentuated across regions. The logic of this argument holds up only if peer effects are present. Policy makers often express great concern about the digital divide, i.e., the great disparity in access to the Internet and other information technologies among social groups in the United States In addition to socioeconomic variables, the digital divide has been observed across regions within the UK, Germany, Italy, Japan, and other countries (Chen and Wellman 2004). It has frequently been suggested that this divide can be explained on the basis of individual characteristics such as income and ethnicity, or regional characteristics such as access costs and infrastructure. Our results indicate that peer effects can also be a cause of this disparity.

Yet, peer effects may also be a part of the solution for this problem. The prospects for corrective policy need not be dismal because adoption by some will create a positive influence on others, thereby setting a virtuous cycle in motion. However, in communities where usage rates are low, a half-hearted effort may come to nothing because of the perverse effects of the reluctance of others to adopt. This is likely to be particularly true in more isolated communities. Our paper also points to a need for further studies focusing on the mechanics of social influence, which may be a more useful guide for policy.

From a theoretical perspective, our study complements and extends the literature in IS that has studied social influences in technology adoption and use behavior. In contrast to Brown and Venkatesh (2005) and Venkatesh and Brown (2001), where the traditional referent groups of friends, family, and coworkers are used as drivers of home PC adoption, we defined a broader referent group based on geography. We are thus able to also assess the influence of the behavior of those with whom the focal individual may only come into contact infrequently, such as at community events, at the shopping mall, or at church. Our results underscore the salience of these more distal “referents” in driving adoption of the Internet, responding to the call made in prior work to seek new conceptualizations of important “others” (Rice and Aydin 1991). In their qualitative study of home PC adoption, Brown and Venkatesh (2003) found that nonadoption was driven by a different set of factors, viz., fear of obsolescence and the difficulty of learning how to use the technology, from those driving adoption. Although our theoretical lens in this study is different, it would be interesting to examine what role peer effects play in nonadoption of the Internet, and whether peers can allay or compound such fears.

For ISP firms, the presence of peer effects suggests that marketing campaigns encouraging communication and word of mouth among local communities (peers) are likely to be important. In a different context, Stanley (2003, p. 414) highlights the efficacy of a similar local/community-based strategy to encourage computer adoption. She recommends that because social influence affects computer use policy makers should promote “culturally sensitive, communitybased and -implemented outreach efforts to facilitate computer adoption/use.”

It is likely that gains for ISPs in terms of acquiring new Internet subscribers will accrue from community-based efforts. Some specific strategies that ISPs can adopt could be to organize local awareness and training events and/or to use referral promotional/discount campaigns at community levels.

An understanding of the network effects associated with Internet use is also of importance for the business strategy of firms engaged in electronic commerce. We examined peer effects on Internet use at home only. It is possible that such influence extends to the sites visited online, although this could depend on a much more proximal and intense social interaction. Nonetheless, conversations with people one encounters in community settings that would not typically constitute a referent group (such as a reading group) can have an effect on the specific destinations an individual chooses to visit. Thus, an online merchant’s sales in a region will have the potential to create future sales within that region and his pricing and advertising expenditures should reflect this. The question of whether people are socially influenced in choosing the sites they visit was beyond the scope of the present study, but it clearly merits further attention.

Several additional promising research opportunities can be identified. Although it is abundantly clear that peer effects are present, the precise mechanisms through which social influence exerts itself in this specific context is less understood. We identified social learning and network externalities as two possibilities. There may be others, and our analysis does not allow us to distinguish between them. More qualitative data may shed further light on this issue.

To the extent that peer effects emanate from the behavior of reference groups, a natural question that arises is whether some members of the group are more salient and “influential” than others. Rogers (1995) alludes to such individuals in his identification of opinion leaders as key forces in the diffusion of innovations. The identity of opinion leaders in Internet adoption with local reference groups would be an interesting and important area for future research.

Finally, we focused on regional variations in Internet use within the United States, which is a highly developed nation. Globally, variations in Internet use across and within nations in the developing world are even more stark (OECD 2001, Chen and Wellman 2004). Although we expect our findings to be relevant within other nations, the specific definition of community within these nations may be different, e.g., not based on geography. It behooves policy makers not to neglect the community-based nature of diffusion here. It also requires careful attention to ascertaining the precise boundary of the appropriate reference group.
