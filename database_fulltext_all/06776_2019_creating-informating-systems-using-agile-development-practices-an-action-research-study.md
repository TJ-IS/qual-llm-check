# Creating “informating” systems using Agile development practices: an action research study

Nanda Chingleput Surendra and Salman Nazir

Management Information Systems, Chambers College of Business & Economics, West Virginia University, Morgantown, WV, USA

## ABSTRACT

We use action research to understand the concepts of automating and informating, and study whether agile development practices could be an enabler of informating functionalities. Two questions based on these contrasts and connections motivate this research. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question is: What are the characteristics of an informating functionality and how does an informating functionality di<sup>f</sup>er from an automating functionality? Our second research question is: What role could agile practices play in the development of informating functionalities? In this paper, we discuss the actions, re<sup>fl</sup>ections and lessons learned from three action research iterations while developing an admissions application for a business college.

## 1. Introduction

Firms have invested heavily in information systems for improving organizational processes in order to achieve radical transformations through quality improvement programs, Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) implementations, or E-commerce implementations (Chuang & Lin, 2017; Grover & Kettinger, 2000; Overby, 2008). As organizations strive to improve their information systems, one issue that remains unresolved is how to create systems that empower the organizational users to become mindful actors rather than achieve mindless automation of organizational processes (Cram & Newell, 2016). This dichotomy of information systems for enabling mindful action versus mindless automation is represented by the concepts of informating and automating functionalities, respectively (Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1985). According to Zubo<sup>f</sup>, an informating system functionality empowers the organizational user to understand the business process and to create innovative solutions to organizational problems. An automating system functionality, on the other hand, seeks to replace human e<sup>f</sup>ort. An automating functionality has the e<sup>f</sup>ect of creating a low-level understanding of the business process thereby reducing innovation in organizational processes. While Zubo<sup>f</sup>’s work has been widely acclaimed and cited, only a few information systems studies have used these two concepts (e.g., Kohli & Kettinger, 2004; Tafti, Mithas, & Krishnan, 2007). Most studies have referenced them at a very cursory level (Burton-Jones, 2014), employing these ideas without an in-depth analysis (Gallivan & Depledge, 2003). How the concepts of informating and automating relate to software development is extremely under-researched (Burton-Jones, 2014). As organizations and researchers focus on systems and functionalities that empower people, not replace them with automation, they could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from knowing what characteristics distinguish informating functionalities from automating functionalities. This distinction could help Information Systems researchers and software developers to deliberately, not accidentally, study or develop informating functionalities. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question is: What are the characteristics of an informating functionality and how does an informating functionality di<sup>f</sup>er from an automating functionality?

Since agile development has become widely used in industry, agile practices were applied to develop the system for this research. Studies that have investigated the relation between agile development practices and software functionality have typically conceptualized functionality as a monolithic idea by simply measuring whether user needs were met or not (Cram & Brohman, 2013; Drury-Grogan, 2014; Lee & Xia, 2010). To truly understand how software functionality is a<sup>f</sup>ected by agile practices we need to open the black box of software functionality to reveal the hidden characteristics of automating and informating functionalities and study how agile practices may di<sup>f</sup>erently a<sup>f</sup>ect the two types of functionalities. This in-depth examination could help Information Systems researchers and software developers better understand how agile practices relate to automating and informating functionalities, leading to more e<sup>f</sup>ective study or use of these practices. Hence, our second research question is: What is the role of agile development practices in developing informating functionalities?

We used an action research methodology to study our research questions. Action research aims to advance research understanding while at the same time solving a real-world problem (Baskerville & Myers, 2004). The real-world problem is to develop an admissions system for a business college.

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we discuss informating and automating concepts. Then, we present an overview of agile systems development. We follow with a discussion of action research methodology. In the <sup>fi</sup>fth section, we discuss the research setting and data collection details. The sixth section is the core of this paper in which we discuss our three action research iterations, analyse the research data, and discuss lessons learned. We conclude by revisiting each research question and the related lessons learned, discussing limitations of this research, and providing a few suggestions for future research.

## 2. Informating versus automating

Information systems have the potential to informate as well as to automate (Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1985, 1988). While automating systems functionalities seek to replace human e<sup>f</sup>ort and skill in order to reduce costs, improve e<sup>fi</sup>- ciency, and increase accuracy, informating systems functionalities seek to bring to the surface and make explicit the underlying processes that accomplish work. This enables improvement and innovation in both the work process and the resulting product (Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013).

The vision of automating functionalities is limited by the assumption that more technology means that less sta<sup>f</sup> will be needed, and that the remaining sta<sup>f</sup> will have diminished skill requirements. Greenbaum and Kyng (1991) state that automating functionalities dehumanise by increasing division of labour and routinisation. In contrast, informating functionalities bring quality of work and worker skill into the foreground.

Zubo<sup>f</sup> (1985) provides an insightful contrast between automating and informating. Using a smart machine to merely accomplish work indicates a focus on automating, relying on the machine to provide the intelligence and employing less skilled workers who unquestioningly rely on the machine’s guidance with little understanding of how it functions. The automating functionality of any machine stems from its ability to quickly perform computational tasks with greater e<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Burton-Jones, 2014). On the other hand, using a smart machine that facilitates smart people to accomplish their work using a smart machine indicates a focus on informating, an interdependence between the machine and users. Essentially, the power to informate emanates from information technology’s capability to record all details about organizational operations being performed, empowering the user with deep processrelated knowledge, thus creating a new resource that organizations can use to learn and improve (Burton-Jones, 2014).

Although the concepts of informating and automating have been cited profusely, a review of the literature citing Zubo<sup>f</sup>’s (1985) work reveals that there is not a single study that allows us to understand how these speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics can be developed in information systems. There are several studies that discuss di<sup>f</sup>erent information systems and their informational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. For instance, studies con<sup>fi</sup>rm that when infor mation systems, such as DSS, ERP and SCM systems, have informating characteristics they tend to lead to operational, process, managerial and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Chand, George, Hunton, Owhoso, & Vasudevan, 2005; Saldanha, Melville, Ramirez, & Richardson, 2013; Su & Yang, 2010). The literature also suggests that using tracking technology such as RFID can allow companies to capture more information about their logistics function and enables them to achieve the capability to make informed decisions (Baars, Gille, & Struker, 2009). Studies also assert that having top management support in organizations allows them to develop a focus on achieving a transformative vision rather than an automating vision (Al-Taie, Lane, & Cater-Steel, 2014). We also know from the literature that for <sup>fi</sup>rms in industries where IT played an informating role (improved information <sup>fl</sup>ows throughout organizations), there is a strong positive association between future earnings and IT spending (Anderson, Banker, & Hu, 2003). Moreover, the literature also suggests that focusing on implementing informating functionality allows organizational transformation (Abraham & Junglas, 2011). Despite all these studies that mention the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of informating information systems, we know little about the characteristics that cause this informating capability. Most studies cite Zubo<sup>f</sup>’s (1985) work in a passing manner and simply list the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of informating without discussing how these informating characteristics can be developed. This study aspires to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap in understanding the di<sup>f</sup>erences between informating and automating functionalities and explores how researchers and practitioners can deliberately focus on studying and developing informating functionalities.

## 3. Agile development

Agile development has the following characteristics (Schmidt, 2016; Surendra, 2009; Sutherland & Sutherland, 2014; Wang, Conboy, & Pikkarainen, 2012): (a) an acceptance that requirements will change, (b) an adaptable development process, (c) short iterations that deliver tangible functionality, (d) frequent, open communication between software developers and customers, and (e) system designs that are as simple as possible. The essential values of agile development are the following (Agile Alliance, 2001; Schmidt, 2016; Surendra, 2009; Sutherland & Sutherland, 2014):

(1) “Individuals and interactions over processes and tools” emphasizes the value of software developers both as individuals and as teams instead of promoting software development processes and tools.

(2) “Working software over comprehensive documentation” keeps software developers’ attention on delivering value to customers by providing software functionality rather than expending time and e<sup>f</sup>ort on creating extensive documentation that becomes outdated and unusable as the system evolves and grows.

(3) “Customer collaboration over contract negotiation” reminds software developers to view customers as partners and collaborators who are an integral part of the development process rather than untrustworthy adversaries.

(4) “Responding to change over following a plan” emphasizes the need to adapt to changing user requirements and environments to deliver a useful system rather than rigidly follow a pre-established plan disregarding those changing requirements and environments.

Software development research has focused on several aspects of agile development practices. Dingsoyr, Nerur, Balijepally, and Moe (2012) present an interesting review of the work done in agile software development. They suggest that earlier research works focused on adoption of agile methods and the e<sup>fi</sup>cacy of pair programming (e.g., Boehm, 2002; Nawrocki & Wojciechowski, 2001; Nerur, Mahapatra, & Mangalaraj, 2005; Williams, Kessler, Cunningham, & Je<sup>f</sup>ries, 2000) while later research streams have focused on team dynamics issues such as trust, self-organization, and communication (e.g., Moe, Dingsøyr, & Dybå, 2009). Research has also focused on issues related to consequences of testdriven development (Erdogmus, Morisio, & Torchiano, 2005; Janzen & Saiedian, 2005), adoption and post-adoption (Cao, Mohan, Xu, & Ramesh, 2009; Mangalaraj, Mahapatra, & Nerur, 2009), and challenges of agile development in distributed settings (Ramesh, Cao, Mohan, & Peng, 2006), and the like.<sup>1</sup> This wide range of studies have investigated the e<sup>f</sup>ects of agile practices on di<sup>f</sup>erent performance metrics, the most notable one being software functionality. Interestingly, the idea of software functionality is often conceptualized as a monolithic concept that captures whether the users’ needs and technical needs are met. For instance, Lee and Xia (2010) used on-time completion, on-budget completion, and software functionality as their dependent variables and de<sup>fi</sup>ned functionality as meeting users’ needs and technical needs. Drury-Grogan (2014) also investigated the concept of functionality and de<sup>fi</sup>ned it as completing the tasks that were decided upon during requirements gathering, while Cram and Brohman (2013) de<sup>fi</sup>ned it as the delivery of system functions that best meet customer requirements. We contend that in order to truly understand how functionality is a<sup>f</sup>ected by agile practices we need to open the concept of software functionality and study how automating and informating functionalities are di<sup>f</sup>erent and how agile practices may differently a<sup>f</sup>ect the two types of functionalities. The current literature lacks a clear understanding of how agile practices could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the two di<sup>f</sup>ering functionalities. This study aspires to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by opening the software functionality black box to better understand how agile development practices relate to them.

## 4. Action research methodology

This research uses the methodology of action research, which aims to advance research understanding while at the same time solving a real-world problem (Baskerville & Myers, 2004). In this research, we are trying to understand the di<sup>f</sup>ering characteristics of informating and automating and the role that agile development practices could play in developing informating functionalities. The real-world problem involved developing a system to overcome the limitations and problems of a slow and labour-intensive admissions system for a business college.

Unlike other research methods, where the researcher seeks to study organizational phenomena but not to change them, the action researcher simultaneously creates organizational change and studies the process and implications of the change (Argyris, Putnam, & Smith, 1985; Baskerville & Myers, 2004). Action research provides the researcher with the ability to intervene and improve the system, as lessons are learned (Kohli & Kettinger, 2004).

We conceptualized our action research in terms of a research understanding (U), a problem-solving methodology (M), an area of application in which to apply M (A), and a speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem situation in which to investigate A (P). This conceptualization is based on the original work of Checkland and Holwell (1998) and extended by other action researchers (Davison, Martinsons, & Koch, 2004; Mckay & Marshall, 2001; Oates, 2006; Oates & Fitzgerald, 2007).

U: When we started our research, we had initial research lenses rather than a research understanding. These were the lenses of agile development practices and Zubo<sup>f</sup>’s concepts of automating and informating. However, we had no initial understanding of how the concepts of automating and informating would manifest in system functionalities or how the agile practices could in<sup>fl</sup>uence or impact the automating and informating characteristics of the system.

M: We used agile development as our problemsolving methodology.

A: Our area of application was developing system functionalities using agile development practices.

P: Our problem situation was an admissions system for a business college.

We conducted our action research through three iterations of the action research process of diagnosing, planning, acting, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on and evaluating outcomes, and specifying learning (Baskerville, 1999). Each iteration correlated with one admission cycle, a period of eight weeks, starting with students applying for admission to the college and ending with the academic advisors making o<sup>fi</sup>cial admission decisions. ## 5. The research setting and data collection

This action research project was conducted in a business college at a large state university in midwestern United States over a period of four years. During the academic years when the research was conducted, the college had approximately 1,000 undergraduate students while the university had approximately 28,000 students.

The software development project and the action research were mutually initiated by the users and the <sup>fi</sup>rst author who was the action researcher (Avison, Baskerville, & Myers, 2001; Surendra, 2009). The users consisted of the director of undergraduate programs and advising at the college (who was also an academic advisor), two undergraduate academic advisors, a support sta<sup>f</sup> member who maintained the college student records, and three graduate assistants who assisted the academic advisors. The users’ problem was a labour-intensive admissions system that made the application and decision process slow and susceptible to errors. It was di<sup>fi</sup>cult for students to submit their applications to the college, and for the advisors to process the applications, make informed and correct admission decisions, and communicate their decisions to students in a timely manner. The action researcher was a faculty member in the Information Systems department in the business college who taught courses in software development. Since the college could not assign their limited number of IT personnel to work the required number of hours on the project and did not have the estimated US\$50,000 to US\$100,000 in their budget to hire an outside company to develop the system, the users approached the action researcher and asked if he could develop the system using his software development knowledge. The researcher said he would be willing to work on developing the system without any monetary compensation if developing the system could constitute his action research study. The researcher made clear to the users that their participation in the research study was entirely voluntary and that they could choose to discontinue participating at any time for any reason. This action research study was initiated based on this mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial agreement.

The action researcher was the primary software developer and project manager. Two employees from the business college IT department with the title “database developer and application programmer” worked with the action researcher on developing the functionalities. The college’s IT department personnel were not assigned full time to this project. They usually worked with the action researcher after they completed their allocated tasks for the day.

## 5.1. Admission process

The business college, which o<sup>f</sup>ers three undergraduate degrees and six majors, admits students twice a year, approximately 350 students during the fall semester and 150 students during the spring semester (Surendra, 2009). Prior to their junior year, students in the university who are interested in a business degree apply for admission to the business college. Applicants need to meet the following three requirements to be eligible to apply to the college:

Action research process applied in this study (summarized).

(1) They should attain junior status at the time of o<sup>fi</sup>cial admittance to the college.

(2) They should have a GPA greater than or equal to 2.50.

(3) They should complete a list of 10 speci<sup>fi</sup>ed courses with a required grade. The grade requirement ranges from a pass to a B depending on the course and the major for which the student applies.

Once the applications are in the system, the business college academic advisors start the process of making admission decisions. They request applicants’ transcripts from the university’s students records o<sup>fi</sup>ce to calculate each applicant’s total credits and GPA. Using total credits and GPA, the advisors search for ineligible applicants (total credits less than required for junior status or GPA < 2.50), who are removed from the pool of applicants.

The advisors arrange eligible applicants in two lists. The <sup>fi</sup>rst list is sorted by descending GPA. The second list is sorted by degree, major, and descending GPA. The advisors confer with the college deans using these two lists to determine the cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA – applicants at or above this GPA are accepted while those below are denied admission. The advisors and deans set the cuto<sup>f</sup> GPA to meet the college’s targeted enrolment numbers. Applicants who are above the cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA are given provisional admission, subject to getting the required grade on 10 prerequisite courses. Applicants who satisfy all course requirements, in addition to having the requisite GPA and course credits, are o<sup>fi</sup>- cially admitted to the college.

## 5.3. Agile development characteristics of the system developed for this research

Most agile development methodologists (Cockburn, 2005), agile practitioners (Sutherland & Sutherland, 2014) and agile researchers (Schmidt, 2016) state that agile development is not a speci<sup>fi</sup>c process that can be followed. Rather, agile development is a philosophy and a way of thinking about software development. We followed the major principles of agile development, as described in the Agile Manifesto, in developing the system for this research. While we did not apply all agile development practices, we practised the following major characteristics of agile development (Schmidt, 2016; Surendra, 2009; Wang et al., 2012):

(a) Frequent communication with users: We frequently communicated with the primary users, the business college academic advisors and their graduate assistants, through face-toface meetings and email (as catalogued in Tables 1 and 2). We met with users at least twice a week, with each meeting lasting at least an hour, during the <sup>fi</sup>rst three and a half months of the project when we developed and released most of the system functionalities.

(b) Short, iterative development cycles: At least once a week during the <sup>fi</sup>rst three and a half months of the project, we demonstrated new or changed functionalities to the users and discussed whether these functionalities met the users’ requirements. The users gave detailed feedback (whether requirements were met or not; if not, why not) that we incorporated into our next system development cycle. When a functionality met all requirements and passed all tests, we released the code module containing that functionality into production. We released a total of 24 functionalities during the <sup>fi</sup>rst action research iteration.

Paired programming helped us complement each other’s strengths (one was a stronger designer while the other was a stronger coder) and identify errors in logic and code faster.

## 6. Action research analysis and discussion

We went through three action research iterations. Each iteration mirrored the following admission cycle: (a) students apply for admission to the business college (a 4-week period), (b) advisors and their graduate assistants process the applications during that same 4 week period, (c) advisors and college deans make provisional admission decisions (a 2-week period), (d) two weeks before the start of the admission term, advisors verify whether provisionally admitted students have met grade requirements on 10 prerequisite courses to <sup>fi</sup>nalize admissions (a 2-week period).

For each iteration, we start by discussing user bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Then, we introduce the relevant empirical data and re<sup>fl</sup>ections that led us to our lessons learned. The action researcher and users participated in these discussions and re<sup>fl</sup>ections. The lessons learned from the discussions and re<sup>fl</sup>ections are from the perspectives of both the action researcher and the users. At the end of each iteration, we discuss how our research understanding evolved based on lessons learned during that iteration.

## 6.1. First action research iteration

## 6.1.2. Relevant empirical data, re<sup>fl</sup>ections and lessons learned

## 6.1.1. User bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

We developed two websites. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was an application website for students that enabled them to (a) verify that they met requirements for a particular business degree and major and (b) if they met requirements, complete and submit the application form. This website helped students better understand the more complex application requirements resulting in fewer ineligible applications. The second enabled advisors to (a) set dates for when the application website was open and closed for each admission semester and (b) edit the content of three form letters e-mailed to admitted, denied, and ineligible applicants.

We developed a desktop system for advisors to process applications and make their decisions. This system, named the “Application Processing System,” was installed on the computers of the advisors and their graduate assistants, a total of seven primary users. The dean’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce personnel are secondary users who use the system only when the provisional admission decisions are made, helping determine the cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA for each semester. We developed functionalities for this system in chronological order of need for the users, as indicated by the numbers above each functionality in Figure 2.

6.1.2.1. Integration with users’ workflow. In the <sup>fi</sup>nal phase of the admission process, the advisors need to verify that every provisionally admitted student has completed all 10 required courses with the requisite grades. Grades for courses taken the previous semester are <sup>fi</sup>nalized two weeks before the admission semester starts. Hence, there is a long delay after the provisional admissions are made and before this <sup>fi</sup>nal phase begins – 6 weeks for spring admissions and 6 months for fall admissions. This phase is a critical part of the advisors’ work in the admissions process. It is imperative that they communicate with provisionally admitted students who have not met course requirements to help them make alternate study plans before the semester starts. During our discussions, the advisors re<sup>fl</sup>ected that this was a particularly stressful phase of the process for them because of the time constraints within which this veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and communication needed to be done and the potentially serious consequences for students in case they made errors. The process was tedious, especially since it involved checking several courses for each student, and there were hundreds of students to be checked. Despite the care taken, there were a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant number of errors with some of them causing major disruptions to students’ graduation timelines. Since these errors were discovered by the students at the beginning of the semester when classes had begun, the advisors had to scramble desperately to patch together courses to <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to help mitigate these students’ problems. These problems and the associated stress and disruption both to students’ course schedules and advisors’ work schedules were a major impetus for the advisors initiating this project.

Application Processing System functionalities.

To better understand what caused this process to be so stressful and susceptible to errors, we observed an advisor and her graduate assistant performing this veri<sup>fi</sup>cation process. They described and discussed what they were doing during each step of the process. First, they searched for provisionally admitted applicants who had not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed all course requirements for admission into the college. Then, for each applicant who had not met all course requirements, they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the de<sup>fi</sup>cient courses and determined whether the applicant had not taken the course or had obtained a lesser grade than required. Finally, they e-mailed each of these applicants stating the speci<sup>fi</sup>c course requirements that were not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. To email each of these applicants, we observed the advisors copying the email address for each applicant from the Application Processing System and pasting the address into the “To address” textbox of an email client program after opening a new email message window. This was a cumbersome and timeconsuming process that prevented the advisors from easily accomplishing their veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and communication work. Based on this observation, the advisors and action researcher discussed the system changes that might help streamline this process. We made changes to existing functionalities and added a new email client functionality to the Application Processing System to integrate these functionalities with the users’ work practices. The functionalities are now seamlessly integrated with the users’ threestep work<sup>fl</sup>ow as follows:

(a) Using drop-down list options, the advisor searches for applicants who are provisionally accepted but who have not met all course prerequisites.

(b) The advisor drills down to get the details for each of these applicants by clicking on one speci<sup>fi</sup>c applicant. In this detailed view, she gets the list of courses (with the required grade and the applicant’s grade) for which the applicant has not met requirements.

(c) The advisor clicks the “Send email” button, which opens an email client window with the from-address, to-address, subject, and salutation pre-<sup>fi</sup>lled. She adds her comments and sends an e-mail message to the applicant listing the prerequisites he has failed to meet.

The users said that these changes and additions to the Application Processing System prevented disruption to their work<sup>fl</sup>ow and thinking – their need to be conscious of opening another program (the email client), copying and pasting email addresses, writing and sending messages, and then moving back to the Application Processing System. They re<sup>fl</sup>ected that they could just focus on getting their work done which, in turn, helped them avoid making errors such as copying and pasting the wrong student email addresses and writing messages about courses not completed or failed to the students who had actually passed all courses required for admission. This system integration into the users’ work<sup>fl</sup>ow made the system “ready-to-hand,” being “invisible” in helping them accomplish their work, rather than “present-at-hand,” making them aware of its presence while they try to accomplish their work (Winograd & Flores, 1986). Making this set of functionalities “ready-to-hand” enabled the advisors to quickly and easily accomplish the extremely critical work of identifying and communicating with students in danger of being denied admission. These observations and discussions led us to two lessons from the research perspective. First, we learned that it is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to separate automating and informating functionalities in a system. The functionalities tend to merge relatively seamlessly from automating into informating and vice versa. Individually, each of the three functionalities constituting the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and communication process might be considered automating functionalities. However, when these three functionalities integrate with the users’ work<sup>fl</sup>ow to help them accomplish critical work objectives, they acquire informating characteristics. Second, we learned that making a functionality ready-to-hand could help transform it from an automating role to an informating role. When the functionalities were seamlessly available for the advisors (ready-to-hand), they did not need to spend time on gathering information from several systems – when switching between systems, their focus was on ensuring that they were using the di<sup>f</sup>erent system functionalities correctly (present-at-hand). With the functionalities being ready-to-hand, they were able to focus on accomplishing their work objectives. Our discussions and observations also made us realize that agile practices provide opportunities for informating what would otherwise have been automating functionalities. We would not have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the informating opportunity for the veri<sup>fi</sup>cation and communication system functionalities were it not for the agile development principles of (a) releasing functionalities iteratively and incrementally and (b) constant communication with the users with the objective of incorporating feedback into the next system development cycle. Plan-driven development typically freezes requirements and, hence, does not provide the developer the means to identify informating opportunities that involve modifying or adding functionalities to enable users to seamlessly accomplish critical work objectives.

6.1.2.2. User control. Prior to setting up the two new websites for students and advisors, the advisors discussed that the process of making the application website available to students was controlled by IT personnel. They stated that this process was timeconsuming and error prone. The advisors had to contact the college webmaster to upload and download web pages on speci<sup>fi</sup>c dates each semester to enable and disable student application submissions, a process made more cumbersome when the deadline for submitting applications needed to be extended. Every semester, at least some of the content of the web pages had to be changed. Since the advisors had neither the technical knowledge to make changes to the web pages nor access to the web pages, they had to request that speci<sup>fi</sup>c changes be made by the webmaster before uploading the <sup>fi</sup>les to the college web server.

Similarly, the previous application processing system relied heavily on a database programmer to do the work of getting data into and out of the database. The programmer would retrieve a list of applicants from the database, obtain transcripts for these applicants, change the transcript data into a format that was compatible with the database, and <sup>fi</sup>nally import that data into the database. This work was done over several days and the advisors were required to set up several appointments with this programmer.

Both these earlier systems and associated work processes required the advisors to rely heavily on IT personnel to accomplish their work. This led to uncertainty for the advisors regarding the availability of the IT personnel, whether the work would be done by the required date, and when the work did get done, whether it was done correctly.

The advisors were very relieved to learn from the action researcher that they did not have to call upon IT personnel for making the application website available to students, specifying the dates on which they should be available, and if needed making extensions to the deadline for submitting applications. They said that this sense of taking control served as a major initial impetus for them to not only start using the system but to also start providing suggestions to make the system more useful and usable for them. The administration website we developed for the advisors enabled them to set opening and closing dates for the application website, easily make changes to the content of web pages, and edit the content of three form letters e-mailed to admitted, denied, and ineligible students. The advisors said that this ease of use and control of the websites made them realize that the Application Processing System could also provide a similar level of ease and control – and so they actively participated in helping build those characteristics in this system. They suggested designing a one-click process to get a listing of applicants to send to the university’s student records o<sup>fi</sup>ce requesting transcripts, which we built. When the transcripts were received, they suggested that we add another one-click functionality to enable them to import transcripts into the database. All these system functionalities were totally under the control of the users and did not require any communication with IT personnel, which allowed the users to focus on accomplishing their work, in a timely manner with the con<sup>fi</sup>dence that it was done correctly. The lesson we learned from the research perspective was that an important characteristic of informating functionalities is greater user control. Informating functionalities provide users more control over the system enabling them to perform their work without depending on IT personnel. This characteristic <sup>fi</sup>t very well with the agile development principles of user-centred development and users’ control over the inclusion or exclusion of any system functionality.

## 6.1.3. Research understanding (U1)

Based on lessons learned during the <sup>fi</sup>rst iteration, our research understanding at the end of that <sup>fi</sup>rst iteration could be summarized as follows:

It is not easy to separate the automating and informating functionalities of a system. A functionality moves from an automating role to an informating role along a continuum, rather than being distinct and separate entities. Functionalities that are ready to-hand and provide more control to users are two key characteristics of informating systems. Hence, two seemingly similar functionalities may play either an automating or an informating role depending on how much of “ready-to-handedness” and user control characteristics they have in them. Agile development practices could help enhance functionalities with informating characteristics, making them ready to-hand and under the control of users.

## 6.2. Second action research iteration

## 6.2.1. User bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

We developed all 24 user functionalities in the <sup>fi</sup>rst iteration. So, the second iteration did not involve the development of new functionalities. Instead, this iteration involved identifying and correcting problems with existing functionalities. We detected and corrected two major problems. The <sup>fi</sup>rst problem involved advisors accidentally importing the same student transcript data <sup>fi</sup>le more than once. Typically, advisors request transcripts from the university’s student records o<sup>fi</sup>ce at least thrice during the 4-week application period. This results in multiple transcript data <sup>fi</sup>les containing transcripts for di<sup>f</sup>erent groups of students. On two occasions, an advisor and a graduate assistant accidentally imported the same data <sup>fi</sup>le twice resulting in about 100 students getting credited twice for the same set of courses. To prevent multiple transcript imports for the same student, we created a program module to check if the transcripts in a data <sup>fi</sup>le had been imported earlier. If the transcript data <sup>fi</sup>le had been imported earlier, we provided the user a warning message and prevented the transcripts from being imported. In the second case, errors occurred based on a particular sequence in which users carried out operations. If a user attempted to access the functionality to update a student’s grades on courses taken outside the university before importing transcript data for that student, the system generated an error not understandable to the user. We corrected this error by checking the sequence of user operations and providing a message that a user could understand (“Transcript data not available for this student”) if she attempted to access the functionality to update a student’s grades before importing transcript data.

## 6.2.2. Relevant empirical data, re<sup>fl</sup>ections and lessons learned

6.2.2.1. Users’ perspective. Three heavy users of the system, an academic advisor and two graduate assistants, apologized several times to the system developers (one of the developers was the action researcher) that they “made dumb mistakes” and “messed up” when using the Application Processing system. We discussed the two speci<sup>fi</sup>c cases which they termed “dumb mistakes” in the user bene<sup>fi</sup>ts section. The <sup>fi</sup>rst case happened on two occasions. An advisor and a graduate assistant accidentally imported the same data <sup>fi</sup>le twice resulting in about 100 students getting credited twice for the same set of courses. The second case was that errors occurred based on a particular sequence in which users carried out operations. If a user attempted to access the functionality to update a student’s grades on courses taken outside the university before importing transcript data for that student, the system generated an error not understandable to the user.

The users were embarrassed that these “mistakes” made them call upon the systems developers to <sup>fi</sup>x and reverse problems, thinking that they were responsible for increasing the workload of the systems developers. The action researcher realized that these users were implying that they considered the system always correct and infallible and that they considered themselves the weak links in the process of accomplishing work involving use of the system. He held a meeting with the users to clarify and emphasize that the system’s role was to help them e<sup>f</sup>ectively accomplish their work and that they should not consider the system an infallible “smart machine.” He encouraged the users to take an informating perspective (asking how a functionality can help them better accomplish their work) instead of an automating perspective (asking how to use a functionality so that they did not “mess up” while using it). Initially, the automating perspective was the predominant user perspective about this system, epitomized by the message from an advisor when setting up the initial project meeting, “We will be having a needs assessment meeting to discuss our current application process for admission to the College” (emphasis added).

After several discussions, the users said that they were starting to think about how the system could help them better perform their work instead of assigning blame to themselves when anything went wrong when using the system. By knowing that the system developers actually wanted to know about system problems, they said they were less hesitant to contact the developers when they encountered these problems. Instead of feeling embarrassed about burdening the developers, they said they felt they were helping the developers by reporting these problems. For the developers, the users’ willingness to report problems encountered while using the system meant that they were able to detect problems that are di<sup>fi</sup>- cult to detect except when users use the system in the organizational context, such as the two problems corrected during this iteration. Users blaming themselves prevented developers from learning about system <sup>fl</sup>aws and improving the system to meet their needs. Users adopting an informating perspective helped ensure that they were willing to provide feedback and suggestions to the system developers to improve the system.

As the action researcher re<sup>fl</sup>ected on the second iteration, one of the key lessons of this iteration was that a functionality does not become an informating functionality by the system developers’ designation that it was so. Instead, he realized that it acquires its informating capability by users adopting an informating perspective – asking how the functionality can help them better accomplish their work objectives – when using that functionality. A related lesson was that even though the system developers might think that a functionality has all characteristics of an informating functionality, it is not necessary that it will be used as an informating functionality unless the user’s perspective changes.

The action researcher’s observation that users adopting an informating perspective helps ensure that they are willing to provide feedback and suggestions to the system developers to improve the system led to the emergence of an interesting insight which probably would have been harder to discern if this was not an action research study. When users adopt an informating perspective, it improves and further enables the agile development process since users become more willing to provide critical feedback for improving the system. This leads to the lesson that users’ adopting an informating perspective could help improve the practice of agile systems development. This, in turn, leads to the lesson that informating functionalities and agile systems development could have a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial relationship; not just a oneway relationship of agile development practices being better suited for developing informating functionalities. On the other hand, during the initial stages when the project started the users assumed that the focus was on automating functionalities and, as a result, adopted an automating perspective. When adopting an automating perspective, the users’ view was that the functionalities provided the “intelligence” to perform the tasks that needed to be done and they considered themselves subordinate to the functionalities. Whenever an error occurred while using the functionalities, they thought that they were the reason for “messing up,” the functionalities could not be the problem. With this mindset of subordination and avoiding mistakes, they were reluctant to provide feedback and suggestions to the developers about the functionalities. This lack of feedback and suggestions from the users is not conducive to the e<sup>f</sup>ective practice of agile development. Hence, the counter lesson to developing informating functionalities is that a focus on automating functionalities could negatively impact agile development practices.

Based on lessons learned during the second iteration, our research understanding evolved from U1 to U2. The revised understanding, U2, was the following:

It is not easy to separate the automating and informating functionalities of a system. A functionality moves from an automating role to an informating role along a continuum, rather than being distinct and separate entities. Functionalities that are readyto-hand and provide more control to users are two key characteristics of informating systems. A functionality does not become an informating functionality by the system developer’s designation. Instead, it acquires its informating capability by users adopting an informating perspective when using that functionality. Users adopting an informating perspective could result in better practice of agile development principles. Hence, informating functionalities and agile development could have a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial relationship.

## 6.3. Third action research iteration

## 6.3.1. User bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

In this iteration, we helped the primary users by developing one extension and by NOT developing the second requested extension. Since both functionality extension requests were from potential secondary users, we were wary of scope creep. To resolve our con<sup>fl</sup>icting objectives of (a) following agile development principles by responding to user feedback and (b) avoiding scope creep, we asked the question: How to judge whether a proclaimed user is an actual user?

The <sup>fi</sup>rst functionality extension request was from an employee in the business college’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce of alumni relations. She requested that a functionality extension be added to aid the alumni relations o<sup>fi</sup>ce by identifying applicants who were related to college alumni. Adding this extension would result in cluttering the application form and introduce a problem of having to integrate disparate databases. We discussed with the primary users whether developing this extension was going to help them perform their work and concluded that it would not help. Hence, we judged this potential secondary user to be a non-user from the perspective of the primary users and decided not to add this functionality.

The second functionality extension request was from an administrator in the university’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce of student records who provided the advisors with transcripts for applicants who had taken courses outside the university. The advisors were requesting these transcripts using the applicants’ names and the names of the outside universities. These paper-based transcripts were organized chronologically by year and semester of student entry into the university. The absence of entry year and semester in the advisors request made the students records administrator’s task of locating transcripts di<sup>fi</sup>cult and timeconsuming. She requested a functionality extension to have the applicants provide their semester and year of entering the university and include them in the advisors’ transcript requests. This extension would help her respond more easily and quickly to the advisors’ request for applicants’ outside university transcripts. Since this extension would help the advisors make their admission decisions in a timely and informed manner, we judged this potential secondary user to be a legitimate user and added the requested extension to the system.

## 6.3.2. Relevant empirical data, re<sup>fl</sup>ections and lessons learned

6.3.2.1. Assumptions and power. The business college deans and advisors had used a common cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA for admission to all business college majors for

several decades. After deploying the Application Processing System, the academic advisors could obtain reports stating percentages of acceptances and denials by major, data that was previously neither known nor available. The action researcher met with the academic advisors to review and discuss the reports. These reports showed the advisors that a higher percentage of applicants to certain majors, such as Marketing, were accepted compared to other majors, such as Management Information Systems. Further, since the system enabled “drill downs” into the details of each applicant, advisors were able to determine why students applying to a particular major may have a lower GPA than students applying to other majors. For example, several students applying for the Management Information Systems major were transferring from Engineering programs or Computer Science programs where courses are more challenging compared to the courses taken by students who were applying for other business college majors. The academic advisors were excited to have the capability to do this in-depth analysis of the applicants. The advisors said that this analysis, enabled by Application Processing System functionalities, revealed that the admission decision process depended exclusively on GPA and that factors such as an applicant’s interest in the subject and the level of di<sup>fi</sup>culty of courses previously taken were not considered. The hidden assumption that GPA is the best way to determine admission or rejection had now been revealed by the system. Each applicant’s interests and willingness to take challenging courses had also been revealed. Due to these revelations, the advisors said that they were questioning whether admis sion decisions should be made by major (with di<sup>f</sup>erent cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPAs for each major) or continue being made using one common cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA applied to all majors. Since the faculty of most departments wanted to accept a certain number of students each semester, it seemed in both the applicants’ and most of the college departments’ interests that GPA cuto<sup>f</sup>s be established separately for each major. The advisors met with, and advised, the admitted students on a regular basis until they graduated. They wanted to use the additional information and indicators other than just GPA in admitting students so that they knew that they were advising motivated students who wanted to thrive in their respective majors, rather than students just going through the motions of getting a degree. Since both departmental faculty and the academic advisors wanted to base the admission decision on more than just the applicant’s GPA, the advisors told the systems developers to develop a functionality to make admission decisions by major, allowing each major to have di<sup>f</sup>erent cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPAs. During these three action research iterations, the advisors discussed the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using this functionality with the college deans, including showing them the di<sup>f</sup>erences in reports of applicants admitted using the di<sup>f</sup>erent criteria (GPA only versus a combination of GPA with the other criteria), trying to convince them to adopt this potentially better way of making admission decisions. By the end of the third action iteration, it became clear to the advisors and to the action researcher that the college deans preferred to maintain the status quo by continuing to use a common cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA for all majors. Hence, this functionality was disabled and made unusable during the third iteration.

A lesson from this iteration was the contrasting visibilities of automating and informating functionalities. A system’s automating functionalities are usually more visible than its informating functionalities. However, informating functionalities are more likely to surface, and bring into question, hidden assumptions and taken-for-granted work processes (Zubo<sup>f</sup>, 1988). In this study, this happened when the Application Processing System revealed the hidden assumption that GPA is the best way to determine admission or rejection. Each applicant’s interests and willingness to take challenging courses had also been revealed. Once these hidden assumptions had been revealed to them, the academic advisors wanted to base the admission decision on more than just the applicant’s GPA and allow each major to have di<sup>f</sup>erent cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPAs. However, the college deans preferred to maintain the status quo by continuing to use a common cut-o<sup>f</sup> GPA for all majors. The general tendency for people in power and control is to want the status quo to continue as it helps them maintain their power. So, they tend to prefer that assumptions continue to be hidden and work processes continue to follow a taken-for-granted approach. As a result, informating functionalities are more likely to face resistance from people in power who wish to hinder their deployment compared to the deployment of automating functionalities. In this study, the college deans resisted and scuttled the functionality to make decisions using other criteria in addition to GPA, thereby maintaining the status quo.

A functionality moves from an automating role to an informating role along a continuum, rather than being distinct and separate entities. ## 6.4. Re<sup>fl</sup>ections on using action research

This section is written from the perspective of the <sup>fi</sup>rst author who was the action researcher. Since he was a member of the organization in which this action research was conducted, it was much easier for him to get acceptance from the action research participants. He knew the academic advisors, who were the primary users of the system, prior to the research project. For the same reason of being a member of the organization, he did not have to expend signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time and e<sup>f</sup>ort in understanding the context and background in which this action research was conducted.

While most of his interactions with the users and other system developers (who were employees of the college’s IT department) were smooth, there were occasional problems. The main cause for these problems was the blurring of the lines between his different roles (faculty member of the college, action researcher, and system developer). During the initial stages, some of the users were intimidated by the technology and were reticent to speak with him about problems they were experiencing using the system. This reticence seemed to be due to them considering him a colleague, in his role as a faculty member. They might have felt more comfortable talking about these system problems to someone they knew only in the role of a system developer. When they did start speaking with him about these problems, they kept apologizing stating that they were making “dumb mistakes” and that they should use the system in a way that it did not result in errors. After extensive discussions with the users in his role as an action researcher assuring them that they were not to blame themselves and that they should expect the system to be meeting their needs rather than vice versa, the users became comfortable sending messages to him (either by email or phone) whenever they experienced a problem. However, other than some occasional tension during the software development period, his relations with all of them were cordial.

## 7. Conclusion

We conclude by revisiting the two research ques tions that motivated this research and review the lessons we have learned from acting and re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on them over three action research iterations. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question was: What are the characteristics of an informating functionality and how does an informating functionality di<sup>f</sup>er from an auto mating functionality? We learned <sup>fi</sup>ve lessons regarding informating characteristics. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst lesson was that it is not easy to separate the automat ing and informating functionalities of a system. Our observations during this action research suggest that a functionality moves from an automating role to an informating role along a continuum rather than being two discrete and separate func tionalities. We found that as the same functional ities were re-designed to become more integrated with the users’ work processes, they enabled the users to accomplish their work in a seamless manner by empowering them with information obtained from disparate systems. This allowed the transfor mation of functionalities that were previously con sidered mere automation functionalities into informating functionalities. Second, informating functionalities tend to be more “ready-to-hand” rather than “present-at-hand,” enabling seamless system integration with the users’ work practices. We observed that automating functionalities are often intrusive as they pose hindrances to achieving the task at hand in a smooth fashion, as shown in the case of the advisor who had to pull information from three di<sup>f</sup>erent systems just to be able to write an email to students to let them know about their application status along with the reason for rejec tion, acceptance or conditional admission. On the other hand, when the functionalities became ready to-hand, the entire process was accomplished in a smooth one-click fashion which enabled the advi sor to concentrate on the task at hand rather than focus on overcoming the hindrances caused by the application. Third, informating functionalities pro vide greater user control over the system, enabling them to perform their work without having to rely on IT personnel. We observed that when users themselves were able to set the start and end dates for when the application website would be accessible to the students to submit applications as well as make changes to content on that site, it made them feel empowered and was a key factor that led them to take ownership of the Application Processing System. Fourth, a system functionality does not become an informating functionality by the software developer’s designation. Instead, we observed that it is the users’ adoption of an informating perspective that helps ensure that functionalities are used in an informating manner. Fifth, while automating functionalities tend to have more visibility, informating functionalities unearth taken-for-granted work practices and underlying assumptions that threaten the status quo. As a result, informating functionalities are more likely to encounter resistance, especially from people in positions of power and control. We observed this in the case of the system enabled “drill downs” into the details of each applicant. This capability enabled advisors to determine why students applying to a particular major may have a lower GPA than students applying to other majors. Thus, the takenfor-granted practice of simply considering the student GPA as the sole admission criteria was shown to be problematic by the system. Changing this policy, however, threatened the status quo and encountered resistance from the dean’s o<sup>fi</sup>ce.

Our second research question was: What is the role of agile development practices in developing an informating functionality? We learned three lessons regarding this question. First, since agile development focuses on constant interaction with users, short iterations in the development cycles, and immediate feedback from users, it is ideally suited for identifying informating opportunities for the system. Second, agile development emphasizes user-centred and usercontrolled development. This <sup>fi</sup>ts well with achieving the informating characteristic of more user control. Our third lesson was an interesting insight that further informs the agile practices and informating systems relation. We observed that when users adopt an informating perspective, they are more willing to provide higher quality and quantity of feedback compared to when they adopt an automating perspective. High quality and quantity of user feedback is vital for agile development. We observed this in the case of advisors who became more involved in giving critical feedback to the developers after the action researcher had several discussions with them to explain that the system is to enable them in performing their task and that it needs to conform to their needs and not the other way around. This, in turn, helped the agile development process as it engendered in the users a strong inclination to be part of the development process by providing critical feedback. This lesson suggests that informating systems and agile systems development could have a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial relationship; not just a one-way relationship of agile development practices being better suited for developing informating systems.

An additional research insight that emerged from studying these two research questions in juxtaposition was that users leverage the short cycles and iterative characteristics of the agile development process to experiment with, test and tweak the boundaries and interactions between automating and informating functionalities until the system <sup>fi</sup>ts their socio-technical setting. One of the instances when we observed users doing such intricate manoeuvring, testing and tweaking was during the development and usage of the functionalities comprising the work-<sup>fl</sup>ow for determining which students did not meet admission requirements and then communicating the decision to those students, informing them about the reasons for their non-admission.

## 7.1. Contributions and limitations

This study makes several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions to research and practice. Research on software function ality has treated this construct as a monolithic concept. For instance, studies often conceptualize software functionality as on-time and on-budget completion (Lee & Xia, 2010), or as meeting targets stipulated during the analysis phase (Drury-Grogan, 2014), or even simply as meeting customer requirements (Cram & Brohman, 2013). Although software functionalities and their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are a common theme in IS development research (e.g., Cao et al., 2009; Moe et al., 2009), there is no research that opens the black box of functionalities to understand their di<sup>f</sup>erences. The literature lags in providing a deeper understanding of how functionalities can di<sup>f</sup>er, what the characteristics are of the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of functionalities and how they can have di<sup>f</sup>erent impacts (Burton-Jones, 2014). This study extends prior research with a <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained conceptualization of software functionality which expands our understanding of the characteristics of informating and automating functionalities. This <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained conceptualization helps us understand that it is not easy to separate the informating and automating functionalities as they move on a continuum. An automating functionality can become an informating functionality if it seamlessly integrates into the user’s work process, is ready-to-hand and provides a higher level of user control. Moreover, we found that a functionality does not become an informating func tionality just because the system developer designates it to be one. Instead, it is the user’s perspective that helps this functionality to move to the informating side of the continuum. Another resulting contribution is that informating functionalities can help us uncover taken-for-granted work processes and hidden assumptions that may be detrimental to the organization. Awareness of these implications of such <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained conceptualizations of software functionalities could help the research community better formulate future research studies.

This study also extends the literature on agile development practices. Prior agile development literature has focused on important issues, such as team dynamics, trust, self-organization, communication, test-driven development (Boehm, 2002; Nawrocki & Wojciechowski, 2001; Nerur et al., 2005; Williams et al., 2000) and adoption (Erdogmus et al., 2005; Mangalaraj et al., 2009) There is no research that has studied whether agile practices have any e<sup>f</sup>ect on automating and informating functionalities. We learned that the relation between informating functionalities and agile practices is actually a two-way relation which suggests that it is not just that agile practices help develop informating functionalities, but also that engaging the users in the process of developing informating functionalities, in turn, helps agile practices. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd this mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial relation between agile practices and developing functionalities when the users’ perspective was that the system consisted entirely of automating functionalities. A subtle insight that emerged from this action research was that the users use the agile development characteristics to tweak the boundaries and interactions between automating and informating functionalities until the system <sup>fi</sup>ts their socio-technical setting. Uncovering these subtle di<sup>f</sup>erences and the emergent insights in the relation between agile practices and informating and automating functionalities would not have been possible if this study had conceptualized the software functionalities as a monolith.

For practice, this study highlights the characteristics that di<sup>f</sup>erentiate informating and automating functionalities. As practitioners invest heavily in developing information systems to improve organizational processes such that they can achieve radical transformations, they would need to know how to develop speci<sup>fi</sup>c capabilities in the software (Chuang & Lin, 2017; Cram & Newell, 2016). This study gives practitioners speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines to enable them to develop informating or automating functionalities in a deliberate rather than accidental manner. Thus, it is likely that practitioners may be able to gain signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant user acceptance by better matching software functionalities with user needs. With informating functionalities, they may have the capability to uncover hidden assumptions and taken-for-granted work processes which may cause resistance from people in positions of power. Software managers may need to be prepared to encounter and manage such resistance in organizations that plan to develop software with transformative capabilities (Chen,

Mocker, Preston, & Teubner, 2010; Marabelli & Galliers, 2017). Finally, another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding for practitioners is that there may be a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial relation between developing informating systems and agile development practices. As agile development practices create a socialized environment, it may promote learning and knowledge sharing causing the adoption of an informating perspective to development (Bunderson & Reagans, 2011). This, in turn, may encourage team members to become active contributors to the agile process. Managers may want to garner user support and involvement during the agile development process by engendering in the users an informating perspective. This has the potential to help agile practices such as user-centred development, constant interaction, and immediate feedback. When involved and invested in the development process, we found that users use the agile development characteristics to tweak the boundaries and interactions between automating and informating functionalities to develop a system that best meets their socio-technical needs.

We need to make note of some limitations of this study. First, the lessons learned in this study are based on the development of single application in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of organization (university). Due to this reason, these lessons should be considered as research understandings that need to be studied and extended in other contexts. Future research could attempt to conduct similar studies in other organizational contexts with di<sup>f</sup>erent types of systems to determine if these lessons and <sup>fi</sup>ndings are strengthened or weakened. Second, in an action research setting, it is possible that the action researcher may become too deeply immersed in the problem-solving activity and may lose sight of other important phenomena that could a<sup>f</sup>ect his research understanding. For this reason, future research could investigate the informating and automating characteristics of applications and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of agile practices on informating using other research methodologies and research lenses.
