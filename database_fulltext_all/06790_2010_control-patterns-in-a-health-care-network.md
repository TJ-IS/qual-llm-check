# Control patterns in a health-care network

Vera Kartseva<sup>1</sup>, Joris Hulstijn<sup>2</sup>, Jaap Gordijn<sup>3</sup> and Yao-Hua Tan<sup>4,5</sup>

## Abstract

To keep a network of enterprises sustainable, inter-organizational control measures are needed to detect or prevent opportunistic behaviour of network participants. We present a requirements engineering method for understanding control problems and designing solutions, based on an economic value perspective. The methodology employs a library of so-called control patterns, inspired by design patterns in software engineering. A control pattern is a generic solution for a common control problem. The usefulness and adequacy of the control patterns is demonstrated by a case study of the governance and control mechanisms of the Dutch public health insurance network for exceptional medical expenses (AWBZ). European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 320–343. doi:10.1057/ejis.2010.13; published online 23 March 2010

Keywords: governance and control; e-health; community-based services

## Introduction

Developing information systems for the health-care sector is notoriously difficult (see, for example, Currie & Guah, 2007). The development involves many stakeholders, like central and local government agencies, health-care providers, budget administrators and patients. Some of these stakeholders will have diverging interests. Many problems with the development of inter-organizational information systems arise from misunderstanding the governance and control structures of the collaboration in a network organization. It is the purpose of early requirements engineering to offer techniques that can reduce such misunderstanding (Mylopoulos et al., 1997; Wieringa et al., 2005). One way of doing that is by making detailed models of the network, of the projected collaboration and of the way it will be regulated. It is particularly crucial to get an understanding of the business opportunities that motivate collaboration in a health-care network. Observe that the health-care sector is highly regulated. The regulations that govern the health-care system are subject to frequent changes. Because these changes are the outcome of a political process, the way they will develop can remain unclear. When health-care regulations change, business opportunities for collaboration in the healthcare network may well change too. For these reasons, it is important to develop a generic method for analysing and developing governance and control regulations, and for discovering corresponding business opportunities Important elements of such a method are (i) a representation language and graphical modelling tool for the analysis and redesign of control procedures, (ii) a set of general guidelines to assist in analysing and redesigning control procedures, based on accounting principles, and (iii) a library of generic control mechanisms, that have been validated and can serve as ‘best practices’.

Regarding (i), the e3-control methodology already provides a representation language and graphical modelling tool (Kartseva et al., 2005a).

The e3-control methodology is based on the e3-value ontology, which analyses network organizations in terms of the transfer of economic value (Gordijn & Akkermans, 2003). The resulting model is called a value network or a value model. In this paper we extend this work with guidelines (ii) and best practices (iii). These are presented as a set of control patterns. A control pattern is a description of a generic and re-usable control mechanism that solves a control problem, to be selected based on the context of application. A control problem is an identifiable risk for opportunistic action by one of the other network participants. Control patterns are inspired by the design patterns approach, which was proposed in architecture (Alexander, 1979) and is successful in software engineering (Gamma et al., 1995). Patterns have also been applied in the business domain, to administrative processes (Motschnig-Pitrik et al., 2002), organizational structures (Coplien & Harrison, 2004) and business process re-engineering (Beedle, 1997).

In this paper we present the results of an extensive case study to validate the usefulness and adequacy of the control patterns. Although an approach based on economic value has been shown to work well in commercial settings, one could question its suitability for the public sector. We are therefore especially interested in the applicability of e3-value and the control patterns in a highly regulated sector, which involves public–private partnerships. The case study investigates the value network for the Dutch public health insurance system AWBZ (Exceptional Medical Expenses Act). Governance and control aspects of the AWBZ are interesting, because it is funded by taxes, and therefore lacks direct feedback on the quality of services. The system is undergoing changes, one of which is the introduction of a personal budget. This facilitates the development of a kind of market for care providers. Such a market requires that patients know enough about the available services and providers to make an informed choice. Currently, however, patients perceive the health care offer as fragmented, and not fitted to their needs (Meerveld et al., 2004). Little information is available about the services offered by different care providers, and about the quality of those services. The research is carried out as part of the FrUX project (Dro¨es et al., 2005). The project develops a dynamic interactive social chart for dementia care (DEM-DISC): an interactive website that will provide an overview of the health-care services available in a region, and provide personalized advice about possible combinations of health-care services, so called service bundles (Baida, 2006). The Social Chart is particularly designed for the relatives and informal caregivers of patients with Alzheimer’s disease or other forms of dementia. The aim is to bridge the information gap alluded to above.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we present the definitions of e3-value. The control patterns and the underlying control theory are presented in the subsequent section. In the section after that, we apply the control patterns to a case study of a health-care provision network, reverse engineering how the network may have developed. Further details about the research method are also given in the section. The paper ends with discussion and conclusions.

## Design of controls

A sustainable network organization needs mechanisms to govern and control the interaction among network participants. In most cases, the interaction is encoded in contractual arrangements, and implemented through procedures and regulations. However, regulations can be violated. From this perspective, a network organization model can be either ideal, meaning that no (known and relevant) possibilities for errors, opportunistic behaviour or fraud exist, or sub-ideal, meaning that situations or scenarios do exist in which errors, opportunistic behaviour or fraud occurs. Sub-ideal situations or scenarios indicate the presence of a control problem, with risks for governing the network organization. The term ‘ideal’ is inspired by deontic logic, where it is used to describe situations in which a norm is not violated (Meyer & Wieringa, 1994).

## Conceptual models to design control

To design controls we employ a set of conceptual models. The models are meant to identify and represent the essential characteristics that collectively describe a network organization. We argue in favour of dealing with the economical, social and procedural aspects of controls in unison. The concept of ‘control’ is defined differently in many disciplines. In the accounting literature, design of controls usually boils down to securing and auditing information systems (see, for example, Starreveld et al., 1994; Romney and Steinbart, 2003), which supposes an understanding of business processes (Malone & Crowston, 1994). Accounting controls are typically analysed from an operational or procedural perspective, with process models and flow charts. In other fields, such as transaction costs economics (Williamson, 1979) and agency theory (Eisenhardt, 1989; Shapiro, 2005), controls are viewed as contracts, for example financial incentives. In addition, there are various social control mechanisms to create trust (Gambetta, 1988; Mayer et al., 1995). At this stage, we focus on the economic perspective and propose a value-based approach. Thus, we emphasize the need to analyse network organizations from an economic perspective before designing procedural controls. On the basis of a series of case studies (Kartseva et al., 2005a, b, 2006a, b; Kartseva, 2008), a value-based approach of control design can be motivated as follows. First, in a network, valuable objects (goods, services, money) are transferred between enterprises. Since the correct transfer of these objects is the foundation for a sustainable operation of the network, it is important to understand the transfers between enterprises. Second, networks consist of financially independent units, which are interested in their own profitability.

Controls can also be seen as services with their own price tag; they require financial resources and therefore have effects on the sustainability of an enterprise. Finally, implementing controls can lead to new business opportunities, as demonstrated in this paper. This may change the existing network.

Ideal value models There are several methodologies that address the design of business models for network organizations, for example Business Model Ontology (Osterwalder, 2004), value webs (Tapscott et al., 2000) and e3-value (Gordijn & Akkermans, 2003). See Pateli and Giaglis (2004) for an overview. Out of these options, e3-value is the only one with a formal semantics and a specific focus on value transfers between enterprises. The method provides a formal ontology, that is a mathematically precise description of the shared concepts in a domain. The use of the method is supported by graphical modelling tools.

An e3-value model provides a conceptual model of the value transfers in a business network, encoded in the e3-value ontology (Gordijn & Akkermans, 2003). We apply the e3-value ontology for the description of socalled ideal models, which express organizations that behave in compliance to procedures and regulations. Sub-ideal models, on the other hand, are expressed using e3-control, a modification of the e3-value ontology, used to describe opportunistic behaviour of actors (Kartseva et al., 2005a). The differences are explained in the ‘Sub-ideal value models’ section below. The approach initially only deals with the ideal situation as otherwise the resulting models soon become rather complex, and disturb executive decision making. In our experience, it is already difficult for analysts to design a network under such ‘idealized’ conditions. The complexity of negotiating controls is added later.

The e3-value constructs have a graphical notation. Figure 1a shows an example of a buyer who obtains goods from a seller and offers a payment in return. According to the law, the seller is obliged to pay value-added tax (VAT). This can be conceptualized by the following e3-value constructs (in bold). Actors, such as the buyer, seller and the tax office are economically independent entities. In some cases, a group of actors is treated as a unit (e.g., group of patients). In that case we speak of a market segment, depicted as a ‘stack’ of actors. Actors transfer value objects (payment, goods, VAT) by means of value transfers. For each value object, some actor should be willing to pay, which is shown by a value interface. A value interface models the principle of economic reciprocity: actors are only willing to transfer a value object, in return for some other value object. So only if you pay, can you obtain the goods and vice versa. A value interface consists of value ports, to represent that value objects are offered to and requested from the actor’s environment. Actors may have a consumer need, which, following a path of dependencies, will result in the transfer of value objects. Transfers are either dependent on other transfers, or lead to a boundary element. Dependency paths can be connected by a choice fork (triangle) or parallel execution (bar). The e3-value methodology also allows the assignment of monetary values to value transfers. The expected profitability of a business idea can then be evaluated.

An e3-value model can be checked for internal validity (Gordijn, 2002). Checking the internal validity of an e3-value model involves checking mathematical constraints. It is beyond the scope of this paper to explain all validity requirements, so we give the two most relevant practical requirements here.

R1. A value interface should have at least one ingoing and at least one outgoing value port. This corresponds to the notion of economic reciprocity: ‘one good turn deserves another’. Note that in a network, the incoming and outgoing values do not need to come from the same actor, but they do need to be connected by a single value interface to indicate the conditional nature of the exchange: only when the incoming value is guaranteed, will the outgoing value be transferred.

R2. A dependency path may not contain any cycles, so formally it is an acyclic directed graph. A dependency path is used for counting the total number of value transfers, as well as the total amount of value being transferred, to satisfy a particular consumer need. Cycles are not allowed, because they would be meaningless: a cycle would imply that a seller would (indirectly) buy its own products.

A free downloadable tool allows analysts to quickly draw diagrams and to perform validity checks, among other things (http://www.e3value.com). The tool is also capable of generating so-called net value flow sheets for each actor involved. These are a kind of spreadsheets listing incoming and outgoing value flows, to be used as a first indication of the economic feasibility of the network. The tool can sum the net value flows of actors in a market segment. It can also concatenate a series of e3-value models, each model representing a particular timeframe, and sum up all the net value flows over a certain period by applying the discounted net present value technique.

Sub-ideal value models In e3-value, it is assumed that actors behave in an ideal way, meaning that all value transfers occur according to norms. But in reality, actors may commit fraud or make unintentional errors, for example actors will not be paid, or will not obtain the right goods. Often, but not always, this implies that actors do not respect the principle of economic reciprocity (R1), or that dependency paths contain loops (R2). All invalid e3-value models indicate a sub-ideal situation, but not all sub-ideal situations correspond to an invalid model. After all, a control problem may be hidden on the process level. The specific value transfer that is compromised by a sub-ideal situation is graphically represented by a dashed arrow. By analogy, such a transfer is called a sub-ideal value transfer. For example, Figure 1b shows a sub-ideal value transfer in which the seller does not pay VAT tax. Still, dashed arrows are nothing more than a notational convention. Usually they are used to denote the absent or insufficient incoming value transfer that leads to a value leak: a situation in which there is insufficient net value for an actor to compensate the outgoing value.

Process models Both ideal and sub-ideal value models consider the transfer of value objects, like money, a good or a service. This is essentially the transfer of ownership rights (Kartseva et al., 2006a). But the implementation of such a transfer requires operational activities to be performed, by multiple actors. Which activities are to be performed by which actors can only be expressed using a process model. Moreover, value models abstract away from the temporal order in which objects are being transferred: they only represent that objects are being transferred, but not in which order. In accounting control theory, the order in which activities take place forms a crucial part of many control mechanisms (Starreveld et al., 1994; Romney & Steinbart, 2003).

Suppose in Figure 1 that the seller will only transfer the goods after having verified that the buyer has transferred the money. This will put the buyer at risk: there is a possible sub-ideal scenario, in which the buyer transfers the money, but the seller does not transfer the goods. Likewise, when the goods are transferred before the money is, this will put the seller at risk. These risks can be solved by trust, or by introducing controls such as a down payment, or a trusted third party acting as Escrow (Hu et al., 2004). In the absence of such controls, a sub-ideal situation remains. Value models usually abstract over procedural matters, unless they become relevant for the sustainability of the business model. Only when the analyst has identified a sub-ideal scenario as relevant, the compromised value transfer is labelled by a dashed arrow.

Therefore in addition to value models, we need process models to capture who is doing what activity, at what time, in response to what events (Starreveld et al., 1994). We represent the process aspects of control problems and their solutions by Unified Modelling Language (UML)-activity diagrams (Rumbaugh et al., 1999). We define ideal and sub-ideal process models as those that do or do not prevent value leaks, respectively. Similarly, ideal or sub-ideal activities are defined as those that implement an ideal or sub-ideal value transfer, respectively.

Information models Evidence documents form an important part of the administrative control mechanisms studied in this paper. Think for example of receipts or tickets. Nowadays, documents are often certified files in a distributed information system, of which the paper document is only a trace. For an analysis of the structure and content of documents, UML-class diagrams are a suitable representation format. Other issues are related to the management of information. Which party should store the documents? How are confidentiality, integrity and availability of the data preserved? For such issues, we refer to textbooks like Romney and Steinbart (2003). In this paper, we focus only on the procedural role of evidence documents in control mechanisms, and do not discuss information models and information security further.

## Design process

Kartseva et al. (2005a) suggest that designing controls should include at least the following subsequent three steps. These steps are a part of the e3-control method.

1. Identify an ideal configuration of exchanges of value objects in a value network (see section ‘Ideal value models’).

2. Identify a possible sub-ideal situation (threat or vulnerability), with opportunities for unintentional errors, opportunistic behaviour or fraud. Such a subideal situation indicates the presence of a control problem.

3. For each identified sub-ideal situation, construct a solution that mitigates the control problem.

The result of step 3 is a repaired configuration of value exchanges, expressed by a value model and a corresponding process model, which no longer allow the sub-ideal situation. However, these repaired models may not be ideal in all other respects. The newly introduced controls may, for example, trigger new value transfers, which also need to be controlled. Therefore, steps 1–3 should be repeated for as many sub-ideal situations (threats or vulnerabilities) as are deemed relevant for the sustainability of the business model. Although the method is developed for the design of controls, the process can also be used for analysis purposes. In that case, the analyst can re-design control mechanisms using the three steps above, and compare the suggested results with the existing situation.

## Control patterns

The e3-control design process in the ‘Design of controls’ section is fairly general. To our experience, it requires quite a lot of specialized knowledge and expertise about control problems and control mechanisms. To increase the usability of the process, it is therefore important to bring in commonly accepted knowledge about control problems and solutions. We do so by introducing control patterns. The control patterns form the main contribution of this paper.

## Pattern definition

Traditionally, a design pattern has four essential elements: pattern name, problem, solution and consequences (Gamma et al., 1995). We separated the description of the context in which a pattern is to be applied, from the problem, which motivates the selection of a pattern (Coplien & Harrison, 2004). The context describes the business network with the actors, their relationships, like trust, and the activities to be controlled. The problem specifies a risk to be detected or prevented by the solution of the pattern. The solution describes the value network and the corresponding process model after implementing the control mechanism encoded in the pattern.

Definition (control pattern): A control pattern is a description of a generic and re-usable control mechanism for a recurring control problem, selected partly on the basis of the context. A control pattern consists of the following elements:

name: a descriptive name, to select patterns from a pattern library.

context: a description of the business network to be controlled, modelled from an ideal perspective, meaning that no one behaves opportunistically The context is represented by a value model, and if needed to understand the context, also by a process model.

problem: a statement of risks for opportunistic behaviour. A control problem exists if there is some deviation of the prescribed transfers of economic value. So we model the problem by a sub-ideal value model, using sub-ideal value transfers. Again, if needed, we also use a process model.

solution: description of a control mechanism, to detect, prevent or correct the control problem. The solution is described by both process models and value models.

## Patterns elicitation

Pattern development usually consists of the identification, collection and codification of accepted knowledge and expertise (Seruca & Loucopoulos, 2003). Identification and collection of the control patterns was done based on literature reviews and a series of case studies. The control patterns proposed are based on a combination of agency theory, accounting control theory and ideas about trust. Below we discuss each of these fields.

Agency theory A well-known theory on controls is agency theory, also called principal-agent theory. We refer to Eisenhardt (1989) or Shapiro (2005) for a survey. Agency theory studies the relationship between two parties: the principal, who delegates some activity, and the agent, to whom the activity is delegated. The theory argues that if (1) the principal and the agent try to maximize utility with bounded rationality, and (2) there is information asymmetry in favour of the agent, the agent may behave opportunistically. Agency theory distinguishes two types of opportunistic behaviour. The first type is caused by hidden information: the principal cannot be sure that the agent accurately presents his ability to do the work. For example, a producer (agent) generally has better information about the product he is producing, than someone who wants to buy the product (principal). The generally accepted control mechanism against hidden information is partner screening (Keil, 2005), in which the principal must collect information about the reliability of the agent, before agreeing on a transaction. The second type is caused by hidden action: the principal cannot be sure whether the agent did his work according to the contract or not. For example, the producer may use low-quality components to produce a product. As a result, the quality of the product could be lower than agreed in the contract. Accepted control mechanisms against hidden action are monitoring the agent, and the creation of incentives to motivate the agent not to behave opportunistically (Eisenhardt, 1989). In general, the agency theory assumes that implementation of monitoring mechanisms is costly and, therefore, focuses on design of incentives, mostly by taking a gametheoretical approach.

Accounting control theory Accounting control theory describes mechanisms on how to mitigate risks posed by control problems inside an organization (Starreveld et al., 1994; Romney & Steinbart, 2003). Accounting control theory views an organization as a set of operational activities, called events. Furthermore, it considers that each event can be the source of certain control problem, for example the risk that a shipment will not arrive on time. Consequently, a control is defined as a set of policies and procedures introduced to mitigate the risk (Romney & Steinbart, 2003). In traditional accounting systems, every event that takes place in an organization is required to be verified with respect to its correctness, compliance and legitimacy. Thus, a control is understood as an activity performed to minimize or eliminate risks related to business events. In general, one can distinguish preventative, detective and corrective controls. Because controls involve the execution of activities, they can be delegated to actors who provide them as a commercial service.

It is not sufficient to add a control activity; it should be added in the proper way. This is described in another component of our approach, which we distilled from accounting control theory: control principles. An example of a well-known control principle is segregation of duties (Starreveld et al., 1994). It requires among other things that a control activity and the activity it controls are executed by different actors, who should be socially detached. Another example is the requirement to perform a control activity, such as verification, immediately after the activity it controls. A further requirement has to do with the transfer of evidence. Evidence obtained from witnessing some controlled activity, should be transferred directly to the party with a control interest in the evidence. This should reduce the opportunity of tampering with evidence.

Some work on applying accounting control theory to inter-organizational processes has been done by Chen (1992) and Bons et al.(1998) in the context of interorganizational trade procedures. In the control patterns we build upon this work by developing the value perspective as a way of representing trade scenarios, and by extending the scope of the considered control problems.

Trust A network organization can be interpreted as a number of value transactions between actors. When parties in a network do not have an existing business relationship, lack of trust is likely. Trust has been defined as: ‘The willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other party will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party’ (Mayer et al., 1995, p. 712). Without prior trust, the party who invests in a transaction, called the trustor, is uncertain whether the other party, the trustee, will perform its part of the deal or will defect and behave opportunistically. The uncertainty of the trustor about possible opportunistic behaviour also comes out in Gambetta’s definition: ‘Trust is the subjective probability by which an individual a expects that another individual b performs a given action on which its welfare depends’ (Gambetta, 1988).

However, trust does not have to depend on the trustor and trustee alone. Institutional control measures can be used to guarantee performance according to contract. An example of a preventative control mechanism for international trade is an Escrow service (Hu et al., 2004). In such a scenario, the money of the buyer is deposited with a trusted third party, called Escrow agent. Only when the Escrow agent confirms to the seller that the money has been deposited, the goods are shipped. This solves the risk of the seller (non-payment) and of the buyer (nondelivery). So in general, the purpose of an interorganizational control mechanism is to reduce the uncertainty of the trustor and provide enough guarantees for both parties to engage in a transaction.

## Vocabulary

The three components of the underlying theory, agency theory, control theory and trust, can be combined. On the basis of this mixed background, we identify a vocabulary of terms (in bold). Consider a transaction scenario, as shown in Figure 2. There are two agents, called primary actor and counter actor. From a value perspective, we can say that the primary actor transfers a value object, called primary object, to the counter actor, and the counter actor returns a value object called counter object. For those rare situations that do not involve a binary transaction, a counter object is defined as any value object, which a primary actor receives from the environment in return for providing the primary object. From a process perspective, one value transaction is modelled by two operational activities: the primary actor performs a primary activity, and the counter actor executes a counter activity, each resulting in the corresponding value transfers. Please note that this ‘primary-counter’ terminology is based on Bons et al.(1998), and inspired by contract law using the terms primary obligation and counter party.

Figure 2 shows a value model of such a transaction on the left, and the corresponding process model on the right. The order in which the primary activity and counter activity occurs is deliberately left unspecified. This is indicated by the UML notation for parallel execution (thick bar). This model is similar to the scenario found in agency theory, in which a principal delegates some activity to an agent. The control risks of the transaction are generally assessed from the point of view of the primary actor, who – by definition – does not trust the counter actor, and must therefore design control mechanisms against possible sub-ideal behaviour. Therefore, in such scenarios we could say that: primary actor ¼ principal ¼ trustor and counter actor ¼ agent ¼ trustee.

Procedural controls almost always involve a verification of some activity or its outcome (Bons et al., 1998). A verification activity compares the execution or outcome of an activity, against a norm or general rule, in the form of a document to-be-verified. Often, the execution or outcome of the activity is represented by some independent trustworthy evidence in the form of supporting documents, to support judgements about the legitimacy, quality or quantity of the activity, or its outcome. One way of obtaining evidence about the activity is by a witness activity (Bons et al., 1998). The result of a verification is usually a decision to perform some action or not, or else an evidence document stating the decision. When a party signs a contract, it thereby issues evidence of a commitment (Weigand and De Moor, 2003). The evidence of commitment contains a kind of promise of the party to execute some (primary or counter) activities in the future, and is therefore represented in the case study with an activity called promise. To model the processes behind the transfer of a value object, we use special activities called request and provide. When the value object is a fee, we use the activity pay to represent its transfer.

## Library of control patterns

Table 1 shows an overview of our library of control patterns. On the basis of literature and case studies, we identified potential patterns. These were modelled with e3-control and activity diagrams, and compared in a commonality–variability analysis. The resulting patterns are validated in various case studies (See ‘Discussion’ Section), one of which is presented here. Table 1 shows the name. problem and suggested solution of each pattern. The context variable is not shown, as the context is taken to be similar for all patterns. The patterns assume a context that can (roughly) be modelled as the abstract transaction scenario of Figure 2. When applying a pattern, roles such as primary actor or counter object are instantiated with entities from the actual context.

Please note that this version of the patterns differs from some previous publications. The pattern Execution Monitoring was previously called Post-Verification or Execution Verification. The pattern Partner Screening was called Commitment Confirmation. The pattern Certification was called Commitment Authorization. The pattern Verification, also called Evidence Collection, was removed due to redundancy. See Kartseva (2008) for a motivation of these alterations.

In the overview below, we illustrate the patterns by a simplified transaction scenario, in which a buyer (primary actor) has ordered some goods, and does not trust the seller (counter actor) to deliver. Therefore in this case the primary activity is payment; the counter activity is delivery of the goods.

The Execution Monitoring pattern requires the primary actor to verify execution of the counter activity, before executing the primary activity (Chen, 1992; Bons et al., 1998; Weigand and De Moor, 2003). It roughly corresponds to the monitoring practice of agency theory, hence the name. The associated control problem is related to hidden action: without monitoring, the primary actor is not certain whether the counter actor will execute the counter activity as agreed. In our example, the buyer is not certain about delivery. According to the pattern the buyer should only pay, after having inspected that the right goods were delivered. When both actors apply this pattern, we get into a conflict, and an intermediate solution like a down payment must be applied.

The Execution Confirmation pattern requires the counter actor to confirm that the primary activity was performed as agreed (Bons et al., 1998). The associated control problem is that, in case of a dispute, the primary actor will not have independent evidence to prove that the primary activity was executed as agreed. In our example, the buyer will require a quittance (a receipt) from the seller, as evidence of payment.

The Proper Contracting pattern requires the counter actor to confirm, that is provide documentary evidence of the transaction commitment (Chen, 1992; Bons et al., 1998; Weigand and De Moor, 2003). Normally, this is done by signing a contract. The associated control problem is that otherwise the counter actor may refuse to recognize that he made a commitment to the primary actor, and may therefore not execute the counter activity. In our example, the buyer will require a price quote or offer, which commits the seller to deliver at a certain price.

The Partner Screening pattern requires the primary actor to collect evidence about the past conduct of the counter actor, and verify whether the past record and reputation of the counter actor conform to standards of trustworthiness, before making any commitments (Keil, 2005). The control problem concerns the risk of making a commitment to an unreliable partner (hidden characteristics).

Certification is a special case of partner screening, in which verification of credentials is delegated to a trusted institution or regulatory body. Often the partner screening procedures are delegated to a specialized party. In our example, before making any commitments, the buyer can ask an agency like the Chamber of Commerce to check credentials of the seller.

The Authorization pattern is another variation of Partner Screening. It considers a situation in which a primary actor delegates his primary activity to a trusted party, but screens the counter actor himself. Consequently, the trusted actor is only allowed to make commitments to the counter actor when the primary actor authorizes them. Authorization often appears together with Certification. The primary actor certifies the counter actor; only if such certification exists, the transaction between the counter actor and the trusted actor is authorized. Such authorization is needed when the primary actor is at least partly responsible for (funding) the primary activity. This is often the case when the primary actor is a regulatory body. For example, the EU is responsible for the safety of products. Instead of controlling every product up front, the EU first introduces a certification procedure for producers, and then authorizes consumers to buy only from certified producers.

In addition, we present some organizational design patterns (Table 2). These patterns often occur in control contexts, but are strictly speaking not control patterns. Instead, they rather serve general managerial objectives. Compare, for example, Malone and Crowston (1994) and Coplien and Harrison (2004). In particular, we use a pattern to describe delegation of an activity to some external actor. For this pattern, we assume that the actor, to whom the activity is delegated, is trusted by the primary actor, so no additional control measures are required. Otherwise, the usual control patterns apply. The pattern describing decomposition of activities into separate parts is often used for efficiency reasons. It is sometimes also needed for control reasons, as for example when a payment is made in two instalments, one before and one after delivery (down payment).

## Execution Monitoring pattern with delegation

For the purpose of illustration, we describe in detail one of the control patterns that we use in the case study of this paper (see Figure 3). Details about the other patterns can be found in Kartseva (2008) or on the website (http://www.e3value.com/e3family/e3control/ patterns/).

Context: A primary actor and a counter actor agreed to execute a primary activity and a counter activity. As a result, they exchange a primary object and a counter object. Agreements about the execution and conditions of the counter activity are expressed in a so-called supporting document.

Problem: The counter actor executes the counter activity sub-ideally, so that it does not comply with the supporting document. In e3-control, this problem is modelled with a sub-ideal transfer of a counter object. In the process model, the problem is modelled with a sub-ideal counter activity.

Solution: The primary actor must verify if the counter activity complies with the agreements in the document to be verified. Only if compliance is established, the primary actor should execute the primary activity. In addition, the verification procedure should comply to certain control principles (Chen, 1992; Bons et al., 1998). Some control principles require the verification to be based on actually witnessing the counter activity by either the primary actor or a trusted party. The trusted party must be independent and socially detached from the counter actor. As a result, in the process model this solution is modelled as follows. The primary actor executes an activity Witness, which has as an input the outcome of the counter activity, and information about the counter activity’s outcome is represented with a to-be-verified statement. The two statements are taken as an input in the verify activity, which reconciles information in these two statements. The primary activity is executed only if the statements are consistent with each other (positive statement).

Note that the value perspective will only change if we consider delegation of some activity. In Figure 3 we show a situation in which a witnessing activity is executed by a trusted actor on behalf of the primary actor. In fact, this solution is a combination of the Execution Monitoring pattern with the Delegation pattern. As a result, the value model also includes a trusted actor, who delivers a value object ‘Witnessing Service’ to the primary actor and receives ‘Some Value’ in return. In a situation without delegation, the witness activity is executed by the primary actor, and the value model stays the same as the context.

## Validation in health-care provisioning

In this section, we show how the control patterns can be applied to the AWBZ case. In particular, we focus on the fact that the AWBZ case exemplifies a highly regulated value network that involves many public–private partnerships. The case study is presented in two parts. The first part tries to explain, by means of the control patterns, how the current governance and control mechanisms may have developed. In the second part, we study the control problems that arise from the introduction of a personal budget, and show how the Social Chart could provide a solution. We discuss three future scenarios for exploiting an information service like the Social Chart.

## Research method, data collection and application of the patterns

Requirements engineering techniques need to be validated. Therefore also the adequacy and usefulness of the e3-control methodology and the control patterns approach must be shown in practice. To do so, we have conducted a series of case studies in various industries (for an overview, see the ‘Discussion’ Section and Kartseva (2008)). For each of the case studies, we have drawn detailed models, to capture the crucial aspects of the case study. These models were then verified by domain experts. Here, we follow the general practice of requirements engineering (Loucopoulos & Karakostas, 1995, Wieringa et al., 2005): ‘The purpose of requirements validation is to check whether the requirements specification complies with stakeholders intentions. Input is the requirements specification, output is a requirements model that is in sync and consistent with the stakeholders’ expectations’ (Gordijn, 2002, p. 17). The previous case studies concentrated on validating the use of the e3-control method. In this paper we focus on validating the control patterns. We present the results of an extensive case study of the Dutch public health insurance scheme (AWBZ), with the purpose of validating the usefulness and adequacy of the control patterns. Although an analysis approach based on economic value works well in commercial settings, one could question its suitability for the public sector. We are therefore especially interested in aspects of the case related to regulation and public–private partnerships.

The case study was conducted according to the method advocated by Yin (2002). Generally, a case study provides a systematic way of looking at events, based on collecting data, analysing the data according to a model and reporting the results. Note that the use of e3-value models and process models to represent the essential elements of a case strengthens the systematic nature of the research. Applying such conceptual modelling techniques demands a certain rigour. The research

## Reverse engineering the AWBZ

AWBZ stands for Algemene Wet Bijzondere Ziektekosten (Exceptional Medical Expenses Act). In the Netherlands, the AWBZ scheme deals with long-term and chronic diseases, such as protracted illness, invalidity, learning disability, mental disorders and geriatric diseases. Because this kind of care is too expensive to insure privately, the system is arranged as public health care. A patient only pays a small part of the costs; the largest part of the costs is reimbursed to the care provider by a government fund, collected from taxes. In discussing these control problems, we first focus on the care provider; the problems related to the patient are dealt with subsequently.

Control Problem 1a. A care provider receives a budget, without being held accountable for the money. Control theory dictates that every activity should be verified with evidence of its execution (Starreveld et al., 1994; Bons et al., 1998). In the model, that means that the two dependency paths should be linked. The control problem is that the care provider may receive a budget (primary object) that stands in no relation to the total of care actually provided to individual patients (counter object). Therefore we select the Execution Monitoring pattern (Table 4).

Control Problem 1b. All the costs of the care provider are reimbursed by the government. There is no incentive for care providers to try and work more efficiently. Such an ‘open-ended’ system is one of the general reasons behind the increase in health-care spending. Currently, there is much interest in budgeting schemes to reduce this problem, for example by output budgeting. We interpret budget agreements between government and care provider as a mutual commitment, and select the Proper Contracting pattern (Table 5). Before setting a budget, the government needs commitment from the care provider that they can and will provide care for a certain specified budget. Increasingly, the budget is calculated by standardized care intensity packages (Diagnose-Behandeling Combinaties).

The combined result of applying these two patterns is shown in Figure 6 (value network) and Figure 7 (process model). The task of controlling the budgets of care providers, and verifying the evidence, is delegated to an independent local agency, called Administration Office. For its administration task, the Administration Office receives a yearly budget from the government. This budget is fixed; it does not depend on the amount of care delivered by the care providers. By contrast, the size of the budget for the care provider, labelled ‘1’ in the diagram, is proportional to the number of care services actually delivered, labelled n.

It is analogous to, for example, a buyer having no direct transaction with a transport company, when delivery is outsourced, although there is physical contact.

Control Problem 2a. Now we concentrate on the control problems related to the patient. Although every citizen is entitled to health care when needed, access should be restricted to patients whose care is (medically) necessary. Because this step is about restricting entitlements, a form of commitments, we frame this problem as an instance of Partner Screening. The government or some government agency must verify on the basis of evidence like medical tests or a diagnosis from a physician, to which kinds of exceptional care the patient is entitled to. This form of screening is called ‘needs assessment’.

Control Problem 2b. Initially, before March 2003, needs assessment was combined with the task of allocating actual care services. Both were carried out by Regional Indication Centres (RIO). However, in some cases the needs assessments turned out to be inadequate: patients from different regions were given different care services for the same diagnosis. Such differences are considered unfair.

These problems can be addressed by two applications of Partner Screening, applying the general accounting principle of standardization (Starreveld et al., 1994). In the AWBZ, care is currently categorized in standardized functions: domestic care, personal care, nursing, supportive assistance, activating assistance, treatment and institutional care. For each function, there are different classes, which specify the intensity of the treatment. Which care needs correspond to which functions and classes is laid down in the so-called Care Entitlement Regulation (Besluit Zorgaanspraken AWBZ). The Care Entitlement Regulation can be seen as a kind of Authorization policy. Recall that Authorization was presented as a variation of the Partner Screening pattern.

In addition to these screening activities, a patient is also required to pay a limited ‘personal contribution’. The motivation was to demonstrate to patients that care is not ‘for free’.

The resulting value transfers are shown in the top half of Figure 8. Now there are two verification steps (Table 6) delegated by the government to different agencies. In the first step, ‘evidence of care needs’ is compared with the ‘care entitlement regulation’. The result is a ‘right for functions’ granted to the patient. This assessment is performed by a national agency, the Central Indication Centre (Centrum Indicatiestelling Zorg or CIZ). In the second step, a local administration office translates the ‘right for functions’ into an actual ‘right for care’. Because each administration office has an overview of the available care providers in a region, this actor is in a position to advice patients on where to get the best care they need.

Control Problem 3: No single actor in the network is responsible for controlling the quality of care products. As a result, provisioning care of low quality could remain undetected. Administrative evidence is used for budget control of care providers, but not much evidence of the actual quality of care is used. However, a basic form of quality control does exist. The government is in a position to select care providers. Before being authorized to enter the network, the ability of care providers to provide the care functions for which they are known must be assessed. This accreditation is the result of applying the patterns Certification and Authorization, which are variations of Partner Screening (Table 7). The resulting value transfers are visualized in the bottom part of Figure 8. Accreditation is delegated to the Healthcare Insurance Board (College van Zorgverzekeringen, CVZ). Therefore in Figure 8, the administration office is only authorized to assign patients to care providers with accreditation from CVZ. ## Personal budget and the Social Chart

The exceptional health-care system described above has a number of problems. The right for health care, through needs assessment, is disconnected from the care that is actually available. For each care provider, the budget is limited. Care providers do not have an incentive to provide services above their budget. Moreover, this supply-driven system results in a fragmented and unbalanced care supply in some regions. Therefore, the government is moving towards a more demand-driven system. In particular, the government introduced the possibility for patients to buy their own care services, from a so-called personal budget (Persoonsgebonden Budget). The personal budget is allocated by the administration office on the basis of the ‘right for functions’. The personal budget can cover care from the following six functions: domestic care, personal care, nursing, supportive assistance, activating assistance and short-term institutional care. The budget does not cover medical treatment, permanent institutional care or pharmaceuticals. Furthermore, the rules for care providers are liberalized. Any institution or private person may now provide care for part of the personal budget. This liberalization has led to an enormous growth in the number of care providers, which creates more choice for patients, and in some cases allows them to regain control over their lives. We call the new care providers alternative care providers. The situation is shown in Figure 9.

From a control perspective, we can observe the quality control problem again. But because of the large number of alternative care providers, there is no way CVZ can accredit all of them. Therefore, alternative care providers are generally not required to have an accreditation from CVZ.

## Exploitation of the Social Chart

Figure 10 presents only one of many possible exploitation scenarios. A kind of Social Chart could be set up, for example, by the patients’ association, by commercial parties like an insurance company or information broker, or by the local or national government. There are some indicators that virtual communities of patients are indeed able to provide community-based quality control (Dannecker and Lechner, 2006). There are already successful virtual communities for patients with a chronic disease. For example, Leimeister and Krcmar (2005) discuss a forum for cancer patients. Regarding dementia care, we already find a relatively successful virtual community, hosted by the Dutch patients association Alzheimer Nederland. They collaborate with local government to improve dementia care (Meerveld et al., 2004). So a combination of the patients’ organization and local government seems a viable option for setting up and exploiting a kind of Social Chart.

## Case study findings

The objective of the case study is to validate the use of the control patterns. In particular we are interested in modelling a highly regulated setting, with public–private partnerships.

Control patterns have a ‘constructive’ element, but reconstruction can also be used for analytic purposes. In this case study, we applied the control patterns to a hypothetical health-care provision system based on solidarity. We have re-engineered the administrative control mechanisms in the AWBZ. We have shown that crucial aspects of the AWBZ controls can be motivated by the application of control patterns. In particular, forms of budget control are provided by the Execution Monitoring pattern. Budget control arrangements can be seen as a kind of transaction, with mutual commitments laid down in a contract (Proper Contracting). Evidence documents (needs assessment, evidence of ability to provide care) are generated by verification steps, as part of the Partner Screening pattern. Limited forms of quality control can also be established by Partner Screening, in particular by Certification and Authorization, when verification of credentials is delegated to a regulatory body. In addition, the delegation of tasks to separate agencies makes a large difference. So although Delegation is not a control pattern, it does play a role as an organizational pattern. However, providing information about care providers and care supply in order for patients to make a good selection, the first function of the Social Chart, could not be established as the result of any control pattern.

In addition, the analysis demonstrates that there is a quality ‘control gap’ for alternative care providers. We have indicated a particular solution to fill this control gap: an interactive Social Chart, which could provide information about care supply and care providers in region, and possibly provide community-based quality control. The use of quality feedback to make a decision can be interpreted as application of the Partner Screening pattern.

In re-engineering the AWBZ scheme we have highlighted some aspects, ignoring others. In particular, we have had little to say about the information aspects of the administrative procedures. Such aspects may be relevant, but they are not brought out by the control patterns. For application in this case study, we could say that the control patterns are possibly too much focused on processes and administrative control, and too little on the provision of information and evidence.

The use of a value-based modelling technique in a highly regulated setting raises some issues for discussion. Similar issues are discussed in Kartseva et al. (2006c).

Indirect reciprocities. In the private sector, in which relations are often regulated by contracts, we mostly see direct reciprocities: a customer obtains a good or service and pays for it accordingly. But in this case, the economic reciprocal relation is at most indirect. For example, the indication centre receives a reward for its task, but it does not receive proportionally more funding, when it issues more rights for AWBZ. It turns out that in all cases of indirect reciprocity, the dependency path between a consumer need and the source of funding in the e3-value model is broken. Thus, the value transfers ‘budget’ and ‘right for functions’ are not connected by a dependency path. The same situation holds for the other governmental agencies. In the administrative care office, the value transfers ‘budget’ and ‘right for care’ are not connected with the dependency path; in CVZ, the value transfers ‘budget’ and ‘accreditation’ are also not connected.

Solidarity. In the AWBZ, the provision of care is largely paid for by the tax payer. This system implements a solidarity principle, when a majority of people pay for a minority. In a way, citizens pay for the guarantee that they will have access to health care of a certain quality, in case they would need it. In the value model, we use an OR-fork and an AND-fork at the actor citizen/patient to model that everyone pays taxes, while not everyone uses the care services. Solidarity is not particular to highly regulated environments. We find it whenever a group is needed in order to provide a service to an individual. However, solidarity schemes are often managed by the government, with corresponding regulations. A solidarity system also requires modelling indirect reciprocities. Without the solidarity system, there is direct reciprocity between care provider and patient: the care provider performs a medical treatment for the patient and gets paid in return. In the solidarity system in Figure 10, the reciprocity between the money (‘tax’) and the treatment (‘care’) is indirect: they are modelled in different value interfaces and are even transferred between different actors.

Regulatory rights as value objects. Public–private partnerships are heavily regulated. Regulation can take the form of a system of legal rights, to restrict access to a service. Examples in the case study are ‘right for functions’ and ‘right for care’. Legal rights can be seen as a value object because they guarantee access to a product or service; such rights are of real economic value to actors. For example, the ‘right for functions’ is of value to patients, because it allows them to get access to the AWBZ services.

Evidence documents. This case shows the need for various evidence documents: 'evidence of care need'. 'evidence of right for functions’ and ‘evidence of care’. Although collection and interpretation of evidence is usually modelled as part of the regular business process, here evidence documents are seen as value objects. Evidence documents are required to obtain other objects (rights, AWBZ care) and are thus also of economic value for the actors. Maintaining accountability and legitimacy of decisions is a core goal of government agencies. Therefore, for agencies like the indication centre or administration office evidence is of value. For example, the indication centre needs ‘evidence of delivered care’ to be able to prove that the budget is being spent for a just purpose. It also needs ‘evidence of rights for functions’ to be able to prove that the AWBZ care is given to those who have a proper indication.

Community-based reciprocity. A community-based quality control, like a feedback or recommender system, only works when sufficient members actually contribute to it. Sharing and exchanging valuable information, like experiences about care providers, is also based on solidarity and membership of a community. Similar to solidarity, this can be modelled in e3-value by a value transfer between different instances of a so-called market segment (stack of actors), aggregated over a longer period of time. One patient benefits from the contributions of other patients.

Control services A highly regulated environment requires many control activities. Examples in the case study include needs assessment (indication centre), allocation of actual care services, the allocation of personal budgets (administrative care office), providing information about care providers and care provider assessment (Social Chart). Performing control activities can be seen as a commercial service, which can in principle be outsourced. This is clearly modelled: all controlling parties in the case study, whether they are government agencies or not, need to be funded or paid. Control services may even spawn off new business opportunities, as in the case of the Social Chart. In general, implementation of a control measure may alter a value network in the following ways: (1) by adding an actor (e.g. trusted third party to verify documents), (2) by adding a value transfer (e.g. fine; reward), (3) by adding a control activity, which takes effort that needs to be compensated by a value transfer (e.g. fee), and (4) by adding a scenario path link in an e3-value model, to signal the dependence of two value exchanges (e.g. fee only for verification service).

## Discussion

How does this paper relate to other research by ourselves and others? The paper is an extended and improved version of Kartseva et al. (2007) and is part of a series of publications about e3-value and e3-control (Gordijn & Akkermans, 2003; Kartseva et al., 2005a, b, 2006a, b, c, 2007; Kartseva, 2008). The particular contribution of this paper lies in the introduction and motivation of control patterns, and especially in the evaluation of the control patterns in a highly regulated environment, namely health-care provisioning. Other papers in the series focus on the economic value perspective (Gordijn & Akkermans, 2003), on the development of the e3-control method (Kartseva et al., 2005a, 2006a) and on its evaluation in other sectors, in particular renewable energy (Kartseva et al., 2005a), internet radio (Kartseva et al., 2005b), international trade procedures (Kartseva et al., 2006a) and the redesign of customs regulations (Kartseva et al., 2006b). An overview of these case studies is given in Kartseva (2008).

Regarding methodology, the research reported here is set in a tradition of requirements engineering (Loucopoulos and Karakostas, 1995; Wieringa et al., 2005). It is one of the tasks of requirements engineering to provide methods to understand the context of a particular information systems development project, including the stakeholders and their interests. These methods often take the shape of formal or semi-formal models, so that properties of the design can be verified (Mylopoulos et al., 1997). A particularly popular method focuses on models of the dependency relations between stakeholders (Yu, 1997; Fuxman et $a l . ,$ 2004). It can be shown that an e3-value model can be translated into such a dependency model (Gordijn et al., 2006). However, dependency models often fail to take the underlying business proposition into account. Those models that do take a business perspective, such as Tapscott et al. (2000) and Osterwalder (2004), often lack a formal ontology.

Concerning work about governance control, there is a lot of expertise in the fields of accounting information systems (Romney & Steinbart, 2003) and management accounting (Eisenhardt, 1989; Shapiro, 2005). The costs of applying controls to a network are investigated by transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1979). Nowadays, decisions about the application of a particular control mechanism are often taken in the form of a risk assessment (COSO, 2004). We expect that the economic value perspective taken in this paper is particularly suitable to model the trade-offs between accepting a risk and applying a set of control measures. This expectation needs further investigation. Note that nowadays, most governance and control measures are implemented using information systems. The information systems community is therefore in an excellent position to carry out the required multi-disciplinary research.

We observed that there is relatively little research about governance and control in the health-care sector. Most research about health-care provisioning focuses on macroeconomic aspects and budget control (Drummond, 2005). There is a growing interest in the difficulties of developing information systems in the health-care sector (for example Currie & Guah, 2007), but this research often focuses on systems containing medical information, or on privacy of electronic patient records. The FruX project has demonstrated that patients and caregivers also lack information about the availability and affordability of health-care services (Dro¨es et al., 2005). Our case study shows that it is possible to provide a micro-level analysis of administrative controls in a particular health-care provisioning network. This might pave the way for further research concerning the relative advantages and disadvantages of particular health-care insurance schemes, for particular stakeholders, such as patients or informal caregivers. Such research could complement existing macro-level analyses.

What are the ‘key’ insights about the use of control patterns and requirements engineering techniques in a case study of governance and control mechanism in health care?

We showed that all of the control mechanisms actually used in the AWBZ scheme could be related to only a handful of control patterns, namely Execution Monitoring, Proper Contracting and Partner Screening. The only exception was the provisioning of information for patients in order to make a good selection of care providers, which could not be established by one of the existing patterns. One would expect that such information provisioning would be part of Partner Screening. Or rather, such information provision should be part of the preceding phase of Partner Identification, during which an actor decides with which other actors she is willing to do business. This suggests that the list of control patterns should be extended with a pattern for Partner Identification. Trust would also play a major role in such a control pattern.

We are glad that the idea of re-engineering the AWBZ scheme from first principles turned out to be possible at all, and produced models that were recognized to be valuable by domain experts. During the validation workshop, experts found the e3-control diagrams easy to understand. This was remarkable, since most have no training in formal modelling. Moreover, the diagrams triggered a lot of discussion, for example about the funding of a Social Chart. Participants said that they liked the diagrams, because they were about the real issue, not about some technical details. This reconfirms the idea of Gordijn and Akkermans (2003) that one of the advantages of value modelling is that it focuses on strategic issues and abstracts over technical details.

We are aware that there are limitations to the use of control patterns, and to the general research method.

The current set of control patterns focuses on procedural aspects and administrative controls. Much less attention is paid to information aspects. This is unfortunate for this case study, because information provision is one of the main functions of the Social Chart. Management information issues related to the Social Chart have not been studied. For example, not much is said about ways to continually ensure the quality of medical or legal information supplied by an online information service. Also the security of information provided by such information remains an open issue. For more on the relation between the value perspective, the process perspective and the information perspective, we refer to (Gordijn, 2002, Ch 9).

The case study research methodology that we have adopted also has some limitations. The case study was conducted on the basis of interviews with experts and publicly available information. The initial value models were drawn by the researchers, and subsequently verified by experts. The application of the control patterns as such has been done by the researchers. Like all modelling, this ‘reverse engineering’ exercise involves a certain amount of subjectivity. Proper evaluation of the usefulness of the control patterns would require a much larger empirical study among practitioners. One could think of an experiment in which a representative group of domain experts would be asked to solve a relevant control problem. One half of the group would be instructed to use e3-control and the control patterns, while the other half would use the methods they preferred. We would then need to score the quality of the obtained solutions by means of some objective measure and compare the results. However, apart from the fact that such an experiment is difficult to organize and raises many methodological issues, we believe that such a comparative evaluation is not necessary for the purpose of this paper, namely to introduce and motivate the use of control patterns. The main contribution of the control patterns is conceptual, so it suffices to demonstrate the usefulness and adequacy of the models at a conceptual level.

We have focused on administrative control mechanisms to prevent or detect control problems. They mainly concern the execution of business processes. A different kind of controls is based on generating incentives for the agents not to behave opportunistically. In particular, ideal behaviour can be rewarded and sub-ideal behaviour can be punished. Such incentives are also used in the AWBZ, for example in the application of budget controls. In our ongoing research, we investigate the patterns of applying such incentives for control purposes. Initial findings indicate that the economic value perspective of the e3-value and e3-control frameworks is particularly well suited to model this kind of incentives (Kartseva, 2008).

Another weakness of the current version of control patterns is that they focus on a single actor displaying sub-ideal behaviour. It does not cover risks resulting from collusion: various parties conspiring to abuse the existing agreements in a value network. Examples of collusion are not uncommon in health-care provisioning. Consider a care provider going along with the wish of a patient to prescribe addictive medication, without medical necessity. We expect that in principle such cases can be detected, because they violate the control principle of segregation of duties. Further research is needed to determine the suitability of e3-control for detecting collusion.

It is quite possible that we have overlooked a potentially useful pattern. Control patterns are meant to encode existing expertise and best practices. It is generally recognized that a patterns approach needs an active user community to be continuously improved (Gamma et al., 1995). More details about the control patterns can therefore be found on a website (http:// www.e3value.com/e3family/e3control/patterns), to facilitate wider adoption and feedback.

The results of the case study may also have an impact on practitioners working on the development of information systems in the health-care sector.

One of the ‘lessons learned’ is that setting up an information service is relatively straightforward, but the difficulty lies in implementing a network with a sustainable business model. In particular, the information service needs a believable ‘host’, who provides longterm credibility and expertise to maintain the quality of information. This suggests that governments should not (only) provide start-up subsidies, but also provide sustained financial support for those initiatives that align with government policy. For example, if the government wants to stimulate self-reliance of patients, it ought to support empowerment initiatives by patient organizations. Such initiatives are currently emerging at the local level.

One of the control problems highlighted by the case study is quality control. Quality control signals one of the weaknesses of a (partial) market for health-care services. In a proper market, consumers can ‘vote with their feet’. Patients, however, are dependent. Patients or relatives are generally not in a position to compare health-care providers and demand quality. Even with improved information and patient empowerment, supported by initiatives like the Social Chart, it remains a government task to guarantee basic health-care quality standards.

Finally, the case study shows that performing control activities on behalf of another actor can be seen as a service in itself, which can be delegated. This may in turn trigger new business opportunities, such as the development of a community information service like the Social Chart. What has started as a control problem may turn out to be an opportunity for reform.

## Conclusions

The health-care sector is subject to continuous revision. Regulations are added or withdrawn, and new policies are introduced. An example of such changes is the introduction of a marketplace for health-care services. Such a marketplace presupposes that patients are well informed about the available health-care services. In general, it is much harder to set up, develop and maintain an information system to provide such information, when the development context is subject to change. When regulations change, for example, the business opportunities for exploiting such an information system may well change too. Therefore, a generic method for analysing and developing governance and control mechanisms for network organizations is needed. We argue that control patterns provide a generic method for understanding governance and control mechanisms for network organizations. A control pattern is a description of a generic and re-usable control mechanism for a recurring control problem, to be selected based on the context of application Like design patterns, control patterns capture ‘best practices’ in a domain.

In this paper, we have presented a representation language for expressing control problems and their solutions, called e3-control. The representation language is largely based on the existing e3-value method, which models network organizations from an economic value perspective. Using e3-control an analyst can identify the control problems associated with a particular value model, or with the implementation of a value model in specific business processes.

On the basis of literature about internal control, agency theory and trust, and an extensive series of case studies, we have developed a library of generic control patterns. The control patterns provide guidelines for the application of existing control measures to mitigate the control problems. The control patterns are: Execution Monitoring, Execution Confirmation, Proper Contracting and Partner Screening. In addition we identified two auxiliary organizational patterns for Delegation and Decomposition.

Finally, we have evaluated the usefulness and adequacy of the control patterns on a non-trivial case study. We have reconstructed the development of the governance and control mechanisms of the Dutch AWBZ scheme for provision of exceptional care, by applying control patterns. The case study shows that crucial aspects of the administrative controls used in this scheme can indeed be motivated by the control patterns. In particular, evidence documents like a needs assessment are generated by the Execution Monitoring pattern. A limited form of quality control can be established by Partner Screening, or in particular a combination of Certification and Authorization. Budget control arrangements can be seen as the application of Proper Contracting, combined with Execution Monitoring, just like in business contexts. The control patterns have proved to be useful, in the sense that they have allowed us to indeed analyse the vulnerabilities of governance and control mechanisms in a highly regulative environment. The individual patterns have been adequate for such an analysis, in the sense that each of them captures some crucial aspects of the existing administrative controls, and may even suggest potential improvements. Also the set of control patterns has proved to be adequate: almost all control problems that we encountered in the case study could be mitigated by a control measure suggested by one of the patterns. There was one exception: the required provisioning of information for patients to select good quality care providers could not be established by one of the existing patterns. In a way, this reconfirms the general finding that quality control is difficult to organize in the health-care domain. It is encouraging to see that communities of patients increasingly step in to provide such quality information services.
