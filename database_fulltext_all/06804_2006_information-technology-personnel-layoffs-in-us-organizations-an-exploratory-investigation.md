# Information technology personnel layoffs in US organizations: An exploratory investigation

C. Ranganathan <sup>\*</sup>, Kedar Samant

Department of Information & Decision Sciences, Liautaud Graduate School of Business (MC 294), University of Illinois at Chicago, 601 S Morgan St., Chicago, IL 60607, USA

Received 14 March 2003; received in revised form 10 February 2005; accepted 18 June 2005 Available online 15 August 2005

## Abstract

The new century has been marked by an unprecedented number of layoffs of IT personnel. But despite the increasing industry concerns over IT worker downsizing, the knowledge base and scholarly literature on IT personnel layoffs has been sparse. This paper suggests some exploratory, descriptive insights into such downsizing efforts. Data on 569 layoff events in the US for years 2000 and 2001 were gathered and analyzed to help gain an understanding of the phenomenon. We examined the trends in IT personnel layoffs over time, across different industries, their nature and extent, the stated reasons given for downsizing and the financial environment of firms laying off IT workers. The findings and their implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

‘‘Disney to cut 1000 IT jobs’’ (Orlando Sentinel, 13 May 2005)

‘‘Nasdaq announces layoffs, restructures IT.’’ (Computerworld, Jul 2, 2001)

‘‘Layoffs crank up again, chip away at economic growth’’ (USA Today, 18 Sep 2003)

Such announcements have been and continue to be routine in the US business press. Facing competitive pressures, a slowed down economy and dwindling business performance, many firms downsized their personnel in an attempt to reduce costs and improve profitability. Though these efforts are prevalent across all industries, layoffs involving IT personnel are particularly interesting. Only a few years ago, IT employees enjoyed higher salaries, had multiple job opportunities and enjoyed attractive perquisites such as stock options. In fact, the demand for IT personnel also increased so rapidly that high turnover became a major concern. However, the year 2000 heralded a downturn in US labor force needs, especially of IT workers. Traditional brick-and-mortar firms as well as dot-com firms resorted to downsizing IT personnel in an effort to ensure their survival.

The statistics on IT personnel layoffs have shown increased downsizing [15,36]. According to a study by AEA (formerly the American Electronics Association), technology firms shed 437,000 jobs in US between March 2001 and June 2002. Moreover, technology jobs declined every month during that period, falling from 5.72 to 5.27 million [40]. Another study by the Information Technology Association of America estimated that IT personnel layoffs were about 2.6 million in 2001 [25]. An international outplacement firm, Challenger, Gray and Christmas Inc. estimated that the total number of IT-related job cuts were about 696,000 in 2001, though this number fell to 468,000 in 2002 [18,38]. In the dot-com sector alone, there were about 101,000 layoffs in 2001, which was two and half times that in 2000 [6].

While much has been written on IT personnel layoffs in the business press, academic literature has been sparse. Researchers have examined issues concerning turnover and retention of IT workers [10,21,37], their motivation and nurturing [12,13,16], their work attitudes and stress [4,26], human resource management issues [1,5,17], the value of their skills [8], job satisfaction and career orientation [29] and their compensation plans and productivity [34]. However, little effort has been made to explain IT personnel layoffs [24]. This is surprising given the increasing industry concern. It is important to understand IT worker layoffs as these decisions can affect and influence the attitudes of IT work force. Calls have been made for a better understanding of issues pertaining to IT personnel, their work context and the forces that affect the growth and decline of IT human capital [33].

This paper is an exploratory, descriptive study that attempted to understand IT worker layoffs. There were three distinct motives, to:

1. Document the IT personnel downsizing practices in the industry.

2. Consider and extend the multiple perspectives that have been used to understand organizational downsizing.

3. Examine IT related human resource practices, specifically documenting the trends and organizational experiences in order to provide some guidelines on IT human resource management.

## 2. Organizational downsizing

Budros [7] defined downsizing as ‘‘an organization’s conscious use of permanent personnel reductions in an attempt to improve its efficiency and/or effectiveness’’. McKinely et al. [28] viewed downsizing as ‘‘intended reductions of personnel’’. From these definitions, it is clear that layoffs or downsizing do not happen to a firm by chance. Layoffs reflect an intentional effort, involving reduction in the number of personnel in order to achieve greater of organizational efficiency or effectiveness.

A number of terms have been used to denote personnel reduction; they include firing, layoffs, jobcuts, retrenchment and work force reduction to indicate a sense of corporate anguish. Words such as reengineering, rightsizing and downsizing indicate business adjustments to improve business health. However, they all denote reduction of human resources. In our paper, we use the words downsizing and layoffs interchangeably.

Researchers have paid considerable attention to the reasons for organizational downsizing. Budros identified the following: (i) deregulation; (ii) economic troughs and downswings; (iii) competitive intensity; (iv) poor financial health; (v) greater levels of consolidation activities, such as mergers and acquisitions (M&A); (vi) use of labor-saving technologies; (vii) higher levels of employee compensation; (viii) large size of the firms; (ix) actions of major firms.

The last decade was characterized by deregulation in the US, especially in telecommunications and IT. And its end was characterized by economic recession and downturns. As the markets slowed down and resources became limited, there was increased competition. More firms were fighting for fewer resources. The financial health of several became poor, forcing them to adopt drastic measures and survival tactics: many of them resorted to downsizing.

The extent of M&A activities in the IT sector was also high [31,39]. Use of IT, such as CAD, CAM, work flow automation and CASE tools, allowed firms to operate with fewer personnel. Moreover, the higher compensation levels during the dot-com boom days [22] suggested that layoffs could provide significant cost savings. Further, larger IT firm size suggested overstaffing and inefficiency. Finally, since downsizing was prevalent even in Fortune 1000 companies, other firms copied them rather than searching for other ways to improve their business health.

Casico [11] suggested that there were several benefits, such as lower expense ratios, enhanced profitability, increased return-on-investments and high stock prices; however, they have not always been realized [30]. Layoffs may not result in significant benefits if the organization fails to address the fundamental shortcomings in the organizational activities, processes and strategies. Several suggestions and recommendations have been made for effectively carrying out the downsizing efforts [2,9,19,32].

## 3. Objectives of the study

Our goal was to explore the basic issues in IT personnel layoffs that could lead to a better understanding and pave the way for rigorous research. To this end, we gathered and analyzed data on IT personnel layoffs in the years 2000 and 2001. Specifically, we intended to answer the following questions:

(i) What was the extent of downsizing of IT personnel in US organizations? What kinds of firms and what industries downsized IT workers?

(ii) Were there any differences in the IT personnel layoffs across different industry segments?

(iii) What were the primary reasons behind the IT personnel layoffs? Were there any differences in the motives of firms removing higher and lower percentages of its IT workers?

(iv) What were the financial characteristics of firms laying off IT personnel? Could financial parameters help explain the layoff decisions?

## 4. Data assessment and key findings

## 4.1. Data collection

Our data collection strategy involved a search of public announcements made by firms on layoffs involving IT personnel. We used two sources for the data: the layoffs database complied by HRLIVE (www.hrlive.com), which compiles and provides weekly reports on the layoffs announced through different news media, as a starting point, followed by a search of the Lexis–Nexis academic universe database for announcements on IT personnel layoffs. We particularly focused on wired reports, such as PR Newswire and Business Wire, which cover key corporate events, such as layoffs.

These steps generated over 1600 announcements. We took a series of measures to verify and include only those announcements that were relevant for our purposes. First, we carefully scanned each announcement to check that it pertained to layoffs involving IT personnel. Second, since we were interested in assessing the financial parameters and other firmspecific characteristics, only firms whose financial information was available in the COMPUSTAT database were included. Third, government and state agencies and dot-com firms that were not publicly traded were excluded. Finally, layoff announcements that did not provide adequate information on the variables of interest were removed. These restrictions resulted in a sample of 569 layoff announcements from 417 firms for analysis.

## 4.5. Industry-wise break-up of IT layoff events

The North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) was used to identify the industries to which the sample firms belonged; the distribution of layoff events is presented in Table 4. The software industry, consisting of sub-sectors of packaged, application and system software production, accounted for about 28% of the total layoff events. The hardware industry, involved in manufacturing computer and related electronic products, accounted for about 21% of the layoffs in 2000 and 2001. The Internet-sector and IT consulting firms together accounted for about 30% of the events. IT userindustries, such as wholesale, real estate, finance and insurance and other services, together accounted for less than 12% of the entire IT personnel layoffs. Though business press has been vocal about the layoffs and outsourcing in user firms, it is the ITproducing firms that engaged in major layoffs. This finding is consistent with that of a study performed by the Information Technology Association of America.

We also examined whether there were any significant differences across industries in terms of the percentages of IT personnel laid off. An ANOVA was performed to test this. Industries with fewer than five firms were excluded from the analysis. The analysis yielded an F-value of 2.58, significant at 1% level, implying significant differences across industries in their personnel downsized. The Internet-related and publishing industries downsized most. This was evident both from the mean and medians of the laid off workforce shown in Table 5. As the large standard deviation values implied considerable variance in the data, median values were more reliable in assessing the extent of the layoffs. Both median and mean values suggested that the degree of layoffs across other industries (not Internet and publishing) were similar.

## 4.7. General linear model analysis

In order to assess our first three research questions, we used a general linear model (GLM) to explore the possible associations between extent of IT personnel layoffs and the downsizing reasons, firm size and industry segments. GLM is appropriate where the independent variables are both continuous and categorical and the dependant variables are continuous. In our research, the extent of IT layoffs and firm size were continuous but the industry and reasons were categorical variables. In our GLM formulation, the dependant variable was the extent of IT personnel layoffs in a firm, with industry and reasons as fixed effect factors and firm size (sales in 1999) as a covariate. The results of the GLM tests are shown in Table 8b.

The GLM model explained 28% of variance in the extent of IT personnel layoffs. GLM-test for betweenindustry effects proved significant at the 10% level, indicating some significant differences among the industry segments. Similarly, we found significant differences in the extent of layoffs among firm groups with different reasons for downsizing. The interaction term between industry and reason was also found to be significantly associated with the extent of the layoffs.

To determine the significance of differences between means, a Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc test was used for pair-wise comparisons between different groups. Computer and electronic product manufacturing group significantly differed from Internet-related (mean difference = 8.4, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and publishing groups (mean $\mathrm { d i f f e r e n c e } = 1 0 . 4 , ~ p < 0 . 0 1 )$ in the extent of IT personnel layoffs. No significant differences were found for pair-wise comparisons between layoffs in software, services, wholesale and other manufacturing industries and all the other segments. ## 4.8. Financial characteristics of firms downsizing IT personnel

Downsizing is often considered to be a relatively painless way to boost profit margins and provide better performance. It offers a simple way to reduce labor costs and boost short-term earnings. The financial environment of the firm plays a key role in the downsizing related decisions made by organizations. Several scholars have identified financial environment of the firm to be a major determinant of downsizing decisions [23,27].

McKinley et al. examined 210 layoff announcements by 119 firms for the years 1982–1989 and compared the financial performance of the firms engaged in downsizing efforts. They found that the financial metrics of the downsizing firms showed a decline every year until the year of downsizing, with temporary improvement in the year following downsizing. In line with such studies and to address our fourth research question, we examined the financial environments of the firms undertaking IT layoffs using three key dimensions of the financial environment of firms: (i) cost efficiency, (ii) productivity and (iii) growth. These parameters have been widely used in MIS evaluations (e.g., [35]) and in organizational downsizing studies. Table 9 shows the operational measures of the metrics used in assessing the financial environment.

Two metrics were used to measure the cost efficiency. The first was the ratio of selling, general and administrative expenses (SG&A) to sales (enhancing the firm’s operational efficiency by cutting overhead costs reflected in the SG&A expenses). The ratio represents the extent to which the firm has been cost-efficient in running its business. The second metric was the ratio of operational expenses (calculated by adding the cost of good sold and SG&A expenses) to sales.

We assessed firm productivity in two ways. First, we computed employee productivity (the extent to which the employees contributed to firm income) by finding the ratio of total sales to number of employees; this reflected the dollar contributions made by an employee to the sales. Second, asset productivity (contributions of assets to firm’s sales) was measured by the ratio of sales to the total assets of the firm. To measure firm growth, we computed the change in the firm’s sales for each year: this reflects the extent to which a firm had experienced a decline or increase in sales.

A number of factors in the industry environment of the firm could potentially impact its financial performance. Therefore, it was important to control and account for the industry-specific effects on the financial parameters. To control for industry effects, a common method employed in accounting and finance research is to form a control group of firms from the industry for each firm that is being examined. The financial metrics of the firms in the sample are then industry-adjusted by subtracting the mean or median of that metric from the firm’s control group. A common method of forming the control group is to include all those firms in the same industry as the firm’s control group (based on the SIC or NAICS code).

For each firm in our sample, we obtained the relevant financial data from the COMPUSTAT database; it was gathered for the year of the layoff and the three years preceding it. For each firm, we created a control group that included all the firms (except those in our sample) with the same six-digit NAICS code. Then, we computed all the financial metrics for the firm, as well as that of its corresponding industrial control group, for a period of 4 years. Subsequently, for each firm and each financial metric, the median value of the control group was subtracted from the firm’s financial metric to arrive at a median industry-adjusted value of the financial parameter: the median was used because the means were sometimes affected by extreme values in the dataset.

In analyzing the financial environment of firms that announced IT layoffs, we further screened and pruned our dataset. We searched the Lexis–Nexis database to see whether the firms that announced IT layoffs in the 2000 and 2001 time period had announced any IT worker layoffs in the three preceding years. Eighty-six firms that had previously announced downsizing initiatives were eliminated. Though this step reduced the size of our dataset, it helped us obtain a cleaner sample that would provide a better picture of the financial environment in downsizing IT personnel.

A non-parametric approach using Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test was used to determine whether the number of downsizing firms that bettered the industry metric significantly differed from those that underperformed.<sup>1</sup> This test was chosen because: (i) it is a distribution-free technique that does not require any underlying distributions in the data, (ii) the distribution of financial ratios tends to be non-normal and nonparametric techniques have been recommended to deal with financial ratios [3,14,20] and (iii) it deals with the signs and ranks of the values and not with their magnitude (thus not influenced by outlier data points).

In the year of layoffs (year = 0), firms had a mean (median) SG&A/Sales ratio that was about 40% (21%) higher than that of its relative industry control group. Similar interpretation could be made for the other figures in Table 10. The results of the Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test indicated that for all the years, firms that had a higher SG&A/Sales ratio than their industry control group outweighed the number of firms that had lower SG&A/Sales ratio with respect to their industries. For the Operating Expenses/Sales metric, this was also true: firms in our sample had higher overhead costs than their respective industries in the three preceding years and the year of layoff. It is important to note that though the mean (median) values of the SG&A/Sales and Operating Expenses/ Sales reduced over the 3 to 0 period, implying that the firms managed to bring down their overhead costs, these costs were still higher than those of the rest of the industry. Given the higher overhead costs in our sample firms as compared to their industries, layoffs became a good option for controlling and reducing costs.

We detected neither significant differences in the asset productivity for any of the years nor did we observe any trends in the movement of this metric. However, we found some interesting trends in the employee productivity across the years. In year = 3, the firms had a higher mean (median) sales/employee ratio than their industries. Three years prior to layoffs, the firms utilized their employee base to generate higher sales, as compared to the other firms in their industry. Moreover, the employee productivity continued to increase in the next 2 years. In comparison with the industry, these firms were able to generate higher sales for their employee base. This implied high levels of employee productivity and better utilization of the IT workers.

Our sample firms had a better sales growth than their industry across all the years. The Wilcoxon’s test results also indicated that the number of firms with positive growth significantly differed from those with negative growth. It should be noted that these years of growth belonged to the later part of the dot-com boom period in the US when the technology firms registered impressive growth. Though there were increased sales, the growth was accompanied with an increase in overhead costs (as seen from the trends of SG&A/sales and Operating expenses/Sales). The increased cost structures seem to have offset the sales growth as well as the worker productivity, leading to a decline in overall financial health.

In summary, firms in our sample seem to have enjoyed positive sales growth relative to the industry. They also had utilized their worker base effectively as indicated by the employee productivity metrics. However, they seem to have had significant problems in managing their costs. Our results indicated that firms downsizing ITworkers were less cost efficient than their industry counter-parts. They typically had higher administrative and overhead costs. Therefore, costefficiency, rather than productivity or growth seemed to be the dominant factor in IT worker layoff decisions.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions

Despite an increasing trend in IT personnel downsizing, knowledge base and scholarly literature on this topic has been sparse. We have offered some exploratory, descriptive insights into downsizing efforts involving IT personnel. Data on 569 layoff events in years 2000 and 2001 were analyzed to gain a preliminary understanding of the IT layoff phenomenon.

Our analysis indicated that the overall number of layoff events increased between 2000 and 2001, and peaked in the second quarter of 2001. About 50% of the layoff events involved reduction in IT workforce in the range of 10–30%. Though IT producer firms downsized more IT workers than IT user firms, the extent of the reduction in IT labor was almost identical across the industries. Internet and publishing industries had higher layoffs than others.

Among the reasons stated for layoffs, performance (cost cutting and productivity) was the top reason. However, many firms stated that restructuring and business refocus were primary motivations. Except for the group of firms citing closure, the amount of IT labor reduced was not different across other groups with varying motives. Advanced tests using general linear model confirmed firm size to be a pertinent factor.

We also examined the financial environment of firms reducing their IT work force: these firms enjoyed positive growth and productivities with respect to the industry. However, they had significant problems in controlling costs. The firms downsizing IT workers were less cost efficient than their industry counterparts. The high productivities and sales growth were marred by high administrative and overhead costs.

Our research had some limitations. First, we examined only those firms that had made public announcements about downsizing IT workers. There could have been many others, especially private firms. Second, our analysis was dependant on the disclosures made by the firms. We relied on publicly available rather than on primary data. Third, our results on the associations between financial environmental variables and downsizing events are suggestive only. They did not imply a direct causal relationship.

The key contributions of our paper for research and practice are: (1) we provided some exploratory insights into an important industry phenomenon on IT workers; (2) we shed light on the key trends, industry differences, motivations and financial environment of firms downsizing their IT personnel; (3) our results should help managers and IT workers to understand the dynamics underlying layoffs.
