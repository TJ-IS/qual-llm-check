# IMPACT OF RIDE-HAILING SERVICES ON TRANSPORTATION MODE CHOICES: EVIDENCE FROM TRAFFIC AND TRANSIT RIDERSHIP <sup>1</sup>

Kyunghee Lee Department of Management and Information Systems, Mike Ilitch School of Business, Wayne State University, Detroit, MI, U.S.A. {kyunghee.lee@wayne.edu}

Qianran (Jenny) Jin Department of Decision Sciences and Managerial Economics, CUHK Business School, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, HONG KONG {jennyjin@cuhk.edu.hk}

Animesh Animesh Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University Montreal, QC, CANADA {animesh.animesh@mcgill.ca}

Jui Ramaprasad Department of Decision, Operations, and Information Technologies, Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, MD, U.S.A. {jramapra@umd.edu}

The rise of technology-enabled ride-hailing services has affected individuals’ transportation-related decisions. The impact of these ride-hailing services likely varies across traveler segments that differ in their usage of various modes of transportation. In this paper, we develop and leverage a framework that allows us to examine the impact of ride-hailing services on the transportation mode choice for three traveler segments: drivers (who primarily use a personal automobile to travel), riders (who primarily use public transit to travel), and walkers (who primarily use non-motorized modes of transport). We first develop a framework outlining how the behavior of different traveler segments would be impacted by the introduction of ride-hailing services and show how this affects traffic congestion and public transportation ridership. To test the framework, we compiled a rich dataset, combining data on public transportation ridership, traffic congestion, and individual transportation mode choice. Employing a difference-in-differences methodology, we show that the Uber entry in a market enabled those who were walkers and riders prior to the entry of Uber to travel more conveniently, leading to an increase in traffic congestion, and induced those who were drivers to substitute their use of private automobiles with a combination of Uber and public transit. We introduced urban compactness to assess the heterogeneous impact of ride-hailing services for cities that differ in their distribution of traveler segments. We found that Uber entry increases traffic congestion and reduces public transit demand more in cities with higher levels of urban compactness, i.e., where the proportion of riders and walkers is higher than that of drivers. This work provides a holistic framework to understand the mechanism underlying the impact of ride-hailing services on public transit and traffic congestion. Urban planners and policy makers can leverage our framework, methodology, and empirical results to guide city planning decisions that have implications for sustainability.

Keywords: Uber, ride-hailing, traveler segment, transportation mode, public transit, traffic congestion, transportation ecosystem, urban compactness

## Introduction

Technology-based innovations are disrupting the transportation industry (Goodall, 2016). One of the major disruptions is the emergence of online “ride-hailing” or “ride-sourcing”<sup>2</sup> platforms, such as Uber and Lyft, which employ location-based mobile applications to enable on-demand “taxi-like” services by connecting riders with for-hire drivers (The Economist, 2015). These technology-enabled ride-hailing platforms not only increase the supply of rides on the road by enabling any qualified individual with a car to offer a cab-like service, but they also increase the efficiency of the system by using sophisticated algorithms and large-scale data analytics to optimally match customers with drivers, both in terms of geography and time (Beales, 2016). Compared to traditional cab services, this leads to lower prices for rides, in general. Further, location-based mobile applications simplify the ordering and payment process by eliminating the need to hail, phone, or search for a cab; reduce the uncertainty of pick-up time by providing real-time GPS tracking of the vehicle; and reduce quality uncertainty by providing driver ratings. As a result of such provisioning of cheaper, faster, and more convenient cab rides, the number of passengers adopting these ride-hailing platforms has rapidly increased (Hathaway & Muro, 2017).

The adoption of ride-hailing services can affect traveling behavior and many transportation-related decisions, such as activity patterns and mode choices made by different types of travelers (Circella & Alemi, 2018), e.g., drivers, riders, and walkers. The observed increase in the adoption of ride-hailing services motivates questions around the impact of such services on an individual’s choice of transportation mode: Does the entry of ride-hailing services induce (1) those who drive their own private automobile (“drivers”) to switch to public transit (in conjunction with the use of a ride-hailing service)?, (2) those who use public transit (“riders”) to switch to a ride-hailing service?, and/or (3) those who use non-motorized transit<sup>3</sup> (“walkers”) to switch to a ride-hailing service?

To fully understand the broader societal implications of such services (i.e., in terms of sustainability, including traffic congestion), we must first understand how people would respond to Uber entry based on their traveling behavior before the entry of ride-hailing services. Why? Because assessing whether a behavior change is beneficial is contingent on whether the past behavior was beneficial or not. More Uber rides may not necessarily lead to negative outcomes if these

Uber riders drove previously—the number of hours of private vehicles on the road may not change or may even decrease if Uber riders incorporate public transit for some portion of their trips. On the other hand, ride-hailing not only has the potential to replace the use of public transit and/or private vehicles but is also likely to substitute for nonmotorized modes of transportation. From a sustainability perspective, walking and bicycling are far better modes of transportation than public transit and private vehicles (Circella & Alemi, 2018). Without incorporating all modes of transit into examining the impacts of ride-hailing, it is not possible to attain a comprehensive understanding of the implications of ride-hailing for sustainability and other societal impacts related to the choice of transportation mode.

We thus examine not only the role that ride-hailing services play in changing public transportation demand and traffic congestion but also examine another theoretical component that can only be captured by including a “walker” segment—a segment that current work on the topic has ignored. Walkers are not observable in public transportation ridership, and, to the best of our knowledge, prior research has neither theorized nor empirically examined the impact of ride-hailing services on the traveling behavior of the walker segment. However, changes in the behavior of the walker segment may have implications for traffic congestion. On the one hand, researchers have suggested that ride-hailing substitutes for nonmotorized modes of transportation (Hampshire et al., 2017; Henao & Marshall, 2018; Circella & Alemi, 2018). On the other hand, the existence of ride-hailing services also increases the use of walking/bicycling for a small segment of users (Circella & Alemi, 2018). For example, a trip that may have been entirely made in a private automobile may be replaced with a combination of walking to public transit for one part of the trip, then riding on public transit, and finally taking a ride-hailing service from the public transit station to the ultimate destination. However, given that little is known about the impact of ridehailing services on the walker segment and that previous research supports the potential of both a positive and negative effect of ride-hailing on the demand for this mode of transportation (Circella & Alemi, 2018), it is important to examine the heterogeneous impact of ride-hailing services across traveler segments, including walkers.

The goal of this study, therefore, is to empirically examine the impact of ride-hailing services on different traveler segments whose transportation mode choices have implications in terms of sustainability. To do this, we first develop a conceptual framework that categorizes travelers, based on their existing traveling behavior, into three segments—drivers (who primarily use a personal automobile for transport), riders (who primarily take public transit for transport), and walkers (who primarily take non-motorized modes of transport). In developing this framework, we propose arguments regarding the heterogeneous effects that the introduction of ride-hailing services would have on these different traveler segments: For example, under what circumstances would riders start using Uber (therefore impacting traffic congestion)? Likewise, in what conditions would drivers start riding (therefore impacting the use of public transit)? Drawing from this framework, our empirical analysis starts by looking at how the entry of a ride-hailing service impacts traffic congestion and how this varies across traveler segments. Given that impacts on traffic congestion go hand-in-hand with the choice to use public transit (e.g., a transit rider may switch to Uber, which would increase traffic congestion and decrease transit ridership), we also examine the impact of the presence of a ride-hailing service on public transit demand for each of the traveler segments.

By supplementing the traffic congestion analysis with the public transit analysis, combined with measures that reflect the proportions of drivers, riders, and walkers across geographical areas, we gain a holistic understanding of how the behavior of different traveler segments may be differently impacted by the entry of a ride-hailing service and infer impacts on sustainability. Combining the results of these two analyses allows us to get at the underlying shifts in behaviors of different traveler segments that are responsible for changes in traffic congestion. Understanding these behavioral shifts—the mechanism that explains the impact on public transit and traffic congestion—allows for more targeted and informed policy analysis.

Finally, we introduce urban compactness as a measure from the transportation literature that can be used to assess the heterogeneous impact of ride-hailing services for cities that differ in terms of the distribution of these three traveler segments. Given that urban compactness is a more readily available measure, resource-limited policy makers and city planners who do not have access to fine-grained data about the proportion of different traveler segments can effectively use the urban compactness measure to evaluate the impact of ridehailing and propose policy interventions to address any potential negative consequences.

We focus on Uber as the ride-hailing service of interest, which is the dominant player in the U.S. market with a 74% market share in 2017 (Buildfire, 2018), to understand how transportation mode choice changes after the entry of Uber. One challenge in empirically identifying the effect of ridehailing services on transportation mode choice is the availability of individual-level data that accurately captures the distribution of drivers, riders, and walkers in different cities across time, i.e., both before and after the entry of the ride-hailing service. We addressed this issue by compiling a rich dataset that not only includes data on public transportation ridership and the degree of traffic congestion (over the period of 2012-2015) but also incorporates survey data on individual transportation mode choice to estimate the proportion of different traveler segments in a given metropolitan statistical area (MSA). We conducted a difference-in-differences analysis by leveraging the varied entry dates of Uber into 48 MSAs. We included demographic and socioeconomic covariates to control for potential confounders. We estimated the main effect of Uber entry on traffic congestion and public transit ridership as a baseline and then estimated the same model including the two measures that allow us to get at the moderating impact of traveler segments: (1) proportion of drivers, riders, and walkers in an MSA prior to the entrance of Uber, calculated from rich survey data on individual transportation mode choice, and (2) urban compactness—a measure encompassing the structure of a geographical area, which serves as a proxy for the distribution of the number of drivers, riders, and walkers in a given area.

The results of our empirical analysis show that Uber allows walkers and riders to utilize Uber to travel more conveniently while inducing drivers to substitute their use of private automobiles with a combination of Uber and public transit. The analysis using urban compactness as a moderator suggests that Uber entry decreases traffic congestion and increases public transit ridership in less compact cities (i.e. those with greater sprawl). This type of urban structure correlates with a higher proportion of drivers versus walkers and riders. However, our findings indicate that Uber entry increases traffic congestion and decreases public transit ridership when the urban structure of a city is more compact, which correlates with a higher proportion of walkers and riders versus drivers. These findings together support our theory on the heterogeneous effects of ride-hailing services on different traveler segments and remained consistent over a set of robustness tests where we relax our identification assumptions and model specifications. Importantly, our results highlight sustainability implications associated with the entrance of ride-hailing services, suggesting that the entry of ride-hailing services may have negative impacts on sustainability for cities that previously had higher walker and rider proportions; in contrast, cities with higher proportions of drivers see positive impacts on sustainability related to the introduction of ride-hailing services.

We make several contributions to the literature. First, we provide a clear indication of the existence and importance of three different types of travelers—drivers, riders, and walkers—and their relative compositions in cities with different levels of urban compactness. We also show how each segment’s behavior has changed after the technologyenabled ride-hailing service (Uber) entered. Specifically, we show that the net impact of Uber on traffic congestion and public transit depends on the relative proportion of the different types of travelers in a city. While prior work has provided a foundation to understand the societal impacts of ride-hailing services, ignoring the heterogeneous impacts across different types of traveler segments, which is an important aspect in examining the ramifications of the entrance of such digital platforms, leads to ambiguous or inconsistent results and does not provide actionable implications for policy makers and urban planners. Further, unlike prior studies, we leverage not only aggregate-level data (MSA-level urban compactness) but also individual-level data (on driving, riding, and walking behavior) in our analysis. Second, our conceptual and empirical framework itself is a contribution. The framework, along with our analysis using urban compactness, provides a structure for urban planners and policy makers to study the impacts of such technology platforms and understand the underlying shift in transportation mode choices for city planning and policymaking purposes. While Uber entry increases the likelihood of drivers using Uber in combination with public transit, this positive impact is dampened by the increased likelihood of riders and walkers switching to Uber. This impact varies depending on the distribution of these different traveler segments in different geographical areas. Third, at a broader level, our work contributes to the discussion on whether digital ride-hailing platforms are beneficial or harmful to outcomes that are important for sustainability and have significant implications for public policy. Fourth, we contribute to the small but growing literature in information systems that examines the societal impact of technology platforms (Burtch & Chan, 2019; Chan et al., 2016; Chan & Ghose, 2014; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017), once again suggesting that these platforms have important yet nuanced impacts on society.

## Relevant Literature and Conceptual Framework

## Transportation Mode Choice

Recognizing the impact of consumers’ transportation modechoice behavior on the sustainability—the energy requirements, ecological damage, and traffic congestion— researchers in various fields, including marketing, transportation, and economics, have engaged in identifying and examining the factors that affect transportation modechoice behavior (Barff et al., 1982). Extant research has assessed the impact of trip characteristics (e.g., work vs.

shopping), transportation mode characteristics (e.g., travel time, cost, comfort, convenience, safety), and consumer characteristics (e.g., auto ownership and availability, income, geographic location, gender, occupation, age) on the transportation mode choice (Barff et al., 1982; Desalvo & Huq, 1996; Frank et al., 2008; Pinjari et al., 2011).

Researchers have also examined the impact of the introduction of new transit systems on travel behavior (Dubé et al., 2011; Forsey et al., 2013; Patankar et al., 2007; Train, 1978). Recently, ride-hailing services have emerged as a technologyenabled alternative mode of transit whose impact on travel behavior is not clear. It has been suggested that ride-hailing services will motivate the substitution of private automobile rides with the public transit/ride-hailing combination (Galbraith, 2016), i.e., the new service mitigates the last-mile problem and acts as a complement to public transit. On the other hand, it has also been suggested that in the absence of ridehailing services, some people who now take trips with Uber or Lyft might have stayed home (Galbraith, 2016). While there is an intense ongoing debate among practitioners about the impact of ride-hailing platforms on existing modes of transportation (Fitzsimmons, 2017; Jaffe, 2015; Murphy, 2016; Winick, 2018; Ferentein, 2014; Leblanc, 2018), with the exception of a few working papers and reports that use surveys or empirical models, there is a lack of rigorous research on this topic. We discuss this literature next, describing existing work on ridehailing services and our specific interest—the individual traveler segments, i.e., drivers, riders, and walkers.

Practitioners and researchers have highlighted the impact of ride-hailing services on the rider segment (i.e., those travelers who use public transit as their primary mode of transportation). It has been suggested that existing riders may switch to ride-hailing services once they become available due to its advantages over public transit, and that the introduction of ride-hailing services thus decreases demand for public transit. For example, Mallett (2018) examined data from the American Public Transportation Association (APTA) and found that the annual national public transportation ridership declined by 3% between 2014 and 2016. Others suggest that ride-hailing services provide trips at times and places not wellserved by transit and allow urban dwellers to live without a personal vehicle, thus complementing public transit and increasing ridership (Murphy, 2016). This argument assumes that a significant proportion of the driver segment (i.e., those travelers who use personal automobiles as their primary mode of transportation) will switch some of their trips to public transit due to the presence of a ride-hailing service. For example, Stiglic et al. (2018) used a computational model to show that integrating ride-hailing and public transit can significantly enhance urban mobility.

To explain this variation, a group of researchers has focused on the differences between various types of transit service (Clewlow & Mishra, 2017; Babar & Burtch, 2020). Babar and Burtch (2020) argued that commuter rail has a more significant potential for complementarity than the city bus on the basis that compared to the bus, rail has a distinctive edge over Uber—rail service is faster—and thus is not in direct competition with Uber. Also, people can utilize Uber to solve the last-mile problem associated with rail service. They found that Uber entry has a positive impact (complementary effect) on commuter rail and a negative impact (substitution effect) on the city bus. Interestingly, they also found no significant effect for the other types of service, including light rail, heavy rail (subway), and commuter bus, which is inconsistent with the a priori predictions of their conceptualization based on right of way and last-mile connectivity. This inconsistency suggests that the type of service does not explain and capture the underlying theoretical mechanism by which Uber may affect public transit demand. Unlike Babar and Burtch (2020), Clewlow and Mishra (2017) found that while the majority of respondents did not change their behavior after ride-hailing services entered, those whose behavior did change were more likely to use ride-hailing services as a substitute for public transit such as the city bus or light rail and/or to complement heavy rail.<sup>4</sup> These inconsistent findings call for a new framework that not only provides a systematic explanation for the observed outcomes in the existing literature but also provides a robust framework for policy decisions going forward.

Another group of practitioners and researchers have studied traffic congestion to understand the impact of ride-hailing services on the number of drivers on the road, and based on their results, draw conclusions about the rider segment. They focus on the number of automobiles on the road, employing measures of traffic congestion as a proxy for the number of drivers on the road. Using traffic congestion, they attempted to understand whether the presence of ride-hailing services decreases the driver segment due to the substitution of private automobile rides. They showed that the reduced traffic congestion realized by the public transit/ride-hailing services combination was greater than the increase in traffic congestion caused by riders switching to ride-hailing services (Galbraith, 2016).

In contrast to papers that focus on the demand for public transit only, we examine the effect of not only drivers but also of walkers on traffic congestion. The walker segment is comprised of travelers who use nonmotorized modes of transit such as walking or biking as their primary modes of transportation or simply avoid going out due to the cost and inconvenience. To the extent that this walking segment switches to ride-hailing services after its introduction and now takes trips with Uber or Lyft when otherwise they might have stayed home, we would observe an increase in automobiles on the road, which would result in higher traffic congestion. In fact, researchers found that 8% of 380 passengers surveyed in San Francisco in 2014 used ondemand ride services to take trips that they otherwise would not have taken (Galbraith, 2016). Consistent with this observation, it has been suggested that ride-hailing apps are making urban traffic worse (Basak et al., 2020; Jeffrey-Wilensky, 2019). However, another paper using annual traffic congestion data from 1982 to 2014, found that Uber entry decreased traffic congestion (Li et al., 2017).

We argue that these conflicting results in studies focusing on traffic congestion are perhaps a result of the analysis that shows an aggregate effect and thus does not allow for concrete conclusions about individual segments and, for example, does not reveal the underlying changes in the rider and walker segments that lead to the observed changes in the driver segment. Perhaps it is this inability to observe each segment’s behavior that led to the inconsistent findings across prior research. That is, since these studies focus only on the usage of automobiles, the empirical results only provide an indirect inference that a greater proportion of the driver segment started using public transit for some of their traveling needs after the entry of ride-hailing services (Li et al., 2017) compared to the proportion of walkers and/or riders who replaced some of their walking/riding trips with ridehailing trips (Galbraith, 2016; Jeffrey-Wilensky, 2019), or vice-versa. However, these studies cannot identify whether the walker segment, in particular, was affected by the entry of ride-hailing services.

More generally, prior studies that focus on aggregate effects cannot disentangle the underlying dynamics of the different traveler segments that influence the observed effects on public transit ridership and traffic congestion, which may lead to inconsistent results across studies. Our objective is to get at this missing piece to understand the holistic impact of ride-hailing services on the choice of transportation mode. To do this, we first developed a conceptual framework that incorporates all of the three traveler segments—drivers, riders, and walkers—and theorized the impact of their behavioral changes on traffic congestion and the related impacts on public transit ridership. Together, the conceptual framework and our empirical analysis facilitate our understanding of the nuances of the impact that ride-hailing services have on these three traveler segments.

Conceptual Framework: Uber Entry and Drivers, Riders, and Walkers

Understanding how the introduction of a novel mode of transportation or access to transportation impacts individuals’ choices of the existing mode of transportation is relevant for urban planners and policy makers. The transportation literature has long been concerned with travelers’ choice of mode of transportation, examining questions around the role of land use (Zhang, 2004), free parking (Hess, 2001), and employer density (Reilly & Landis, 2003) on transport choices. In information systems, existing work on channel substitution in, for example, news media (Xu et al., 2014) and the retail industry (Xu et al., 2017) can inform our understanding of how a new platform’s entrance may impact existing channels by acting either as a substitute or a complement. Importantly, this stream of literature has found that choices and impacts are often heterogeneous, varying based on consumer attributes. In light of this, we ask: How are different “traveler segments” impacted differently by the entrance of a ride-hailing application?

To understand how different traveler segments have changed their behavior, we systematically examined how the introduction of a ride-hailing service affects each segment’s choice of transportation mode. Specifically, we conceptualized categories of travelers into three segments— drivers, riders, and walkers—based on their existing (prior to the entry of a ride-hailing service) traveling behavior. The driver segment comprises travelers who traveled primarily by automobile prior to the entry of a ride-hailing service. The rider segment consists of travelers whose travel, prior to the entry of a ride-hailing service, involved public transit. Finally, the walker segment comprises users who would have walked or biked for a given journey or stayed at home due to the cost and/or inconvenience of making the trip.

Next, we analyze how the choice of transportation mode for each type of traveler segment is impacted by the entry of ride-hailing services and how that manifests in the key observable dependent variables: traffic congestion and public transit ridership.

## Driver

For the driver segment, the presence of ride-hailing services may facilitate the use of public transit by solving the last-mile accessibility problem. While public transit is a cheaper (MacKechnie, 2017) and more sustainable mode of transportation (Haider, 2013) vis-à-vis driving a personal automobile, it is often less efficient in terms of the duration of travel (Badger, 2014; Haider, 2013). Even in large cities, e.g., Chicago, Washington DC, Philadelphia, San Francisco, and New York, with extensive public transportation networks, only small areas of these cities are efficiently served by public transit. In fact, even for those who live close to the transit line (i.e., public transit hubs/stops), it may be easier and faster to access the city by driving a personal automobile (Badger, 2014). The inconvenience cost for these travelers associated with the use of public transit may be more than that can be offset by the relative advantage of the lower cost of public transit.

One of the reasons for the inefficiency of public transit is the “last-mile problem,” which refers to the fact that even though public transit could theoretically move people most of the way to and from a destination, it is the first and/or last stretch of the journey to connect to public transit that presents a significant challenge to travelers in terms of time, flexibility and convenience. Therefore, travelers who can afford to own and drive an automobile to work may opt to drive to work rather than take public transit. Given that ride-hailing services are more convenient to access from anywhere and to go anywhere (even for short distances) than cab services and are usually cheaper, it is likely that the travelers who were at the margin (i.e., whose relative utility from driving their personal automobile was not significantly higher than taking public transit due to the lower disutility from the use of public transit and/or whose relative utility from using a cab service as a solution to the “last-mile problem” was lower than driving their personal automobile due to the high cost of the cab service) will use public transit combined with a ride-hailing service for the last-mile segment of travel instead of their personal automobile.<sup>5</sup> To the extent that there is a significant proportion of “drivers” who may switch to using public transit and a ride-hailing service combination to travel, we would expect an increase in public transit ridership. This is likely to lower traffic congestion as this change in traveling behavior replaces longer distances covered by personal automobiles with a shorter distance covered by ride-hailing services, which are used in conjunction with public transit.

## Rider

For the rider segment, ride-hailing services have the potential to become a public transit substitute, i.e., individuals may replace their use of public transportation with a ride-hailing service. That is, ride-hailing can serve as a substitute for less convenient public transit. In general, ride-hailing services, being point-to-point services similar to cabs, are more convenient than public transit, which has fixed schedules and stations.<sup>6</sup> Therefore, while the cost of a ride-hailing service may be more than public transit, a segment of existing public transit users, who may have found the net utility of using a cab service lower than taking public transit due to the high price of cab service, may now switch to using a ride-hailing service due to its lower cost and greater convenience. If a significant proportion of public transit users replace at least a part of their public transit usage with ride-hailing services, we will observe an increase in public transit ridership.<sup>7</sup> Such substitution will likely increase traffic congestion by increasing the number of cars (i.e., ride-hailing service cars) on the road. Thus far, we have only looked at the two “visible” transportation modes. We discuss non-motorized transport next.

## Walker: Uber as an Enabler of Latent Travel Needs and Replacement of Walking/Biking.

The walker segment is also susceptible to switching to a ridehailing service because of its relative net utility vis-à-vis walking, biking, or staying home. As mentioned earlier, researchers have suggested that ride-hailing services are used as a substitute for nonmotorized modes of transportation (Hampshire et al., 2017; Henao & Marshall, 2018; Circella & Alemi, 2018) and allow individuals to take trips that they may not have otherwise taken (Galbraith, 2016). If a significant proportion of travelers in this segment starts using ride-hailing services for trips that they either would not have taken or would have completed by walking/biking in the absence of such services, there would be no impact on public transit ridership but traffic congestion would increase due to an increase in the number of cars on the road.

Putting it together, we expect a significant variation in how these different segments change their travel behavior in response to the introduction of ride-hailing services. The driver segment is expected to switch to a mix of nondriving modes of transportation (public transit and ride-hailing services), as ride-hailing services solve the last-mile problem and make nondriving modes of transportation more attractive. This would increase demand for public transit and decrease traffic congestion. On the contrary, the rider segment is likely to replace public transit with ride-hailing services, lowering the public transit demand and increasing traffic congestion. The walker segment is also likely to replace walking trips with ride-hailing services, thus increasing traffic congestion.

Therefore, the average change due to ride-hailing services in a given location will depend on the relative share of traveler segments. For instance, in a region with a larger driver segment than rider/walker segments, we would expect to observe a higher demand for public transit and a lower demand for driving because the shift of previous drivers toward nondriving transportation modes would be more significant than the shift of riders and walkers toward ride-hailing services. In contrast, in regions with larger rider and/or walker segments, the shift from riding/walking to ride-hailing services would be stronger than the shift of drivers toward nondriving transportation modes, resulting in lower demand for public transit and higher demand for driving. Hence, we propose an analysis that allows us to understand the heterogeneous impacts of the entry of a ride-hailing service depending on the proportions of drivers, riders, and walkers in a given geographical area. To do this, we estimated the proportions of each traveler segment in different MSAs and examined the impact of the entry of Uber on the contribution of each segment to the observable dependent variables: traffic congestion and public transit demand.

## Methodology and Analysis

We examined Uber, the pioneer in the ride-hailing space and the current market leader. Uber was founded in 2009, and launched its first service, UberBlack, a luxury car service, in San Francisco in 2011 with a price 1.5 times that of a taxi. Soon after, in July 2012, Uber launched UberX, the event of interest in our paper, in cities such as San Francisco and San Jose, followed by other major U.S. cities in 2013 and 2014. UberX provided a cheaper service compared with UberBlack, and soon gained in popularity worldwide. In 2014 alone, Uber expanded from 66 to 266 cities and from 29 to 63 countries (Griswold, 2014).

The temporally staggered launch of UberX across cities provided us with an opportunity for econometric identification as it minimizes possible distortion from the temporal shocks that might exert an influence on the traffic congestion and public transit demand—our two main dependent variables—that is systematic across regions (Pierce et al., 2015).

## Data and Measures

Our sample includes 48 metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs) that Uber entered during the sample period of January 2012 through December 2015. The MSA is a geographical concept, which is the basis of various socioeconomic statistics supplied by U.S. government institutions; MSAs include urban areas with relatively high population density centers at their core and close economic ties in the area. The coverage of our sample is limited due to the availability of the primary data sources used in this study. We maintained the same list of MSAs across these data sets (described below) so that we could estimate the treatment effects on a consistent sample of treated units.<sup>8</sup> In addition, it is essential to include data on the demand for each mode of transportation to show the transportation mode choice shift—how people reallocate their travel demands amongst the alternatives in response to the availability of Uber. For this, we used two data sets—traffic congestion and public transit ridership.

## Traffic Congestion

To understand how Uber entry impacted traffic congestion, we used the monthly traffic data from the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and Texas A&M Transportation Institute for the period of January 2012 through December 2015. This data set has a monthly, normalized congestion measure called the Planned Time Index (PTI). PTI is the ratio of the 95th-percentile peak travel time to free-flow travel time.<sup>9</sup> For example, a PTI of 1.8 indicates that for a trip taking 20 minutes without traffic, one would need 36 minutes during hours with excessive traffic (95th percentile). We used this measure as a proxy for traffic volume.

## Public Transit

To examine the impact on the number of public transit riders, we collected monthly public transport ridership from the APTA.<sup>10</sup> The data covers the same 48 MSAs<sup>11</sup> for the same period. In each MSA, there are many transit agencies supplying various modes of public transportation (e.g., bus or rail). There are a total of 160 agencies for the 48 MSAs in our dataset. The exact modes of public transportation reported include the city bus, commuter bus, light rail/streetcar, commuter rail, and heavy rail (subway). The APTA database keeps the tally of passenger trips for each transit mode supplied by a given agent, which is the dependent variable of our public transit models.

## Control Variables

Control variables were gathered from various sources. From the APTA, we collected several travel characteristics such as the average fare (fares), the average length of trips with a transit mode (trip length), and the vehicle revenue hours per capita (transit supply). MSA characteristics are from the

American Community Survey (2015), and include the MSA’s average income (income), population (population), estimated annual population growth (population growth), unemployment rate (unemployment), average commute time (travel time), and average number of vehicles per household (vehicle count). Lastly, we collected data on the number of taxi cabs (taxi) from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Following Wooldridge (2016), the natural log was taken for variables in dollar amounts (fares, income, gas price) and for variables that have larger integer values (population, taxi, trip length). The rest of the variables—unemployment, population growth, commute time, vehicle count, trip length, transit supply (per capita)—are used in their original form. The descriptive statistics and correlations of these variables for the traffic and ridership data are presented in Table 2a and 2b, respectively.

## Identification Challenges

For Uber, which city to enter is an endogenous decision that may involve factors that correlate with public transit ridership and traffic congestion. Thus, it is crucial to identify the potential confounders that simultaneously affect Uber’s entry decision and the city’s transit outcomes. Given that Uber is a profit-maximizing agent, the most significant factors are likely to be the size of the market (population), affordability (income levels and employment rate), and other factors that determine the relative cost involved in choosing incumbent transit modes (fares, service coverage and supply, etc.). In addition, there may be some region-specific idiosyncrasies that are not readily observable and yet could affect the transportation dynamics of that region, e.g., there may be some regions where the collective attitude of people is to move away from car ownership because they favor having flexibility in choosing the mode of transportation to meet their needs.

To deal with these identification challenges, we employed a difference-in-differences (DID) model with a rich set of control variables to control for observed confounders. Our model specification includes most of the significant decision factors, which will be explained in detail later. We use preentry regions as control groups (i.e., where Uber had yet not entered at a given time but eventually entered during the sample period). This allowed us to compare changes in the outcomes of the treated units with regions that Uber had not yet but did eventually enter. That is, regions that are similar enough to the treated group in one time period that Uber then eventually also entered. We also included regional effects to condition unobserved time-invariant confounders; the difference between the pre- and post-entry periods canceled out any unique characteristics of a particular region that were constant over time.

We further considered whether the assumption—i.e., the unobserved effect having the same partial effect on the outcome in all time periods—was too strong. To allow unobserved confounders to have different effects across time, we augmented the base DID model with unit-specific time trends (Wooldridge, 2010, p. 376), allowing each region to have individual time trends, thus capturing another source of heterogeneity. Despite the restriction that the form of heterogeneity must be linear or quadratic, this approach has been adopted in many disciplines for its ability to handle timevarying unobservables with simple implementation (Burtch et al., 2018; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016; Pierce et al., 2015).

With this augmented model, our identification assumption is that the treatment, Uber entry, is independent of the outcomes, conditional not only on the observable covariates but also on time-invariant and time-varying unobservables (to some extent). We will discuss the validity of these modeling assumptions later in a separate section.

In this regard, we estimate the average changes using the following model:

$$
Y _ {(i) j t} = \mathbf {X} _ {(i) j t} ^ {\prime} \pmb {\gamma} + \beta U _ {j t} + \mu_ {(i) j} + \tau_ {t} + \rho_ {j} t + v _ {j} t ^ {2} + \varepsilon_ {(i) j t}\tag{1}

Note that the unit of analysis differs between the outcome variables: traffic is at the MSA-level (j) and public transit is at the agent-level (i). $\mu _ { ( i ) j }$ represents the MSA effects for the traffic and agent effects for public transit, and $\tau _ { t }$ represents time effects (year-month), which control for unobserved time-invariant heterogeneity and common temporal shocks. In addition, we allowed each MSA to have a separate time trend, or the MSA-specific linear $( \rho _ { j } )$ and quadratic time trends $( \upsilon _ { j } ) _ { : }$ , to mitigate potential effects from unobservable and time-varying confounders. $\varepsilon _ { ( i ) j t }$ is the error component.

$U _ { j t }$ is the indicator of the presence of Uber in MSA j at time t. ${ \mathrm { X } } _ { ( i ) j t }$ is a vector of control variables that are specific to each outcome variable, and ?? is a vector of their coefficients. For traffic congestion, only the MSA-level predictors are included, given that the unit of analysis in these models is at the MSA-level. For public transit ridership, we include the travel agent characteristics, e.g., mode of public transportation—commuter bus, commuter rail, heavy rail, light rail, or bus—the average fare, the average trip length, the service density, and the vehicle revenue hours of a transit mode by an agent. ## Moderation Analysis and Effects

Table 3 shows the average effect of Uber’s entry on traffic congestion and public transit ridership. This result indicates that traffic congestion increased by 0.0726 or 3.3% (the mean PTI is 2.18) after the entry of Uber and that public transit ridership increased by 0.39%, but the magnitude of these coefficients is not statistically significant. Looking only at this average effect, we cannot conclude whether Uber had no significant impact at all on the transportation outcomes or whether there is heterogeneity across traveler segments that is discernible but cancel each other out. This necessitates our conceptual framework, which outlines the importance of understanding the heterogeneous behavior of drivers, riders, and walkers.

Our primary interest is to examine how these three segments would react differently to having Uber as an alternative transportation mode. Our theory proposes that drivers may take public transit and Uber instead, and riders and walkers may replace their entire transit and walking trips with Uber. Aggregating all these segments as a whole therefore dilutes the true effect on outcomes and tells us an incomplete story of the impact of the entry of ride-hailing services. We propose two ways of getting at this heterogeneous effect: (1) by examining the moderating impact of the proportion of the different types of travelers (as determined by their “mix” of transportation modes), and (2) by examining the moderating impact of the MSA-level measure of urban compactness. These two different approaches get at different aspects of transportation mode choice, and we corroborate the results of the two analyses to increase our confidence in the findings.

We utilized a unique National Highway Transportation Survey dataset and developed a novel index to directly measure the proportion of different traveler segments— specifically the proportion of drivers, riders, and walkers in each of our sample MSA.

Measure—proportion of drivers, riders, and walkers: Identifying traveler segments and their respective travel behavior requires detailed trip records that include individual transportation mode choices alongside the demographic and socioeconomic factors that may influence the decision.

The NHTS is a trip-level data set that records every individual trip that participants make over a 24-hour period on the designated trip day, including the mode of transportation. This reveals the participants’ travel patterns; e.g., some people mostly walk while others mostly drive. We aggregated the triplevel data to an individual level dataset, where for each traveler, we calculated the proportion of each transportation mode (auto, public transit, walk, and taxi) they chose on their designated trip day. This data-driven approach offers an advantage over self-defined categories based on artificially chosen thresholds (e.g., if someone drives more than 90% of the trips, label that person as a driver), as it does not impose any restriction on data but rather allows it to speak freely. Thus, it offers a way to test the validity of our theoretical foundation.

The dependent variable is a normalized index for the level of traffic congestion, PTI. A higher PTI indicates higher traffic congestion in that area. To avoid the multicollinearity issue and identify all three segments together, we dropped the constant term of the interactions, Uber entry. In Model (1), the negative coefficient of the interaction with driver% indicates that in regions with more drivers present, Uber reduces traffic congestion. Specifically, traffic decreases by 0.01 or 0.5% in response to a 10% increase in the proportion of drivers. The proportion of drivers varies from 0 to 1, and the percentage-wise effect is calculated with the mean PTI value of 2.18 in our sample. Recall that PTI is the ratio of the 95thpercentile peak travel time to the free-flow travel time—a PTI of 1.8 indicates that for a trip taking 20 minutes without traffic, one would need 36 minutes during times with excessive traffic (36/20 = 1.8). Thus, the 0.01-point decrease in PTI corresponds to 0.2 minutes less over a 20-minute free-flow trip.

The interaction with $r i d e r \% ^ { 1 3 }$ has a positive coefficient, suggesting that in regions with a higher proportion of riders, Uber results in more traffic. That is, traffic increases by 0.24 or 10%, as the proportion of riders increases by 10%, which is equivalent to adding 4.8 minutes to a 20-minute free-flow trip.

## Urban Compactness as a Moderator

The distribution of drivers, riders, and walkers in a given location (e.g., many drivers but fewer riders and walkers) is not an exogenously determined characteristic but rather a result of the collective responses of its residents to the uniqueness of that location’s urban spatial structure. There are many aspects of urban structure that influence the demand and cost structure of residents’ transportation mode choices, such as road density, population density, and the distribution of workplaces (Bento et al., 2005). Subsequently, the collection of individuals’ independent decisions based on the spatial structure of the city they are living in leads to a unique composition of these three segments.

A stream of research has developed a comprehensive measure—called urban compactness or the urban compactness index (UCI)—that reflects the diversity of the geospatial aspects of an urban area. There is variation in how this concept is defined and operationalized, but the essence is that it is to capture “any development pattern in which related land uses are highly accessible to one another.” The proximity that a city’s compact spatial structure brings about therefore minimizes the need for automobile travel and reduces the demand for driving. The literature collectively suggests that people are less likely to drive a car and more likely to opt for nondriving modes in more compact cities because the need for driving is less apparent when everything is in close proximity (e.g., Bento et al., 2005). Hence, this structural compactness of a city, or how close everything is, is an important predictor of the transportation mode choice and thus the distribution of driver, rider, and walker segments in a particular region (see Figure 2).

Therefore, it can be argued that urban compactness can be a good proxy of the distribution of driver, rider, and walker segments and can be used as a moderator to assess the heterogeneous impact of Uber on different MSAs. Specifically, the complementary effect of Uber entry (less driving) is likely to be observed in cities with low compactness because there is a larger driver segment. On the other hand, the substitution effect (more driving) is relatively more prevalent in cities with high compactness because riders and walkers are likely to outnumber drivers.

Measure—urban compactness index: Measuring the spatial compactness of a city is a challenging task. It encompasses many facets of urban structure that an isolated study cannot possibly match the scope of work required. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has recognized the importance of having a measure that captures multiple dimensions of urban structure for the purposes of urban planning and public health and has sponsored research to develop this comprehensive measure of metropolitan compactness/sprawl. This research resulted in an urban compactness measure that has been subsequently employed in the public health literature to examine how urbanization affects residents’ health (Ewing & Hamidi, 2014; Lopez, 2004). The most recent refinement includes the four aspects of urban structure—density, land use, concentration of activity (population and employment concentration in the central business district or subcenters), and street accessibility. The four dimensions are the principal components of relevant geospatial characteristics and are equally summed and adjusted to the MSA size to create a composite index (Ewing & Hamidi, 2014). We collected the data on urban compactness/sprawl from the National Cancer Institute for 47 MSAs in our sample, excluding the one MSA in our sample (Boston) where the data was not available.

To assess the validity of the urban compactness measure, we examined the correlation between the urban compactness index of an MSA and the proportion of drivers, riders, and walkers in that MSA. The negative correlation between driver% and the UCI suggests that people are less likely to drive in more compact cities. The correlation matrix suggests that the three traveler segments measure that we have proposed is consistent with the index based on the geographical/spatial characteristics of an MSA.

## Robustness Checks

Our DID design uses pre-entry MSAs as control groups while controlling for possible unobserved confounders. In this section, we further investigate the assumptions we made for identification to support the robustness of our findings.

## Adding More Control Samples

In our sample MSAs, Uber entry dates are dispersed throughout the sample period. We exploited this variation and constructed control groups using areas where Uber had not yet but eventually entered during the sample period. This approach ensures that the two groups—treated and control—share similar characteristics; that is, whatever the decision factors related to Uber’s entry in an area, the not yet treated control groups also possess them. This closeness leads to a minimal between-group difference in the pre-entry covariates, which makes the control group a more reliable counterfactual of the treated group.

This approach might be problematic if covariates by treatment status (treated vs. control) are vastly different. If so, the regression estimate of a treatment effect would tend to be sensitive to model specifications (Imbens & Wooldridge, 2009). Given that our control group consists of a yet to be treated group that shares as many similar characteristics as possible, we believe it is unlikely that these two groups are highly distinct. Another possibility is that toward the end of the sample period, there will be some MSAs whose entry date is so late that the number of control samples is insufficient to draw a credible estimate, as they all become treated at some point. If this distortion is severe, then the estimates of these last few MSAs could be subject to bias. We believe this is also an unlikely scenario because there are relatively few samples that might be subject to the distortion.

Nevertheless, to rebut this point, we collected additional data on the MSAs that Uber had not entered at all during the sample period and used them as control groups.<sup>14</sup> This ensures that, regardless of when the entry occurs, all have enough control samples to estimate. Furthermore, we employed matching techniques, entropy balancing (Diamond & Sekhon, 2013; Hainmueller & Alvarez, 2011), and propensity score matching (PSM) to improve the covariate balance, the failure of which could potentially contribute to increasing estimate sensitivity. Table A2 reports the comparison of the observable covariates between the treated and matched samples using entropy balancing and PSM, respectively. The differences among the variables disappear when the control samples are weighted by the weighting factor that is obtained from the matching/balancing procedure; each pair is statistically not different from one another.

Table A3 in the Appendix shows the results of Equation (2), with the new control group added to the sample. The first panel presents the results of merely appending the new control samples, the second show the results of employing entropy balancing, and the third presents the results of applying PSM. Compared with our original findings in Tables 5 and 6, the estimates are very similar across these models, which shows no indication of any of the concerns described above.

## Parallel Trend Assumption

A critical assumption for the identification of DID models is that a treated group behaves similarly to the control group in the absence of treatment. In other words, there are no unobserved (time-varying) confounders that could alter the trends of one group compared to the other (say, $\mu _ { j t }$ , instead of $\mu _ { j }$ in Equation 2).

Several aspects of the data and our model specification support this assumption. As noted, the event of interest, Uber entry, occurs at different times for what seems not related to trends of the outcome variables (ridership and traffic). Since the entry timings are different for all the treated units, it is less likely that any systematic, global shocks exist in the trends of the pre-and post-entry periods. To empirically observe this, we added the leads and lags of the interaction between Uber entry and the traveler segments to the model to see if there is any sign of an anticipatory response. Figures A1 and A2 in the Appendix show little evidence of such an effect.

Our model specification allows unit-specific time trends in order to directly capture regional heterogeneity in the outcome; any region-specific linear and quadratic trends in outcomes can be ruled out. In addition to this approach, in the matching/balancing process mentioned earlier, we included lagged pre-entry outcomes among the others (2012 first quarter and 2012 second quarter) to ensure that there was no imbalance in outcome levels prior to the event. Adding lagged outcomes to the covariate balance process has a benefit when addressing time-varying unobservables because it resembles implementing a model with interactive effects, which allows factors to freely vary over time and factor loadings (Gobillon & Magnac, 2016). In other words, it allows each cluster or region to react differently not just to linear trends but to any form of temporal shocks. The two quarters of pre-entry lags may not be long enough to meet the full capacity of the interactive effect model but they nevertheless complement our approach of allowing unitspecific time trends. The results in Appendix Table A4 again indicate no evidence of any systematic difference.

## Compositional Data Analysis

Next, we mitigate concerns that the compositional nature of the traveler segments—that is, the interrelation of the percentage of drivers, riders, and walkers, meaning that an increase in the percentage of drivers would naturally decrease the percentage of walkers and/or riders—might affect our findings. The standard regression employed above assumes an unconstrained real space and does not consider the co-dependence of compositional data—the variations of the compositional data are bound such that compositions sum up to a constant. To reflect on this, we adopt compositional data analysis (Aitchison, 1982; Hron et al., 2012), where the composition is transformed into a real space without such a constraint so that we can safely use the standard regression estimation on the compositional variables (See Appendix for detail). The results of the compositional analysis (Table A4) are consistent with the regression estimates with no consideration of the constantsum constraint, suggesting that our findings are not sensitive to the compositional nature of the traveler segment.

## Post Hoc Analysis: UberPool

It is important to highlight that our prior analysis focuses on examining the impact of individual private rides offered by ride-hailing platforms on public transit demand and traffic congestion through the changes in the behavior of different user segments—i.e., walkers, riders, and drivers. Although most ride-hailing trips continue to be private rides, ridehailing platforms are also heavily investing in improving and promoting their shared service, also referred to as carpooling (Schaller, 2018). For example, UberPool is a carpooling service offered by the ride-hailing platform Uber, which also offers individual private rides.

We conducted a set of analyses leveraging UberPool entry dates. Our first analysis provides evidence that our main findings are not affected by the availability of UberPool. Here, we extended our main analysis by removing cities with UberPool and obtained similar results. Therefore, we believe that our main results are attributable to the effect of Uber entry (and not UberPool). Next, we look deeper into the cities where UberPool entered the market.

## Why We Should Also Examine the Impact of UberPool

An UberPool ride can consolidate multiple ride requests such that more than one unrelated passenger can share a ride by matching passengers whose destination is the same or close enough. Such sharing of rides results in a lower fare as compared to each passenger taking an individual Uber. However, it can come at the expense of longer pick-up times, potentially longer commute times, and the discomfort of sharing a vehicle with other unknown passengers. So, these carpooling services are likely to attract a different user segment and will have a different impact on sustainability than ride-hailing platforms that offer individual private rides.<sup>15</sup>

Since carpooling services, such as UberPool, allow multiple unrelated passengers to share one ride and split the fare (as per the distance traveled), it has been suggested that increased utilization of shared rides such as UberPool would lead to a reduction of overall miles of driving. Thus, one could argue that UberPool is a more sustainable service than individual private Uber rides. However, this is an assertion that has been rarely questioned (Schaller, 2018). If the driver segment switches to UberPool, the overall driving miles would be reduced (and the public transit demand would also increase if the driver segment used UberPool in conjunction with public transit); such a behavioral change would result in reduced congestion. However, if the lower cost of UberPool attracts a significant proportion of the rider and walker segments, then the demand for public transit may decrease and traffic congestion would therefore increase. Although the marginal increase in traffic congestion due to one (shared) UberPool ride would be at a lower rate than one (private) Uber ride, the overall net impact would depend on the relative size of the driver, rider, and walker segments switching to UberPool. Therefore, such an assertion needs to be examined empirically (Schaller, 2018).

## Impact of UberPool

UberPool was rolled out in multiple cities where Uber already existed. However, the impact of UberPool on public transit ridership and traffic congestion is unclear. A priori, we would expect that UberPool would reduce public transit ridership by providing less expensive rides than Uber. However, a reduction in public transit ridership due to UberPool is likely to have less of an adverse impact on traffic congestion than private Uber rides; while it only takes one rider switching to Uber to increase the number of cars on the road by one, it would require more than one rider switching to UberPool to do the same.

While UberPool may appear to be a more sustainable solution, our theoretical framework suggests that the impact of Uber and UberPool varies depending on the relative proportions of existing riders, walkers, and drivers. In contrast to Uber, UberPool comes at the expense of longer pick-up times, potentially longer commute times, and the discomfort of sharing a vehicle with other unknown passengers. If we take into account the greater inconvenience of UberPool, one could argue that UberPool is more likely to be utilized by rider/walker segments than by the driver segment. The rationale is that unlike riders and walkers, drivers need to coordinate the timing of the public transit options and UberPool, which may not always work out. Further, drivers may also have a greater willingness to pay for comfort. If this is true, we would expect to observe a larger decrease in public transit demand and a (higher) net increase in traffic congestion (which is not offset by the size of the driver segment switching to UberPool) following the introduction of UberPool.

At the same time, if UberPool leads to the cannibalization of individual private Uber rides among the rider and walker segments (i.e., if the rider and walker segment that adopts UberPool had already switched to Uber and is substituting Uber with UberPool), then the negative impact on public transit demand as well as traffic congestion will be smaller. For the sake of exposition, let’s assume the complete cannibalization case—that is, all those who adopted UberPool were riders and walkers who had previously switched to Uber. Then there would be no change in public transit demand while traffic congestion would decrease (as each UberPool ride substitutes for multiple individual Uber rides).

The other extreme case, i.e., no cannibalization would mean that all those who adopt UberPool were riders and walkers who had not switched to Uber. Then there would be a significant decrease in public transit demand (proportional to the size of the price-sensitive rider and walker segment) and traffic congestion would increase (as additional vehicles i.e., UberPool would be running on the roads). Since the actual level of cannibalization certainly lies between these two extremes, we expect that after UberPool launches, the public transit demand is likely to be further reduced. However, the effect on traffic congestion would depend on the strength of the cannibalization. Therefore, to answer this empirical question, we conducted additional analysis, as explained below.

## Data and Methodology

Although carpooling services like UberPool are being promoted as a more sustainable alternative to private individual ride-hailing services, ride-hailing companies have begun to offer carpooling services in only a few large cities (Schaller, 2018). In our sample, only two<sup>16</sup> cities, New York (2014/12/4) and LA (2015/2/20), qualified for the estimation of the UberPool effect.

Given the limited data and very few treated samples, the DID approach cannot produce credible results in this case; therefore, we adopted a comparative case study approach using the synthetic control (SC) method. The SC method is a systematic way of choosing control units based on the idea that “a combination of units often provides a better comparison for the unit than any single unit alone” (Abadie et al., 2010; Abadie & Gardeazabal, 2003). Specifically, it generates a set of weights for control units such that the weighted average of the observed covariates and the preevent outcomes of the control units resembles that of the treated unit. This weighted control group, or synthetic control group, is considered the counterfactual of the treated unit because balancing pre-event outcomes over a sufficiently long period ensures that one can imitate the counterfactual process with unobserved time-varying confounders (Abadie et al., 2010).

## Analysis and Results

Our results (presented in Appendix Figure A3) suggest that public transportation ridership starts to decline after UberPool enters cities. The effects of UberPool (the gaps between the treated and synthetic control groups) are statistically significant. The results support our conjecture on the UberPool effects in highly compact cities such as New York and Los Angeles (i.e., cities with large rider and walker segments). Further, we do not observe a statistically significant increase in traffic congestion. The between-group differences in traffic are relatively small and statistically insignificant. Thus, these results suggest that UberPool service introduction may have led to the cannibalization of individual private Uber rides such that traffic congestion does not increase (despite the reduction in public transit) but the level of cannibalization is also not high enough to have a significant negative impact on traffic congestion. Caution should be exercised, however, in applying these findings to other cities due to the limited set of cities available for this analysis.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The emergence of ride-hailing apps has prompted an emphatic debate around their impact on individuals (Greenwood and Wattal, 2017; Hall & Krueger, 2018; Burtch et al., 2018), firms/industries (Lee et al., 2018; York et al., 2015), and society (Greenwood and Wattal, 2017; Burtch et al., 2018). We contribute to the substream of research on the societal impact of ride-hailing platforms (such as Uber and Lyft) by examining how ride-hailing platforms impact traveler behavior, which, in turn, has sustainability implications.

Prior work in this stream has shown that the introduction of ride-hailing platforms benefits society. For example, it has been shown that ride-hailing platforms may lower the rate of alcohol-related motor vehicle fatalities (Greenwood & Wattal, 2017). However, it has also been shown that such ride-hailing platforms can have negative societal impacts. For example, it has been suggested that such platforms have a negative effect on crowdfunding campaign launches, thereby decreasing entrepreneurial initiatives (Burtch et al., 2018). We examine a critical yet less focused question around ride-hailing apps, which has implications for sustainability.

One perspective on analyzing sustainability is looking at the impact on public transit demand. There is an ongoing debate around the question of whether ride-hailing services serve as a complement to public transit by offering a solution to the last-mile problem or as a substitute for public transit (Fitzsimmons, 2017; Jaffe, 2015; Murphy, 2016; Winick, 2018). We propose another perspective in looking at this question: namely, that one must understand all of the underlying traveler segments (including riders, walkers, and drivers) and how these segments’ behavior changes with the introduction of ride-hailing services.

Specifically, our paper focuses on the impact of Uber entry on the three different traveler segments and examines how the introduction of technology-enabled ride-hailing services changes the transportation mode choice of the walker segment (i.e., those who use nonmotorized modes of transit), rider segment (i.e., those who use public transit), and the driver segment (i.e., those who use private automobiles), which will be reflected in traffic congestion as well as public transit demand. Prior work has only looked at two of these segments—riders (explicitly, by measuring public transit ridership) and drivers (implicitly)—and does not acknowledge the existence of the walker segment. However, the walker segment can change the dynamics of the whole system: if the walker segment were to switch to ride-hailing services, it would increase traffic and have an adverse impact on environmental sustainability, which may not be captured when only looking at drivers and riders. Excluding the walker segment limits the understanding of the impact of such services and has relevant policy implications. Therefore, our study examines the holistic impact of ride-hailing services on transportation mode choice—walking, riding, or driving— which is critical to understanding the sustainability implications of ride-hailing services.

We developed a conceptual framework to first introduce the different traveler segments, and then understand how the presence of ride-hailing services would impact these different segments. Our conceptual analysis also shows how these changes in transportation mode choices vary across geographical areas with different distributions of these segments and is thus a contribution in itself. Guided by our conceptual framework, we then conducted an empirical analysis to identify the impact of the introduction of ridehailing services on drivers, public transit riders, and walkers. Our analysis includes a comprehensive measure of urban sprawl—urban compactness—along with a novel, granular dataset from the NHTS that allowed us to explicitly test the underlying mechanism proposed in our conceptual framework. This approach is unique in the literature and acknowledges a heterogeneous impact of Uber entry on the demand for different modes of transportation that has thus far been ignored.

Our results address two important ongoing discussions in the existing literature. First, we contribute to the debate about whether Uber leads riders to substitute public transit for Uber or whether it leads drivers to switch to public transit, by showing that Uber acts as both a substitute for and complement to public transit. We show that while Uber leads to a positive impact on the environment by increasing the likelihood of drivers taking Uber in combination with public transit, this positive impact is dampened by the increased likelihood of riders and walkers switching to Uber as well. Our results provide evidence for the underlying mechanism proposed in our conceptual framework and shows that the net impact of Uber on public transit depends on the relative proportion of riders and drivers in a city.

Second, we contribute to the question of how Uber affects the walker segment (Circella & Alemi, 2018) by showing that (a significant proportion of) walker segment may also switch (a significant proportion of their walking trips) to Uber. These results are consistent with the survey conducted in seven major U.S. cities, between August 2015 and January 2016, which shows that 17% of walking trips and 7% of bike trips were replaced with ride-hailing services (Clewlow & Mishra, 2017). The results of our analysis provide insights for policymakers and a framework that can be broadly applied. We show that it is important to consider the walker segment, in addition to the rider and driver segment, in order to understand the impact of ride-hailing services on “sustainable transportation.” One cannot draw these conclusions by looking at the public transit mode choice alone: our combined analysis of public transit demand and traffic congestion and our conceptual framework incorporating drivers, riders, and walkers will allow policy makers to make informed decisions about how to meet sustainability goals.

Further, we contribute to the theoretical and empirical research on this topic by highlighting the need to consider heterogeneity in urban sprawl across cities. For example, in a highly compact city like New York City, more riders and walkers are present than drivers, which results in greater substitution of public transit after the entry of ride-hailing services (Bresiger, 2018; E.

Fitzsimmons & Hu, 2017). Thus, the conclusions drawn from existing studies that examine the consequence of ride-hailing platforms are likely to be inconsistent (Jeffrey-Wilensky, 2019; Li et al., 2017) if the composition of the sample cities in these studies varies significantly in terms of the proportion of drivers, riders, and walkers.

In addition to using individual-level traveler data (from NHTS survey) to create proxies for the driver, rider and walker segments, we also identify another proxy to capture the heterogeneity across cities in terms of the proportion of different traveler segments. Specifically, we argue that the proportions of drivers, riders, and walkers in a city depend on the characteristics of each city—e.g., spatial, infrastructural, and demographic—and identify a novel measure to capture such city characteristics. Specifically, we adopt the urban compactness index (UCI), which has been employed in public health research as a proxy for the composition of a city in terms of the proportions of different traveler segments. We show that a city’s UCI can be used to predict whether public transit in a city will face a net complementary or substitution effect. Our results show that a city’s UCI moderates the impact of ride-hailing services such that Uber leads to lower public transit demand in cities that have high UCI. Consistent with these results, we find that Uber also leads to increased traffic congestion (i.e., the road gets more crowded) in cities with high UCI. These results further reinforce that researchers who develop theoretical as well as empirical models should include urban compactness as a moderator when examining the impact of Uber entry. More importantly, using UCI as a moderator has implications for policymakers. We suggest that city planners and policy makers who do not have access to fine-grained data about the proportion of different traveler segments should use the urban compactness index to understand the impact of ride-hailing services and design optimal responses and policies for building sustainable cities.

This paper is not without limitations. First, our sample is limited to 48 MSAs in the U.S. Although the sample is quite diverse in terms of geo-economic characteristics, caution must be made when applying the findings to other cities or countries. Second, various policy interventions have been made to Uber and other ride-hailing services across different states, which could confound our findings. As a pioneer in ride-hailing services and the sharing economy in general, Uber continues to be at the center of nationwide political and public debates. There have been anti-Uber protests, the inaction and retraction of Uber halt policies in various states, and California’s recent passage of a bill forcing Uber to legally hire drivers as employees. Nevertheless, it is unlikely that such interventions influenced our findings because these are relatively recent activities and our sample period ends in 2015. Further, there have been no federal regulations or nationwide activities that would affect all the cities in our sample. Also, this study does not consider competition with other ride-hailing services like Lyft, which may have affected our findings. We believe, however, that the temporal and geographical dispersion of the Uber entry dates likely allay this concern.

This paper provides a basis for future research. While we show that ride-hailing services have a heterogeneous impact on different traveler segments, future research could examine how various socioeconomic indicators may interact with these traveler segments. For example, the measure of urban compactness consists of four dimensions, each of which captures a unique aspect of urban structure. It would be interesting to see which aspect(s) correlate with the proportions of drivers, riders, and walkers the most. Further, future research could also explore the effect of external factors such as weather conditions that may moderate the ride-hailing service entry effect.<sup>17</sup>

To conclude, our analysis allows us to infer how different commuting segments’ behavior is affected by Uber entry and demonstrates that the analysis focusing simply on the net effect of Uber entry on public transit demand or traffic congestion does not tell the whole story. Focusing on different traveler segments and the changes in their travel behavior allows us to better understand how ride-hailing apps play a role in environmental sustainability. Analyzing the empirical results in light of our framework, we suggest that Uber allows walkers and riders to utilize Uber to travel more conveniently, while the driver segment substitutes its use of private automobiles with a combination of Uber and public transit. Consequently, the increase of traffic congestion from the walker and rider segments switching to Uber balances out the reduction of traffic congestion resulting from drivers, who use Uber to solve the last-mile problem.

There has been a recent wave of interest in shared mobility or multimodal transportation, where policy makers and practitioners seek to promote nonmotorized modes of transportation, thereby improving the sustainability of the transportation ecosystem. Given that, in our study, individuals who previously engaged in more environmentally sustainable actions are now increasingly engaging in less sustainable actions, our study has implications for policy makers and ridehailing services. These stakeholders should devise strategies to help mitigate this negative effect by incentivizing the use of electric vehicles, or more generally, vehicles that have fewer negative environmental impacts. For example, policy makers could encourage collaboration between ride-hailing companies and transit agencies in cities with a low compactness index to plan fixed routes and schedules so that ride-hailing platforms can serve as a sufficient solution to the first/last-mile problem.

## Compositional Data Analysis

The compositional data analysis is a set of techniques to transform a constrained space into a real space (Aitchison, 1982; Hron et al., 2012). Formally, the constrained space can be represented as the simplex: $S ^ { d } = \{ x = ( x _ { 1 } , \dots , x _ { d } ) , x _ { i } \geq 0 , \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { d } x _ { i } = k \}$ . One can remove the constant-sum constraint $\textstyle ( \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { d } x _ { i } = k )$ by transforming the composition ?? in the constrained space $S ^ { d }$ to a log ratio composition ?? in $R ^ { d } ,$ , d-1 dimensional real space: $z _ { i } = \log ( x _ { i } / x _ { d - 1 } ) , ( i = 1 \ldots d - 1 )$ . The components of the logratio composition ?? can vary independently and thus are identifiable without the loss of information.

The literature offers two different approaches to the logratio transformation. The first one is to regress the response on logged compositions with the constraint that the coefficients of the logged compositions sum to zero (i.e., the logcontrast condition) (Aitchison & Bacon-Shone, $1 9 8 4 ) \colon E ( Y \vert x ) = \beta _ { 0 } + \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { d } \beta _ { i } l n x _ { i } , ( \sum _ { i } ^ { d } \beta _ { i } = 0 )$ .

The second one is to perform an isometric logratio (ilr) transformation. The idea is to construct an orthonormal basis on the simplex and use the new coordinates in a regression model without any constraint on the coefficients (Hron et al., 2012; Chastin et al., 2015). Following Hron et al. (2012), we adopt a method called sequential binary partitioning. While we leave technical details to the paper, it is worth noting that only the first coordinate of an orthonormal basis captures the full information about the compositional part $x _ { i } ;$ the rest of the coordinates contain only partial information. Thus, we need to perform ?? different ilr transformations by permuting the indices ?? such that each component takes the role of $x _ { 1 } .$ . In our case, we need to perform the ilr transformation for the driver segment by putting it as $x _ { 1 }$ and then get the regression coefficient of the first ilr coordinate $z _ { 1 } ^ { D }$ , which explains the effect on the outcome of the driver segment relative to the others. Then the process is repeated for the rider and walker segments to obtain the first ilr coordinate $z _ { 1 } ^ { R }$ and $z _ { 1 } ^ { W }$ . Formally, we estimate: $\begin{array} { r } { E ( Y | x ) = \beta _ { 0 } + \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { d - 1 } \beta _ { i } z _ { i } ^ { l } . } \end{array}$ $l = ( D , R , W )$
