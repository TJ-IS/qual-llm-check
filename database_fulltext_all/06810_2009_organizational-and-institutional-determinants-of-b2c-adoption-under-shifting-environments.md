# Organizational and institutional determinants of B2C adoption under shifting environments

## Abstract

This study examines the adoption of business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce by bricksand-mortar companies comprising the Standard & Poor’s 500 (S&P 500) listings between 1992 and 2003. B2C represents a Type III information systems (IS) innovation that integrates IS with core business technologies. Extant studies on Type III innovations have examined organizational and institutional factors, solely or collectively, in explaining adoption, but not how their effects change under shifting environments over time. We develop an integrated model comprising organizational factors (i.e., espoused values and resources) and institutional factors (i.e., normative and mimetic pressures), as well as the moderating influence of shifting environments (i.e., early period and late period demarcated by changes in the environment). Using a piecewise event-history model specification, we examine the adoption of B2C innovations by 93 organizations over time. Our results show that both organizational and institutional factors influence B2C adoption; however, their effects varied with the environmental shifts. Specifically, senior IS executives influenced adoption in the early period whereas bandwagon mimetic pressures and business norms influenced adoption in the late period. The findings of our research demonstrate the importance of explicitly modeling environmental shifts in theorizing organizational adoption of innovations.

## Introduction

B usiness-to-consumer (B2C) innovations have significantly altered the ways organizations conduct business and the ways in which consumers shop. Forrester Research Inc. expects online retail sales to hit US\$184 billion in 2008 and US\$271 billion in 2011 (US Census Bureau, 2006). Jupiter Research expects online sales to grow by at least 9% every year until 2011 (New York Times, 2007). Market analysts also report that consumers continue to shift their shopping preferences to online channels (USA-Today, 2006).

B2C innovations belong to the rubric of Type III information systems (IS) innovations that integrate IS products and services with core business technologies (Swanson, 1994; Chatterjee et al., 2002). Despite the considerable research in this area (e.g., Neo et al., 1994; Teo et al., 1995; Grover et al., 1997; Premkumar et al., 1997), our understanding of organizational adoption regarding Type III innovations is limited by two related and tacit assumptions in extant literature. Prior studies generally have assumed one of two things: either (a) organizations faced similar influences to adopt the innovation over time, even as the environment may have shifted, or (b) all organizations adopted the innovation during a relatively short period in which the environment remained unchanged. These assumptions have led to research models that did not explicitly take into account shifts in the environment over time. However, adoption typically resembles an S-shaped curve over time, indicating that organizations within a population adopt the same innovation at different points in time, during which environmental changes are not uncommon.

In this study, we seek to address this limitation in prior research on organizational adoption of Type III innovations by examining two related research questions: (a) What organizational and institutional factors influence organizational adoption of B2C innovations? and (b) How do shifting environments (over time) impact the effects of organizational and institutional factors on organizational adoption of B2C innovations?

To answer these research questions, we examine the adoption of B2C innovations by Standard & Poor’s 500 (S&P 500) companies. We explicitly model shifting environments using a piecewise event-history model that segmented our study period based on important events in the broader environment, and allowed the effects of the antecedents to be examined over different time periods. Our findings provide initial evidence that the influence of organizational and institutional factors on B2C adoption (and consequently Type III innovations) changes over time under shifting environments.

## Prior literature on Type III innovations

Type III innovations, such as B2C (Chatterjee et al., 2002), combine IS products or services with core business technologies and enable organizations to gain strategic advantage, develop new markets, create new organizational forms, or redefine organizational boundaries (Swanson, 1994). A variety of Type III innovations such as electronic data interchange (Premkumar et al., 1997), inter-organizational systems (Grover, 1993), material requirements planning (Grover et al., 1997), and electronic business (Zhu and Kraemer, 2005) have been examined in prior literature (see Table 1).

Several dependent variables such as intention to adopt, adoption, diffusion, adaptation, and infusion have been used to examine how organizations have dealt with Type III innovations. Prior research generally has focused on either organizational factors such as organization size and organizational slack (Grover et al., 1997; Truman, 2000), or institutional factors such as peer pressure and coercive influence (Neo et al., 1994; Hart and Saunders, 1998), or both (Grover, 1993; Premkumar et al., 1997; Zhu and Kraemer, 2005) as predictors of adoption.

As can be seen from Table 1, prior studies have forwarded universalistic views of innovation adoption, in which organizational and institutional factors exerted similar types and levels of influence on adoption regardless of the passage of time since inception of an innovation. Stated differently, early and late adopters of innovations would be guided by similar factors on adoption, no matter the changes in their environments over time. This is a somewhat unrealistic assumption since environmental changes are ubiquitous and impact organizational behaviors (e.g., Edelman, 1992; Sutton and Dobbin, 1996).

## B2C innovations and shifting environments

Several events in the technological, legal, and economic environments shaped the creation and development of the B2C innovation as well as its adoption and implementation by organizations over time. The earliest technological changes can be traced back to 1987 when the commercial Internet backbone was initiated (Moschovitis et al., 1999); it was restricted to research and educational facilities, difficult to use, and required considerable technical skills. However, it served as the foundation for the development of the World Wide Web (WWW) between 1989 and 1991, which provided a user-friendly mechanism for implementing Web applications including B2C systems. This was followed by the introduction of the Mosaic Web browser in 1992 and the Netscape Web browser between 1993 and 1994. The standardization of the WWW protocols, such as HTML 4.0, in 1997 (WWW-Consortium, 1991) allowed Web browsers to function similarly across different platforms and systems. Further, the secure electronic transaction (SET) protocols were made available in 1997 (ComputerWorld, 1997).

Some notable events in the legal environment also enabled B2C innovations. The High-performance Computing Act of 1991 allowed for the promotion and development of commercial data communications standards (NCO-NITRD, 2008). Later revisions and amendments such as the Information Infrastructure and Technology Act of 1992 and the National Communications Competition and Information Infrastructure Act of 1993 enabled the development of the National Information Infrastructure that served as the foundation for later innovations (Electronic Frontier Foundation, 1993). Between 1993 and 1994, these legislations gradually allowed private-sector organizations to be responsible for the development of the Internet infrastructure, which until then was within the purview of the US government. By 1995, organizations such as Compuserve, Prodigy, and America Online assumed roles as network providers.

On the economic front, amazon.com opened as an online bookstore in 1995, questioning traditional beliefs about conducting business. The company made its initial public offering in 1997 and raised US\$54 million on opening day at 30% above the opening price (which had been raised twice before markets opened) with a market capitalization of US\$438 million, lending credibility and legitimacy to the fledgling online industry (C-Net News.com, 1997). Several other online companies such as priceline.com, webvan.com, and pets.com also came into being between 1997 and 1999, and enjoyed high market capitalizations. Traditional companies such as Albertsons, Barnes and Noble, and Walgreens soon followed with their own online operations. Between 2000 and 2001, the dot-com boom ran out of steam and the stock markets collapsed (Nataraj and Lee, 2002). While the developments in technology, legislation, and the economy created a hospitable environment for the growth of B2C, the dot-com bust helped to highlight not only the benefits but also some of the problems of adopting B2C operations.

## Research model and hypotheses

To provide a more nuanced analysis of the dynamics surrounding the adoption of B2C innovations, we developed an integrated model of organizational and institutional factors and explicitly modeled environmental shifts over time (see Figure 1).

## Early period effects (1992–1997)

According to neoinstitutional theory, institutionalization of a practice is a process that occurs over time. Practices start out in organizations as innovations to deal with a specific set of conditions and eventually may be taken for granted within an organizational field as the way things should be done. Typically, it takes time for innovations to gain this level of acceptance and during this period, the potency of organizational and institutional pressures to adopt shifts (Zucker, 1977; Tolbert and Zucker, 1996). With the introduction of an innovation, there is little initial consensus about its value and adopters are relatively few in number. Organizations’ decisions to adopt or resist new innovations are guided by internal technical, economic, or political factors; adoption decisions largely are made independently of what other organizations are doing (Tolbert and Zucker, 1983, 1996).

Espoused values ‘reflect what senior managers actually believe their organizations to be like, what they would like or prefer their organizations to be like, or what they would like significant stakeholders to believe the organization is like’ (Kabanoff and Daly, 2002). They are the domain of senior managers and often are expressed in public statements, such as firms’ annual reports (Daly et al., 2004). As such, they are statements of how they want others to view their organizations and can be a tool for impression management. Since espoused values can be used to understand differences between organizations (Kabanoff and Daly, 2002), it is conceivable that organizations that claim different sets of values may act differently in their practices. Two specific types of values that may be important for B2C adoption are customer-centrism and innovation-centrism.

Some companies claim that they value providing good customer service (Michela and Burke, 2000). B2C may represent a means for serving customers well since consumers can interact with organizations from the comfort of their own homes, 24/7, and minimize visits to physical stores (Evans and Wurster, 2000). From the organizations’ perspective, they can have another channel of communication with consumers and institute support services, such as home delivery, to serve consumers (Kalakota and Robinson, 2001). Consequently, organizations that present themselves as being concerned with serving customers may be more interested in adopting B2C than other organizations (Hypothesis 1).

Another espoused value that differentiates one organization from another is the degree to which it believes it should pursue innovation. B2C involves changes that entail innovative behavior in organizations. First, B2C may require the formulation of new business models that may be different from the traditional business models (Kalakota and Robinson, 2001). Second, B2C may require changes to existing organizational practices and routines (Willcocks and Plant, 2001). Thus, organizations may be more likely to adopt B2C when they claim to value innovation (Hypothesis 2).

B2C entails significant investments in technical equipment and skilled personnel. First, B2C systems need to be running 24/7 without glitches (Turban et al., 2000). Second, B2C storefronts are generally information technology (IT)- based systems that need to be designed, developed, and maintained (Kalakota and Robinson, 2001). Finally, implementation of support operations required for order fulfillment involves considerable staff and investments (Willcocks and Plant, 2001). Organizations may obtain the resources necessary to invest in equipment and personnel in one of two ways. First, organizations may look to themselves to finance the B2C initiative. This is possible when organizations have slack resources at their disposal which can be routed for the B2C-related expenditure. Alternatively, organizations may obtain resources from outside. Generally, organizations that are in better financial condition will have greater access to external capital. Consequently, organizations that possess internal slack resources (Hypothesis 3) or that have access to external capital (Hypothesis 4) will be more likely to adopt B2C innovations.

During the early period, B2C systems were new and not well understood yet. The technologies and standards surrounding B2C (WWW-Consortium, 1991; ComputerWorld, 1997) were still evolving and internal or external knowledge about B2C systems was lacking. Organizations considered their physical stores to be the primary mechanism for engaging potential consumers (Winner, 1997). B2C systems were not very user-friendly or accessible to many consumers yet (Moschovitis et al., 1999). Organizations did not yet understand the potential impact of B2C systems on consumers or their success among consumers, and did not know who their consumers were (ComputerWorld, 1995), and were wary of going the online route to reach consumers (ComputerWorld, 1994). Therefore, organizations experimented with B2C systems to understand its capabilities and to determine the extent to which B2C systems could benefit their operations (Earl, 2000). Organizations felt little pressure to adopt B2C systems since there was no consensus on the legitimacy of B2C (Tolbert and Zucker, 1996) and there were not many adopters to model organizations’ own responses to B2C. Consequently, organizations’ internal characteristics such as espoused values and resources will be influential on organizations’ decisions to adopt B2C innovations during the early period (Hypothesis 5).

## Late period effects (1998–2003)

As an innovation starts to gain acceptance, its use in an organization signals the environment that the organization is legitimate. Consequently, organizations may feel pressure to adopt in order to maintain or gain legitimacy. Once an innovation is infused with value, adoption increases across organizations and organizations are influenced by the behaviors of external actors. Adoption may be influenced by societal expectations and by a particular occupational group that has professional interests in seeing the innovation used in the organization and who therefore promotes adoption (Tolbert and Zucker, 1996; Rao, 1998). These normative pressures generally are transmitted from the environment through professionals, professional associations, social networks, educational institutions, and the media (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Similarly, organizations look to their peers and competitors to see if they have been among the adopters and will be guided by the behavior of their industry peers. In other words, after the initial introduction of an innovation, as organizations compete for resources and customers, normative and mimetic pressures for isomorphism with the environment should become more powerful in innovation adoption (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983).

Senior IS executives function as ‘gatekeepers’ of relevant information and industry practices from external sources that may be assimilated by the organization. Senior IS executives may act as ‘idea champions’ for B2C, reflecting both the expectations of their profession which they glean from their affiliations with professional associations and their knowledge about the potential for exploiting IS capabilities using state-of-the-art innovations such as B2C. Further, senior IS executives may actually participate in strategic decision making (Stephens et al., 1992) and may help determine organizational responses to B2C innovations (Hypothesis 6).

Organizations also may actively scan the environment to learn about industry practices and norms that have captured the public’s attention. These scanning activities represent a mechanism for keeping up with broader societal and cultural expectations and may include the perusal and assimilation of information from the popular press, including newspapers, magazines, professional and trade publications, white papers, case studies, etc. (Lounsbury, 2001). Organizations may view the B2C innovation favorably and be more likely to adopt when information from external media sources is also favorable (Hypothesis 7).

A second type of pressure influencing isomorphism is mimetic pressure, which leads organizations to model themselves on others in their organizational fields in order to gain legitimacy (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Three mimetic mechanisms, each associated with varying amounts of information richness gleaned from the actions of other organizations may influence adoption (Kraatz, 1998).

The bandwagon model of imitation (Hypothesis 8) suggests that organizations follow suit when a growing number of other organizations have adopted a particular innovation, practice, or behavior (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf, 1993; Kraatz, 1998). Focal organizations generally only have the knowledge that the other organizations have adopted a practice; other information about the practice, such as the benefits for its adopter, is not transmitted. Such imitation may spring from the organization’s own uncertainty regarding the extent to which nonadoption of the practice may hurt its competitive standing in the marketplace or its legitimacy with patrons (Kraatz, 1998).

The status-driven model of imitation (Hypothesis 9) argues that organizations tend to adopt the practices previously adopted by larger or more prominent counterparts in their fields (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Kraatz, 1998). Focal organizations typically know and watch the different coping mechanisms pursued by larger or more prominent organizations. This form of imitation may derive from the focal organizations’ beliefs that imitating larger or more prominent organizations is a reasonable strategy to cope with uncertainty and that it may lead to gains in legitimacy.

The social learning model of imitation (Hypothesis 10) proposes that organizations may engage in similar behaviors as others when there is evidence that the other adopters have derived benefits from adopting a practice (Kraatz, 1998). Focal organizations have access to meaningful knowledge such as the extent to which the adoption has benefited the other organizations. Organizations are viewed to be sensitive to and cognizant of the extent to which the innovation satisfies its potential when adopted.

During the late period, B2C systems had grown more popular as evidenced by the entry of amazon.com into the competitive landscape and the addition of other organizations, such as Barnes and Noble, starting B2C operations (Earl, 2000). The technologies and standards supporting B2C systems, such as HTML and SET, had matured considerably and organizations were in a position to exploit the technological advances (WWW-Consortium, 1991; ComputerWorld, 1997). The capabilities of B2C were better understood following the experimental initiatives by organizations during the early period. There was a greater recognition of the advantages of B2C such as value-added customer service and market expansion by organizations (Business Wire, 1999). B2C was now recognized as a legitimate way of conducting business and organizations started to include it in their strategies (Earl, 2000). The increasing number of B2C adopters also served as a reminder for other organizations to consider B2C for their operations. B2C systems were more user-friendly and were more easily accessible to consumers through private network service providers such as America Online and Compuserve (Moschovitis et al., 1999). Consumers also began to recognize the convenience and value of B2C systems and expected organizations to supplement their traditional operations with B2C systems (Lohse and Spiller, 1998). The popular press continued to report on new adopters from a variety of industries as well lists of top online retailers, the potential of B2C for organizations, and the ways in which organizations may benefit from adopting B2C systems (e.g., Business Wire, 2000). As a result, organizations were pressured to mimic others and be seen as legitimate as well, even if they perceived B2C to be not as useful or advantageous to their operations. Consequently, institutional factors will be influential on organizations’ decisions to adopt B2C systems in the late period (Hypothesis 11).

## Research method

Our sampling frame consisted of the companies that comprise the S&P 500 lists in 1991 and 2003. Since Internet-based B2C systems were possible only after the invention of the WWW in 1991, we assumed 1992 as the origin point and 2003 as the cutoff point for our analysis. (Since the Internet was adopted in 1987, we checked to see if B2C was used before 1991. All organizations in our sample adopted after 1991, eliminating the potential for left censoring of the data.) Therefore, our study tracks B2C adoption since inception.

The authors obtained the S&P 500 lists published in the years 1991 and 2003 and selected all US organizations that were listed for both years. This resulted in 230 organizations. Since B2C systems typically facilitate transactions between an organization and its consumers (Korper and Ellis, 2000), we believed that only those organizations that had a consumer-direct component in their business models were likely to adopt B2C systems. We use the term ‘consumer-direct’ to indicate those organizations that sell their products or services directly to consumers without relying on other mechanisms such as resellers or intermediaries. We reviewed the business models of the 230 companies and determined if their business models included a consumer-direct component. (Each organization’s business model was examined by at least two authors and disagreements were resolved through discussion.) This resulted in a sample size of 93 organizations.

## Dependent variable

Year of B2C adoption was determined for those organizations in our sample that had adopted B2C prior to or in 2003. To facilitate data collection, the authors first visited the company Web site for each organization and determined if it had B2C functionality. We used Web site components such as shopping carts, consumer login, payment screens, etc., to conclude if an organization had adopted B2C systems. For each organization that had adopted B2C, we looked for data on the year in which it first introduced B2C. Specifically, we reviewed historical information available on the company Web sites, company press releases, and newspaper articles (found through Lexis Nexis) for information regarding B2C adoption. Of the 93 organizations included in our analysis, 80 organizations had adopted B2C and 13 organizations had not adopted B2C by 2003. Figure 2 shows the hazard plot of B2C adoption for our sample. Companies were coded 1 for the year in which they adopted B2C and 0 for each year up to the year of adoption. For analysis, we converted the year of adoption to a measure that captured the time elapsed since 1991 for adoption.

## Independent variables

We measured the espoused values of customer-centrism and innovation-centrism as dichotomous variables: 1 represented a customer-centric or innovation-centric value orientation and 0 otherwise. For both sets of espoused values, we gathered data from company annual reports from the year prior to B2C adoption. The authors read through the reports and attempted to formulate a holistic understanding of the espoused values of each organization. Specifically, we looked for descriptions of ‘customer service’ and ‘innovativeness’ that conveyed the value orientations of organizations. Each annual report was read and coded by at least two authors. The initial inter-rater agreement between the two coders for customer-centrism exceeded 95% and was 89% for innovation-centrism. Disagreements were first discussed and resolved between the two responsible coders, and then among all four authors if still unresolved. The final inter-rater agreement between the coders for both variables was 100% each.

We used return on equity to represent the slack resources at the organizations’ disposal (Grover et al., 1997) and debt to equity to represent the organizations’ access to capital. Both measures were set up as continuous measures and were captured for every year in the study period (up to year of adoption). We obtained these data from the Compustat database.

For setting up the normative pressures measure for IS executives, we obtained data on whether each organization had a senior IS executive in its ranks prior to adopting B2C or the study’s cutoff period if the organization had not adopted B2C. We considered individuals with the titles Chief Information Officer, Executive Vice President of IS or IT, and Senior Vice President of IS or IT as senior IS executives. We obtained data on senior IS executives from multiple sources such as the S&P’s Register of Companies and Executives, company annual reports, and 10-K reports. For each organization, we coded normative pressures as a dichotomous variable for each year prior to B2C adoption or the cutoff period for those companies had not adopted B2C: 1 represented the presence of a senior IS executive and 0 otherwise.

The normative pressures measure for business norms was constructed using the articles regarding B2C reported in the popular press. Specifically, we gathered all articles on B2C published in Information Week during each year of the study period. (We used key words such ‘B2C,’ ‘business to consumer,’ ‘electronic commerce,’ etc., on Lexis Nexis to find the articles on B2C published by Information Week.) Two authors read and coded each article for whether the article provided a ‘positive,’ ‘negative,’ or ‘mixed’ review about B2C. Finally, we computed the measure as the ratio of the number of ‘positive’ articles to the total number of articles published each year.

The three mimetic pressures (bandwagon, status-driven, and social learning) measures were based on the S&P 500 organizations that competed against the 93 organizations in our sample in their respective industries. For each organization in our analysis, we obtained the list of all organizations in its industry over the entire study period from the Compustat database. (The industry criterion for each organization was based on the North American Industry Classification System: NAICS 2-digit codes.) We found a total of 235 organizations that may have influenced the 93 organizations in our analysis. Using company Web sites, press releases, and periodicals, we then gathered the year in which each prominent organization first adopted B2C. (We were not able to obtain adoption data on 25 prominent organizations since they had been acquired by other organizations while still at the risk of adoption.)

We subsequently constructed the three mimetic pressures variables as continuous measures as follows. The bandwagon mimetic pressures for each organization in our sample for each time period was computed as the ratio of the ‘number of competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization) that had adopted B2C prior to adoption by the focal organization (numerator) to the ‘number of competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization)’ (denominator). The status-driven mimetic pressures for each organizations in our sample for each time period was computed as the ratio of the ‘number of prominent or prestigious competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization), where prominent or prestigious organizations for a focal organization were defined as those organizations whose market capitalizations were at least one standard deviation of the mean market capitalization for all competitor organizations within the industry above the focal organization’s own market capitalization’ (numerator) to the ‘number of competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization)’ (denominator). The social learning mimetic pressures for each organization in our sample for each time period was computed as the ratio of the ‘number of competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization) who had adopted B2C and had realized increases in sales in the subsequent period’ (numerator) to the ‘number of competitor organizations (with the same NAICS 2-digit code as the focal organization)’ (denominator). Data on market capitalization and sales, used in the computation of status-driven and social learning measures, were obtained from the Compustat database.

## Control variables

We included industry type and organization size as control variables in our models. We control for industry type since some industries may adopt B2C sooner than other industries due to industry-specific practices or norms. For each organization, we gathered its industry type from the Compustat database using the NAICS 2-digit codes. We categorized the 93 companies into six groups for analysis: manufacturing (21 organizations), retail (21), information (16), finance (14), utilities (10), and miscellaneous (11), including hospitality, healthcare, mining, and transportation among others. In the analyses, the miscellaneous group was the referent category.

We control for organization size since organizations that possess extensive human resources have the potential to allocate such resources toward environmental scanning and experimentation. We measured organization size as the number of employees. For each organization, we obtained organization size from the Compustat database for each year in our study period. We computed the log of employee size for testing the theoretical model.

## Analysis

We used event-history (i.e., survival) analysis techniques, using Stata 9.0 published by the Stata Corporation, to examine the influences on organizational adoption of B2C innovations; this technique accounts for right-censored data that are created from having a study cutoff year of 2003 (Allison, 1995). Data on the 93 organizations for the study period from 1992 to 2003 were first transformed into annual spells, yielding a data set with 758 organizationyears. All independent variables were time-dependent and lagged in our event-history analysis models.

Organizations stayed in the data set until year of adoption; organizations that did not adopt B2C by 2003 were in the data set for the entirety of the study period.

We segmented the data into two sub-periods and used a piecewise specification to examine the predictors in two different time periods based on the shifting environments. The sub-periods, that is 1992–1997 and 1998–2003, were constructed based on events in the environments related to B2C. The start of the first sub-period was set as 1992 based events such as the invention of the WWW and Web browsers. The start of the second sub-period was set as

1998 based several events until 1997, including the standardization of the WWW protocols such as HTML and the market reaction to amazon.com’s IPO. (For comparison purposes, we also used a complementary log–log model to examine the various predictors without any sub-periods.)

## Results

The hazard plot (Figure 2) indicates that organizations did not start to adopt B2C until 1994. In general, the risk of B2C adoption increased each year until 2000, and decreased subsequently. However, the slope of the hazard plot after 1997 was substantially steeper than before 1997. This pattern is consistent with our argument for establishing these periods: the moderate increase until 1996 followed the invention of the WWW in 1991and the creation of Web browsers in the early 1990s. The subsequent steeper increase until 2000 corresponded to the time the WWW protocols were standardized in 1997 and the ongoing reduction in the US government’s role in the Internet in the mid-1990s, signaling increasing legitimacy/acceptance and commercialization of the Web. The decrease in 2001 is most likely associated with the dot-com bust in 2000.

The non-periodized model was significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } =$ 72.73, Po0.001) and showed that of the organizational factors, innovation-centrism was positively related to B2C adoption. Further, institutional pressures (bandwagon imitation, social learning imitation, senior IS executives, and business norms) were positively related to B2C adoption.

The dummy variable representing the late period was significant indicating that the segmented periods in the analysis were quite distinct and that the developments in the environment did indeed matter for B2C adoption. During the early period, the organizational factors were not significant, providing little support for Hypothesis 5 and only the senior IS executives was significant for B2C adoption. During the late period, customer-centric espoused values, industry norms, and bandwagon imitation were significant. These results provide support for Hypotheses 7 and 8. Thus, both organizational factors and institutional pressures influenced adoption in the late period. Hypothesis 11, which suggested that institutional factors would be influential in the late period, was supported.

Although both non-periodized and periodized models show that organizational and institutional factors influence B2C adoption (in answer to our first research question), the periodized model shows the effects of organizational and institutional factors vary over time in conjunction with the shifting environments (in answer to our second research question). Normative pressures (senior IS executives) were significant in the earlier period whereas mimetic pressures (bandwagon) and normative pressures (business norms) were influential in the later period. Similarly, customercentric espoused values were significant in both models; however, the two-period model shows that it was significant only in the later period. The remainder of our discussion is based on the findings of the periodized model.

## Discussion

The findings of our study show that organizations exhibited different behaviors with respect to B2C under shifting environments; we found that shifts in the environment created the conditions for organizations adopting B2C. Consistent with our expectations, institutional factors influenced B2C adoption in the late period. More specifically, normative pressures (represented by business norms) and bandwagon mimetic pressures influenced B2C adoption during the late period. Contrary to our expectations, however, none of the organizational factors were significant during the early period. We also found that customer-centric espoused values were influential during the late period and normative pressures (represented by senior IS executives) were significant during the early period.

The type of normative pressures was different under shifting environments. Although senior IS executives were significant in the early period but not in the late period, business norms were significant in the late period but not in the early period. That is, the expert knowledge of senior IS executives is particularly important during the early stages when the innovation is still new and is not well understood, and business norms surrounding the innovation are virtually non-existent. However, in the later stages, when the characteristics and capabilities of the new innovation are better understood, and the innovation has gained some legitimacy, business norms regarding the innovation that are portrayed in environment are sufficient to move organizations to adopt innovations.

Bandwagon mimetic pressures were significant predictors of B2C adoption in the late period, but not in the early period. The increasing number of adopters among peer organizations over time signals the growing acceptance of a practice. In the context of B2C, as adoption expanded among peer organizations, focal organizations tended to adopt so that they too would appear legitimate by engaging in industry-appropriate practices. Moreover, the significance of bandwagon mimetic pressures, but not statusdriven or social learning mimetic pressures, indicates that organizations may not have adopted B2C innovations as mindfully as may be possible (Kraatz, 1998). The nonsignificance of status-driven mimetic pressures indicates that organizations did not model themselves on more prominent organizations but rather ‘jumped’ on the B2C bandwagon nevertheless. The non-significance of social learning mimetic pressures, in part, may be due to the nonavailability of performance-related information on B2C adoption by others.

Customer-centric espoused values were significant in the late period and not in the early period. This may be because innovations frequently take time to gain acceptance, and a tight link between B2C and customer service was not necessarily achieved during the early period. Both organizations and consumers may have struggled with B2C early on – organizations may have had difficulty considering B2C as a replacement for their physical locations and consumers may have missed the richness of the physical locations. Therefore, even when organizations claimed to value customer service, they may have had difficulty appreciating the customer-service dimension of B2C. Over time, both organizations and consumers quite possibly recognized the value-added capabilities of B2C innovations, which influenced adoption.

Organization resources, slack as well as capital, were not significant in either of the two periods. This is somewhat surprising as it implies that organizations did not rely much on resources in adopting B2C. Two potential explanations are possible. First, organizations may have gone with a ‘skunk-works’ approach to B2C, especially in the early period when B2C was still very much in the experimental stages. Thus, organizations may not have relied on resources explicitly. Second, B2C innovations (that incorporated the online ordering functionality and hence may be considered as transaction-based systems) are really a natural extension of the previous generation of Web-based systems (that introduced the company and its products to consumers and hence may be considered as information-based systems) (Earl, 2000). Thus, organizations may have already allocated resources to the Webbased initiatives even before they considered B2C.

## Implications for theory

Deviating from the universalistic views of innovation adoption prevalent in prior research, our study recognized and explicitly modeled shifting environments that shape organizational responses to innovations. In doing this, we highlight two characteristics of the antecedents that are typically hidden in prior research.

First, organizational and institutional factors – our antecedents – showed varying influences on adoption over time. Second, contrary to the simultaneous influence of antecedents as portrayed in prior research that predominantly used cross-sectional designs, our study showed that there may be a sequential pattern of influence on adoption of innovations. For instance, senior IS executives, embodying professional norms and knowledge, initially were influential in adoption. IS executives can provide tailored information or advice about how the innovation will be beneficial to their particular organization. Given the uncertainty about the value of the innovation, generally, the IS executive is in a good position, as an insider, to sift through nascent information in the environment to judge whether the innovation is suitable given his/her company’s situation. Following neoinstitutional theory’s proposition that organizational factors should be significant early in the institutionalization process, IS executives, acting as boundary spanners between the organization and the environment, were influential resources in moving an organization toward early adoption. However, as the innovation started to gain broader acceptance, more diffuse pressures, such as positive portrayals in the press and the growing incidence of adoption by industry peers, were sufficient to influence adoption. Similarly, customer-centrism was not influential for adoption in the early period, indicating that organizations did not adopt B2C even when they espoused customer-centric values. Rather, organizations waited until there was some evidence that B2C was regarded as a tool that enhanced customer service, as indicated by the significance of customer-centrism in the late period. Thus, different factors mattered at different points in time and the ordering of the sequence may be significant.

Finally, our study demonstrates how factual measures (gathered from secondary data sources) may be used for operationalizing research variables. In contrast to prior literature that has predominantly relied on perceptual measures (Table 1), our study allows for a more objectified view of the phenomenon of innovation adoption.

## Implications for practice

Our research also has implications for practice. First, our findings suggest that early adopters had an advantage in that they had the opportunity to evaluate the innovation for themselves, to see how it fit with their own strategies, and help formalize their industry’s business norms. Late adopters, however, may not have such opportunities for evaluation but rather model their behaviors on the business norms already established (with some amount of effort from early adopters), even somewhat ‘blindly’ (as indicated by the significance of bandwagon mimetic pressures but not status-driven or social learning mimetic pressures). Second, our findings show that the rate of B2C adoption in the late period subsequent to the introduction of HTML standards and market response to amazon.com was considerably higher than the rate of adoption in the early period. That is, late adopters probably had an easier time adopting B2C since standards and practices had become commonplace as B2C matured whereas early adopters had to experiment with the innovation and determine ways in which they could effectively use nascent innovations.

Finally, our findings demonstrate the importance of the senior IS executives in adopting innovations, especially during the early periods when the innovation is still viewed with much uncertainty. The position of the senior IS executives would seem momentous under uncertain environments. With their specialized knowledge, they are able to interpret new information and influence organizations toward particular behaviors and actions. Our results suggest that organizations with IS professionals in senior decision-making positions are more likely to be early adopters of Type III innovations which can affect the strategic trajectory of the organization. Furthermore, for organizations that desire to integrate the newest technologies into their operations and become leaders in their industries, installing an IS professional into the strategic ranks of the organization would facilitate that status.

## Limitations

The findings should be interpreted in the light of the limitations of our study. First, our study was based on archival data from several secondary sources such as company annual reports, company press releases, Compustat databases, etc., and we had no control over the quality of the data available from such sources. However, we did take precautions to mitigate potential biases by employing at least two researchers to code relevant data and conducting inter-rater reliability verifications. Second, the use of secondary data also means that the findings are not based on data provided firsthand by the organizations in our sample. As a result, our interpretations of an organization’s peers (for computing mimetic pressures) may not mirror the organization’s own beliefs. However, we made reasonable assumptions such as the peers competing in the same industry as the focal organization in computing mimetic pressures.

## Directions for future research

Our research provides preliminary evidence on modeling environmental shifts using longitudinal analysis. More longitudinal research, especially ones that involve environmental shifts, would be required to validate the findings of our study. Such longitudinal designs would be useful for examining innovation adoption since not all organizations become adopters in a relatively short period of time and not all environments remain stable over time. In particular, investigations of adoption of the newest technological innovations in organizations, such as Web 2.0 and Web 3.0, could inform our conclusions about factors that affect adoption patterns over time and would expand the generalizability of our findings.

Another avenue for future research would be to explicitly consider the roles of senior executives from other functions or divisions within the organization. Our study examined the role of only the senior IS executives considering that B2C innovations required IS expertise. However, it is possible that the strategies for B2C initiatives are not the sole purview of the senior IS executives, but perhaps also senior marketing, advertising, and sales executives.

## Conclusions

This study argues the importance of considering both organizational and institutional factors in understanding innovation adoption by organizations. It also demonstrates the necessity for explicitly considering the shifting environments in theorizing about innovation adoption. Integrating these considerations into the research on technology adoption will improve our understanding of the timing of adoption and why organizations incorporate these innovations into their practices.

## About the authors

Anand Jeyaraj is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Information Systems and Operations Management at the Raj Soin College of Business. His research interests include the adoption, use, and discontinuance of information systems and practices; diffusion of information systems innovations within social networks; information systems success; impacts of information systems; and information systems development methodologies. His research has appeared in journals such as Management Science, Information & Organization, and Communications of the ACM. He served as the Program Chair for the 2008 Diffusion Interest Group in Information Technology (DI-GIT) workshop.

Deborah B Balser is an Associate Professor of Business Administration and Public Policy Administration. She received her Ph.D. from Cornell University in Industrial Relations. Her research interests include workplace conflict, disability issues in the workplace, and non-profit and social movement organizations.

Charles Chowa is a Ph.D. candidate in the School of Business at the University of Missouri in Saint Louis. His research has appeared in several academic journals and conference proceedings. IS alignment, knowledge creation, and electronic commerce are his main research interests. He is currently a visiting Assistant Professor in the Bryan School of Business at the University of North Carolina in Greensboro.

Gary M Griggs is an active duty Army Officer for the past 26 years. He has served in numerous automation assignments throughout his Army tenure and currently is serving as the Chief Information Officer for the United States Military Academy at West Point, New York.
