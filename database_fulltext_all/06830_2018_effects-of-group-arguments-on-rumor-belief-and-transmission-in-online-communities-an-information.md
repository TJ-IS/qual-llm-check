# Effects of Group Arguments on Rumor Belief and Transmission in Online Communities: An Information Cascade and Group Polarization Perspective

## ABSTRACT

The proliferation of the Internet and online communities has greatly increased the number and transmission speed of online rumors. To take more effective measures to counter the negative effects of these rumors, researchers and companies should understand the underlying mechanism of online rumor transmission. Therefore, this study takes an early initiative by focusing on users’ arguments in online discussion forums on the rumors and their effects on an individual’s belief in rumor and belief change, which is guided by the informational cascade and group polarization theories. In turn, belief and belief change on the online rumor lead to intention to spread the rumor. A lab experiment was conducted to test research hypotheses, which support most of the key hypotheses. The results, implications, limitations of the study, and conclusion are presented.

Online rumor, group arguments, informational cascade, group polarization, rumor transmission intention

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The number of global online users hit a dramatic number of three billion<sup>1</sup>, and a large percentage of them have been frequently participating in various types of online communities, such as discussion forum or social media sites, to post and exchange their opinions or comments on particular issues, products, or organizations [1]. However, the anonymity and openness of the Internet provides users with opportunities to comment in an unrestricted way, which potentially can propagate numerous negative online rumors. For instance, there was rumor that KFC in China supplied mutated chicken to customers, which aroused anger among customers and made KFC sue the Chinese companies for spreading the rumor. Other famous companies such as Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble, and Starbucks have constantly been the targets of online rumors about harmful ingredients in the products or misconducts of the company<sup>2</sup>. For luxury brands, an online rumor accusing Michael Kors of skinning dogs alive and transforming them into luxury handbags originated from an US petition website and later rapidly transmitted to the global market3. Online rumor can cause detrimental financial harms in some cases. The rumor of a start-up company LeEco was reported to be lying off more than 1,000 employees due to fast expansion and cash crunch4. After the rumor spread at famous social network sites, LeEco’s stock price immediately dropped by 9.9% per share. Hot debate about the recent rumors that some leading Chinese companies are moving their manufacturing facilities out of mainland China to other countries with lower costs also hurts the reputation of these companies and their executives that people criticize them to be unpatriotic.

Beyond the commercial field, other civil and political events are frequently affected by online rumors as well. During the presidential election held in 2016, numerous rumors about the negative reports of the two candidates (Donald Trump and Hilary Clinton) appeared in online news websites, online communities, and social network sites. New rumors emerged frequently throughout the entire presidential campaigns, and these rumors were quickly spread across various online communities such as famous social media websites (e.g., Facebook). These rumors were rapidly transmitted in the entire community or network when users believed they were true and thus followed these rumors and even reposted them in their personal pages. Analysts claimed that these rumors might significantly affect individual voters’ opinions and their votes, leading to controversial voting result. Though many of them proved to be fake news afterwards, these rumors caused detrimental impacts on the target persons and misled the judgment of the common public.

In these cases, after the rumor appears on online forums, continuous commenting, questioning, and addition of so-called evidence increasingly attract Internet users to participate in the discussion and rumor spreading. As a result, more and more people pay attention to the rumor. Some, irritated by the negative points contained in the rumor, comment on it emotionally and transmit it further online, which evolves into reprimanding the target organization [2]. It is obvious that the impact of online rumors on individuals is vastly different from traditional word-of-mouth dissemination of information. Because of the ease to post comments online and the difficulty for readers to verify the comments, individuals are highly likely to be influenced by various viewpoints. One of the unique characteristics of online rumors is that they involve arguments from a large audience that is visible to other users. In other words, users are exposed to both the online rumor itself and its related discussion posts by other users. Nevertheless, online rumors are a relatively new phenomenon, so there is a lack of literature that systematically investigates the impact of online rumors and arguments about online rumors. This lack of research raised our research question: How do Internet users’ group arguments in online communities affect a user’s responses to online rumors, including the belief and transmission behavior?

Previous research suggests that rumor transmission results from the thematic importance of an incident and its ambiguity [3] and that the main route of transmission is word of mouth [4]. Most studies mainly focused on individual perspective to discuss the influential factors or motivation of rumor transmission [5], such as credulity, affective commitment, outcome-relevant involvement [6], or other psychological factors [3, 7]. In contrast to traditional rumor, due to the anonymity, openness, and rich information presented in online communities, the online rumor transmission mechanism has changed fundamentally into a kind of social interaction on a group basis [7-9]. Group arguments for the rumor, which refer to the collective comments made by all participants in the online community, may greatly affect the rumor transmission scale and influence. Although some researchers are conscious of the influence of group arguments and study them with content analysis [8, 10], the arguments have not been scrutinized empirically.

In this study, we believe that group arguments influence users’ rumor responses through the mechanisms of information cascade and group polarization effect. First, the anonymity of networks makes it difficult for users to confirm the source reliability. Without enough information, users usually judge by observing and following others’ behavior, which is the information cascade effect [11]. Second, due to the assimilation of bias, users can find peers with common opinions through discussions in online communities, where they can try to influence or refute those with different opinions. During the process of group expansion and thought exchange, the opinions of participants in the discussion tend to develop toward a particular direction, whether supportive or unsupportive. Many opinions converge to be more or less consistent and evolve into an extreme circumstance in which the majority of participants share the same point of view, which leads to group polarization [12, 13].

Theoretically, this research contributes to the understanding of antecedents, mechanisms, and outcomes of group argument on users’ beliefs and behavior toward online rumor and its transmission. It not only enriches existing literature and theory on rumor but also has significant practical implications for corporate online marketing, public relations marketing, and public policy making. By understanding how group arguments function in rumor transmission, companies can implement measures to prevent or handle online rumors effectively.

The rest of the article will be organized as follows. First, we present the literature and theories on rumor and rumor transmission. Second, we develop a research model and hypotheses on the effects of group arguments on online rumor transmission. Third, we talk about the research methodology and data analysis of this study. Last, the discussion of results, contributions, and limitations of this study are presented with the conclusions.

## 2. LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Defining Rumor

For the definition and connotation of rumor, scholars have different understandings. Allport and Postman (1947) provide one of the early definitions, stating that a rumor is “a specific (or topical) proposition for belief, passed along from person to person, usually by word-of-mouth, without secure standards of evidence being present” [14]. Later, rumor was defined as a proposition for belief of topical reference disseminated without official verifications [4]. According to Shibutani [15], rumor is a community's construction of a definition of a situation in uncertain circumstances, that is, improvised news. He conceptualized rumor as a collective problem-solving procedure. It can be considered as the product of collective efforts to interpret a problematic situation [16] rather than a process of detail distortion [14]. Rumor itself involves a communication between public and individuals [17], which seems to be a group behavior in either individual or social events [18]. Furthermore, rumor discourse has been conceptualized as an attempt to reduce anxiety and uncertainty via a process of social sense-making [8]. It is a rich conversation, involving an exchange of ideas, opinions, and viewpoints, which is much more obvious on the Internet. Thus, it can also be traced [7, 8], serving as a basis for our study.

## 2.2 Research on Traditional Rumor Transmission—From an Individual’s Perspective

Many researchers in psychology and sociology find that the reliability of the source of rumor, the rumor involvement, and the psychological factors such as anxiety and uncertainty are the key factors that make people believe and propagate a rumor [3, 7, 19].

First, researchers have indicated that the source reliability of a rumor has a strong positive correlation with its transmission [7]. A rumor is more frequently repeated when the story behind it is believable instead of unbelievable [20, 21]. For example, the more that people think a rumor is true, the more likely they will be to spread the rumor [21]. Second, when involvement is high, a rumor may be spread regardless of what people believe; conversely, when involvement is low, belief itself may be insufficient to motivate

#

transmission [21]. Although intriguing, the interaction between belief and involvement on rumor transmission has yet to be fully confirmed [22]. For example, Rownow [21] suggested that the effects of belief on rumor transmission may be moderated by involvement. Similarly, it is found that the importance of the rumor moderates the relationship between initial belief and rumor transmission [23]. Lastly, some researchers believe that the transmission of rumor was due to people’s anxiety and uncertainty [7]. Esposito [24] found that situational anxiety (i.e., anxiety about the general environment in which the rumor is produced) instead of rumor anxiety (i.e., anxiety about the rumor content and consequences) influenced rumor transmission concerning a mass transit strike. Walker and Beckerle [25] showed that manipulated increases in situational anxiety led to greater transmission regardless of the characteristics of the rumor. However, it is also found that there was no obvious correlation between people’s anxiety and rumor transmission. Instead, anxiety increased the impact of belief on rumor transmission [22].

Overall, most research has studied the factors that influence the transmission of rumor in a traditional individual way. Online communities—because of their anonymity, openness, and ability to support asynchronous transmission—have grown to be a hotbed of rumor. Therefore, the rumor transmission through online communities tends to be a process of group activity, which means the mechanism of rumor transmission has also changed in our context [7-9].

## 2.3 Research on Online Rumor Transmission—From a Group’s Perspective

Traditionally, it is difficult to obtain immediate information about a rumor’s transmission in the offline environment. In contrast, the Internet brings about new opportunities for researchers to track the details of a rumor’s transmission, which greatly supports in-depth research on the issue.

For instance, research shows that source reliability can affect the transmission of rumor in online communities, which are moderated by involvement [6]. However, the mechanisms of online rumor transmission may not be fully illustrated because anonymity makes it hard to identify the identity of the rumor transmitters, which leaves few clues for readers to justify the reliability of the information. Instead, people have to judge and act by observing others’ behaviors, which may lead to the conformity effect [26]. This phenomenon is common on the Internet and has been verified as a type of herding behavior, such as when people buy books online by reviewing sales history and the comments of other customers [27, 28]. Therefore, the conformity effect may also lead to rapid transmission of rumors on the Internet. Similarly, based on dynamic social impact theory, recent studies show that people’s focus on particular rumors and belief polarization (i.e., increased confidence in believing the truth of the rumor) can be strengthened if they belong to the same social network clusters [29]. People’s thoughts about the rumor can be affected by in-group peers who hold consistent opinions about the rumor and thus lead to consensus about the rumor within the group [30].

Some studies are based on analyzing the content of the rumor. For example, in online discussion groups [10], rumor transmission as a collective, problem-solving interaction is sustained by a combination of anxiety, uncertainty, and credibility [3]. Rumor transmission is not a passive retelling of a narrative [8]. Instead, it is a rich conversation involving an exchange of ideas, opinions, and viewpoints. People attempt to persuade others to believe or disbelieve the rumor by providing information that confirms or

#

disconfirms the rumor. In online communities, users’ ability for selective information filtering can make a rumor attractive to people with similar viewpoints, which originated from their attitudes toward an event or an object (e.g., the subject of the rumor). By holding similar opinions, whether positive or negative, people will naturally get together in the form of an opinion group that supports a particular stance. Through further discussion, they will strengthen the original viewpoints that lead to polarized opinions [31]. Particularly, Internet users are more likely to display the tendency of group polarization, since the rumor spread in online communities may be more ambiguous in its credibility. As a result, users will rely heavily on the attitude of others in making judgments. In fact, the likelihood of group polarization occurring online is reported to be twice that of being formed in an offline environment [31].

We conclude from the above that due to the anonymity of online communities, online users prefer to judge the rumor by observing others’ behaviors. When more and more people tend to believe a rumor, others are likely to follow. This herding effect emerges as a reflection of informational cascade that makes people’s opinions increasingly consistent. Moreover, the viewpoints discussed by a group will be strengthened, which leads to the group polarization effect.

## 2.4 Theories of Online Rumor Transmission Mechanism

Inspired by previous research on the mechanisms of online rumor transmission, we find it highly possible that in online forums, users will leverage information and beliefs delivered by other users in peer interactions in the forum, thereby guiding the formation of their own attitudes and beliefs toward the rumor in question. In turn, the continuous accumulation of similar viewpoints from numerous online forum participants establishes a powerful source of attraction to new viewers of the comments, which can result in more people believing the arguments and standpoints. Over time, one particular direction of the comments supporting or opposing the rumor will prevail. The description of such online rumor transmission through the commenting contributions of users of online forums highlights the two most relevant theoretical streams: information cascade and group polarization, which are adapted as the theoretical basis for our research development.

## 2.4.1 Informational Cascade

Proposed by [11] and [32], the theory of informational cascade, as a theory of explaining conformity effect, has been widely applied to explain individual behaviors for various areas including finance and information systems. According to informational cascade theory, when a person’s information is limited, he or she may make decisions based on the behavior of others. In an online forum, the collective opinion of participants can become an informational reference point for another user. Eventually, this could lead to a herding effect [27].

Informational cascades can be found in digital worlds, such as online auctions, in which numerous buyers tend to bid for products that already have bids while ignoring similar or more attractive products without any bids [33]. In other words, when individuals with private information make decisions in a sequential way and the sequential process of decisions is uncertain, it can lead to informational cascades [11]. This phenomenon is common in online communities because most users lack related information and therefore cannot thoroughly evaluate a rumor. As a result, the arguments presented by a

#

number of group members related to the rumor are relied on as a reference to an individual’s decision making, which forms the informational cascade effect [26].

In recent years, the theory of informational cascade is applied to online research. Take online books purchasing, for example. The sales volume of best-selling books tends to increase as individuals purchase them in response to established sales records [28, 34]. The informational cascade effect also has been empirically examined in the context of online software adoption [35]. It was found that online users’ choices of software products exhibited distinct jumps and drops with changes in download ranking. On the basis of these studies, an individual’s belief of an online rumor and transmission behavior can depend on the number of postings (i.e., arguments) by other online users who are involved in the rumor discussion and transmission.

## 2.4.2 Group Polarization

Moreover, online forum participants tend to hold supportive, opposing, or neutral opinions toward a particular rumor. These discussions and arguments among each participant, in turn, can affect an individual’s belief of the rumor on an ongoing basis. Group polarization is such a phenomenon, in which group discussion leads members to intensify their initial attitudes, resulting in more extreme decisions than the initial inclination of members [4]. In group polarization, group decisions tend to be more extreme than the initial inclination of group members [36].

In the extant literature, theoretical explanations of group polarization have evolved into two streams of theories: social comparison theory and persuasive arguments theory. Social comparison theory explains group polarization from a group perspective. People like to highlight their positions when their opinions are different from or even superior to others’ positions, so they are likely to continually process information about others’ opinions and adjust their own opinions. Similarly, dynamic social impact theory suggests that people’s opinions about rumor can be influenced by peers in the same social network clusters, and the group opinions will change dynamically toward reaching consensus on the rumor and stronger belief [29, 30]. Persuasive arguments theory presents an information-processing explanation for group polarization. It holds that group discussions cause individuals to shift their initial inclinations toward the direction indicated by other members’ arguments [37]. Some studies have also confirmed that social comparison and persuasive arguments may appear at the same time in group polarization [37, 38].

Since users communicate and discuss with each other anonymously online, group polarization exists and can become highly influential [13, 31, 39]. The anonymity of the Internet easily contributes to a number of provocative statements, which can lead to group polarization [40]. In online communities, people have no need to conform to the expectation of strangers when making decisions. Thus, informational influence exerts a stronger influence on users than normative influence [28]. According to persuasive arguments theory, group arguments in online communities can influence the polarization of users’ attitudes. With the continuous increase in the quantity and conformity of group arguments, users’ attitudes toward rumors likely will become more polarized.

## 3. RESEARCH MODEL AND HYPOTHESIS

## 3.1 Research Model

Rumor transmission in asynchronous interactive online environment, to a large extent, is a type of group activity. In this process, members of a temporary group exchanged ideas, opinions, and viewpoints about a rumor and attempted to persuade others to believe or disbelieve the rumor by providing confirmation or disconfirmation [7, 8]. Based on the literature review and theoretical guidance, a research model was proposed (Figure 1). In this model, users’ responses and behavior to the online rumor were measured by the intention to spread rumor, which is used in most research to measure rumor transmission behavior [7, 41]. We proposed that users’ intention to spread the rumor is influenced by the volume and consistency of group arguments, which are motivated by the two mechanisms of rumor transmission, namely informational cascades and group polarization. Through these two mechanisms, we aim to investigate the influences of other users’ arguments on the rumor on an individual’s belief and behavior. In other words, when users lack relevant information, they may simply follow the informational signals given by others to make a decision to believe the rumor or not, which is reflected in the construct of belief in rumor. Even the exchange of information during group discussion may affect preexisting beliefs that are built on the rumor, thus leading to the belief change after accessing the arguments of other users. We present the hypotheses and associated arguments as follows.

## 3.2 Arguments on Belief—the Effects of Informational Cascades

An informational cascade in rumor spreading is a learning process in which a user would observe volume and consistency of arguments and then adopt them without considering his/her own information [11, 32]. For example, software download ranking and user reviews have an impact on users’ follow-up decisions and choices [35]. User rating consistency can moderate the positive relationship between rating level and potential users’ purchase decisions, such that the relationship would be stronger for products with higher rating consistency [42]. When browsing rumors and feedback on online communities for our research, we learned that users are concerned about the rumor and its related arguments. A high volume of arguments implies that the rumor might be popular, raising interest among people and moving some to believe the rumor. In contrast, a low comment volume suggests that the rumor may not deserve wide attention, and its credibility likely will suffer. Second, if there are strong arguments supporting the messages delivered in the rumor, the informational cascade effect will cause users to

#

believe the rumor more than when they are exposed to less consistent arguments. Third, the effect of argument volume on beliefs is enhanced by argument consistency, which indicates a moderating effect. It means that when a large volume of arguments is available and consistently head in one direction (e.g., the supportive direction), a user will be more likely to follow the opinions of others (i.e., stronger informational cascade effect) and join them to form a larger group of people with the same viewpoint on the rumor (i.e., stronger group polarization effect). In contrast, a large group of users holding more diverse opinions can make it difficult for a user to judge which direction to take (i.e., to support or reject the rumor); thus, the user is less likely to form a belief on the rumor itself. Therefore, we propose that

Hypothesis 1. Other conditions being equal, users are more likely to believe online rumors with a large argument volume than rumors with a small volume of arguments.

Hypothesis 2. Other conditions being equal, users are more likely to believe online rumors with consistent supportive arguments than rumors with inconsistent support.

Hypothesis 3. Argument consistency moderates the positive relationship between argument volume and users’ belief in the online rumor, such that the relationship is stronger for rumors with consistent supportive arguments than for rumors with inconsistent support.

## 3.3 Arguments on Belief Change—The Effects of Group Polarization

According to the persuasive arguments theory, group discussion will cause an individual to modify his/her existing viewpoints. That is, others’ arguments may cause an individual to shift his/her attitude based on the strength and direction of group arguments [37]. Due to the anonymity in using the Internet, more users prefer to share their opinions and to propose novel and persuasive viewpoints. As a result, group polarization can easily form. For example, it is found that in group discussions in the form of computer-mediated communications, the group polarization effect is stronger than in a face-to-face setting [13] that the majority influence can make people follow the discussion of the crowd and lead group decision making toward groupthink [43] which refers to conformity to group values and ethics [44]. Anonymous group discussions allow group members to exert a strong influence on peers, to easily polarize opinions [12]. With the continuous increase in the quantity and conformity of group arguments, users’ attitudes toward rumors are polarized. This effect is even stronger when others’ opinions are consistent, which heavily influences users one type of users who may not believe the rumor to change their view and begin to believe the rumor and affects the other type of users who initially believe the rumor to increase the degree to which they believe it. For the first type of users, they do not believe the rumor very much after reading the news article. But a large volume of highly consistent arguments from other users supporting the truthfulness of the rumor may bring in a new perspective for them to think about their original opinion for a second time in order to be part of the group. To change from disbelieving to believing the rumor, a user needs to go through a more comprehensive and systematic information process to reconstruct the thoughts [45]. For the second type of users, the change in belief in the rumor is less extreme but is a matter of degree, i.e., believing the rumor more after reading the arguments from other users. To achieve this kind of belief change, a user only needs sufficient information that can support or confirm his/her original opinion (e.g., using the heuristic information processing strategy) and thus do not need to engage in

#

substantial information processing to completely change the belief [45]. The polarization effect is thus strengthened.

In this study, we first propose that when there is a higher volume of supporting arguments from other users, a user may be more likely to support the rumor in order to join the larger force of peers. The higher the volume of the supporting arguments, the more likely a user will change the original belief, even from a disbelieving to a believing state. Second, more consistent arguments supporting the rumor (i.e., fewer opposing views among the entire set of arguments) can help a user confirm his/her supportive opinion more easily because there are fewer influences from conflicting viewpoints. When most of the arguments from other users are supporting the truthfulness of the rumor, the group polarization effect emerges by engaging more users to enhance the belief on the rumor. For a user who did not quite believe the rumor in the beginning, more consistent supporting arguments are strong reasons for him/her to reconsider the original belief and thus may change the mind after taking the arguments’ standpoints into account. For a user who initially believes the rumor, the more consistent arguments are valid information to confirm the original thoughts. In both cases, more consistent arguments supporting the truthfulness of the rumor can lead to change of belief on the rumor. Third, greater argument consistency can strengthen the effect of argument volume on a user’s change toward believing the rumor more. When a larger volume of arguments supporting the rumor is true, the trustworthiness of the rumor increases, motivating a user to change from disbelieving to believing the rumor or believing the rumor to a greater extent. Hence, we propose that

Hypothesis 4. Other conditions being equal, users are more likely to believing the online rumor more when the volume of other users’ arguments is higher than when it is lower.

Hypothesis 5. Other conditions being equal, users are more likely to believing the online rumor more when other users’ arguments are more consistent in supporting the rumor than when it is more diverse.

Hypothesis 6. Argument consistency will moderate the positive relationship between arguments volume and change in beliefs about the online rumor, such that the relationship will be stronger for rumor with high consistent arguments supporting the rumor.

## 3.4 Effects of Belief and Belief Change on Intention to Spread Rumor

Many researchers have found that belief in rumor is significantly correlated with transmission of rumors. A main effect of belief on rumor transmission has been found [5]. That people are more likely to spread a rumor they believe to be true than a rumor they believe is false [22]. When an individual’s belief about a rumor is high, he/she develops high anxiety about the situation and greater propensity to transmit rumor to seek confirmation or disconfirmation from experts or peers [20]. The impact of belief is even stronger for spreading negative than positive rumors [46], which is consistent with the focus of this study. In addition, if an individual’s initial belief in the rumor is changed by the arguments to support the rumor, the belief in the credibility of the rumor becomes even stronger, and it will enhance the propensity to spread the rumor. Thus, we hypothesize

Hypothesis 8. Users’ beliefs in online rumor are positively related to the intention to spread rumor.

Hypothesis 9. Argument-induced belief change is positively related to the intention to spread rumor.

## 4. METHODOLOGY

## 4.1 Experimental Design and Procedure

To verify the research hypotheses, we conducted a controlled lab experiment that employed a 2 (high argument volume vs. low argument volume) ×2 (high argument consistency vs. low argument consistency) between subject design. Participants were recruited from a local public university in a major city of Eastern China. To reward their participation, the students received \$3 in US cash. Their average age was 20.4 years, and all have experience using the Internet. Of the 240 participants, about 40% were females and 60% were males.

All the participants were randomly assigned to one of four experimental conditions by a computer program when they arrived at a computer lab to participate in the experiment. They were given an instructional booklet and were told that they were participating in a study that requires them to read an article about a company’s rumor about its behavior of polluting the environment leading to its bankruptcy posted on an online discussion forum, including users’ arguments on the story, and then to offer their own opinions and make related decisions. The participants first read the article containing rumor only on a discussion forum and reported their belief on the rumor, as well as answered several demographic questions. After a one-minute break, the participants were required to read arguments of various volumes and consistencies and then to report their belief and rumor spread intention in a follow-up survey questionnaire. Responses were recorded for further analysis.

## 4.2 Stimuli

To avoid any ethics issue that may be caused by the rumor that we used in our study, we created a fictitious paper manufacturing company as the target of the online rumor. However, all of the stimuli of the experiment, including the misbehavior and bankruptcy rumor and the arguments, were collected from actual online communities. To sort the various arguments, we recruited five judges (undergraduate and graduate students and faculty members) in the electronic business major to categorize them into different directions. First, after we omitted arguments with no substantial content (such as reply of “support”), the five judges used a five-Likert scale to answer the belief of each argument on the rumor (from strongly disagree to strongly agree). Second, we calculated the average mark each argument received and categorized it into either supportive argument or unsupportive argument, based on the benchmark of three points in the five-Likert scale. Third, we chose 40 arguments vs. 10 arguments to represent high vs. low argument condition and 9:1 vs. 5:5 for the proportion of supportive vs. unsupportive arguments as a measure of argument consistency. In addition, to reduce the order effect of arguments, we randomly displayed all the arguments for each participant with all the information of the same argument unchanged. That is, both supportive and unsupportive arguments are mixed and shown in a random order so that each participant read all the arguments but in different orders.

## 4.3 Measures

The online questionnaire administered to participants adopted established items and scales from [47]. This study measured three dependent variables: belief in online rumor, belief change in online rumor, and intention to spread online rumor. Participants’ beliefs in the Internet were captured by three items: (1) “Do you believe that the paper manufacturing company applied for bankruptcy?” (1 = definitely not to ${ 7 } = \mathrm { d e f i n i t e l y }$ ${ \mathrm { y e s } } ) ; ( 2 )$ “How much confidence do you place in the truth of the paper manufacturing company’s applying for bankruptcy?” (1 = no confidence at all to $7 = \mathrm { v e r y }$ confident); (3) “How believable do you find the paper manufacturing company’s applying for bankruptcy?” (1 = not at all to $7 = \mathrm { { t o t a l } }$ believable). These items were measured twice after participants were exposed to rumor only and to rumor with arguments. The three items were averaged to form belief in rumor-only condition (Cronbach’s ${ \mathfrak { a } } = . 9 1 )$ and belief in rumor with arguments condition (Cronbach’s ${ \mathfrak { a } } = . 9 5 )$ . The later belief scores were then subtracted from the earlier belief scores to form a measure of belief change in rumor. To measure participant’s intentions to spread the rumor, they were asked, “How many people are you going to tell about the paper manufacturing company’s applying for bankruptcy?” (1 = nobody to 7 = everybody I know and talk to).

## 4.4 Control and Manipulation Checks

Several F-tests found no significant differences for participants in the four treatments in terms of gender, age, and Internet-usage experience. Thus, control over subject characteristics through randomization was successful.

Two questions assessed the manipulation of argument volume and consistency: “How much of the posts do you remember?” (1 = 1–10 posts, $2 = 1 1 - 2 0 , 3 = 2 1 - 3 0 , 4 = 3 1 - 4 0$ 5 = more than 40) and “The ratio of posts believing the paper manufacturing company’s applying for bankruptcy to the whole is?” (1 = extremely low to 5 = extremely high). Participants in the high argument volume or consistency settings agreed on the questions significantly more than participants in the low argument volume or consistency settings (MHV = 3.66 vs. MLV = 1.89, p < 0.001 and MHC = 4.31, MLC = 2.99, $\mathrm { \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 }$ respectively). The experimental manipulation was successful.

## 7. DISCUSSION, IMPLICATIONS, AND CONCLUSION

As an early initiative in the online rumor research field, this study focuses on the effects of arguments in online forums on a user’s belief in the rumor, his/her belief change in terms of the rumor’s truthfulness, and the influences on his/her intention to spread the rumor. This perspective particularly emphasizes the unique characteristics of an online rumor and its transmission. Other users’ arguments in online forums are critical to the belief and decision of an individual user. Based on a lab experiment that utilized a real rumor and manipulated the presentation of online arguments, we found significant main effects and interaction effect between argument volume and consistency on rumor belief and belief change. Argument volume had positive relationship with belief in rumor and belief change, while argument consistency had positive relationship with belief change. When argument consistency was high, the volume of arguments had stronger positive relationship with user belief in the rumor as well as user belief change. In turn, both the belief in rumor and argument-induced belief change positively influenced users intentions to spread the rumor. The only insignificant effect of argument volume on belief change implies that the amount of user discussion and reply may not be enough to change an individual’s thoughts about the rumor, but the content of the discussion (i.e., argument consistency) will determine their belief change.

By investigating a salient social phenomenon of online rumor and its transmission, this study is able to contribute to the literature, theoretical development, and practices in several ways. First, this study serves as an initiative to focus on the effects of user arguments about online rumors in online forums. In particular, we theorize the two prominent antecedents to user belief in rumor and belief change, which are argument volume and argument consistency. The findings of our study enrich our understanding of the critical drivers of users’ responses toward the online rumor, that is, the arguments of the rumor rather than the rumor itself. This view well reflected the unique characteristics of online rumors and forms a theoretical basis of rumor transmission mechanisms. Second, this study found unique mechanisms of how informational cascade and group polarization affect individual performances by linking online rumor characteristics (i.e., argument volume and consistency) with individual thoughts and behavior (i.e., belief, belief change, and transmission). The results enhance our understanding of the theories and extend the boundaries of the theories’ application that both argument volume and consistency can lead to greater belief in rumor and/or belief change. Argument consistency can further exaggerate the effects of argument volume, suggesting that information cascade and group polarization effects can occur at the same time and strengthen one another. Third, by applying the experimental approach, we were able to capture the realistic online rumor transmission process, which is also guided by theories. Individuals’ beliefs and belief changes are found critical to their rumor transmission intention, which further strengthen the relevance and significance of online arguments in the online rumor transmission process. Overall, this study contributes to the development of the online rumor literature, the theories of online rumor transmission mechanisms, and the methodologies appropriate to exploring this kind of phenomenon.

From a practical perspective, this study can provide guidance to companies on how to monitor and intervene in users’ online discussions and transmission of company-related rumors. Overall, our findings show that to stop the rumor from spreading to more users, practitioners should try to make users disbelieve the rumor or prevent them from changing their mind from disbelieving to believing the rumor. With this general guideline in mind, more measures need to be considered to reduce the belief in rumor or belief change. First of all, companies should always be cautious about any potential rumors or false information released at any online communities in the very beginning before users start to pay attention, talk about them, and even spread them. This concurs with our findings that by preventing the increase of higher argument volume, companies will be able to reduce users’ beliefs in the rumor. Especially in online forums, companies should take proactive actions by participating in the discussions about their products or businesses and interact with users to resolve their problems and questions. With their doubts being addressed, users are less likely to participate in the online forum discussion when rumor appears and post more arguments about the rumor in the future, thus reducing the possibility of high rumor-related argument volume in affecting other users’ beliefs. Second, after detecting any online rumor, companies should take immediate measures, such as disclosing the truth online or explaining to users who have already been exposed to the rumor and have concerns, to prevent the rumor from being discussed among more users as early as possible. Moreover, online rumors that have been discussed among a large number of users who have highly consistent opinions should be paid most attention. It shows that the rumor may have been spread very widely and believed by a majority of the many users. The results can be detrimental to companies because these users may further spread the rumor to more people and make other users who did not believe the rumor to change their mind. According to our findings, highly consistent arguments on believing the rumor can augment the impact of a large volume of arguments to make more users believe the rumor or change their mind to believe the rumor. Thus, if companies find that the scope of rumor transmission has reached a certain level that is out of their control, they should take legal actions to stop the rumor transmission and clarify the truth to the public in mass media instead of only clarifying it among a relatively small amount of users in the online forum only. Third, the results of this study can be further extended to other fields such as governmental services or social activities and other online platforms such as social media so that online rumors harmful to national security or societal stability can be effectively managed.

#

Some limitations of this study also motivate us to continue the exploration on the online rumor phenomenon. First, future research can consider the role of a company’s action responding to the rumor and online users’ arguments in affecting the rumor transmission. For example, early intervention of a company (e.g., clarify the truth when rumor emerges) to stop the argument volume from increasing in online communities may be more effective than later intervention (e.g., massively communicate the truth in public media) to prevent more users from believing the rumor when there already exists a large number of arguments with a high degree of consistency. Second, we used a rumor of a company and tested the effects among university students. It is possible that the rumor may not be very relevant to some of the students. Previous literature has found that group membership is a critical factor affecting the probability of the rumor being transmitted that people sharing similar social identities are more likely to transmit rumor among each other [49]. Thus, examining the rumor transmission patterns among a community in which users have a variety of different demographics and background will be helpful for detecting the impacts of social identities of online users on rumor transmission. The arguments from some more proactive opinion leaders who have high identification with a company or a brand should be more influential in affecting other regular users’ beliefs in the rumor and their rumor transmission behavior. Third, the structure of technological network and social network may affect the patterns of rumor transmission. For example, rumor may be spread in different directions or with different pace at online discussion forums vs. microblogs, or among a group of people who are familiar with each other vs. a group of strangers. Since this study is from an individual user’s perspective, we encourage future research to address these issues by enriching the online rumor transmission mechanism and testing the research model in a different context. Fourth, the effect sizes of some statistic analysis results were not very high (ranging from small to medium level) which cautions any explanation that may overestimate the effects of the rumor-related argument number and consistency, as well as their interaction effects, on users’ belief in rumor and their belief change. Nevertheless, we treat the findings significant and meaningful in this study despite some small effect sizes by considering the guidelines of acceptable effect sizes that are generally accepted in social science research (Cohen 1988).

To conclude, the research lies in the phenomenon of rumor transmission in online discussion forums in China and examines its underlying mechanism based on informational cascade and group polarization theory. The findings highlight the influence of users’ arguments, their volume, and consistency on an individual’s belief or belief change in the rumor, which lead to rumor transmission. The findings contribute to both literature and practice, especially in the current Internet age, where rumors have an extraordinary impact on the healthy development of business and society.
