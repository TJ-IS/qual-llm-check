# DO DIGITAL PLATFORMS IMPROVE THE PERFORMANCE OF NONBINDING CONTRACTS? EVIDENCE FROM THE AMAZON FREIGHT PLATFORM<sup>1</sup>

Ali S. Babakan Department of MIS, Terry College of Business University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A. {ali.shirzadi@uga.edu}

He Li and William J. Kettinger Department of Management, Wilbur O. and Ann Powers College of Business Clemson University, Clemson, SC, U.S.A. {hl3@clemson.edu} {wkttngr@clemson.edu}

This research examines how digital platforms influence the performance of nonbinding contracts. Businesses in many industries with high uncertainty, such as trucking freight and construction, simultaneously use nonbinding contracts, which impose no legal sanctions for refusals, and spot markets, which facilitate real-time, flexible transactions with market-determined prices. Understanding the conditions under which nonbinding contracts perform is a major concern in these industries. Leveraging the entry of the Amazon Freight platform in the trucking freight industry, we demonstrate that adding a digital platform-enabled spot marketplace improves the performance of nonbinding contracts. We identified several mechanisms driving this effect: (1) expanding carriers’ transportation capacity, (2) lowering spot market prices, and (3) reducing shippers’ reliance on nonbinding contracts for shorterhaul truckloads. Moreover, the digital platform’s impact on enhancing nonbinding contract performance is particularly pronounced in markets with volatile demand and shorter hauls. This research contributes to understanding the impacts of digital platforms in highly uncertain industries that simultaneously use nonbinding contracts and spot markets. Our findings provide implications to policymakers and business managers on leveraging digital platforms to improve operational efficiency in highly uncertain industries

Keywords: Digital platforms, IT-enabled market design, nonbinding contract, spot market, trucking freight industry, digital freight platforms, tender rejection

## Introduction

In industries with high uncertainty, such as trucking freight, construction, and agriculture, business partners cannot precisely specify their future supply and demand to make an explicit contract. Consequently, they use nonbinding contracts (also known as relational or extralegal contracts) that establish a business relationship but are flexible on execution (D’Agostino & Lisciandra, 2018). While nonbinding contracts help secure business partners’ supply and demand, suppliers may refuse to fulfill contracted buyers’ requests due to unpredictable and volatile supply and demand and the high flexibility of nonbinding contracts. Hence, business partners cannot rely on nonbinding contracts alone to transact.

In response, these industries have evolved a unique, dualmarket structure for transactions—i.e., coexisting with both nonbinding contracts and spot markets (Hendrikse, 2006). Spot markets are external marketplaces that facilitate realtime, flexible transactions without a fixed-term contract based on prices determined by the spot market (Caplice, 2021) in order to fulfill businesses’ needs arising from extra supply or demand. However, this dual-market structure also enables suppliers’ opportunism across the two markets—for example, they can reject requests from buyers with whom they have nonbinding contracts without legal penalties when the spot market has better offers (Hendrikse, 2006; Scott et al., 2020). Hence, the performance of nonbinding contracts, i.e., the extent to which the contracted supplier fulfills the buyer’s service requests, becomes a bottleneck for these industries (Sahinyazan et al., 2021; Scott et al., 2017).

Consider the trucking freight industry as an example, which is economically significant<sup>2</sup> and critically links the U.S. economy because it connects to all industries making or shipping goods (Acocella et al., 2020). Shippers (buyers) and carriers (suppliers) transact using either a nonbinding contract or a spot market.<sup>3</sup> Specifically, shippers sign nonbinding contracts (typically annually) with primary carriers, which specify fixed rates for given trucking lanes but do not make any commitments regarding shippers’ load offers and carriers’ offer acceptances. Shippers send a truckload offer to their primary carrier holding a nonbinding contract. The carrier can accept or reject the offer without legal penalty. Carriers often reject a contracted shipper’s truckload (known as a tender rejection) when there is a lack of capacity or a better offer from either other contracted shippers or the spot market. If the contracted carrier rejects the load request, the shipper can identify a carrier in the spot market and pay the market rate that is “determined on a load-by-load basis” and is often higher than the contract rate (Acocella et al., 2020, p. 2). Given the flexibility of nonbinding contracts and the unpredictability of demand and supply, shippers constantly face the tender rejection problem (Scott et al., 2017).<sup>4</sup> Tender rejections not only negatively affect shippers but also indicate the failure of the current dominant design of freight transportation procurement (Caplice, 2021).

To improve the performance of nonbinding contracts, literature across academic fields suggests relational mechanisms for buyers, such as trust and the “shadow of the future,” in contract design and interfirm relationship governance (e.g., Carson et al., 2006; Gibbons et al., 2023; Kvaløy & Olsen, 2023; Scott et al., 2020). However, given suppliers’ potential opportunism between nonbinding contracts and spot markets, these strategies become ineffective when spot market rates are significantly higher than contract rates (e.g., Acocella et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2020). Hence, to improve the performance of nonbinding contracts, the role of the spot market cannot be overlooked. To this end, prior literature has uncovered the potential of digital platforms to improve market efficiency (e.g., Gawer, 2014; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017). However, we still know little about how digital platforms may affect the performance of nonbinding contracts in industries with a dual-market structure.

Addressing this gap is important for both practical and theoretical reasons. Practically, digital platforms are increasingly available in industries with a dual-market structure. For example, the trucking freight industry has experienced a rapid growth of digital platforms (in the spot market), such as DAT, Uber Freight, and Amazon Freight. The digital freight platform market is projected to be \$33.7 billion by 2032 (The Brainy Insights, 2024). However, industry practitioners have mixed predictions on how digital freight platforms may disrupt the industry. For example, using a survey from 811 industry respondents (Cox, 2020), FreightWaves found that carriers were more pessimistic about the entry of the Amazon Freight platform, while shippers had more positive attitudes, and traditional freight brokers remained neutral. In contrast, the practical analysis by FreightWaves argues the opposite—i.e., carriers may not be negatively affected, but shippers need to worry about Amazon Freight collecting and sharing their data with competitors, and brokers may experience more disruptions to their customer relationships. Such mixed predictions provide a practical motivation to quantify the effects of digital freight platforms on the industry.

Theoretically, extrapolating mechanisms uncovered in existing digital platform research to predict the impact of digital platforms on nonbinding contracts is not clear. The main reason is that spot markets and nonbinding contracts may manifest either complementary or opportunistic effects because of the industry’s high uncertainty and the flexibility of nonbinding contracts. Specifically, prior literature has identified the benefits of digital platforms on market efficiency, including facilitating information transparency, reducing search costs, improving matching efficiency (Gong et al., 2023; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017; Zhu et al., 2021), orchestrating new or small-sized suppliers by lowering entry barriers (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Gawer, 2014), and increasing suppliers’ capacity utilization (Cramer & Krueger, 2016).

On the one hand, operating inside the efficiency of the spot market, increased supply may shrink suppliers’ profit margins, pushing them to commit to their nonbinding contracts. Since digital platforms benefit buyers, they may persuade them to reallocate more demand from nonbinding contracts to the digital platform. This logic suggests a positive effect of digital platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts. On the other hand, building on a technology substitution view (e.g., Babar & Burtch, 2020), suppliers may completely switch from nonbinding contracts to the digital platform due to the digital platform’s benefits of improved matching efficiency, more consistent demand, and higher flexibility for suppliers. Also, the benefits of digital platforms can facilitate buyers’ access to many carriers in the spot market, raising expectations for their contracted suppliers. These mechanisms suggest a negative effect of digital platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts. Given mixed sentiment and theoretical tension, we examine the question: How does adding a digital platform to the spot market affect the performance of nonbinding contracts?

Our empirical analysis leveraging the beta release of the Amazon Freight platform (hereafter, Amazon Freight) provides evidence that digital platforms improve the performance of nonbinding contracts. Specifically, we found that Amazon Freight’s entry, on average, decreased tender rejections by 16- 18% for both the treated freight market and the freight lane between treated markets.<sup>5</sup> Amazon Freight’s entry also reduced tender rejections for lanes between treated and untreated markets by 10-12%, demonstrating a spillover effect from treated to untreated markets. We empirically explored three mechanisms, i.e., spot market prices, demand, and supply, to explain the effects. On average, digital freight platforms significantly improved the performance of nonbinding contracts by increasing suppliers’ transportation capacity and decreasing spot market prices. Although we did not find empirical support for the demand-side mechanisms (i.e., digital freight platforms’ effects on shippers’ demand volume and expectations for nonbinding contracted carriers were insignificant, on average), our heterogeneity analyses indicate that digital freight platforms decreased shippers’ truckload requests to nonbinding contracted carriers in short lanes. In other words, in short lanes only, shippers substituted nonbinding contracts with digital freight platforms, thereby improving the performance of nonbinding contracts.

Our findings contribute to the digital platform literature by demonstrating the impacts of digital platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts in industries with a dualmarket structure and delineating the underlying mechanisms. In addition, we contribute to the understanding of nonbinding contract performance in reference disciplines. Specifically, unlike prior literature focusing on buyers’ strategies and thus overlooking the mutual influence between nonbinding contracts and the spot market, this research highlights the vital role of digital platform-enabled spot marketplaces. This is an important theoretical advancement, as the effectiveness of buyers’ strategies to better leverage nonbinding contracts largely depends on the spot market. We also offer practical implications for business managers and policymakers on leveraging digital platforms to improve the performance of nonbinding contracts.

## Background: Trucking Freight Industry

## Coexisting With Nonbinding Contracts and Spot Markets

In the context of trucking freight, shippers are buyers with truckload service requests (called tenders). Shippers may own private fleets or outsource transportation services to shipping providers (i.e., carriers). The industry is highly uncertain because shippers’ demand and carriers’ transportation capacity are unpredictable—i.e., a carrier constantly “faces random demand with short lead times as its trucks move from delivery to delivery” (Scott et al., 2017, p. 1088). To secure carriage capacity and reduce uncertainty, shippers with consistent shipping demand in given lanes often make long-term nonbinding contracts (typically annually) with carriers (Caplice, 2021; Masten, 2009). To do so, shippers hold a reverse auction— i.e., the shipper invites potential carriers to bid for the requested shipping lanes (Sahinyazan et al., 2021). The shipper determines the winning carrier (denoted as the primary carrier) and several backup carriers for each lane. A routing guide handles the list of these carriers. Such a routing guide is a transportation management system module that automatically helps shippers manage their daily shipment operations (Caplice, 2007).

A long-term nonbinding contract between the shipper and the primary carrier has a fixed annual shipping rate for each lane. Shippers prefer to use contracted carriers because the contract shipping rates are usually lower than the market-defined rates (Acocella et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2017). Also, contractual relationships with trustworthy carriers help ensure safe, reliable, and on-time shipping services because it is hard to build the same level of trust and quality in a one-time pure “buy” contract (Gulati, 1995). Carriers often use long-term contracts to minimize the negative impacts of future volatile demand, better manage their capacity, and keep their trucks operating in lowdemand periods (Sahinyazan et al., 2021; Scott et al., 2017). However, long-term nonbinding contracts do not make any commitments for shippers’ load offers (e.g., volume, frequency, and timing) and carriers’ acceptances (Scott et al., 2020). In nonbinding contracts, shippers often first offer the load on a lane at the contracted rate to the primary carrier. The primary carrier decides to accept or refuse it without legal sanctions.

To match the remaining supply and demand unfilled by the contracted partners, shippers and carriers can leverage spot markets, which are external marketplaces offering spot prices (Caplice, 2007). Specifically, if the contracted carrier rejects the shipper’s tender, the shipper can identify a carrier for a short-term capacity and pay the real-time market rate (Acocella et al., 2020) with no commitments for future business on either side (Scott et al., 2017).

## Performance of Nonbinding Contracts

The performance of nonbinding contracts typically refers to the extent to which the contracted supplier fulfills the buyer’s service requests. Accordingly, the trucking freight industry uses the contracted carrier’s rejection of a shipper’s truckload service requests (i.e., tender rejections) to indicate carriers’ compliance with nonbinding contracts and monitor the market’s supply and demand dynamics (Scott et al., 2020). Tender rejections have significant implications for shippers. Specifically, tender rejections increase shippers’ costs because they have to use a backup carrier or spot markets, which have unfixed prices that are often higher than contract rates (Caplice, 2021). Tender rejections also interrupt scheduled shipments, cause delivery delays, lower service quality, and hurt customer satisfaction (Acocella et al., 2020; Sahinyazan et al., 2021).

Prior literature has identified various determinants of tender rejections. For example, primary carriers are more likely to reject tenders with lower contract rates, higher volume and frequency, higher volatility over time, and shorter lead time (i.e., the time between a shipment being tendered and picked up) (Acocella et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2017). Carriers’ operational limitations and costs, such as limited capacity, relocation costs of an empty truck (referred to as “empty mileage” or “deadhead”), and dwell time for loading and unloading, positively correlate with tender rejections (Acocella et al., 2020). External factors, such as higher spot market prices, drivers’ hours-of-service regulations, and adverse weather conditions, also increase tender rejections (Scott et al., 2017). Accordingly, research in operations management and transportation has suggested that shippers spend considerable effort in contract detailing and relational governance to reduce tender rejections; however, these strategies become ineffective when the spot market price is considerably higher than the contract rate (e.g., Acocella et al., 2020; Caplice, 2021; Scott et al., 2020).

## Digital Freight Platforms

The trucking freight industry is highly fragmented, with small carriers with fewer trucks dominating supply (Caplice, 2007; Guo et al., 2022), which has triggered brokerage services connecting shippers with third-party logistics (3PL) providers. 3PL providers manage a wide range of logistics activities, from managing the supply chain to fulfilling shipments in the spot market. 3PL providers can be classified into asset-based and non-asset-based providers, depending on whether they own their assets or rely on other carriers’ assets, including trucks and warehouses. Non-asset-based 3PL providers, often called brokers, rely on information technologies and human agents to connect shippers and carriers. Brokers play a vital role in reducing information asymmetry in the market (Gong et al., 2016), especially for the low-demand or high-volatile shipping lanes often not served by asset-based 3PL providers.

Brokers increasingly leverage digital platforms to improve efficiency and facilitate communication and transactions between shippers and carriers (Zhou & Wan, 2022). Various types of firms compete in the digital freight platform space. For example, existing brokers are transforming their business models by launching digital platforms, such as J.B. Hunt’s 360 platform and C.H. Robinson’s Navisphere. Technology-based ventures, like Uber, are positioning their businesses on mobile app-based digital freight platforms. E-commerce platforms with large shipping demand are also entering the industry, with digital platforms, such as Amazon Freight, seeking supply chain integration.

How does a digital freight platform work? Consider Amazon Freight as an example. It allows any carrier with valid DOT and MC numbers to enroll on the platform.<sup>6</sup> Upon acceptance by Amazon, carriers can use Amazon Freight and manage their load using the mobile interface of the platform for carriers, i.e., the Amazon Relay mobile app (shown in Figure 1).<sup>7</sup>

Specifically, carriers can use Amazon Relay to search and accept loads on Amazon Freight, allocate drivers, track truckloads, view payments, and report issues. Also, the Amazon Relay mobile app enables drivers to manage load status, report delays, and apply navigation practices. From the shipper’s perspective, any shipper can access the Amazon Freight platform through the website (i.e., freight.amazon.com) to request truckload services. The shipper provides shipment information, including commodity type, value, weight, pick-up and delivery locations, and dates. According to this information, Amazon Freight may accept the request and determine the spot shipping rate. Once a carrier accepts the request, the shipment details are shared with the carrier through the platform. Amazon Freight also provides shippers with a web-based tracking system that can be integrated with shippers’ transportation management systems.

Digital freight platforms differ from traditional brokers in several ways. First, digital freight platforms directly connect shippers and carriers and can easily apply advanced machine learning and artificial intelligence algorithms to better match them (Zhou & Wan, 2022). Digital freight platforms also use algorithms to automate and govern the transaction process with less human input (Heinbach et al., 2022). In contrast, traditional freight brokerage relies more on human input through phone or fax, which has significantly higher coordination costs and often causes delays in the transaction process. Second, compared with a traditional brokerage, digital freight platforms have advantages in leveraging technologies such as tracking and messaging, thus making it easier for shippers and carriers to track loads and communicate in real-time through the platform’s mobile app (Davis & Lucido, 2017). Real-time load visibility and communications are particularly important for trucking freight transactions because disruptions to planned activities frequently occur (Heinbach et al., 2022). Third, digital freight platforms orchestrate an extensive network of shippers and carriers with lower entry barriers than traditional brokers (Grieger, 2003). Given these unique characteristics, digital freight platforms represent a disruptive force to the trucking freight industry’s spot market, which was previously dominated by traditional brokers due to high market fragmentation and uncertainty.

However, compared with traditional brokers and transactions using nonbinding contracts, building trust on digital freight platforms is challenging due to the absence of face-to-face negotiations and interactions. While prior literature has suggested approaches to mitigating this challenge on digital platforms focusing on peer-to-peer sharing, such as implementing user review systems and regulating participant behaviors (e.g., Einav et al., 2016; Sundararajan, 2017), they may not be efficient for digital freight platforms because of the high uncertainty and because carriers often need to make decisions on a load-by-load basis (Acocella et al., 2020). Thus, shippers with frequent loads and expectations of high-quality shipping services in given lanes may still prefer transactions with carriers using nonbinding contracts. Shippers’ reluctance to use digital freight platforms may be even stronger if the platform owner is not responsible for carrying the load (Goldsby & Eckert, 2003). Hence, digital freight platforms are not likely to cause the “end of nonbinding contracts” in the industry. Rather, as digital freight platforms revolutionize the spot market, they can readily influence how carriers and shippers leverage nonbinding contracts, thus affecting the performance of nonbinding contracts.

## Related Literature

Prior literature has examined the business and societal impacts of digital platforms. From the business perspective, studies have investigated the effects of digital platforms on incumbent competitors’ performance in related industries, such as ridesharing platforms’ (e.g., Uber and Lyft) impacts on taxi firms (Yu et al., 2020), home-sharing platforms’ (e.g., Airbnb) impacts on hotels (Zervas et al., 2017), classified-advertising platforms’ (e.g., Craigslist) impacts on local newspapers (Seamans & Zhu, 2014), and digital freight platforms’ impacts on traditional freight logistics firms (Zhou & Wan, 2022). Research has also examined how digital platforms affect firms in complementary industries—for example, the impacts of food delivery platforms (e.g., DoorDash and Uber Eats) on restaurants’ profitability (Chen et al., 2022a; Feldman et al., 2023). In addition, studies have shown that digital platforms have spillover effects on unrelated industries by shifting the labor market—e.g., ridesharing platforms’ impacts on restaurants’ service quality (Shin et al., 2023).

From the societal perspective, prior research has identified the impacts of ridesharing platforms on such societal outcomes as alcohol-related vehicle fatalities (Greenwood & Wattal, 2017), new vehicle purchases (Gong et al., 2023), traffic congestion (Li et al., 2022), public transportation use (Babar & Burtch, 2020), crime incidents (Park et al., 2021), and patients’ emergency care access (Sengupta et al., 2023). Research has also identified some unintended consequences of digital platforms. For instance, previous studies have shown that Craigslist increases HIV infections (Chan & Ghose, 2014; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2016), and Airbnb increases crime incidents (Han et al., 2022). Since ridesharing platforms are open to potential service suppliers and offer them employment opportunities (Li et al., 2021), they can reduce entrepreneurial activities (Burtch et al., 2018) and personal bankruptcy rates (Nian et al., 2021). Because home-sharing platforms add alternative channels for suppliers to list properties, they can disrupt local residential markets (Chen et al., 2022b).

These prior studies have mixed findings regarding the impacts of digital platforms on demand, supply, and prices in different industries. From the demand perspective, while Yu et al. (2020) found that ridesharing platforms can considerably capture demand from the traditional taxi industry, Zhou and Wan (2022) showed that digital brokerage platforms do not significantly change demand and thus the profitability of traditional freight brokers. Similarly, Chen et al. (2022a) found that leveraging digital platforms for food delivery does not increase demand for a restaurant if it already has many dine-in customers. From the supply perspective, Zervas et al. (2017) demonstrated that home-sharing platforms can quickly increase supply during high-demand seasons, keeping lodging prices low. Shin et al. (2023) showed that although ridesharing platforms increase supply in the taxi industry, they negatively affect product quality in other industries by increasing labor force turnover. From a price perspective, Yu et al. (2020) demonstrated that reducing taxi fares after the entry of ridesharing platforms can increase the competition between the traditional taxi industry and ridesharing platforms, eventually enhancing social welfare. Similarly, Zervas et al. (2017) found that home-sharing platforms reduce hotel room prices in the short term, improving social welfare. On the contrary, Chen et al. (2022a) demonstrated that a convenient food delivery platform can increase a restaurant’s prices if many customers order using the platform, reducing social welfare. Hence, industry characteristics are important when evaluating the impacts of digital platforms on changes in demand, supply, and market prices.

Regarding industry characteristics, the prior literature on the impacts of digital platforms has tended to focus on industries that have relatively low uncertainty and arrange transactions through either a stable dyadic buyer-supplier relationship using a legally binding contract (e.g., long-term rental) or a one-time transactional relationship that may not even have an explicit contract (e.g., taxis, restaurants, hotel accommodation). Yet we know little about the impact of digital platforms in industries with high uncertainty, especially those with a dual-market structure—i.e., coexisting nonbinding contracts and spot markets.

A dual-market structure has distinctive theoretical means compared with other industries, warranting a deeper investigation of the digital platform’s impacts. First, while nonbinding contracts formally establish the transaction relationship between suppliers and buyers, they do not specify the amount, time, or frequency of transactions and legal penalties for refusals (D’Agostino & Lisciandra, 2018). Although the high flexibility of nonbinding contracts helps accommodate the high uncertainty of supply and demand, it makes governing suppliers’ behaviors challenging. Accordingly, research across several fields, such as strategy, economics, operations management, and transportation, has sought to identify mechanisms to ensure the performance of nonbinding contracts (e.g., Carson et al., 2006; D’Agostino & Lisciandra, 2018; Scott et al., 2020). This literature focuses on relational mechanisms for buyers, such as the “shadow of the future” (e.g., future business continuation, reputation) and trust in contract design and interfirm relationship governance (Carson et al., 2006; D’Agostino & Lisciandra, 2018; Gibbons et al., 2023; Kvaløy & Olsen, 2023; Scott et al., 2020). Second, the efficiency of such a dual-market structure is paradoxical— i.e., while nonbinding contracts and spot markets are developed to complement each other due to the high uncertainty, it enables suppliers’ opportunism across the two markets (Hendrikse, 2006). For example, suppliers often sign nonbinding contracts to keep their businesses running during low-demand periods but allocate more capacity to the spot market when it offers higher prices (Sahinyazan et al., 2021; Scott et al., 2017). Taken together, the relational mechanisms that dominate the literature regarding performance improvements in nonbinding contracts are highly sensitive to the dynamics of the spot market.

As digital platforms revolutionize spot markets, they can ultimately change the performance of nonbinding contracts due to the mutual dependencies between the two markets. However, given the paradox exhibited in the dual market structure (i.e., complementarity versus opportunism across the two markets), predicting the impacts of digital platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts using mechanisms uncovered in the existing literature may not be straightforward. Specifically, prior studies have shown that digital platforms improve the efficiency of the spot market, by, for example, improving the matching of suppliers and buyers, providing more real-time transaction options for them, reducing search costs, and increasing market transparency (Gong et al., 2023; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017; Li et al., 2021; Zervas et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2021). Digital platforms can also engage new or small-sized suppliers by reducing entry barriers (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Gawer, 2014) and can improve existing suppliers’ capacity utilization (Cramer & Krueger, 2016). While a more efficient spot market resulting from digital platforms can reduce suppliers’ opportunism if they use spot markets to complement nonbinding contracts, it can also induce both buyers and suppliers to switch from nonbinding contracts to spot markets. Hence, this research aims to reconcile this theoretical tension and uncover the dominant mechanisms at work.

Furthermore, research on digital freight platforms remains scant across disciplines such as information systems, operations management, and transportation. Specifically, conceptual research has developed the typology of digital platforms for trucking freight (Heinbach et al., 2022) and identified a research agenda focusing on the platform’s operational decisions (Alnaggar et al., 2021). In addition, studies have used analytical modeling approaches to optimize digital freight platform operations, such as shipper and carrier matching (Guo et al., 2020, 2022; Park et al., 2023), truck routing (Miller et al., 2020), and optimal information sharing with shippers and/or carriers (Shi et al., 2023). Research has also adopted an analytical modeling approach to identify shippers’ optimal decisions for leveraging digital freight platforms (Wu et al., 2024). However, little research has provided empirical evidence on evaluating the impact of digital freight platforms, with the exception of Zhou and Wan (2022), who empirically examined the impacts on incumbent carriers’ and brokers’ financial performance. This study contributes to this stream of literature by being the first to empirically investigate the market-level impacts of digital freight platforms on a troublesome issue in the trucking freight industry, i.e., tender rejections.

## Theoretical Development

We extrapolate the theoretical mechanisms identified in prior digital platform studies to the context of trucking freight. Accordingly, we theorize possible mechanisms explaining digital freight platforms’ effect on the performance of nonbinding contracts.

## Spot Price Mechanism from the Market Perspective

Digital platforms can improve information transparency in the marketplace (Gong et al., 2016; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017), which also applies to the trucking freight context (Zhou & Wan, 2022). Specifically, a shipper can quickly register on a digital freight platform and post a shipping request, which will be sent to all available carriers in real time. Similarly, carriers receive all shipping requests that match their schedules. Hence, information regarding the shipper, the carrier, and pricing is transparent to all parties involved during the transaction process. Furthermore, digital platforms can match supply and demand more efficiently and reduce search costs (Greenwood & Wattal, 2017) because they often have an extensive network of suppliers and buyers and can implement more sophisticated matching, pricing, and recommendation system algorithms (Gong et al., 2016). According to economic theories, a more transparent and better-matched marketplace will intensify competition and lower prices. For example, shippers can identify better rates with complete information on all potential carriers and shipping prices. Also, the competition of multiple carriers for the same truckload will lower prices. Given that the decreased spot prices will negatively affect carriers’ ability to secure alternative profitable offers from the spot market, carriers that leverage spot markets to complement their nonbinding contracts are more likely to commit to nonbinding contracts, thus improving the performance of nonbinding contracts.

## Supply-Side Mechanism from the Carriers’ Perspective

Digital freight platforms can increase the industry’s active trucking capacity in two ways: (1) leveraging previously underutilized trucks operated by new and small carriers and (2) optimizing truckload utilization.

First, digital platforms often encourage the participation of new and small suppliers to enlarge the platform ecosystem (Gawer, 2014). New and small suppliers also benefit from joining digital platforms because this allows them to access the platform’s user base and gain legitimacy (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). In the trucking freight industry, digital freight platforms provide carriers, especially small and neophyte carriers, an opportunity to obtain shipping offers and extend their network (Zhou & Wan, 2022). For example, an Uber Freight representative explicitly expressed their focus on small carriers with fewer than fifteen trucks (Lockridge, 2018). Orchestrating new and small carriers is particularly meaningful in the trucking freight industry because of its high fragmentation (Caplice, 2007)— e.g., as of April 2023, there were millions of trucks operated by over 750,000 carriers in the U.S., of which 95.8% and 99.7% had fewer than 10 and 100 trucks, respectively.<sup>8</sup> In the past, large brokers dominated the spot market and carriers were able to see only a small fraction of all load requests and needed to constantly compete to access more loads.<sup>9</sup> Small and new carriers were especially disadvantaged because they may have not had the assets required for certain operations, such as dropand-hook shipments, or sufficient historical transactions to acquire legitimacy with the broker. In contrast, digital freight platforms enable small and new carriers to access information about nearly all loads in real time, allowing them to benefit from improved matching efficiency to secure loads (Zhou & Wan, 2022).

Second, prior literature has demonstrated that digital platforms can increase suppliers’ capacity utilization. For example, Cramer and Krueger (2016) found that ridesharing platforms such as Uber and Lyft can increase drivers’ capacity utilization rate (i.e., drivers’ time and share of miles with a passenger in their vehicle). Similarly, digital freight platforms can increase the industry’s transportation capacity by optimizing truckload utilization. With access to nearly all shipping requests in real time and improved matching efficiency, carriers’ transportation capacity can be more efficiently filled. For example, carriers and truck drivers using digital freight platforms can identify the next load or backhauls based on the estimated unloading time at the destination of the current load, thereby decreasing deadheads, i.e., the mileage that a truck moves empty (Heinbach et al., 2022).

Depending on whether carriers leverage digital freight platforms to complement or substitute for nonbinding contracts, the digital freight platform’s improvement in trucking capacity may lead to divergent consequences on nonbinding contracts. On the one hand, if carriers use spot markets to complement their existing nonbinding contracts, they are more likely to commit to nonbinding contracts to secure loads when the market’s transportation supply increases. Also, for some loads requested by the contracted shippers that do not fit well into their optimal route, carriers can use a digital freight platform to find a suitable match for their backhauls and reduce potential deadheads. Hence, digital freight platforms will likely improve the performance of nonbinding contracts. On the other hand, the benefits of digital platforms for carriers’ capacity utilization may motivate carriers to switch from nonbinding contracts to digital freight platforms. Also, carriers can use digital freight platforms to optimize truck utilization and reduce deadheads, which may lead to carriers’ increased rejections of requests from nonbinding contracts that do not fit well into their optimal route. Hence, carriers’ potential substitution of nonbinding contracts for digital freight platforms suggests a possible negative effect of digital freight platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts.

## Demand-Side Mechanism from the Shippers’ Perspective

Digital freight platforms can attract shippers due to the large number of available carriers, improved information transparency and matching efficiency, and reduced costs of searching for carriers. Consequently, shippers may use digital freight platforms to fulfill their truckload demand, especially on lanes that lack contracted carriers’ truckload capacity, thus decreasing tenders sent to the contracted carriers. The decreased demand, through nonbinding contracts, will lead to a reduction in their refusals.

The increased matching efficiency and reduced search costs on digital freight platforms may also alter shippers’ expectations of tender lead time—i.e., the time carriers require to schedule their capacity and pick up the load. Specifically, digital freight platforms facilitate faster transactions between shippers and carriers. With such an alternative option, shippers may decrease their tender lead time. A shorter tender lead time can significantly improve shippers’ performance by reducing delivery time. Literature has demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between tender lead time and tender rejection rates—i.e., with a too-short (or too-long) tender lead time, carriers may be unable to schedule their capacity to meet the requested pickup date, which will likely increase tender rejections (e.g., Scott et al., 2017). Hence, digital freight platforms can reduce the shipper’s expectations of tender lead time, leading to a decrease or increase in tender rejection rates that need to be empirically delineated.

In summary, the mechanisms extrapolated from the prior digital platform literature lead to inconsistent predictions of how digital freight platforms in the spot market influence the performance of nonbinding contracts. Hence, it is important to empirically identify the effect and tease out the dominant mechanisms at work.

## Method

## Quasi-Experimental Design

We utilized the quasi-experimental design opportunity of Amazon Freight’s entry. Amazon registered as a third-party logistics (3PL) provider with FMCSA in March 2016 and launched Amazon Logistics Inc. in March 2017. Amazon Logistics started its freight brokerage practices in August 2017 (Ashe, 2018) and launched a beta version of Amazon Freight in August 2018 (Stinson, 2019). Amazon Freight does not provide Amazon’s own fleets and transportation capacity on the platform but instead connects external shippers to its third-party trucking partners (Aouad, 2019). Amazon Freight can quickly scale because it leverages Amazon’s existing line-haul service partners in its transportation network and allows external shippers to use the platform (Smith, 2019).

Amazon took a few months to pilot the platform design and operations and did not publicly announce it. The Amazon Freight platform became widely noticed on April 26, 2019, when The Wall Street Journal and other media outlets reported Amazon’s entry into the trucking freight brokerage market (Stinson, 2019). At that time, Amazon Freight presented a major disruptive change to the trucking freight industry. For example, the transportation and logistics industry experienced a decline of over \$2.3 billion in stock value within one business day after The Wall Street Journal’s media coverage (Hill, 2019). The stock value of C.H. Robinson Worldwide Inc., North America’s largest freight brokerage company, declined by more than 10% in the last week of April 2019 (Smith, 2019). Given the megaphone impact of The Wall Street Journal’s media coverage and its market impact, we used April 26, 2019, as the experiment date in our empirical design.

The Amazon Freight beta version initially operated in five states, i.e., Connecticut, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania; in February 2020, it became available in all U.S. states. Hence, we used freight markets in these five states as the treatment group and freight markets in other states as the control group.<sup>10,11</sup> Furthermore, Amazon Freight provides shippers with a full truckload service using a 53-foot dry van and does not support shipping hazardous and refrigerated items. Thus, we used 53-foot dry van freight services to identify the treatment effects. We leveraged the refrigerated freight services as falsification tests to further isolate the treatment effects. Figure 2 graphically summarizes the empirical design.

## Data Collection

We collected data from SONAR Inc. (formerly FreightWaves SONAR),<sup>12</sup> a unique freight market dataset widely used by practitioners. SONAR collects and aggregates freight data from over a thousand logistics sources (e.g., transportation management systems, Internet of Things vendors, fuel purchasing systems, shippers, carriers, 3PLs, and customers) and generates practical indices on freight activities and logistics in the market. SONAR further breaks down these indices into 135 geographical freight markets<sup>13</sup> and pairwise lanes between these freight markets for each type of truck, including dry van, refrigerated truck (i.e., Reefer), and flatbed. The indices are also granularized based on the tender’s origination or destination— i.e., “outbound” refers to the target market as the origin of the tender, and “inbound” represents the target market as the destination of the tender. We collected daily truckload data of all available U.S. geographical freight markets and lanes using the SONAR API six months before and after the experiment date (i.e., April 26, 2019). We also collected macroeconomic data at the state level, including monthly labor force and unemployment rates data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and quarterly personal income per capita data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis. We provide detailed descriptions of variable constructions, descriptive statistics, and correlations among all variables in the transparency materials (https://osf.io/c6p4t/).

## Variable Definitions

Our main analysis was conducted at the market-day level. Consistent with prior literature (e.g., Acocella et al., 2020; Sahinyazan et al., 2021; Scott et al., 2017, 2020), we used tender rejections to measure the performance of nonbinding contracts. Tender rejections are the percentage of truckload tenders rejected by the primary carriers under nonbinding contracts from the total requested truckloads tendered for shipping.<sup>14</sup> Furthermore, a freight market can be an origination (i.e., outbound tender) or a destination (i.e., inbound tender); therefore, we used both outbound and inbound tender rejection rates. Specifically, OutTenderReject<sub>it</sub> is the log-transformed percentage of rejected tenders by the primary carriers originating from the freight market i at day t. InTenderReject<sub>it</sub> is the log-transformed percentage of rejected tenders by the primary carriers destined to the freight market i at day t.

Following a quasi-experimental design, we used two dummy variables to identify the treatment effects. Specifically, Treat<sub>i</sub> equals 1 if the freight market i was in the five treated states— i.e., Connecticut, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania, and 0 otherwise. The FreightWaves SONAR data aggregates cities geographically nearby into freight markets and assigns a reference city and reference state to each freight market based on the geographical location of the market and the prevalence of freight transactions. We used the reference state of each freight market to identify the treatment and control groups in the main analysis. We ran several robustness checks for freight markets spanning across states.<sup>15</sup> In the main analysis, our dataset included 14 freight markets in the treatment group out of a total of 135 U.S. freight markets. After<sub>t</sub> indicates whether the day t is after the treatment date (i.e., April 26, 2019), which equals 1 for the times after the treatment date and 0 otherwise.

## Empirical Approach

We employed the difference-in-differences (DID) estimations specified in Equation (1). We included the market fixed effects to control for time-invariant unobservables. We added time-fixed effects to account for time-specific heterogeneity, such as global shocks occurring across all the markets. We clustered the error term at the market level to account for the potential autocorrelation.

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} O u t T e n d e r R e j e c t _ {i t} \\ I n T e n d e r R e j e c t _ {i t} \end{array} \right] = \beta_ {1} T r e a t _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {t} + \lambda_ {i} + \nu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where i indexes the freight market, t is the day indicator, $\beta _ { 1 }$ represents the treatment effects, $\lambda _ { i }$ represents the marketfixed effects, $\nu _ { t }$ refers to the time-fixed effects, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ refers to the vector of error terms. Treat<sub>i</sub> and After<sub>t</sub> were omitted due to their collinearity with market and time-fixed effects.

The DID estimation has a critical assumption of the parallel trends—i.e., the dependent variables on the treatment and control groups should evolve in a similar pattern over time in the case of no treatment. Thus, we estimated relative time models to examine the parallel trend assumption of the DID estimation (Angrist & Pischke, 2008; Burtch et al., 2018; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017). Specifically, we adopted the following specification:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{c} O u t T e n d e r R e j e c t _ {i t} \\ I n T e n d e r R e j e c t _ {i t} \end{array} \right] = \gamma T r e a t _ {i} \times r _ {m} + \lambda_ {i} + \nu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where $r _ { m }$ represents a set of relative monthly dummies indicating the chronological distance between Amazon Freight’s entry and time t. m ranges from 6 months before Amazon Freight’s entry through 6 months after. ?? represents the vector of coefficients on the interaction between the treatment indicator and relative time dummies to be estimated.

We ran robustness checks to ensure the estimation’s stability and report them in the appendices. Appendix A presents the robustness checks concerning the freight markets across the treated and controlled states. Given that the market-day level of analysis mixed the shipping loads between treated and control markets, we conducted a lane-day level of analysis to demonstrate the results’ robustness and gain additional insights into possible spillover effects from treated to control markets (see Appendix B). Appendix C illustrates efforts to isolate further the treatment effects of Amazon Freight’s entry, including the random implementation test, falsification tests using refrigerated tenders, and estimations ruling out the potential confounding effects of the Uber Freight platform. Appendix D demonstrates the robustness of our results through the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) of our DID estimations. Appendix E reports the DID analysis on a matched sample.

We report additional checks in the transparency materials, including using (1) the volume of rejected tenders as the dependent variables, (2) models with various sets of timevariant control variables, and (3) an alternative level of clustering for standard errors. Overall, all robustness checks yielded consistent results. Hence, we focus on reporting our main results, testing mechanisms, and identifying important boundary conditions in the next section.

## Testing Mechanisms

## Transportation Capacity Mechanism

We used the daily number of trucks index to proxy carriers’ trucking capacity. Precisely, the index Trucks<sub>it</sub> measures the number of trucks in the market i at day t with a base value of 100 for the number of trucks in the market i on April 1, 2018. For example, a value of 105 for Trucks<sub>it</sub> means that there were

5% more trucks in the market i on day t than on April 1, 2018.<sup>17</sup> Table 3 shows the results following Baron and Kenny’s (1986) four-step approach to test the mediation effect of Trucks<sub>it</sub>. As shown in Step 2 in Table 3, the treatment effect is positive and significant for both outbound $( \beta = 0 . 0 3 3 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and inbound $( \beta ~ = ~ 0 . 0 3 1 8 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ tenders. Economically, compared with controlled markets, Amazon Freight’s entry increased active trucks in the treated markets for outbound and inbound by 3.45 and 3.23 percentage points relative to their base values on April 1, 2018. As shown in Step 4, Trucks<sub>it</sub> is negatively associated with both outbound $( \beta = - 0 . 6 2 3 9 , p <$ 0.001) and inbound tender rejection rates $( \beta = - 0 . 6 9 6 2 , p <$ 0.001). The treatment effect in Step 4 remains statistically significant but is smaller than the estimation in Step 1. Hence, the results demonstrate a partial mediation effect—i.e., Amazon Freight’s entry reduced tender rejections by increasing carriers’ trucking capacity. The results also rule out the alternative mechanisms of carriers potentially reallocating trucking capacity from nonbinding contracts to spot markets, which would have increased tender rejections. Instead, carriers were more likely to use the digital platform to operationalize their idle capacity and fill out their extra supply, increasing overall supply and reducing tender rejections in the treated freight markets.

## Demand-Side Mechanisms

We used tender volume to measure shippers’ nonbinding contractual demand. Specifically, OutVolume<sub>it</sub> and InVolume<sub>it</sub> measured shippers’ total number of truckload requests to the contracted carriers originating from and destined to market i at day t, respectively. Both indices used the national base value of 10,000 measured from volumes on March 1, 2018.<sup>18</sup> As shown in Step 2 in Table 4, Amazon Freight’s entry, on average, did not significantly affect outbound (β = 0.0743, p = n.s.) or inbound tender volumes (β = 0.0272, p = n.s.). Hence, on average, the theorized mechanism related to shippers’ switching from nonbinding contracts to spot markets is not supported.

To test the mechanism related to shippers’ expectations, we used the tender lead time index—i.e., the number of days between the date that shippers tendered a truckload and the requested pickup date. Specifically, OutLeadTime<sub>it</sub> and InLeadTime<sub>it</sub> indices refer to the average lead time of all truckload requests originating from the market i and destination at the market i at day t, respectively. Hence, the theorized mechanism of Amazon Freight influencing tender rejection rates by altering shippers’ expectations of tender lead time is not supported.

In summary, shippers, on average, adhered to their nonbinding contracts and did not change their behavior in tendering their demand regarding volume and lead time to the contracted carriers after Amazon Freight’s entry. One possible explanation is that shippers may have still benefited from the nonbinding contracts due to the higher coordination costs of tendering loads in the spot market and the trusted service quality from the contracted carriers. Literature also suggests that shippers are less likely to behave opportunistically (i.e., bypass contracted carriers and directly offer loads in the spot market) because contracted rates are usually lower than spot rates (Acocella et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2020). Furthermore, the manifestation of the demand-side mechanisms may be contingent on other factors. First, the effect may become significant in the longer term as the digital freight platform continues to improve. Specifically, in the short term, after launching a digital platform, expanding the number of participants to seize the benefits of network effects is often more important for the platform owner, which also applies to the digital freight platform. However, in the long term, after the digital freight platform orchestrates a large part of the ecosystem, the platform owner is more likely to work on reducing participant transaction costs. For example, Uber Freight acquired Transplace after a few years of operations to improve its technical capabilities. It later updated its TMS systems with better integrations of supply chain activities in 2023, which helps shippers run timely operations and monitor the processes (Kingston, 2024). Uber Freight also released Uber Freight Exchange to allow shippers to run all procurement activities, including contracts, bids, and spot auctions (Kingston, 2023). Second, given that the digital freight platform often focuses on small and medium-sized carriers, which may have limited transportation capabilities, the demand-side mechanisms are more likely to manifest only in shorter lanes. For example, our later analysis in the Effect Heterogeneity section demonstrates that the entry of a digital freight platform only decreases the tender volume of shorter lanes (see Column 4 in Table 9), which can decrease tender rejections.

## Spot Price Mechanism

Given that the spot price data is specific to each lane, we collected weekly average spot rates of dry vans per mile for all the U.S. freight lanes available in the FreightWaves SONAR dataset. The spot rates are collected and updated weekly from Truckstop.com and have only been available since 12/24/2018 (18 weeks before our treatment date). Hence, we performed a DID analysis using data from 18 weeks before to 18 weeks after Amazon Freight’s entry. The final sample covered 66 lanes; among these, five lanes had a treated market origin (i.e., Treat = 1), five lanes had a treated market destination (i.e., Treat = 1), and two lanes were between treated markets (i.e., Treat<sub>jk</sub> = 1). ## Takeaways From Mechanism Tests

Overall, the results demonstrate the support of increasing trucking capacity and decreasing spot prices in explaining the negative effect of Amazon Freight’s entry on tender rejections. The causal mediation analysis results, as shown in Appendix F, also confirm the significance of their indirect treatment effects. Given that tender volumes did not change overall, the results indicate that carriers rejected fewer requests from their contracted shippers after Amazon Freight’s entry.<sup>19</sup> We also empirically confirmed these two supported mechanisms in the same model at the lane level through the lane-specific spot prices. Specifically, as shown in Column 5 in Table 6, we included both the lane’s spot prices (SpotPrice<sub>jkt</sub>) and the origination market’s trucking capacity (Trucks<sub>jt</sub>) along with the direct treatment effects on tender rejections at the lane level. We included the origination market’s trucking capacity because the active trucks in a freight market each day are often not predetermined for a particular lane. Also, tender rejections on a freight lane are less likely to be affected by active trucks in the destination market.<sup>20</sup> Comparing the results in Columns 1 and 5 in Table 6, we see that these two identified mechanisms combined explain around 30% of the treatment effects—i.e., 33.39% for lanes from a treated to an untreated market, 27.92% for lanes from an untreated to a treated market, and 31.97% for lanes between treated markets. The results also suggest the existence of other possible underlying mechanisms explaining the treatment effects due to the complexity of the industry, such as the high volatility of supply and demand. Thus, we acknowledge that we could only identify a partial list of underlying mechanisms and encourage future research to paint a more complete picture of this problem.

## Demand Volatility

The supported mechanisms that digital freight platforms increase transportation capacity and decrease spot prices should be more prevalent when demand is volatile for two reasons. First, the primary carriers have more difficulties planning their fleet when demand is more volatile. However, using digital freight platforms, shippers can search for on-demand transportation capacity for their volatile loads, and primary carriers can match their extra capacity with the best on-demand tenders. Thus, digital freight spot marketplaces can play a more crucial role in filling additional on-demand transportation capacity and smoothing out inconsistent demand over time in a volatile environment versus a scenario where demand is predictable and consistent. Second, volatile demand increases transportation costs per load (Acocella et al., 2020) and the fluctuation of spot prices because the supply-demand relationship changes dramatically and is unpredictable. If a digital freight platform is available, shippers can access an extensive network of small or new carriers who can provide ondemand shipping services. Thus, although demand is inconsistent and unpredictable, digital freight platforms can match it in near real time due to their advantages in scalable suppliers and better matching capabilities. Thus, digital freight platforms can help stabilize the supply-demand relationship, smoothing tender rejections.

Following prior literature in other contexts (e.g., Barry et al., 2006; Turner & Weigel, 1992), we measured demand volatility as the difference between the daily change of tender volumes and the average daily change during the study period.<sup>21,22</sup> Table 7 presents the results of the moderating effects of demand volatility using the market-day level of analysis. ## Lane Distance

Given their newness and limited transportation capacity, new and small carriers attracted to the digital freight platform are more likely to transport loads in shorter lanes. This becomes more significant when considering the hours-of-service regulation—i.e., new and small carriers may not be willing or able to take long-haul loads that cannot be completed by one driver’s on-duty daily service hours. Hence, the identified treatment effect is likely stronger for shorter lanes. Accordingly, we empirically tested the moderating effect of lane distance using the lane-day level of analysis. The SONAR dataset provided each freight market’s point location (i.e., latitude and longitude) based on the market’s major airport. We constructed the measure Distance<sub>jk</sub> as the miles (in hundreds) between the origin and destination markets’ point locations using the DistanceMetric function in the sklearn package in Python. As shown in Column 1 in Table 9, the coefficients on the interactions between the treatment effect and lane distance are positive and significant, indicating that the negative treatment effect was stronger for shorter-haul truckloads.

We also examined how the theorized mechanisms may manifest differently for short vs. long lanes. First, as shown in Column 2 in Table 9, lane distance weakened Amazon Freight’s negative effects on spot prices. Second, consistent with the market-level analysis, Amazon freight’s entry, on average, did not significantly influence tender volume at the lane level (see Column 3 in Table 9). However, as shown in Column 4 in Table 9, lane distance significantly weakened the negative effect of Amazon Freight on tender volume, indicating that Amazon Freight’s entry was likely to reduce tender volume in shorter lanes. A possible explanation is that shippers might prefer to offer some truckloads for shorter lanes directly to the digital platform (instead of offering them to the contracted carriers first), reducing contracted tender volume. Overall, the findings suggest that the treatment effect of Amazon Freight’s entry on tender rejections was stronger for shorter lanes, possibly because the platform could reduce tender volumes, and had a stronger effect on reducing spot prices for shorter lanes.

## Discussion

This research examines digital platforms’ impacts on the performance of nonbinding contracts in the trucking freight industry. Our results indicate that the entry of the Amazon Freight platform improved the performance of nonbinding contracts (i.e., tender rejections). We delineated two primary mechanisms explaining our results: reducing spot prices and increasing transportation capacity. The Amazon Freight platform, on average, did not significantly alter shippers demand for trucking services using nonbinding contracts (i.e., tender volume) or their expectations from the contracted suppliers (in terms of tender lead time). However, we found significant reductions in contracted shippers tender volume in short lanes after Amazon Freight’s entry, indicating that demand-side mechanisms may manifest in specific situations (i.e., short lanes only). Furthermore, Amazon Freight’s effect was significantly more potent when the market’s demand was more volatile and for shorter-haul truckloads.

## Theoretical Contributions

This study contributes to digital platform literature by uncovering the impacts of digital platforms in the spot market on the performance of nonbinding contracts in an industry with high uncertainty and with a dual-market structure (i.e., coexisting nonbinding contracts and spot markets). Although prior literature has commonly shown that digital platforms improve market efficiencies across contexts such as taxis, accommodation, and restaurants, extrapolating the uncovered mechanisms to predict how digital platforms influence the performance of nonbinding contracts is not straightforward. Nonbinding contracts and spot markets have complex mutual influences, i.e., suppliers can use spot markets to complement nonbinding contracts or to seek opportunism. Also, as digital platforms offer transaction benefits, buyers and suppliers may leverage them to complement or substitute nonbinding contracts. Addressing such theoretical tensions, this research provides empirical evidence of the overall positive effect of digital platforms on the performance of nonbinding contracts. Importantly, we uncovered two important mechanisms explaining the results: (1) reducing spot market prices, and (2) increasing supplier capacity. The manifestation of these two mechanisms indicates that suppliers mainly use spot markets (including digital platform-enabled ones) to complement nonbinding contracts. From the shipper’s demand perspective, we found that our focal digital freight platform, on average, did not significantly affect shippers’ demand for nonbinding contracts. However, our effect heterogeneity analyses showed a significant decrease in shippers’ tender volume for nonbinding contracted carriers only in short lanes, suggesting that shippers only substitute nonbinding contracts with digital freight platforms in short lanes.

These findings are meaningful, demonstrating that digital freight platforms generally render both contract and spot markets more efficient and less opportunistic. First, digital freight platforms motivate carriers in the contracted market to commit to nonbinding contracts. Despite the benefits of digital freight platforms, contracted shippers mostly prefer to transact with contracted carriers, possibly due to the high coordination costs of using digital platforms to tender many truckloads and the uncertainty of identifying quality and reliable carriers. Similarly, carriers benefit from transactions with contracted shippers to secure consistent demand, especially when digital freight platforms reduce spot prices. Second, digital freight platforms provide an additional channel for both contracted carriers and shippers to fulfill their remaining supply and demand, which may be more convenient and faster than going through proprietary brokers.

In addition, this research contributes to the literature on the performance of nonbinding contracts. Specifically, prior studies have mostly focused on buyers’ strategies, such as contract design and supplier relationship management, to improve the performance of nonbinding contracts (e.g., Carson et al., 2006; Gulati, 1995; Heide & Miner, 1992; Kulchina & Oxley, 2020; Scott et al., 2020). Although the economics literature has theorized the mutual influences between nonbinding contracts and spot markets and highlighted suppliers’ opportunistic behaviors across both markets (e.g., Hendrikse, 2006), existing studies have overlooked the possibilities of designing or restructuring the spot market to improve the performance of nonbinding contracts. We fill this gap by demonstrating how adding a new digital platform-enabled spot marketplace can improve the performance of nonbinding contracts.

Furthermore, our findings contribute to the operations management and transportation literatures concerning (1) the antecedents of tender rejections, and (2) digital freight platforms. First, unlike prior studies on tender rejections focusing on the perspectives of shippers, carriers, shippercarrier relationships, and external environments (e.g., Acocella et al., 2020; Scott et al., 2017, 2020), this research highlights the role of digital platforms as an important determinant of tender rejections. Adding digital platforms to the spot market may address the tender rejection problem more systematically because digital platforms can simultaneously ameliorate the two most important causes of tender rejections—i.e., supply capacity and spot prices. Second, to the scant research on digital freight platforms, this research is the first to demonstrate the impacts of digital freight platforms on market-level operational efficiency (i.e., tender rejections) and the underlying mechanisms. In doing so, our findings offer insights into how digital freight platforms are revolutionizing the industry’s efficiency and business transaction norms.

## Practical Implications

Our findings suggest that policymakers and industry regulators should support the development of digital freight platforms through public policies and/or investments. The U.S. economy relies heavily on trucking freight to transport goods and services; unfortunately, significant gaps between demand and trucking capacity (i.e., often reflected by tender rejections) and high volatility are critical concerns hindering the industry’s operational efficiency. Our findings demonstrate that digital freight platforms can significantly reduce tender rejections, thus making trucking freight operations more efficient. Accordingly, changes induced by digital freight platforms can reduce overall shipping costs and make delivery faster in potentially all industries with shipping needs. While the U.S. government has recognized the importance of closing the gap between the supply and demand of freight movement, it often invests in areas such as highway transportation infrastructure and adding new transportation capacity.<sup>23</sup> Our findings suggest a new investment direction, i.e., incubating the development of digital freight platforms, which should be effective, especially when demand is volatile. Investing in digital freight platforms (e.g., possible tax incentives) can complement existing public investments in freight movement to better balance carriers and shippers. Specifically, existing investments focus more on benefiting carriers to close the gap in trucking capacity; however, digital freight platforms can not only optimize trucking capacity but can also help shippers reduce tender rejections and thus shipping costs. We caution policymakers and industry regulators that our findings only illuminate one aspect of digital freight platforms. Accordingly, there may be some drawbacks to using digital freight platforms that were not uncovered in this study. Additional careful studies will be necessary to provide a more holistic picture to guide regulations on how digital freight platforms should operate.

Our findings also offer business recommendations, especially for shippers in the trucking freight industry. We demonstrate the evident benefits of digital freight platforms for shippers with nonbinding contracts in reducing tender rejections, even though these shippers may not necessarily use digital freight platforms. Hence, large shippers with consistent shipping demand should consider facilitating the development of digital freight platforms by, for example, offering corporate venture capital investments and endorsing their legitimacy.

## Limitations and Future Research

We discuss the limitations of this study and opportunities for future research. First, our findings are drawn from the entry of the Amazon Freight platform, which may not be generalizable to other digital platforms. Also, the identified effects might only be manifested when the digital freight platform is scalable. Future research could generalize our results to other digital freight platforms and examine the heterogeneous effects of different platform business models. Second, the uncovered mechanisms of increasing trucking capacity and reducing spot prices explain around 30% of the Amazon Freight entry’s treatment effects on tender rejections. This provides future research opportunities to identify a more complete list of underlying mechanisms in this highly uncertain and complex industry. Third, although we identified that the Amazon Freight platform has increased the industry’s trucking capacity, our data did not allow us to provide more granular findings regarding the extent to which the effects were driven by new carriers or existing carriers’ capacity reallocation. Future research could provide such nuances using data from other contexts where more granular data is accessible. Future studies could also use firm-level data or conduct qualitative research to examine how buyers and suppliers reallocate their supply and demand between nonbinding contracts and digital freight platforms. Finally, future research could go beyond tender rejection to examine the impacts of digital platforms on other critical issues in the trucking freight industry, such as truck driver turnover and well-being, empty mileage, environmental sustainability, safety, and supply chain outbreaks.

## Robustness Checks of Cross-State Freight Markets

While most freight markets in our data were in a single state, some span multiple states. Thus, the freight markets spanning treated and controlled states may have biased our results. Accordingly, we used the actual cities and states to categorize the identified 14 treated markets in the main analysis into fully and partially treated markets. Specifically, the fully treated market indicator FTreat<sub>i</sub> equals 1 if all actual cities in the treated freight market i were in the five treated states, and 0 otherwise. The partially treated market indicator PTreat<sub>i</sub> equals 1 if the freight market i’s reference state was in the five treated states but not all the actual cities were in the five treated states, and 0 otherwise. This categorization resulted in the identification of eight fully treated markets and six partially treated markets. As shown in Columns 1 and 2 in Table A1, both fully and partially treated markets had significant negative effects on tender rejection rates, demonstrating the robustness of our results. In addition, we observed that three control markets (i.e., their reference states are not among the five treated states) included small parts of the treated states. We excluded these three control markets and found consistent results (see Columns 3 and 4 in Table A1). We also ran a robustness check by identifying these three control markets as partially treated and found consistent results, as shown in Columns 5 and 6 in Table A1.

## Placebo Analysis Using Random Implementation Tests

We conducted a random implementation test to rule out the concern that the identified treatment effects are obtained merely by chance (Greenwood & Wattal, 2017). We first removed all the 14 actual treated markets from the sample dataset and then randomly selected 14 untreated markets as the “pseudo” treatment group. We ran the same DID specification (with the same treatment date of April 26, 2019) and recorded the estimated coefficients on the “pseudo” treatment effect. We replicated this analysis 1000 times and compared the distribution of these coefficients against the actual estimates (see Figure C1). The results show that the estimated placebo coefficients significantly differ from the actual estimates (Z-statistic = -2.6337, p < 0.01 for outbound tender rejections; Z-statistic = -2.8453, p < 0.01 for inbound tender rejections). Furthermore, the pseudo-treatment effects are not significantly different from zero, indicating that the model can adequately control the correlation within the market-time panel dataset. Hence, the probability of the estimated treatment effects being derived purely by chance is low.

## Ruling Out Confounding Events

The estimated treatment effects may be imputed to other confounding events, such as the entry of other digital freight platforms (e.g., Uber Freight). To rule out these concerns, we ran a subsample analysis, excluding the markets in which Uber Freight operated for an extended period. Uber Freight entered Texas first in May 2017. It later expanded to California, Arizona, the Chicago-Midwest region, Georgia, South Carolina, and North Carolina in August 2017 before its nationwide expansion in June 2018. An alternative quasi-experimental design would employ Uber Freight entry as a treatment and estimate the effects of Uber Freight’s entry on tender rejection rates in a time window from early 2017 to May 2018. However, the tender rejection rates and other freight market-level data were unavailable during this period.

Uber Freight operated in all the U.S. markets before the Amazon platform’s entry; therefore, it is unlikely to have confounded our results, as it impacted all the markets identically before our quasi-experimental study. Nonetheless, one may argue that Uber Freight’s effects may no be uniform across all the markets because it entered some markets much earlier than others. In other words, Uber Freight may have been more stable and widely used in the markets that entered early in 2017 compared to those that entered later in 2018. To address this concern, we ran a subsample analysis. We removed all the markets in which Uber Freight started to operate by August 2017 because these markets were more likely to be affected by a greater presence of Uber Freight. As shown in Columns 1 and 2 in Table C2, the results are consistent. To further control the effects of Uber Freight, we followed prior literature (e.g., Nian et al., 2021) and used the Google Trends Index for “Uber Freight” in different geographical regions at the monthly level. We conducted the market-month level of analysis due to the sparse distribution of daily Google search trends. As shown in Columns 3 and 4 in Table C2, the treatment effects of Amazon Freight’s entry remained consistent after controlling for the Google Trends Index for Uber Freight.

## Robustness Checks on SUTVA

We ran additional robustness checks to ensure that our results were robust to the DID estimation’s stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA). Under SUTVA, subjects in the control groups should not be exposed to the treatment. Since trucking freight moves across states, there might have been spillover effects between treatment and control groups, especially in locations near the treatment. To mitigate this concern, we ran a subsample analysis removing all the control markets in the neighboring states of the treatment states. As shown in Columns 1 and 2 in Table D1, the results are consistent with our main analysis. In addition, given that trucking freight may also frequently transport between two states that are not neighbors, we ran an additional check by removing all the control markets with at least one frequent lane from/to any of the treated markets in our sample. We defined the frequent lanes as those recorded in the FreightWaves SONAR lane-day level dataset. Columns 3 and 4 in Table D1 show consistent results. Furthermore, we ran a lane-day level analysis by removing all the lanes originating from or destined to the neighboring states of treated states. ## DID Analysis on a Matched Sample

The Amazon Freight platform may have self-selected into some freight markets in the beta release. To mitigate this concern, we applied propensity score matching (PSM) to create a matched subsample of control markets. We first ran a logit model to compute the propensity score of the freigh markets being treated. We matched control markets based on the covariates that may have influenced the freight market supply and demand, includin tender volume, number of trucks, GDP in the transportation and warehousing sector, and personal income per capita. We performed a 3:1 matching using the nearest neighbor algorithm based on the estimated propensity scores. We used the caliper threshold of 0.05 to avoid bad matches.

We empirically verified the covariance balance in our PSM analysis. Specifically, Figure E1 indicates that the bias between the treated and control groups was significantly reduced after matching, validating the matched sample’s covariate balance. Also, while the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ in the unmatched sample is statistically significant $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 2 8 . 4 , p = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ , it became insignificant after matching $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1 , p = 1 . 0 0 0 )$ , demonstrating that the covariates could not jointly predict the treatment. We then ensured the PSM’s common support assumption. Specifically, we restricted the matched sample to markets on the common support. In addition, as shown in Figure E2, the distributions of the propensity scores between the treated and control groups were largely different before matching but became identical after matching. Then, we reran the same DID specification on the matched sample. As shown in Table E1, the results of DID estimation on the PSM-matched sample are consistent with our main analysis results
