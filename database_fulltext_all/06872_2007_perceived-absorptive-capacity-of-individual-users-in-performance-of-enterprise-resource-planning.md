# Perceived absorptive capacity of individual users in performance of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) usage: The case for Korean firms<sup>§</sup>

Jong-Hun Park <sup>a,1,\*</sup>, Hyun-Ju Suh <sup>b,2,\*</sup>, Hee-Dong Yang <sup>a,3,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business Administration, Ewha Womans University, 11-1 Daehyun-dong, Seodaemun-gu, Seoul 120-750, Republic of Korea

<sup>b</sup> Department of Digital Media, Seoul University of Venture and Information, 1603-54 Socho-dong, Socho-gu, Seoul 137-070, Republic of Korea

## Abstract

We examined the effect of absorptive capacity of users on their use of ERP in a Korean context. The three components considered were understanding, assimilating, and applying ERP knowledge. We found that the capacities of users to assimilate and apply the knowledge had both direct and indirect effects on its value. The users’ ability to understand ERP knowledge was found to influence its performance by their assimilating and applying the knowledge. We also found that organizational support moderated the relationship between their absorptive capacity and performance.

Keywords: Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP); Knowledge transfer; Users’ absorptive capacity; Organizational support; Users’ performance of ERP usage

## 1. Introduction

In order to deal with a rapidly changing external environment and overcome the limitations of legacy systems, many companies have implemented Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems. Unlike ‘functionoriented’ systems that support only specific business activities, ERP systems are intended to integrate company-wide processes by adopting good reference models from efficient firms [63]. Hence, implementation of an ERP system can be regarded as a process of knowledge transfer of best practice from leading companies. Despite this, however, users often have difficulty implementing an ERP system, since it is difficult to understand the operational logic in the unfamiliar modules. This is the primary reason why organizations need to spend a substantial effort in convincing its employees of the practical value of ERP systems and educating them in their proper use [10]. Lack of attention to this is probably why so many implementations have failed to perform as expected [29].

The effective transfer of knowledge between knowledge sources and recipients requires direct and intimate interaction between them [19,28]. However, it is virtually impossible for recipients of ERP systems to engage in direct interaction with sources, so that the systems embody the task-related and process-related knowledge of world-class leading companies [38]. Accordingly, it is critical for a recipient company to be able to absorb the leading-edge knowledge embedded in ERP systems: a firm’s absorptive capacity can be determined by the aggregation of its members’ capacity [15].

Despite this, with the exception of Ko et al. [34], few studies have paid attention to the effects of individual users’ capacity on their ERP performance. Ko et al. demonstrated that individual users’ absorptive capacity played a significant role in knowledge transfer from ERP consultants to members of a recipient organization. They argued that official training and education was not sufficient to have all the ERP users become sufficiently familiar with ERP systems to use them effectively. Accordingly, we assumed in our research that users absorptive capacity was the individuals’ idiosyncratic capability that would help improve individual performance and eventually increase organizational competency. In other words, we argued that individual (or organizational) performance could be enhanced when users reinvented their use of ERP to fit to their task environment and thus that such reinvention would be tightly related to users’ ability to learn to use ERP systems effectively.

We believed that all the knowledge to be transferred in the context of successful ERP implementation was not explicit. Nor could it be externalised. Practices embedded in ERP packages are explicit knowledge that users must understand. However, knowledge involves applying expertise, and creating, sharing and distributing it [2,7]. Users often ask for customization when their tasks and business processes are different from those of the standardized package; indeed, this is one reason why so many ERP installations fail. Thus, consultants technical and users’ business knowledge sometimes collide.

Knowledge transfer from social network (peers and working groups) is also an important factor for successful adoption and use of ERP systems. ERP users are often unwilling to change to the ERP mode of operation. We believe that ERP knowledge has many diverse aspects, including positivistic/anti-positivistic and constructive aspects, and the affordability of such aspects in an organization is susceptible to the status of the absorptive capacity of ERP users.

The purpose of our research was to examine the effect of users’ absorptive capacity on their performance when using ERP. We therefore describe an individual user’s absorptive capacity as three components (for understanding, assimilating, and applying the knowledge in the ERP system) and suggest that organizational support can moderate the relationship between them and his or her ERP usage. Hence, our research questions are:

\- What are the dimensions of an individual user’s absorptive capacity?

\- What are the causal relationships between them? And

\- Is there any moderator determining or affecting the influence of each dimension of an individual user’s absorptive capacity on his or her task performance?

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. ERP implementation

We conducted a thorough literature review of critical success factors in successful ERP implementation (see Table 1). The studies included user training and education as one of the major critical success factors; they were considered especially important during the middle phases of the implementation process (adaptation and acceptance) [39,56]. User learning occurred during education/training and conversations with ERP consultants, colleagues, and IT champions. We investigated how and why ERP users digest this diverse knowledge.

## 2.2. Absorptive capacity of ERP

Many studies have examined the effect of an organization’s absorptive capacity on organizational performance, including the adoption of new technology [45], the transfer of technological knowledge [49], the development of new products [57], and organization learning [36,37]. Boynton et al. [8] asserted that a firm’s absorptive capacity provides the theoretical basis for comprehensive understanding of its usage of IS. Zahra and George [65] suggested that absorptive capacity was an important factor for an organization to implement new IS successfully and organizationlevel prior knowledge and management support were both critical.

Individual users’ absorptive capacity, however, does play a significant role in the knowledge-transfer process. Cohen and Levinthal defined the absorptive capacity as the ability of an organizational member ‘‘to value, assimilate, and apply new knowledge.’’ Mowery and Oxley [43] conceptualized absorptive capacity as a broad set of organization member ‘‘skills needed to deal with the tacit component of transferred knowledge and needed to modify this imported knowledge.’’ Zahra and George [66] further conceptualized user absorptive capacity as ‘‘a set of organizational routines and processes by which firms acquire, assimilate, transform, and exploit knowledge to produce a dynamic organiza tional capability.’’ We defined user absorptive capacity as the ability of an organizational member to value, assimilate, and apply new knowledge. Further, we classify user absorptive capacity into three interrelated components. The first, a user’s capacity for under standing external knowledge, corresponds to ‘‘the acquisition phase in the knowledge life cycle’’. The absorptive capacity is strongly related to the members prior knowledge base about the object [33]. When organization members possess greater prior knowledge, they can absorb new knowledge more effectively [35,47,58]. Hence, a user’s absorptive capacity for understanding external knowledge is one of the important components of absorptive capacity [32]. In the context of ERP systems, a user’s absorptive capacity for understanding external knowledge is the user’s acquired knowledge regarding ERP systems and ERP consulting firms. Second, a user’s capacity for assimilating knowledge is the user’s ability to internalize new knowledge into his or her task environment. Once organizational members understand new knowl edge, they need to assimilate it. This can be affected by how comfortable the user feels when executing th tasks using the technology. The third component relates to applying knowledge to the task. It is similar to ‘‘problem-solving skills’’ and associated with the ‘‘exploitation phase’’ in organization-level absorptive capacity. As Cohen and Levinthal stressed, absorptive capacity is not only the capability to understand and assimilate external knowledge, but the ability to exploit and commercialize it [60]. Hence, a user’s capacity for applying knowledge was defined as the user’s ability to use and share ERP knowledge in performing tasks.

## 3. Theory and hypotheses

## 3.1. Research model

Our research model is shown in Fig. 1. Each component of absorptive capacity was assumed to affect the performance of ERP use directly and positively. In addition, the capacity for understanding knowledge was assumed to affect the performance of ERP usage via the absorptive capacity for assimilating and applying knowledge. The capacity for assimilating knowledge thus affects the performance of ERP usage via the capacity for applying knowledge and the degree of organizational support was assumed to moderate the relationships between each component of absorptive capacity and the ERP use.

## 3.2. Hypotheses

Previous studies revealed that prior knowledge on the knowledge-to-be-transferred positively affected the adoption of new technology, the transfer of process knowledge, and research productivity [14]. Further, organizational members’ prior knowledge base was strongly associated with their capacities to understand new and relevant knowledge. We therefore argued that organization members could absorb novel knowledge about ERP systems more effectively if they had more prior knowledge about the ERP systems. Therefore, we enunciated the following:

Hypothesis 1. Users’ absorptive capacity for understanding ERP systems is positively related to their performance of ERP usage.

After being acquired, knowledge needs to be internalized. Even though acquired knowledge is precious and valuable by nature, it cannot be used when the recipients do not assimilate it into their task environment [25]. Effective assimilation can lead users to enhance task performance through synthesizing prior and newly acquired knowledge.

Research on information systems (IS) has also emphasized the assimilation phase as important in its performance of use (e.g., [17,21,53]). Organizations spend time and money to implement high-quality ISs and educate users. However, users abandon them if they do not understand the embedded in them [11]. In particular, if knowledge recipients merely understand the operational functions of ERP systems rather than their embedded logic, their benefits will be confined to the automation of transactions. In contrast, users can achieve better performance if they understand the embedded logic. These lead to the following:

Hypothesis 2. Users’ absorptive capacity for assimilating ERP systems is positively related to their performance of ERP usage.

User absorptive capacity not only extends the preexisting stocks of capabilities, but also creates new capabilities. The exploitation of newly acquired knowledge into everyday tasks helps organizational members set up routines those perform tasks with newly acquired knowledge, and create new knowledge.

In addition, the capacity of users to apply knowledge can be increased by sharing activities across departments, teams, and the organization [26]. Nonaka [46] contended that absorptive capacity could be developed through the social transfer of knowledge. The ability to share knowledge with colleagues and other members of the organization can thus increase the capacity for applying knowledge. Therefore:

Hypothesis 3. Users’ absorptive capacity for applying ERP knowledge is positively related to their performance of ERP usage.

Purvis et al. [48] showed that prior knowledge about a similar methodology affected the assimilation of a new methodology. In addition, Zahra and George [66] suggested that ‘‘potential’’ absorptive capacity that included acquisition and assimilation dimensions affected ‘‘realized’’ absorptive capacity. This lead to:

Hypothesis 4. Users’ absorptive capacity for understanding ERP systems affects their absorptive capacity for assimilating ERP systems.

Hypothesis 5. Users’ absorptive capacity for understanding ERP systems affects their absorptive capacity for applying ERP systems.

Hypothesis 6. Users’ capacity for assimilating ERP systems affects their absorptive capacity for applying ERP systems.

Of course, organizational support is indispensable for successful acceptance of any system. The organizational context that facilitates knowledge transfer thus includes organizational structure, systems, and culture [24,30].

Even though an individual has enough prior knowledge for assimilating and applying knowledge regarding ERP systems, it is still very difficult to achieve desirable outcomes without organizational support.

Individuals with sufficient motivation and support will be encouraged to apply absorptive capacity to make good use of ERP systems. Therefore, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 7. The degree of organizational support positively moderates the relationship between users capacity for understanding ERP systems and their performance of ERP usage.

Hypothesis 8. The degree of organizational support positively moderates the relationship between users capacity for assimilating ERP and their performance of ERP usage.

Hypothesis 9. The degree of organizational support positively moderates the relationship between users’ capacity for applying ERP and their performance of ERP usage.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

Data were collected from 245 users of 20 Korean companies that implemented ERP packages at least 1 year prior to our study. The companies were identified from various sources, ranging from academic journals to periodicals (such as Management and Computer, Chief Information Officer) and customer lists of ERP vendors.

We used structured questionnaires for data collection. The format and contents of these were developed from our literature review. Most measurement items were adopted from previous studies, thereby ensuring some level of reliability and validity. Since most of the literature was written in English, we had to translate questionnaire items carefully into Korean. The draft of the questionnaire was then pre-tested by 10 practitioners who were familiar with ERP implementation. We then interviewed each practitioner to validate the questionnaire items, continuing refining it until all 10 practitioners had fully agreed to the questionnaire items.

To find the right respondents, we searched and contacted people either in charge of ERP implementation or who had used ERP systems extensively. We explained the purpose of our survey in person.

The survey questionnaire was initially sent to 450 ERP users from 36 organizations directly or via e-mail to those who had agreed to cooperate. 1 or 2 weeks after we had distributed the questionnaires, we made a follow-up call or sent an e-mail to check for any difficulties in answering questions. After 2 months, 279 responses from 20 companies had arrived, representing a response rate of 62%. Of these, 34 responses were discarded due to incomplete data. A final, 245 questionnaires were used for the analysis. To examine for a non-response bias, we conducted a Chi-square test to see the difference in the distribution of firm size between 20 responding firms and 16 non-responding ones. We found no significant difference in the distribution of firm size between the two groups (Chi-square statistics = 0.15, degree of freedom = 1, $p > 0 . 5 )$

Demographics of the respondents and companies showed that 16 (80%) of the 20 companies were manufacturers, and 4 (20%) were service companies. Of the group, 7 (35%) had more than or equal to 5000 employees, 8 (40%) had more than or equal to a 1000, and 5 (25%) had less than a 1000 employees. The total sales of 15 firms (75%) were US\$ 80 million or more, and 4 (20%) had less than this. One (5%) firm did not give information on sales volume.

On hundred eighty-two (74%) of the respondents were male and 62 (25%) female. One person did not give information on gender. 222 (91%) were in their twenties and thirties and 18 (7%) in their forties. Five people did not give their ages. 192 (79%) had at least a bachelor degree but 51 (21%) did not. Two did not give information on this. There were 23 (9%) general managers and deputy general managers, and 219 (90%) managers or a lower level. 76 (31%) had a professional career for 5 years or more, while careers had between 1 and 5 years, distributed evenly. Nineteen (8%) respondents had been using ERP for more than 5 years, 164 (68%) for 1–5 years, and 59 (24%) for less than 1 year. There were 3 (1%) who did not give information on their positions, careers, or period of ERP usage.

Oracle was used by 101 people (41%), followed by SAP (56 people or 23%), Samsung SDS (40 people or 16%), and other packages (41 people or 17% in total). There were 7 people (3%) who did not give information on brands.

## 4.2. Measurement of variables

## 4.2.1. Absorptive capacity

The detailed questionnaire items are given in Appendix A. Six items were developed to measure absorptive capacity for understanding ERP systems (AC1). Two measured general knowledge (PRK1) and specific knowledge (PRK2) about ERP systems. Knowledge about ERP consulting firms was also considered an important component of prior knowledge. There were four items about ERP consulting firms, including general knowledge on consulting firms (CONK1) and consultants (CONK2) and specific knowledge on consulting firms (CONK3) and consultants (CONK4). Items about absorptive capacity in assimilating ERP systems (AC2) were based on selfefficacy (the state of having confidence in one’s capability to perform required activities successfully [5]). It determines the effort expended in learning [50]. Instead of measuring AC2 directly, we adopted selfefficacy as the proxy per Schunk [51,52] who examined the role of self-efficacy in the process of knowledge transfer. This is an important component of learning and requires understanding of internalized terminologies and frameworks by the knowledge recipient (i.e., AC2) [1]. Four items (EFF 1, 2, 3, 4) were selected as computer self-efficacy items per Compeau and Higgins [16] and Thatcher and Perrewe´ [59], and three items (EFF 5, 6, 7) were derived from the technical selfefficacy items of Gist et al. Five measurement items about the capacity for applying knowledge (AC3) were used. Two items measured the degree of exploiting ERP knowledge in performing tasks (ACAP 1, 2). Three items were developed for the capacity for sharing knowledge: two items dealt with the degree of sharing of ERP knowledge with people in the same department or in another department (SCAP 1, 2), and one item concerned the degree of sharing knowledge via ERP with other members of the organization (SCAP 3).

## 4.2.2. Performance of ERP usage

According to Zhang et al. [67], ERP systems implementation success can be measured on four dimensions: user satisfaction, individual impact, organizational impact, and intended business performance improvement. In our study, only the first two measures were considered, because our unit of analysis was individual. Individual impact includes improved individual productivity, task performance improvement, decision effectiveness and quality, and time to make a decision.

We developed five items to measure the increase in performance when using ERP (PER). Four items were derived from Igbaria et al. and Gefen et al. [23] in on the degree of improving job performance (PER1), enhancing productivity of the job (PER2), enhancing speed of performing tasks (PER3), and making it easier to perform tasks (PER4). In addition, the degree of overall satisfaction of ERP systems (PER 5) was included.

## 4.2.3. Degree of organizational support

Six items that measured organizational support (ORS) were used from Bingi et al. [6], and Igbaria et al: the management’s support for the use of ERP (ORS1), allocation of relevant resources (ORS2), assigning people to be in charge (ORS3), recruiting qualified personnel (ORS4), periodical training (ORS5), and rapid problem-shooting (ORS6).

## 4.3. Reliability and validity tests

Reliability and validity tests were conducted for each latent variable and indicator. Reliability of all the variables used was tested to ensure their stability using Cronbach’s alpha [31]. As can be seen in Table 2, three components of absorptive capacity, AC1, AC2, and AC3, displayed satisfactory levels of Cronbach’s alpha (0.91, 0.93, and 0.90, respectively). In addition, values for PER and ORS were 0.94 and 0.90, respectively.

Content and construct validity were then tested. Content validity was established by the adoption of constructs that passed the pilot test. We examined whether the questionnaire items were easy to answer, reflected their intended meanings, and fit the ERP context. Factor analysis by Varimax rotation was used to test the convergent validity. All the loading values of items for the relevant latent factors were greater than 0.5, verifying the convergent validity of all the items [27]. In addition, there was no serious cross-factor loading of items, so the discriminant validity at the item level was verified [12].

To investigate the construct-level discriminant validity, we calculated the weighted average of indicators to obtain the value of first order latent variables (PER and ORS). AC1, AC2, and AC3 were the second order latent factors, so they were calculated by the weighted average of their respective first order latent variables. The squared factor loadings were used as the weights. As shown in Table 3, the correlations among the variables were all significant, and the square roots of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct (i.e., diagonals in Table 3) were greater than 0.5, as were the correlations between the corresponding construct and others [4,9,22]. Therefore, construct-level discriminant validity was confirmed.

## 5. Results

Structured equation modelling (SEM) was used to test our research model and hypotheses. We used the SEM technique since we had two levels of latent variables in the model. AMOS 4.0 was used to test the fit of the research model, and PLS 3.0 was used to test the hypotheses.

## 5.1. Testing model fits

Before testing a hypothesis in an appropriate model, we checked the goodness-of-fit of our research model, where absorptive capacity had three components. We compared our model with a competing model that considered absorptive capacity as a single construct.

The results of comparing the two models are summarized in Table 4 with various indices of goodness-of-fit. The Chi-square difference between our research model and the competing model was significant (degree of freedom = 4, $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ in favour of our model. We also considered the comparative fit index (CFI) and the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), since they are not very sensitive to the sample size and TLI considers parsimony of the model [41]. If we had not considered parsimony, the model with a higher level of complexity would win because complexity produces a higher goodness-of-fit index. Our research model was more acceptable than the competing one; i.e., it is more appropriate to measure absorptive capacity by using three components than one.

Therefore, we concluded that ERP users who had greater prior knowledge of ERP systems could assimilate and apply their knowledge more effectively. The path between AC2 and AC3 was significant, supporting Hypothesis 6. This supported the recent classification of absorptive capacity as ‘‘potential’’ and ‘‘realized’’ absorptive capacity. Zahra and George [66] posited that the absorptive capacity for acquisition and assimilation of knowledge was potential, and the capacity for transformation and exploitation of knowledge was realized. Support for Hypothesis 6 in our study provided empirical evidence to support their argument.

## 5.2.2. Moderating effect of organizational support

The moderation effect of ORS was tested using a method proposed by Chin et al. [13] who suggested that a moderating effect in a SEM could be tested by creating a path from an interaction term between the independent variable and the moderator to the independent variable. Table 6 shows the results of testing moderating effects.

Model 1 in Table 6 shows the effect of independent variables and the moderator on the dependent variable, whereas Model 2, Model 3, and Model 4 show the effect of independent variables, the moderator, and their interaction variables on the dependent variable, respectively. The effects of interaction variables were not significant in Model 2 and Model 4, indicating that ORS does not moderate the influence of AC1 and AC3 on PER. Thus, Hypotheses 7 and 9 were not supported. However, the interaction effect on the dependent variable in Mode 3 was significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , implying that ORS moderated the effect of AC2 on PER. Therefore, our result provides support for Hypothesis 8.

## 5.3. Robustness analysis

Te examine whether our results held under different contexts, we re-examined our analysis by controlling for firm size, the ERP experience of the firm, and the professional career of the respondent. Firm size (measured in terms of sales volume) has been identified as an important external variable influencing ERP system adoption on organizational performance [18,20,40,54,55]. Studies have contended that large organizations adopt a gradual approach to ERP implementation, whereas small and medium sized companies take a big-bang approach.

The ERP experience of the firm was measured by the number of years that the respondent company had been using the system. Shang and Seddon found that more benefits were likely after additional experience with the system and pointed out that each organization had a different expected system life for enterprise systems. In other words, there should be a certain tipping point (time after ERP implementation) when the benefits started to emerge. Supporting this argument, Wieder et al. [62] empirically found that the longer the experience with ERP, the greater the overall performance of the firm.

## 6. Conclusion

An organization’s competitive advantage can be derived from knowledge resources of its employees. In order to effectively leverage the knowledge resources, they must be able to absorb new knowledge and apply it in their task environment.

We examine the effects of individual users’ absorptive capacity on their ERP usage. The transfer of knowledge required interactions between knowledge sources and recipients. However, it is virtually impossible for knowledge recipients of ERP systems to engage in direct interactions with the knowledge sources, since the systems do not describe their taskand process-related knowledge. Accordingly, it is critical for a recipient company be able to absorb the knowledge of their ERP systems.

Results of our empirical analysis were:

\- First, absorptive capacities for understanding, assimilating, and applying knowledge influence the performance of the ERP. The importance of capacity for understanding ERP through other components shows the importance of cumulative prior knowledge. Individual users can assimilate and apply new knowledge more effectively when they have greater prior knowledge. In addition, effective knowledge transfer requires an individual to understand acquired knowledge in the new context and synthesize it into their task environment. Furthermore, a positive effect of the capacity for applying ERP knowledge on performance also shows the need to apply new knowledge to the business processes to improve business performance.

\- Second, the absorptive capacity for understanding ERP systems does not affect their performance directly; this may not seem consistent with previous studies but the significant indirect effect shows that prior knowledge is the foundation for other components of absorptive capacity that affect performance of ERP. The capacity to understand ERP systems explained 16% of the total variance in performance of ERP. Therefore, our study did not refute previous findings that individuals’ absorptive capacity for understanding knowledge was an indispensable factor in enhancing organizational performance.

\- Third, the moderating role of organizational support was confined to the effect of the capacity to assimilate ERP knowledge on the performance. This result implied that the effect of the capacity to assimilate knowledge on the performance of ERP usage was stronger in firms with a higher degree of organizational support.

\- Finally, our results also imply that the capacity to assimilate knowledge was the most challenging component for overall absorptive capacity, so organizations need to make extra efforts to have individual users understand the value and functions of ERP systems from the perspective of their individual tasks. Organizations should make sure that the tight alignment between tasks and ERP systems is not only the issue of IS design, but also should be shared among ERP users when executing their tasks.

We proposed a new method of operationalizing and measuring absorptive capacity in the specific context of ERP systems. Although the definition and measures of absorptive capacity at the organizational level have appeared in previous research, few have approached the absorptive capacity of individuals for successful enterprise-wide implementation of IS.

In terms of contribution to practitioners, we found that organizations need to accumulate sufficient prior knowledge of ERP systems in advance of ERP adoption. In many cases, recipient firms tended to implement ERP adoption in two stages: first, from ERP consultants to the most knowledgeable task force team (TFT) members and then from these knowledgeable users to others in the organization. However, such an approach may create barriers between TFT members and other users. Therefore, all organizational members need to share prior knowledge in advance of ERP adoption.
