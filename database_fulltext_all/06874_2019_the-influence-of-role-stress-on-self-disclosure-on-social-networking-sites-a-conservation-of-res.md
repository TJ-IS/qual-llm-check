# The influence of role stress on self-disclosure on social networking sites: A conservation of resources perspective

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Role stress Conservation of resources Self-presentation Motivation for relationship maintenance Self-disclosure

## A B S T R A C T

There is a growing body of research about the influence of users’ perceived stress on their social networking site (SNS) usage behaviors. In general, stress negatively leads to a reduction in SNS usage (e.g., discontinuous use and self-disclosure). However, very little research has examined how SNS users strive to resolve stress problems from a positive perspective. To fill this gap, we conducted a research study among users of Moments, a large SNS in China. Based on the conservation of resources (COR) theory, we hypothesized that SNS users’ response to role stress, a subtype of stress, might be positive, leading to investments in social resources (e.g., motivation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation) and generating an increased level of self-disclosure on SNS. The survey results revealed the mediating efect of motivation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation on the SNS stress–disclosure relationship. We found that SNS users conserve their resources by maintaining relationships and presenting themselves positively in response to role stress, as predicted. Theoretical contributions and practical implications of the study are discussed, as are its limitations and directions for future re search.

## 1. Introduction

Social networking sites (SNSs) continue to enjoy great popularity and growth throughout the world. Given their popularity, they have received much attention from researchers and practitioners. However, evidence increasingly shows that SNSs are associated with negative outcomes, such as addiction [1]. social overload [2], obsessive-com pulsive disorders [3], cyber harassment and bullying [4,5], and the phenomenon of interest of our study—stress, which is associated with many negative outcomes [6–8]. As such, considerable research has reported on users’ negative perceptions (e.g., social media fatigue, strain, emotional exhaustion, and dissatisfaction) of SNS stress and passive behavioral outcomes [8–12]. In general, these studies examined stress from a negative perspective and indicated that stress negatively afects SNS usage. However, Berger et al. [13] and Zhang et al. [12] argued that this stream of research was limited because it ignored knowledge about the psychological underpinnings and motivational component underlying SNS stress and SNS use (cf., [14]). IS (information systems)

research has called for additional studies on SNS stress, and further efort is required to understand the outcomes of SNS stress.

Inspired by some studies conducted from the positive perspective [15–17], negative events have the potential to generate alternative solutions and action plans that may generate active behaviors (e.g., selfdisclosure) from SNS users [18]. Moreover, according to the literature, stress does not necessarily lead to passive responses (e.g., disengagement) [19,20]. One study reported that although SNSs make users feel bad, they still use such sites [21]. This led us to focus on SNS stress from a positive perspective [22]. In particular, self-disclosure as one kind of active behavior from SNS users is a positive sign to SNS providers and the key to the functioning and thriving of SNSs [23]. As such, we expected to provide an empirical example of how to assess stress and its influences on self-disclosure on SNSs. We aimed to identify how perceived stress may positively influence SNS users’ specific motivations (e.g., certain psychological processes), leading to an increased level of self-disclosure on SNSs. Few studies have empirically investigated the influence of role stress, a subtype of stress, on self-disclosure through

SNSs from a positive perspective. We thus aimed to bridge this gap by more deeply exploring the role stress and self-disclosure phenomena, which may provide more insights and understanding of SNS interpersonal processes. Thus, our research question was “How does SNS users’ role stress afect their self-disclosure from a positive perspective?”

To address the research gaps in the existing literature, we built our hypotheses in light of conservation of resources (COR) theory. The COR theory proposes that individuals attempt to select behaviors appropriately based on existing resources to maximize returns and minimize further resource loss [24,25]. Notably, per a key assumption of the COR theory, it is likely that users could choose to discontinue use of an SNS to avoid further loss when they are experiencing SNS stress. However, the efect of motivation has not been adequately positioned in the stress–discontinue use relationship. Another principle of the COR theory, resources investment [26], means that “people invest resources in order to protect against resource loss, to recover from losses, and to gain resources” ([27], p. 63). Moreover, motivations are goal-directed [28]. They represent investment of resources according to the COR theory [29] and are essential in determining individuals’ self-disclosure [30,31].

Consequently, in our study, we linked motivation in the role stress–self-disclosure relationship. We hypothesized that in the context of role stress, people may respond by having the intent to invest in social resources (e.g., present themselves positively and maintain friendships), not just to escape (i.e., discontinue use), subsequently generating an increased level of self-disclosure. Specifically, we propose a mediation efect of certain motivations (i.e., investment of resources) on the relationship between role stress and self-disclosure. This is useful because it unravels how role stress evokes users’ psychological processes (i.e., motivations) [28], ultimately afecting their voluntary actions (e.g., self-disclosure), a process that has been neglected in pre vious SNS research.

This study contributes to the growing body of IS research that investigates the SNS stress–disclosure relationship from a positive side. It also extends the research domain on the role stress outcomes of self disclosure on SNSs from a COR perspective. Practically, our study provides new insights for both SNS users and providers. Indeed, negative stress from SNSs may motivate users to strive to fulfill motivational goals, as users may want to protect existing resources to obtain desirable relational outcomes. This dysfunctional outcome is useful for SNS providers, who need to improve SNS functionality and interface cap abilities to enhance active behaviors (e.g., self-disclosure) on SNSs. Conversely, it helps users to better understand the mechanisms behind self-disclosure behavior under role stress conditions.

Next, we first review the research on self-disclosure and role stress. We then discuss how the COR theory can be applied to examine the link between role stress and SNS self-disclosure. Then, we build our research on the basis of the COR theory to investigate how two types of investment of resources (i.e., higher motivation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation) motivate users to adjust their SNS self disclosure behavior in response to role stress (i.e., role overload and role conflict).

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Self-disclosure

Self-disclosure has been defined as “to what extent individuals vo luntarily and intentionally reveal about themselves to others – in cluding thoughts, feelings and experiences” ([32], p. 183). It is a for mative scale with five dimensions: amount, depth, honesty, intent, and valence [32]. These dimensions are based on Wheeless’s conceptualization [33,34]. Amount refers to the degree of frequency and duration of an individual’s disclosures; depth refers to the degree of intimacy in the communication; honesty refers to the degree of accuracy with which one communicates information about oneself; intent refers to the degree of an individual’s control and awareness over his or her self-disclosure; and valence refers to the degree of positivity of the information disclosed in communication ([32], p. 183). Self-disclosure generally contributes to the formation of intimate relationships and increases social recognition and acceptance of individuals’ thought [35–37]. The research on SNS has often focused on why people disclose and how to enhance active disclosure behaviors, such as sharing information and status updates [18]. In the current study, we selected all five dimensions of self-disclosure and modified them to our SNS context to explore how SNS users recover from role stress situations to enhance self-disclosure.

## 2.2. Role stress

Role stress [38] refers to when “there is lack of clarity regarding the scope of one’s responsibilities, when one is given more roles than one can handle, or when one is faced with contradictory requirements from diferent aspects of one’s role or from diferent people that one interacts with” ([39], p. 306). It is a second-order construct that includes role overload and role conflict ([39], p. 317). Role overload is defined as the degree to which the resources an individual currently has cannot meet all others’ role expectations [40]. For example, an SNS user may have a high perception of role overload if he/she feels incapable of addressing all available conversations [41]. Role conflict, in contrast, refers to role expectations that are inconsistent and mutually incompatible [42]. When a person assumes a role with particular norms, standards, or values, and role expectations from others are incompatible with these norms, standards, or values, role conflict occurs [43].

To date, within IS research, many scholars have postulated that interpersonal conflict and work–family conflict afect users’ perceptions of using information technologies [44–46]. One study insisted that role overload and role conflict are critical sources of privacy concerns in SNSs [47]. In addition, some studies have suggested that when SNS users feel overburdened by taking on too many roles in an SNS, they may experience conflicting expectations from their peers; hence, as overload feelings and role conflict beliefs emerge, users may choose to withdraw from using the SNSs [2,6,48]. Using the COR theory as a foundation, we postulate that role stress from SNS use could lead to an increased level of self-disclosure when users conserve resources to fight the threat of role stress by seeking support from their network, not just “running way.” Next, we explain how we relate role stress with a specific type of investment of resources.

## 2.3. Role stress and its relationship with investment of resources

This study is based on the COR model [24,25], which is commonly used to explain stress phenomena in a range of settings [49]. It is a resource-oriented model that proposes that people are motivated to accumulate, retain, and protect their existing resources (conservation) and to acquire new resources (acquisition). Resources are things people value, such as objects, conditions, energies, and states [50]. Hobfoll [25] divided resources into five primary categories: energy resources (e.g., time, money, and knowledge), work resources (e.g., status or reputation), material resources (e.g., financial stability), personal resources (e.g., optimism), and interpersonal resources (e.g., friendship and feeling valuable to others). The COR model proposes that individuals attempt to select behaviors appropriately based on existing resources to maximize returns and minimize further resource loss [24,25]. Many studies have successfully applied the COR theory (e.g., [51–53]). For instance, Halbesleben and Bowler [54] demonstrated that a higher stressor (e.g., emotional exhaustion) generates higher communion striving (i.e., energy directed at “obtaining acceptance in personal relationship and getting along with others”) ([55], p. 44), in which an investment in resources to obtain social support through social exchange occurs; the authors concluded that employees working under emotional exhaustion intentionally select the type of motivational resources and invest in them to attain performance goals. In recent years, the COR theory has become a major theory in positive psychology [56], and the literature suggests that motivation can serve as an energy resource to obtain valued resources in response to stress [27,57].

Mitchell [28] defined motivation as “those psychological processes involved with the arousal, direction, intensity, and persistence of voluntary actions that are goal directed” (p. 60). Motivations are goaldirected [28]; they also represent investment of resources according to the COR theory [29]. Motivations stimulate, guide, and maintain behavior [58]. Within SNS research, there are various motivational factors influencing self-disclosure behavior. Motivation for relationship maintenance and motivation for relationship initiation have been suggested as key motivational elements influencing online social network selfdisclosure [59]. From a motivational perspective, Krasnova et al. [30] indicated that the convenience of maintaining and developing relationships, enjoyment, and privacy risks are related to users’ self-dis closure in online communities. Based on social exchange theory, Liu et al. [31] proposed that the convenience of relationship maintenance, self-presentation, relationship building, enjoyment and trust in service providers, perceived anonymity of self, and perceived risk are the motivations for self-disclosure. As suggested by Halbesleben et al. [26], the definition of resources should be aligned closely with other moti vation theories, and resources in the COR theory should be consistent with other constructs from motivation theories. Because a specific coping mechanism driven by specific motivations may motivate SNS users to conserve their resources, we examined the possibility that certain motivational factors (i.e., investment of resources) might in crease SNS self-disclosure in a model of role stress.

It is well established that when individuals are experiencing negative events, they attempt to conserve and seek key social resources, such as interpersonal relationships (e.g., friendship and feeling valuable to others) [55.60.611. Within SNS conditions. it is generally accepted that self-presentation is an individual’s motivation to get others to accept him or her [62,63]. Relationship maintenance refers to individuals' motivation to keep in touch and maintain social bonds with others [64,65]. These two motivations are consistent with investment in social resources and are available to SNS users. Moreover, self-presentation motivates presenters to employ protective strategies to avoid negative outcomes [66,67], and constructive responsiveness from the interaction of friends on SNSs plays an important role in increasing users’ feeling of connection [68.69], which in turn facilitates healthier relational out comes [70]. George et al. [71] showed that when individuals are ex periencing stress, socializing on popular SNSs can help them relieve or avoid unpleasant feelings. Obtaining acceptance from and getting along with others can be regarded as positive social-bonding motives [72]. and they both motivate users to disclose information on SNSs.

In summary, from the perspective of interpersonal relationships, self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance have been selected as motivational mechanisms between role stress and self disclosure. On the basis of the COR theory, we postulate that role stress from SNS use could increase the likelihood of self-disclosure when individuals invest in social resources (e.g., self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance) to thwart the threat of further resource loss. Such an examination may help reveal the relationship between role stress and SNS self-disclosure, while the recovery mechanisms underpinning unfavorable stress and stress responses could also be investigated.

## 3. Hypotheses

In this section, we first discuss how role overload and role conflict are two subconstructs of role stress that increase self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance. Second, we explain the positive influence of self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance on self-disclosure behaviors. Third, we explain the mediating role of self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance on the relationship between role stress and self-disclosure. Our theoretical model is shown in Fig. 1.

## 3.1. Role stress and self-presentation

When individuals experience role overload, they can only convey information with limited time and resources [73] and are unable to cope with multiple role requirements [41]. When members of a social network have diferent or conflicting expectations about the behavior of an individual, the individual will experience role conflict [41,74,75]. Individuals who find themselves experiencing role overload and role conflict will increase their attempts to build personal resources [16,76,77] because these resources can be utilized in response to negative situations [78].

Notably, a study conducted by Koch et al. [16] suggested that to reduce the pressure imposed by multiple role expectations and conflicting role activities, SNS users will generate positive emotions and attempt to express themselves in a way that will obtain social support and thereby gain personal resources in the online community. Selfpresentation as individuals’ intentional impression management [79] is a purposive control possibility that allows for users to manage and control information to deliver the desired information to others [80]. It is pivotal to the establishment and development of social resources (i.e., relationships) [67]. Therefore, we postulate that SNS users experiencing role stress within the communication process will present desirable information about themselves as a control possibility or personal resource investment and reveal personal information to others [81]. Because role stress increases an individual’s self-presentation, we proposed the following:

H1a. Role stress is positively associated with self-presentation

## 3.2. Role stress and motivation for relationship maintenance

When users feel a sense of resource loss of their social networks on SNSs, role stress results. Although previous studies have suggested that SNS users distance themselves from using SNS or switch to another SNSs in response to being threatened, the majority of users may still continue to use the SNSs because the functions of SNSs are powerful resources for them [14]. In efect, social psychologists have proposed that everyone is eager to maintain a healthy relationship with some people through frequent positive interactions and frequent social contact [64], which SNSs facilitate [3]. Motivation for relationship maintenance is a fundamental and pervasive desire among human beings aimed at maintaining social bonds with others [64,65]. Given the importance of maintaining relationships in social groups, it is highly possible that SNS users will remain connected with established friends and participate in events to ensure social exchange, which may reduce further resource loss due to role stress.

Moreover, the social features of SNSs help social resource (i.e., relationship) creation, as users can seek someone to resolve stressful problems. Simple assistance in problem solving makes communally oriented users feel more connected [82]. Friends who are not involved in the problem can also provide a sense of support [61]. Accordingly, we postulate that although SNS users may feel resource loss due to role stress, they may be motivated to secure social bonding with their SNS network to gain social resources and obtain support from their friend ships. In summary, role stress is expected to lead to higher motivation for relationship maintenance. Thus, we postulated the following:

H2a. Role stress is positively associated with motivation for relationship maintenance.

3.3. Self-presentation, motivation for relationship maintenance, and selfdisclosure

As our self-disclosure literature review indicates, SNS users are more likely to present information when they reciprocate, interact, and communicate with SNS friends to maintain relationships in response to role stress because doing so will validate their social identity and the value of using SNSs [83,84]. Self-presentation is the main driving force for people to participate in the online community [85,86]. Self-presentation is an important motivation for self-disclosure, as SNS users may present their achievements, feelings, experiences, and status up dates and participate in online communities to make themselves more attractive ([31], p. 56). Therefore, we propose that users who are likely to present themselves should disclose more personal information.

Moreover, SNS users tend to disclose more information to friends to maintain relationships [59]. An individual’s desire to gain social support is a strong motivation for SNS usage [87], and the desire to maintain established relationships with SNS friends is one of the strongest motivations for individuals to stay with their SNSs [83.88–91]. The available features and functionalities of SNSs make them convenient for users to disclose information, as users wish to present more information to maintain relationships. Users motivated by relationship maintenance tend to make more honest, intentional, and positive self-disclosures [59,92]. Higher maintenance of a relationship is generally associated with a higher level of self-disclosure in the amount, depth, intent, valence, and honesty dimensions. Therefore, we hypothesized the following:

H1b. Self-presentation is positively associated with self-disclosure.

H2b. Motivation for relationship maintenance is positively associated with self-disclosure.

3.4. Relationships among role stress, self-presentation, motivation for relationship maintenance, and self-disclosure

According to the COR theory, although stress leads to lower performance, dissatisfaction, and decreased ICT usage, instrumental appropriation and commitment of resources can help minimize additional resource losses [24,26,54]. Motivation can be regarded as resources that serve to mediate the relationship between stress and stress outcomes [19,54]. Whereas stress outcomes include behavioral outcomes [27,57,93]; self-disclosure is a key behavioral outcome (i.e., revealing emotions and thoughts) in personalized IS and human–computer interaction systems [94–97]. In this manner, self-disclosure served as an outcome variable to measure SNS users’ information-sharing behaviors in the current study. Furthermore, we postulated that self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance may mediate the relationship between role stress and SNS self-disclosure. That is, under role stress conditions, SNS users will attempt to protect existing resources to manage impressions or improve their status to bufer negative responses or minimize further resource loss resulting from role stress; they may show adaptive motivational (i.e., self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance) strategies by changing themselves to become more socially attractive [19,79]. Based on the above postulations, we hypothesized the following:

H1c. The relationship between role stress and self-disclosure is mediated by self-presentation.

H2c. The relationship between role stress and self-disclosure is mediated by motivation for relationship maintenance.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

We chose to test our hypotheses among users of a large SNS in China called Moments. Moments (a SNS within WeChat) is one of China’s most popular SNS. It was launched in 2011 and now has over 64.5 million active users per month, a figure that continues to increase [98]. The study was conducted through an online questionnaire. The survey links were sent to people through SNS platforms, such as QQ (instant message service) and university bulletin board systems in China. To encourage participation, each participant was rewarded with 15 RMB after completing the questionnaire. After two weeks, 410 responses were collected: 75 respondents were excluded because they did not fully complete the survey. We used 335 completed questionnaires for the following data analysis.

Of the 335 responses, 148 (44.2%) respondents were male and 187 (55.8%) were female. The average age of respondents was 24.86 (s.d. = 4.98) years; 39 (11.7%) respondents were under 20 years old, 284 (85.8%) respondents were aged 21–39 years, and 12 (2.5%) respondents were 40 years old or older. The demographic characteristics of the respondents are illustrated in Table 1.

## 4.2. Measures

The measurement items were adopted from the previous theoretical and empirical literature and modified to fit the SNS context (as shown in Appendix A). They were measured on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.” Role overload was adopted from Peterson et al. [99], Ho et al. [73], Tarafdar et al. [39] and Zhang et al. [47]. Role conflict was adapted from Rizzo et al. [100], Moore [101], Rutner et al. [102], Tarafdar et al. [39] and Zhang et al. [47]. Role stress was modeled as a second-order construct based on Tarafdar et al. [39]. Self-presentation was adopted from Krasnova et al. [30] and Walther et al. [103]. Motivation for relationship maintenance was adopted from Park et al. [59]. Self-disclosure, a formative construct that includes the amount, depth, intent, valence, and honesty dimensions, was adopted from Posey et al. [32] and modified to fit our Moments context.

Because the study was conducted in a Chinese SNS context, we first had the measurement items translated into Chinese by four bilingual PhD students. Afterward, the measurement items were translated into English by another bilingualist. After comparing the two English versions, we made some adjustments. Before sending out the survey, the questionnaires were reviewed by several researchers, who were doctoral students and Moments users. Further modifications were made in response to their feedback. Finally, the questionnaires were distributed to Moments users.

## 5. Data analysis and results

In the research model, role stress and self-disclosure were conceptualized as two formative second-order constructs, while the two dimensions of self-presentation and motivation for relationship main tenance were conceptualized as two reflective constructs, as in the literature [32,39]. Each second-order construct was modeled as a for mative construct consisting of multiple subconstructs with reflective items. We constructed a factor score to consolidate the reflective items of each subconstruct of role stress and self-disclosure. Many studies use factor scores to simplify the research model [104–107]. Because there are five subconstructs for self-disclosure [32] and two subconstructs for role stress [39], the number of items for self-disclosure and role stress were reduced from 21 and 11 to 5 and 2, respectively. A similar ex ample can be found in Mithas et al. [108] and Sun et al. [106]. SmartPLS Version 2.0 was used to analyze our model because it is readily applicable to both formative and reflective constructs [109,110].

## 5.1. Results of the measurement model

First, we followed Petter et al. [111] to assess the measurement model of formative constructs. The validity of the formative constructs was assessed by testing the item weights and loadings [112]. Item weights and loadings suggest the relative and absolute importance of the items, respectively. The data analysis results showed strong factorial validity. Because of space limitations, the results are not shown. The reliability of formative constructs was examined using a multicollinearity assessment. As the variance inflation factors (VIFs) of amount, depth, intent, honesty, and valence directed at self-disclosure were below 3.0, and the VIFs of role overload and role conflict directed at role stress were below 3.0 (as presented in Appendix E), we concluded that our model did not sufer from multicollinearity [111].

Second, we examined the validity and reliability of the reflective constructs. Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The cross-loadings table shows that most of the item loadings were higher on each item’s own factor than on the other factors, thereby indicating strong discriminant validity for all the indicators [113]. Discriminant validity can also be assessed by comparing the correlations and square roots of AVEs (average variance extracted), in line with previous studies [114–116]. As the fourth item of role overload, the second item of self-disclosure (valence), the fifth item of self-disclosure (amount), and the third item of self-disclosure (honesty) demonstrated poor loadings, these four items were ruled out in the subsequent analysis. Appendix B shows the item loadings. Next, in the AVE test, the results showed satisfactory discriminant validity (see Table 2): The AVE values for all subconstructs were above 0.5, and the square root of the AVE for each variable was greater than its correlation with other variables (as shown in Table 2, the diagonal with underlining is the square root of AVE). Additionally, analysis of the data indicated high levels of reliability: All composite reliabilities of the constructs were above 0.7 (as shown in Table 2), demonstrating that they were internally consistent [117]. Furthermore, descriptive statistics (see Appendix C) were employed to provide a general illustration of the population using the characterized constructs. For example, the results showed that role stress had a mean of 4.61 (s.d. = 0.89), which indicates that the average Moments user experienced a certain degree of role stress.

## 5.3. Mediating efects test

We performed a mediation analysis to test whether self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance mediated between role stress and self-disclosure following Sobel’s approach [118]. Two PLS models were performed to obtain the required path coeficients and standard errors. In the first PLS model. the independent variable was role stress, and the dependent variables were the two mediators. In the second PLS model, role stress and the two mediators were independent variables. and self-disclosure was the dependent variable. The results suggested that the two mediating variables, self-presentation and mo tivation for relationship maintenance, significantly mediated the efects of role stress on self-disclosure. Specifically, the efect of role stress on self-disclosure was significantly mediated by self-presentation (Sobel = 2.019, p < 0.05), and the efect of role stress on self-disclosure was significantly mediated by motivation for relationship maintenance (Sobel = 5.046, p < 0.001). In summary, H1c and H2c were supported (see Table 3). Furthermore, role stress insignificantly afected self-disclosure (β = 0.081, p＞0.05) in the full proposed model.

## 6. Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrate that SNS users under role stress use constructive strategies to carefully invest in their remaining personal resources to obtain valued resources (e.g., interpersonal relationships) to help themselves recover from stress.

Specifically, the present study showed that role stress is associated with self-presentation, which leads to a higher level of self-disclosure. Thus, hypotheses 1a and 1b were supported. The findings also indicate that SNS users maintain established relationships with their SNS friends as another psychological coping mechanism for role stress because they want to ensure social exchange with and receive social support from their SNS friends. Role stress is positively associated with motivation for relationship maintenance, which is consequently associated with a higher level of self-disclosure. Thus, hypotheses 2a and 2b were supported. In summary, our study has shown that the relationship between role stress and self-disclosure is mediated by self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance, which serve as motivational resources to motivate users to disclose information on SNSs. In sum, hypotheses 1c and 2c were supported.

Notably, a review of the COR theory posits that much more research should be conducted regarding how people invest in resources to make decisions in response to environmental cues [26]. The relationship between role stress and self-disclosure not only requires a deeper exploration of the concept of investment in resources but must also match the tenets (e.g., resources investment) and dynamic nature of the COR theory. Because resources vary depending on the situation, the resources examined should be relevant to the specific context—for instance, an SNS setting. Interpersonal resources (e.g., friendship and feeling valuable to others) have thus far been measured inconsistently within the SNS context, but along with motivational theory, that might promote a better understanding of resource conservation processes. Consequently, role stress can be better treated or prevented. In general, from the perspective of interpersonal relationships, this study extends Hobfoll’s COR framework [50] to individual users of SNSs by identifying two motivational factors in terms of their roles in attaining selfdisclosure.

## 7. Theoretical contributions and practical implications

This study may be the first to have examined the mediating role of investment in social resources (i.e., motivation for relationship main tenance and self-presentation) on the relationship between role stress (i.e., role overload and role conflict) and SNS self-disclosure. We posit that SNS users carefully present themselves in positive ways and constructively maintain existing relationships as a response to perceived role stress to gain certain benefits, rather than discontinuing their SNS usage (e.g., decrease in self-disclosure). Within IS, extensive research has noted that users under stress who use a particular SNS will switch to other SNS platforms or discontinue using SNSs [8,48]. The stress lit erature describes disengagement (i.e., escape strategies) in negative terms [119,120]. Few studies have investigated the influence of stress on SNS use from the positive perspective. This study provides an example to identify the potential of negative events to engender alter native solutions and action plans, which generate active behavioral outcomes from the positive aspect. This diferent view is contributing to extensive development of IS research.

Moreover, the findings of this study suggest how the COR framework could be applied to examine the SNS stress–disclosure relation ship. Specifically, we carefully leveraged the logic of the COR theory in explaining the role stress and self-disclosure relationship, which may contribute to emerging IS research on the SNS stress–disclosure relationship. First, to align COR with SNS self-disclosure, in the current study, we suggested that SNS users perform resource investment actions when anticipating active approaches; accordingly, two forms of investments in social resources (self-presentation and motivation for re lationship maintenance) were selected in our study context. As we demonstrated, to socialize with others, SNS users tend to align the values of conversation in a positive process in role stress conditions. Role stress can lead to investments in existing social resources and generate an increased level of self-disclosure. Notably, the results of this study provide evidence of the mediating efects of self-presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance on the relationship between users role stress (i.e., role overload and role conflict) and self-disclosure (i.e., amount, depth, intent, honesty, and valence). This mediation has profound theoretical implications, as it can help researchers better un derstand causal theoretical model development and makes it possible to eficiently design interventions.

On the practical side, our study provides new insights for both SNS users and providers to understand role stress and self-disclosure phenomena. It can help SNS users better understand how to prevent and recover from role stress conditions. This study suggests that role stress does not necessarily result in a decrease in self-disclosure. It is possible for some users to engage in investment in social resources (i.e., moti vation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation) to seek social bonding and support in response to role conflict and role overload in their SNSs. In addition, this study provides useful suggestions for SNS providers to enhance self-disclosure by improving SNS functionalities to help users prevent and recover from role stress conditions. As discussed earlier, role stress may motivate SNS users to strive to fulfill desirable motivational goals, as they may want to protect existing resources to prevent further loss according to the COR theory. Indeed, SNS providers might focus on improving interface capabilities for users to engage in investment in social resources $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ motivation for relationship main tenance and self-presentation) to trigger the proactive recovery mechanisms underlying self-disclosure behavior in role stress conditions. Providers could make it more convenient for SNS users to maintain relationships and present personal information rather than aligning mechanisms for resolving and avoiding stress problems in SNS use from a passive perspective.

## 8. Limitations and future research directions

This study has some limitations that point to future research op portunities. First, a longitudinal study would be useful to test the variance of self-disclosure and to ascertain the relationship between role stress and self-disclosure on SNSs. The second limitation is related to the generalizability of our sample and our selection of the Moments platform. Our samples were limited to Chinese Moments users with similar educational backgrounds because our questionnaire was distributed among the university students; future research could be extended to larger areas, diferent educational and socioeconomic back grounds, and other SNS platforms. Third, the mean values of role overload and role conflict appeared to be in the high 4 s on a scale of 7, which is a medium range on its scale. It is quite possible that when role stress increases to a much higher level, its associations with the self presentation and motivation for relationship maintenance could change, for instance, in the opposite direction. To this end, we encourage studies to further contextualize the role stress within diferent ranges when examining users’ responses to role stress on SNS. Our final limitation is related to the dependent variable; to increase realism, future research could examine SNS self-disclosure through text mining or other data collection techniques.

## 9. Conclusion

This study may be the first to have examined the mediating role of motivation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation on the relationship between role stress (i.e., role overload and role conflict) and SNS self-disclosure based on the COR theory. We highlighted the positive perspective of role stress, demonstrating that SNS users constructively respond to SNS role stress by conserving and investing in their social resources (i.e., motivation for relationship maintenance and self-presentation) to recover social bonding with their friends rather than discontinuing their SNS self-disclosure. This study has significant practical implications for both SNS users and providers, ofering valuable insights for utilizing appropriate role stress situations to enhance disclosure on SNSs. In summary, our findings provide a venue for future IS research on the SNS stress–disclosure relationship.
