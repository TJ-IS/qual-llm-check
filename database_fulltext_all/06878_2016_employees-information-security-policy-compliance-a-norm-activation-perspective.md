## a b s t r a c t

This study explores the role of norms in employees' compliance with an organizational information security policy (ISP). Drawing upon norm activation theory, social norms theory, and ethical climate literature, we propose a model to examine how ISP-related personal norms are developed and then activated to affect employees' ISP compliance behavior. We collected our data through Amazon Mechanical Turk for hypothesis testing. The results show that ISP-related personal norms lead to ISP compliance behavior, and the effect is strengthened by ISPrelated ascription of personal responsibility. Social norms related to ISP (including injunctive and subjective norms), awareness of consequences, and ascription of personal responsibility shape personal norms. Social norms related to ISP are the product of principle ethical climate in an organization.

## 1. Introduction

An information security policy (ISP) of an organization defines a set of rules and policies related to employee access and use of organizational information assets. It usually describes employees' responsibilities and consequences of policy violations [1,2]. Regardless of the breadth and clarity, without employees' compliance, an ISP cannot serve as an effective countermeasure to information security problems. Therefore, how to motivate employees to comply with the ISP in an organization has received a lot of attention [3–7].

Because employees often work in a group or team within an organization, their behavior or opinions are likely to be influenced by others [8]. In exploring employees' compliance with the ISP, social norms in the form of normative beliefs [1,9], social pressure [10], social influence [11], and subjective norms [12,13] have been recognized as important factors. However, as Torgler [14] state, “when working with social norms, we have the difficulty of specifying their exact meaning” (p. 663). Similarly, the information security literature does not unanimously define or categorize social norms. Some studies refer to them as subjective norms or social pressure from peers and superiors [15], others refer to them as descriptive and subjective norms [1,13], and in some cases, normative beliefs and social pressure have been used interchangeably. Existing studies commonly emphasize external motivators that directly influence behavior. Yet, how such influences are internalized and consequently alter ISP compliance behavior has been barely investigated.

There are two general types of norms for an employee in an organization: social and personal norms [16]. Social norms, grounded in social interactions among individuals, broadly define behavioral rules based on common beliefs about how people should act in a particular situation. They indirectly promote a group's well-being through informal rewards and sanctions founded on group expectations. On the other hand, personal norms are private and internalized norms grounded in one's beliefs and values. They influence behavior when an individual feels their most important values are endangered [17]. Their rewards and sanctions are self-imposed [18]. Personal norms produce norm-based behavior [19], whereas social norms may affect behavior after an individual accepts and internalizes them as personal norms. In fact, the presence of personal norms diminishes the direct effect of social norms in explaining a behavior [20,21].

To broaden the understanding of the role that norms play in motivating ISP compliance behavior, this study proposes a research model rooted in norm activation theory [19], social norms theory [8], and ethical climate literature [22]. We argue that ISP-related personal norms are a determinant of ISP compliance behavior. Through the lens of norm activation theory [19] from sociological literature, we propose that both awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility in ISP compliance not only contribute to the development of ISPrelated personal norms but also strengthen the effect of ISP-related personal norms on ISP compliance behavior. Building off the theory of social norms [8], we argue that ISP-related personal norms are shaped partly via the internalization of social norms, including descriptive, injunctive, and subjective norms. Lastly, we propose that ISP-related social norms are the product of principle ethical climate.

The proposed research model integrates theories regarding norms from social psychology literature [8] to explain ISP compliance behavior. It recognizes the important role of ISP-related personal norms and explores how they are formed and activated. We tested the research model using data collected through Amazon Mechanical Turk, which comprises 201 valid responses from working professionals in a diverse set of organizations. Out results show that ISP-related personal norms lead to ISP compliance. Interestingly, ISP-related personal norms are more likely to influence ISP compliance behavior when employees feel personal responsibility for their ISP-related actions. In addition, ISPrelated injunctive and subjective norms, awareness of consequence, and ascription of personal responsibility shape ISP-related personal norms. And principle ethical climate plays an important role in forming ISP-related social norms.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the theoretical background. Section 3 develops the hypotheses, and Section 4 describes the research method. Next, Section 5 presents the research methodology and results. Finally, Section 6 discusses the theoretical contributions and practical implications as well as future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Norm activation theory

Norm activation theory [19] posits that personal norms are the immediate antecedent of one's behavior. Personal norms reflect an individual's commitment to their internalized values. They are the outcome of an individual's perception of social expectations during socialization, and will be perceived as the sense of moral obligation to engage in the specific behavior [19]. Personal norms are enforced through the anticipation of self-punishments and self-rewards, while social norms are anchored in a social group [19,23]. Accordingly, an individual may engage in socially acceptable behavior to avoid social sanctions, but they may continue following those norms due to internal motives, such as improving their self-image [24].

Norm activation theory claims that feelings of moral obligation are a proper means to measure personal norms, because obligations, like norms, precede actions [19]. In addition, moral obligation reflects past commitment, thereby carrying the possibility of sanctions, which are an integral part of norms [23]. Furthermore, it conveys that an individual is the source of obligations and sanctions [19]. In other words, feelings of moral obligation encompass all the aspects of personal norms including commitment to internalized values, self-sanctions, and the tendency to act.

The theory further suggests that only when personal norms are interpreted as relevant and specific to a situation in which they are formed [23], they will be activated [19,25]. Here, activation refers to directing an individual's focus to their beliefs in their decision-making process. While following personal norms may enhance self-regard and avoid self-criticism, it could also result in such costs as extra effort and time. If the benefits outweigh the costs, the behavior is more likely to happen [26]. However, if the costs exceed the benefits, or the costs and benefits are not clear, an individual may experience conflicts in their decision-making [23]. To mitigate the conflict, an individual may employ defense mechanisms to weaken their feelings of moral obligation by redefining their perception of the situation [23]. Denial of consequences and denial of personal responsibility for the act are the common defense mechanisms [26]. The former involves underestimating the negative outcomes of an action to justify it [27, 28], while the latter involves considering the action in question as beyond one's control or outside one's realm of responsibility [27,28]. If implemented completely, these defense mechanisms allow neutralization of moral obligation without imposing self-sanctions [26]. To overcome such defense mechanisms, two conditions are required. First, an individual must realize that their behavior affects the welfare of others, which is known as awareness of consequences. Second, an individual must accept personal responsibility for the consequences of their behavior. This is termed ascription of personal responsibility. When these conditions are met, defense mechanisms become less effective and personal norms are more likely to be activated, causing a feeling of personal obligation to regulate the behavior [23,29].

At the same time, it is also theoretically plausible that both factors (i.e., awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility) directly contribute to the formation of personal norms [29–33]. It may be difficult to feel morally obligated for an action without feeling responsible for it or knowing the influence of that action on others. The more an individual appreciates the consequence of an action on others, the more likely they develop personal norms related to the action [19,29,33]. The more an individual accepts their responsibility for an action, the more they are expected to attend to the values that generate the action and consequently generate the feeling of moral obligation for the action [19,29,33].

Schwartz [19] originally developed norm activation theory to rationalize altruistic behavior. It was mostly applied in a proenvironmental context to explain pro-environmental behaviors, such as conservation behavior [34], pro-environmental buying [20], recycling [35], travel mode choice [36], and car use [37]. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to apply this theory in the context of information security.

## 2.2. Theory of social norms: personal norms formation

The feelings of moral obligation can be traced to social norms and to the situations under which they are learned [19]. Social norms are implicit and explicit rules and standards that members of a group learn through socialization—direct and indirect communication and interactions with others. They guide or restrain behavior through social sanctions, not the force of law, and can be categorized into descriptive, injunctive and subjective norms [8].

Descriptive norms are grounded in an individual's perception of how others act in a specific situation [8]. For example, people typically stand up and clap at the end of a concert. The desire to follow descriptive norms is rooted in amplifying the efficacy of social behavior. Descriptive norms help an individual determine the appropriate behavior in similar situations [38]. In other words, having noticed what others do in a similar situation, an individual perceives sufficient social support for a particular behavior in a new or uncertain situation [39].

Injunctive norms involve perceptions of approval and disapproval of certain behaviors —the moral rules of a group, and what “should” be done [38]. These behaviors often garner social acceptance or the approval of others [40]. The desire to follow injunctive norms is rooted in an individual's social nature and the tendency to build, develop, and maintain social relationships with others to gain resources and social support.

Subjective norms are defined as what an individual believes regarding what important others (e.g., family, friends, and supervisors) expect them to do in a specific situation [8]. The motivations to follow them are similar to those of injunctive norms: to build, develop, and maintain social relationships and particularly to earn others' approval. However, subjective norms guide an individual based on their perception of what those important to them expect, as opposed to what everyone else, in general, expects [8].

Social norms and cost of deviance in the group encourages members to act in ways that they consider other members think they “should.” The degree to which social norms regulate behavior depends on whether the individual has accepted and internalized them as opposed to just learning them [41]. Internalization refers to the process of an individual accepting a norm as intrinsically satisfying and in line with their own value system [24]. An individual who has internalized social norms is likely to conform to expected behaviors even when they are not observed or externally sanctioned [42]. Through internalization, social norms become personal norms [19,43].

## 2.3. Principle ethical climate

Principle ethical climate refers to employees' shared perception of the company procedures, practices, and policies [44]. At its core, organizational climate is composed of the shared perceptions of company routine procedures [44], which lead to the development of social norms and expectations [45]. Organizational climate typically has a particular focus, such as a climate for diversity [45], safety [46], or ethics [22]. Eth ical climate of an organization describes the moral atmosphere of the workplace, and principle ethical climate is an aspect of it. We include principle ethical climate in the model as it is consistent with the focus of this study, in which ISP compliance may be considered as a specific type of rule adherence behavior [47].

In an environment with high principle ethical climate, employees place a high priority on obeying rules, policies, and professional codes [22], which is factored into their ethical decision-making [48,49]. In other words, employees working in a high principle ethical climate have a positive regard for rule adherence and policy compliance. We contend that principle ethical climate creates a proper standard for ISP-related social norms, and consequently shapes them in terms of ISP compliance.

## 3. Hypothesis

Fig. 1 shows our research model, which consists of three parts. We propose that ISP-related personal norms have a positive influence on ISP compliance (Hypothesis 1). ISP-related awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility not only positively influence personal norms, but also positively moderate the effect of personal norms on ISP compliance (Hypothesis 2–5). Moreover, ISP-related descriptive, injunctive, and subjective norms lead to ISP-related personal norms (Hypothesis 6–8). Finally, principle ethical climate shapes ISPrelated descriptive, injunctive, and subjective norms (Hypothesis 9a–c).

## 3.1. Effect of personal norms

We define ISP-related personal norms as one's feelings of moral obligation to comply with the ISP. Adhering to ISP-related personal norms implies that an employee follows their own belief system when making compliance decisions related to the ISP [29]. According to rational choice theory [50,51], as employees with a high ISP-related personal norms are more likely to experience psychological benefits from complying with the ISP, they may consider ISP compliance behavior as a rewarding act and subsequently adopts it [52]. In other words, if an employee feels that following the ISP is their moral obligation and is psychologically rewarding (i.e. self enhancement), they will be more likely to comply with the ISP. Recent research in the information security literature also revealed that personal norms are a determinant factor that affects the way employees deal with organizational issues, including those related to appropriate computer behaviors and Internet use [53–55]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. ISP-related personal norms have a positive influence on ISP compliance.

We define ISP-related awareness of consequences as the awareness that an employee has regarding how their ISP-related behavior affects the welfare of their coworkers or the organization as a whole. The construct is different from information security awareness [1,56,57], which refers to an employee's general knowledge about requirements stated in security policies and their objectives. Our definition is more in the line with the overall assessment of consequence [1], except, in our definition, the focal group is the employee's coworkers or the organization, while as opposed to focusing on the individuals themselves.

We propose that the more an employee realizes the consequences of their ISP-related actions, the more likely they will accept obligations to follow ISP. The rationale here is that ISP-related personal norms are complex matters that require cognitive elaboration, and awareness of consequences can play a significant role in enhancing such elaboration [29]. In the absence of awareness of consequences, an employee may not even recognize that they are faced with a moral choice [58]. Thus, an employee who acknowledges that compliance can prevent endangering information assets in the organization, is more likely to develop a well-founded understanding of what to do, and hence feel morally obligated to comply with the ISP. Therefore, we propose that:

Hypothesis 2. ISP-related awareness of consequences has a positive influence on ISP-related personal norms.

On the one hand, an employee with a low awareness of consequences has a vague idea of the potential negative or positive outcomes of their acts on their coworkers or the organization. Such a low awareness allows an employee to underestimate the negative outcomes of their actions and as a result, justify denial of consequence. Thus, an

We define ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility as an employee's feelings of responsibility for the negative or positive consequences of their ISP-related behavior. Here, responsibility is the sense of connection with the well-being of the organization and coworkers. Responsibility becomes relevant when an employee recognizes that both they have the authority to perform appropriate ISP-related actions and they are bonded to the consequence of their ISP-related action. For instance, an employee who is required to change their password periodically will feel a responsibility to do so when they recognize both that they have the authority to change the password and that not changing the password compromises the organization and their job. Responsibility is different from the feelings of moral obligation [19]. Responsibility is concerned about an employee's relevant control over their actions and consequences in their assigned role (e.g., as an employee, I am responsible for protecting organization information assets), while moral obligation is related to an employee's feeling that they should act in line with their value system (e.g., I am morally obligated to comply with the ISP because rule compliance is part of my value).

The strength of feelings of moral obligation is a function of responsibility [19]. We propose that an employee who feels they have control over their ISP-related actions is more likely to generate feelings of moral obligation toward ISP compliance. The line of reasoning here is that when an employee recognizes that they have authority over their actions, they are more likely to feel that their effort will make a difference, and hence be motivated to generate the feelings of moral obligation [43]. In other words, an employee who recognizes that they are responsible for the damages if they do not comply with the ISP, may feel that their effort (i.e., complying with the ISP) can prevent such damages, and hence will feel morally obligated to comply with the ISP. Therefore, we hypothesize that<sup>1</sup>:

Hypothesis 4. ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility has a posi tive influence on ISP-related personal norms.

On the one hand, an employee with a low ascription of personal responsibility toward the ISP may feel neither authority over their actions nor a connection to the adverse consequences their ISP-related decisions may have on the welfare of others. Such a low ascription of personal responsibility allows an employee to conclude that they are not bound to the adverse consequences of their actions and thus justify denial of responsibility. Such an employee is more likely to use denial of responsibility to disregard their moral obligation in their ISP-related decisions [60]. Therefore, a low ascription of personal responsibility hinders the transition of ISP-related personal norms to ISP compliance. On the other hand, an employee with a high ascription of personal responsibility toward the ISP is more likely to recognize that they are in charge of their actions, thus being more likely to acknowledge the link between their ISP-related decisions and any repercussions. Such an employee is expected to be less likely to engage in the denial of responsibility [26]. Thus, a high ascription of personal responsibility facilitates a successful transition of ISP-related personal norms to ISP compliance. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 5. ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility positively moderates the effect of the ISP-related personal norms on ISP compliance.

## 3.2. Effect of social norms

We argue that ISP-related descriptive, injunctive, and subjective norms play a significant role in shaping an employee's personal perception of ISP compliance. If an employee perceives that “everybody follows the organization's ISP” (descriptive norms), “everybody thinks it is wrong to violate the organization's ISP” (injunctive norms), and/ or “their manager thinks it is wrong to violate the ISP” (subjective norms), then they are more likely to believe they are morally obligated to follow the ISP (personal norms) themselves. Prior studies have suggested the above line of reasoning in contexts such as proenvironmental buying [20], prosocial [29], and tax compliance [61].

We define ISP-related descriptive norms as an employee's perception of the majority of their coworkers' ISP compliance behavior. In general, descriptive norms arise from the idea that if everybody is doing a particular act in a particular situation, it must be an expected behavior [62,63]. Therefore, if a high number of employees respond to an ISPrelated situation in a similar way, an employee will perceive that particular way as the correct behavior [64]. The information security literature scarcely explored the influence of peer behavior as a motivational source for an individual's behavior in uncertain situations [65,66]. We argue that an employee, in an effort to be more accurate and efficient [8] in their ISP-related decisions, will observe their coworkers' behavior and will internalize the perceived behavior as the correct behavior in regard to ISP compliance. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 6. ISP-related descriptive norms are positively associated with ISP-related personal norms.

We define ISP-related injunctive norms as an employee's perception of what the majority of their coworkers think they should do in ISPrelated situations. The information security literature highlighted the importance of injunctive norms, and showed that they can in uence ISP-related behavior [13]. Thus, in addition to observing the majority of coworkers' behavior (ISP descriptive norms), employees can recognize reasonable and acceptable ISP behavior through their perception of the majority of coworkers' expectations (ISP injunctive norms). Social benefits or penalties are behind the motivation to follow injunctive norms [8]. An employee's perception of the majority's values (injunctive norms) will shape their perception of ISP compliance behavior. An employee who perceives that ISP compliance is what the majority believes should be done is more likely to internalize ISP compliance behavior as part of their values system in order to meet their coworkers' expectations and improve their relationships. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 7. ISP-related injunctive norms are positively associated with ISP-related personal norms.

We define ISP-related subjective norms as an employee's perceptions about what those important to them think they should do in ISPrelated situations. ISP-related subjective norms are injunctive norms that are applicable to a specific reference group. In general, an individual is more likely to follow subjective norms to meet the expectations of those important to them [13]. In the information security literature, studies on the perceptions of employees about the expectations of important others [65,67] suggest that top managers, supervisors and peers in information systems departments are among the most relevant references when they make an ISP-related decision [13,67]. Therefore, when an employee perceives that ISP compliance is what their supervisor or manager thinks they should do, they are more likely to internalize

ISP compliance behavior as part of their own values, satisfying the expectations of their manager and improving the relationship with them. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 8. ISP-related subjective norms are positively associated with ISP-related personal norms.

## 3.3. Principle ethical climate and ISP-related social norms

Employees' social norms regarding the ISP will be influenced by principle ethical climate, the prevalent attitude toward organizational policies and rules. An ISP is a set of rules and policies specifically related to information assets. In an organization with high principle ethical climate, employees place high value on following rules, policies, and standards [22]. It has been shown that the ethical climate provides a strong system of social norms by informing an organization's members what they ought to do and what is expected by others [22,68,69]. Principle ethical climate can offer a useful informational reference for efficient rule-following behavior, thereby influencing one's ISP-related descriptive norms. In addition, by demonstrating the viewpoints of the majority including those of the important others, principle ethical climate presents a sound benchmark for ISP-related injunctive and subjective norms. For example, an employee who perceives that the organization in general values rule-following behavior is more likely to believe that the majority of their coworkers including their managers either comply with the ISP or expect them to comply with it. Thus, overall, principle ethical climate would help provide relevant ideas about the majority's behavior (descriptive norms) and expectations (injunctive norms), including those of the supervisors and upper management (subjective norms). Accordingly, we argue that ISP-related social norms are specific norms that evolve from more broadly-held perceptions of the rulefollowing climate in the organization and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 9a. Principle ethical climate has a positive influence on ISPrelated descriptive norms.

Hypothesis 9b. Principle ethical climate has a positive influence on ISPrelated injunctive norms.

Hypothesis 9c. Principle ethical climate has a positive influence on ISPrelated subjective norms.

## 4. Research methodology

To test our model, we used the survey method. We did a comprehensive literature review and formed the preliminary instrument using pre-existing items from previous studies to ensure the reliability and validity of items [70]. We adapted some of the items to the context of information security policy compliance whenever needed. We did a pretest and refined our instruments. The survey participants were recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) via an online survey.

## 4.1. Instrument development

We started item development by prudent investigation of the literature. We asked several experienced information systems faculty and Ph.D. students who had experience in survey research methods to give us feedback on our preliminary measurement items and ensure that the items are well-defined and consistent with each construct (i.e., face validity). We measured all the key constructs in the model using multiple items with 7-point Likert scales. They were modeled as reflective constructs.

The measurement of ISP compliance behavior was adapted from Tyler and Blader [71] and modified to fit the context of ISP. We adapted the items for ISP-related personal norms, awareness of consequences, and ascription of personal responsibility from the context of social psychology [19,72]. ISP-related descriptive and subjective norms came from prior literature in information security [13,73]. ISP-related injunctive norms were adapted from prior literature in social psychology [8] and tax aversion [61]. Lastly, principle ethical climate was from organizational behavior and ethics literature [22]. Table 1 lists all the constructs along with their measurement items.

## 4.2. Survey administration

We first conducted a pilot study with MBA students at a major university in the southwest United States. We refined our instrument and then carried out an online survey with working professionals recruited through Amazon's Mechanical Turk (MTurk) as the respondents. MTurk is an online crowdsourcing labor market. It provides a platform through which you can hire people to perform diverse tasks in exchange for payment. MTurk has been suggested to be a reliable source for data collection in prior studies [74–76]. Buhrmester et al. [75] found that it represents the American population better than.

student data. Further, it enables us to reach a broader spectrum of respondents. We used Qualtrics to create the online survey. At the beginning of the survey after the consent form, we defined the ISP and gave examples of typical information security policies. We filtered out respondents who were not employed or whose job did not require them to comply with the ISP. We also limited our respondents to those in the United States. We obtained 201 validated responses. The data includes 116 men and 85 women. Table 2 shows other demographic information.

## 5. Research methodology and results

We used the partial least squares (PLS) approach to test and estimate the structural model. A component-based approach for estimation, PLS does not suffer from identification issues and violation of normality compared to covariance-based approaches [78]. In addition, this study focuses on understanding the effect of various factors on ISP compliance, rather than emphasizing the fit between observed correlations and model parameters [79]. Furthermore, our model has not been tested in the information security literature yet, which makes the nature of our study more exploratory, rather than confirmatory. SmartPLS version 2.0.M3 [80] was used in the estimation.

## 5.1. Measurement validation

Prior to testing the structural model, we evaluated the psychometric properties of the measurement items. All constructs were modeled as reflective latent constructs. Item reliability, convergent validity, and composite reliability as well as factor and cross loadings were calculated for each construct to assure the measurement quality of the constructs [72,74,75]. Convergent and discriminant validities were investigated using the following approaches. First, all other cross correlations are smaller than the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) of all constructs [83]. Second, all constructs' AVEs are higher than 0.5, which suggests the construct-related variance is higher than error variance. Third, as the correlations among all constructs are very small, all constructs are distinct from each other [83,84]. Lastly, the loading of all items were highest on their intended constructs [79] (Tables 3 and 4).

All factor loadings were higher than 0.70 [85], showing that around or more than 50% of the variance shared among items for each construct [86]. All the mentioned results suggest sufficient convergent and discriminant validities. The data for the independent and dependent variables were gathered from the same survey instrument. Thus, the common method bias (CMV) could have biased the study [87]. To determine whether it was a concern, we carried out Harman's one-factor test [87]. We executed an exploratory factor analysis with measurement items for all constructs [87]. Eight factors came out from the analysis, which explained 74% of the variance. No single factor was found that explained the majority of the variance. The largest variance explained by a factor was 34%. Therefore, we conclude that CMV is not a major concern. ## 5.2. Data analysis and results

To test the hypotheses, we first ran the main effects model without the interaction terms [89]. We performed a t-test using bootstrapping with 600 re-samples to examine the statistical significance of path coefficients. Fig. 2 shows the standardized PLS path coefficients, paths significance (based on a two-tailed test), and the amount of variance explained. In $\mathrm { P L S } , \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ in the endogenous constructs suggests the explanatory power of the model. Results indicate that the factors studied in the model explain almost 52% of the variance in personal norms and consequently 35% of the variance in information security policy behavior. The model can be considered a satisfactory and valid model since the percentages of variance explained were greater than 10 [90].

The results indicate that ISP-related personal norms have a significant effect $( \beta = 0 . 3 6 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ on ISP compliance, which supports H1. ISP-related awareness of consequences $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and ascription of personal responsibility $( \beta = 0 . 2 7 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ both positively lead to ISP-related personal norms (supporting hypothesis 2 and 4). To test the moderating effects, we followed a product-indicator approach [91] in which by cross-multiplying the items of relevant constructs, a multiplicative term was created and then standardized to prevent multicollinearity [92]. The interaction terms were added to the main effects model. The results indicate that the ISP-related awareness of consequences does not moderate $( \beta = - 0 . 0 5 , \mathrm { T - s t a t i s t i c s } = 0 . 8$ $\Delta { \sf R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 )$ the relationship between personal norms and ISP compli ance (not supporting H3), while ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility positively $( \beta = 0 . 2 9 ; \rho < 0 . 0 0 5 ; \Delta \mathrm { R } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 5 )$ moderates the relationship between personal norms and ISP compliance (supporting H5).

## 5.3. Post hoc analyses

The insignificant relationship between ISP-related descriptive norms and ISP-related personal norms is not in line with what was proposed in this paper. Thus, in search of a possible explanation, we looked at the social norms literature again. One possible explanation is the fact that ISP-related descriptive norms emerge from observing other coworkers, while ISP-related injunctive and subjective norms pertain to the general expectation of coworkers and relevant others, such as a supervisor or boss. Thus, by observing others, an employee can learn others' expectations and gradually develop their perception of ISPrelated injunctive and subjective norms. Therefore, it may be likely that ISP-related descriptive norms shape them. To test this proposition, we conducted a post hoc analysis and found that ISP-related descriptive norms are positively associated with ISP-related injunctive $( \mathrm { { \beta } } = 0 . 4 5 ;$ p b 0.001) and subjective norms $( \beta = 0 . 4 4 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

The lack of support for the moderation effect of ISP-related awareness of consequences may be due to the fact that an employee becomes aware of the consequences of their ISP-related behavior before feeling responsible for it. The information systems literature also revealed that information security awareness, in general, could raise employee's awareness of their responsibilities about an organization's information assets [93,94]. Thus, it is possible that an employee's overall ISPrelated awareness of consequences influences their ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility, rather than moderating the relationship between ISP-related personal norms and ISP compliance. We proposed that (1) ISP-related personal norms positively influence ISP compliance (H1), (2) ISP-related awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility not only positively influence ISP-related personal norms (H2, H4), (3) but also positively moderate the effect of personal norms on ISP compliance (H3, H5). Combining all these hypotheses will result in so-called a second-stage moderated mediation, meaning the magnitude of the mediating process is contingent on the level of moderator [95,96]. In our case, ISP-related personal norms mediate the effect of ISP-related awareness of consequence and ascription of the personal responsibility on ISP compliance, and the magnitude of mediation effects depends on different levels of moderators—ISP-related awareness of consequence and ascription of the personal responsibility.

To test such effects, we used the written SPSS macro by Preacher and Hayes [96,97]. Taking the moderation effects into account, we tested the mediation. The results of this test was in line with previous result (see Table 5), indicating that the magnitude of indirect effect is different for various level of moderator such that indirect effect is stronger for higher level of the ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility, while such an effect is steady through different levels of ISP-related awareness of consequences (see Table 6).

We proposed in this study that principle ethical climate shapes ISPrelated social norms in an organization. Previous literature also pointed out that organizational ethical climate may directly predict unethical behavior such as rule breaking in the organization [98–102]. Thus, it may be reasonable to see the direct effect of such a climate on ISP compliance. We test such a relationship without the presence of ISP-related norms first. Confirming the previous literature, principle ethical climate had a positive effect on ISP compliance behavior $( \beta = 0 . 2 8 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ However, when we added the social norms, the relationship between principle ethical climate and ISP compliance became insignificant (β = 0.14, T-statistics = 1.65), suggesting that the effect of principle ethical climate on the ISP compliance fully goes through norms in the organization.

In our conceptual development, we argued that principle ethical climate can act as a general reference regarding rule perception that can be used for ISP-related social norms, which themselves shape personal norms. We found that without introducing social norms, there is a significant relationship between rules ethical climate and ISP-related personal norms (β = 0.34; p b 0.001). However, this effect decreases with the presence of social norms (β = 0.24; p b 0.001) showing that the effect of principle ethical climate partially goes through social norms.

## 6. Discussion

The main goal of this study is to theorize and empirically test the role of ISP-related personal norms, their antecedents and activators toward employees' ISP compliance, given the rising interest in whether social psychology can help regulate employees' behavior related to a ISP.

## 6.1. Contribution to theory

This study makes a number of theoretical contributions. First, we move beyond the often-studied deterrence and protection motivation theory perspectives in understanding ISP compliance [7,13,103]. Our research model integrates different theories related to norms from social psychology literature [8,17,19] into a single framework to explain ISP compliance behavior. One key contribution is that this study is among the first to investigate the overlooked factor of ISP-related personal norms as an important predictor of ISP compliance behavior. It offers personal norms as a potential alternate explanation for wellestablished factors, such as fear appealing and deterrence for ISP compliance behavior [10,13].

Second, we suggest that in explaining the effect of ISP-related personal norms the moderating condition must be taken into account. Following social psychology literature, we show that the strength of ISPrelated personal norms on ISP compliance depends on the degree to which an employee feels personal responsibility. Disregarding such a moderation effect may lead to a premature conclusion. ISP-related ascription of personal responsibility facilitates the transition of ISPrelated personal norms to ISP compliance.

Third, we go beyond the scope of well-studied normative influence in information security literature [1,9–13] and distinguish the norms regarding the ISP to four different categories of ISP-related descriptive, injunctive, subjective, and personal norms. We further show that injunctive and subjective norms help shape personal norms. In addition, we show that employees' awareness of the consequences of their ISPrelated actions, as well as ascription of personal responsibility, can also play a part in ISP-related personal norms formation.

Finally, this study connects principle ethical climate to the ISP compliance literature. The effect of principle ethical climate is barely explored by the information security community. Drawing from ethical climate literature, we show that principle ethical climate can contribute to the formation of ISP-related social norms and act as a source for efficient and expected ISP-related behavior.

We contend that our model introduces multi-theoretical perspectives on ISP compliance behavior. It can be considered a core framework for future studies examining the role of ISP-related personal norms in information security. Furthermore, this study contributes to norm activation [19] and social norms theory [8] by operationalizing it in an organizational setting. This is noteworthy, considering that norm activation theory primarily has been operationalized in pro-social and environmental settings [19–21].

## 6.2. Implications for practice

We believe the findings from this study could help organizations enhance their employees' compliance with the ISP. Invoking feelings of moral obligation (personal norms) regarding the ISP might be promising in encouraging employees' ISP compliance behavior. However, such an appeal might not be feasible through direct or instant manipulation. The difficulty of such a manipulation rests upon the fact that the stimulating pressure of ISP-related personal norms comes from an employee's inner self, which is not directly accessible. As our result shows, ISP-related personal norms are influenced by external sources such as social norms, as well as factors such as awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility. Accordingly, changing ISP-related personal norms should be done through a proper restructuring of the ISP-related social norms as well as ISP-related awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility. As such, instead of directly appealing to employees' moral obligation, an organization may, via social norms, persuade its employees to behave accordingly.

Drawing from our results, we propose two strategies to initially tune and subsequently activate ISP-related personal norms toward ISP compliance. First, we recommend tuning the organizational environment toward general rule-following and ISP compliance in particular. To establish principle ethical climate across the organization, leadership should attempt to create consistency regarding ethics, and establish training and socialization programs [104]. By extension, we suggest that an organization could design intervention programs promoting doctrine that rules and standards are organizational underlined values, and these values are cherished and respected by all emplovees. Such interventions may eventually build the employees' shared perception toward rule-following and hence shape social norms toward ISP compliance. Organizations could also design campaigns that advertise the ISP-related social norms. Such messages can be framed like this: “Join your fellow coworkers in helping to keep information assets secure.”

Second, we recommend that organizations should enhance the awareness of consequences and ascription of personal responsibility regarding the ISP. Such an enhancement can empower feelings of moral obligation toward ISP compliance. It can also help organizations tackle the defense mechanisms that employees usually exercise to diminish their feelings of moral obligation regarding ISP compliance. Organization can do such an enhancement through intervention programs such as training sessions or informational brochures that explain the consequences of ISP violations and clarify the role of each employee in securing organization information assets. An organization should induce a sense of responsibility by making employees accountable for what happens to the organization or the welfare of others due to their ISP-related decisions. Organizations should highlight that an employee has enough authority to comply with the ISP, and that an organization depends on employee compliance to keep its information assets safe.

In addition, we suggest that organizations design the roles in such a way that responsibility is built into them. They can do so by letting an employee know that they are certainly responsible for the consequences of their ISP-related actions in their role, or by putting an employee in charge of their actions. For instance, a hospital can emphasize that a nurse is responsible not only for the patients they look after but also for the security of the information systems resources they use in their daily routines.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, our results are restricted to employees who work in the United States. Thus, it is possible that cultural values threaten the generalizability of the results. Moreover, our model explains only a fraction of variance in ISP compliance. It is obvious there are factors we did not examine. Future research can explore such factors as employees' personal characteristics or organization culture to enhance the understanding of ISP compliance. Furthermore, we used self-report data regarding employees' compliance behavior. We suggest that future studies use other approaches or data sources to measure ISP compliance behavior.
