# ENTERPRISE SYSTEM IMPLEMENTATION AND EMPLOYEE JOB OUTCOMES: UNDERSTANDING THE ROLE OF FORMAL AND INFORMAL SUPPORT STRUCTURES USING THE JOB STRAIN MODEL<sup>1</sup>

Tracy Ann Sykes Department of Information Systems, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {tsykes@walton.uark.edu}

Understanding how support structures influence job outcomes in the context of enterprise system (ES) implementations is important if we are to advance the rate of success of such implementations. This paper examines how support structures—conceptualized as formal support structures (FSS) and informal support structures (ISS)—affect job outcomes in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation by drawing on the job strain model. Prior research on the job strain model suggests job characteristics (i.e., job control and job demand) and social support together affect job outcomes. A model of these effects on three post-implementation job outcomes (i.e., job stress, job satisfaction, and job performance) is developed. Data were obtained from 152 employees in a longitudinal field study at a large multinational firm. Results show that FSS do not have a direct influence on job outcomes, but interact with job demand to influence all three job outcomes. ISS have direct and interaction effects on all three employee job outcomes. The effect of FSS and ISS on employee job outcomes is mediated by job demand and job characteristics partially mediate the effect of ISS and FSS on employee job outcomes.

Keywords: Enterprise system implementations, advice networks, job strain, organizational support, job stress, job satisfaction, job performance

## Introduction

Organizations rely on large-scale information technologies (ITs), such as enterprise systems (ESs), to gain competitive advantage. These systems are complex and designed to provide efficiency and effectiveness benefits by integrating information and processes across organizational functions (Gattiker and Goodhue 2005; Strong and Volkoff 2010). The macro-level productivity benefits from successful ES implementations can be substantial (e.g., Sedera et al. 2016; Srivastava and Teo 2008, 2010; Zhu et al. 2004) such that organizations continued to invest in them even as they reduced spending on other IT projects during economic recessions (Forrester 2008). Unfortunately, most ES implementations are risk-prone (Devadoss and Pan 2007), with as much as 80% of the projects failing to deliver the expected benefits (Devadoss et al. 2008). The high failure rate of ES implementations is attributed to many factors, such as resistance (e.g., Kim and Kankanhalli 2009; Lapointe and Rivard 2005), lack of fit between the ES and organizational culture (e.g., Strong and Volkoff 2010) and employees’ failure to cope with changes (e.g., Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005). These large and complex systems not only create technological changes in the organization, but also alter business processes (Volkoff et al. 2007) and the structure of work (Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Robey et al. 2002). These changes act as stressors (Tarafdar et al. 2010) that adversely affect employee attitudes and job perceptions (Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Ragunathan et al. 2008) that in turn impact job outcomes. Organizations invest significant resources to help employees cope with such changes—yet, their effectiveness in mitigating the negative effects of ES implementations is somewhat limited (Sykes 2015). Thus, being able to better understand how various forms of organizational support provided to employees ultimately affect job outcomes will be of significant practical value.

Based on an extensive review of recent literature on various forms of organizational support, Sykes (2015) identified training, change management support, online support, and helpdesk support as the most commonly studied forms of formal support structures (FSS)<sup>2</sup> in the IS literature. Examinations of social networks in the context of the shakedown phase of ES implementations have revealed that support in the form of access to information provided to employees through interpersonal networks can influence IS success in general (e.g., He 2005; He and Wei 2006; Sykes et al. 2009) and job outcomes in particular (e.g., Sykes et al. 2014). In fact, Sykes found such informal support structures (ISS), which manifest via social network support, to be far more effective than support from FSS. Nonetheless, few studies have focused on the mechanisms through which the various support structures influence job outcomes (Kossek et al. 2011). Understanding the mechanisms that explain how different types of support structures affect job outcomes is important because doing so will further our understanding of their impacts, and enable managers to design interventions to foster positive job outcomes and minimize negative impacts.

Building on Sykes’ 2015 paper, this work seeks to better our understanding of the impacts and interplay among support structures as well as to position them within a well-accepted theoretical framework focused on job stress and other job outcomes.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, the job strain model—also called the demand–control–support model (Karasek and Theorell 1990)—is employed in this work as the guiding theoretical perspective used to study the effect of job characteristics<sup>4</sup> and support structures on job outcomes in the context of the shakedown phase of an ES implementation. The job strain model has been widely used to explain the effects of job characteristics and social support on job outcomes, particularly in contexts involving work-related stress (see Hausser et al. 2010 for review; for an example in the IS literature, see Bala and Venkatesh 2013)—a context relevant to ES implementations. Further, a key premise of the job strain model is that two job characteristics (i.e., job control and job demand) and social support influence job outcomes.

Prior work on the job strain model has identified three types of effects: additive, mediation, and interaction (e.g., Hausser et al. 2010; Karasek and Theorell 1990; Visweswaran et al. 1999). Building on the arguments underlying the additive, mediation, and interaction effects of job control and job demand on job outcomes and applying them in the context of an ES implementation, an integrated model to explain the effects of job characteristics and support structures on employee job outcomes during an ES implementation is developed. Specifically, the mediating and moderating effects of four key FSS—namely training, helpdesk support, online support, and change management support—and ISS—conceptualized as the employee’s work-related advice network centrality—on job outcomes are studied. This work builds upon Sykes findings and focuses on three key job outcomes: job stress, job satisfaction, and job performance.<sup>5</sup> This work empirically tests the model in a three-wave longitudinal field study in the context of an ES implementation within a large telecommunications firm with data collected before and after the implementation.

This work is expected to contribute to the literature on technology implementations, organizational change, and social support. An examination of the combined effects of job characteristics (i.e., job demand and job control) and support structures on job outcomes extends the nomological network around outcomes of ES implementations (Sykes 2015; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014), particularly in the shakedown phase. Addressing the need to move beyond technology use as the ultimate dependent variable (Markus and Robey 1988; Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Venkatesh et al. 2007; Venkatesh et al. 2016 ), this work examines job outcomes (i.e., job stress, job satisfaction, and job performance) that are critical in the context of new IT implementations. In doing so, this work responds to the call to investigate the impact of workplace stressors and stress inhibitors on job performance in the context of large-scale IT implementations (e.g., Ragunathan et al. 2008). By building on Sykes (2015), this work is expected to contribute to the literature on the role of social support during organizational change by integrating different support structures (i.e., FSS and ISS) into the job strain model. Specifically, in this paper, the underlying mechanism through which FSS and ISS lead to job outcomes (i.e., the mediating role of job characteristics that is moderated by FSS and ISS; related to this, the current work’s contributions could also be viewed in terms of how FSS and ISS shape relationships in the demand-controlsupport model). This work will also contribute to the work on interventions and their influence on post-adoption job outcomes (see Venkatesh and Bala 2008; Venkatesh et al. 2016). Finally, by empirically testing three competing models that link support structures to employee job control and job demand in influencing employee job outcomes, this work is expected to contribute to the body of work on the job strain model (Hausser et al. 2010; Karasek and Theorell 1990).

## Theory

First, the job strain model (Karasek 1979) is discussed, followed by a discussion of FSS and ISS in the context of ES implementations. Next, recent developments on the job strain model are discussed and three competing models from prior work are presented. Finally, the arguments underlying these models are synthesized to develop the integrated model.

## Background

## The Job Strain Model

The job strain model, sometimes referred to as the demand– control–support model, was proposed by Karasek and his associates (Karasek 1979; Karasek and Theorell 1990). It has been widely applied to study the influence of job characteristics on a variety of job outcomes, such as stress, anxiety, well-being, performance and satisfaction (e.g., Fox et al. 1993; Nahrgang et al. 2011; Parker 2003), in a variety of contexts including ES implementations (see Bala and Venkatesh 2013). The model proposes that two job characteristics, namely job control and job demand, together with social support influence job outcomes. Job control, also referred to as decision latitude, is defined as the extent to which an employee perceives that he or she has the ability to exert some influence over the work environment (Ganster et al. 1986; Hausser et al. 2010). Job demand represents psychological stressors that arise from physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects of the job (Ganster et al. 1986; Karasek 1979).

Early formulations of the job strain model focused on stress as an outcome and provided “the underlying theoretical basis for most large scale studies of job stress” (Fox et al. 1993, p. 290). These studies were based on the argument that psychological strain results from the combined effects of the demands of a work situation and the amount of control that employees have in managing these demands. The key argu ment made in the job strain model is that job control and job demand jointly influence job outcomes. Specifically, job demand leads to an increase in employee job strain, whereas job control allows greater latitude to make work-related decisions and reduces job strain. The original job strain model was criticized because it ignored the role of social processes in influencing job outcomes (e.g., Cohen and Wills 1985). Karasek and Theorell (1990) acknowledged this criticism and examined the role of social support in the job strain model to find an association between social support and job outcomes. Social support at work, in terms of the job strain model, refers to the overall level of helpful interaction available on the job from both coworkers and supervisors (Karasek and Theorell 1990). The work of Karasek and Theorell and subsequent developments of the job strain mode (e.g., Dollard et al. 2000; Leong et al. 1996) suggest that, in addition to job control and job demand, social support is one of the most important factors that influence job outcomes. Karasek and Theorell suggest four potential ways in which social support can operate to influence job outcomes. First, social support can act as a buffer, alleviating the adverse influence of workplace stressors on job outcomes. This buffering effect seems to be tied to interpersonal relationships that influence an actor’s emotional responses (Cummins 1990). Second, social contacts and social structure can influ ence psychological processes that lead to well-being and facilitate information acquisition—both emotive and instru mental forms of support—thus improving job outcomes. Third, social support can facilitate active coping that alleviates the negative influence of workplace stressors on outcomes and also promotes productive behavior. Finally, social support can provide a positive sense of identity, thus promoting productive behavior and well-being. Although the social support discussed here is weighted toward emotional support, more recent work on support structures, which is discussed next, focuses more on instrumental support that is received by employees in organizations but even that instrumental support often has a social or emotional support component (see Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2009; Sykes et al. 2014).

## Support Structures

Workplace support can take many forms determined by the type of phenomenon being investigated. For example, Kossek et al. (2011) examined support related to work–family balance. In the context of ES implementations, the support structures related to an employee performing one’s work in the shakedown phase of the implementation is examined. Work on the job strain model has suggested that support from coworkers and supervisors can be categorized as practical (relating to advice, resources or expertise) and emotional (related to listening, showing concern or consideration; Brough and Pears 2004). In this work, the focus is on practical aspects of such support as they received most attention in IS research on ES implementations and they afford organizations avenues by which employee reaction to a new system can be influenced. Specifically, as noted earlier, this work builds upon a recent review of support structures in the IS literature that identified four forms of traditional support (i.e., training, change management support, online support, and helpdesk support) and peer support (see Sykes 2015). As noted earlier (footnote 2), formal support refers to support structures implemented by the organization and employee supervisors—referred to as “traditional support structures” in Sykes—and informal support refers to support structures (i.e., advice ties, implemented and managed by the employees themselves)—referred to as “peer advice ties” in Sykes.

Formal Support Structures: FSS are those implemented by the organization to help employees cope with changes during the implementation process (Boudreau and Robey 2005; He 2005; Srivastava et al. 2009). This work leverages and builds on Sykes who identified four FSS (i.e., training, helpdesk support, online support, and change management support). These FSS are briefly discussed below; the interested reader is referred to Sykes for a more detailed discussion.

Training refers to the instructional sessions provided by the organization that employees receive before or immediately following an ES implementation (see Venkatesh and Bala 2008). Training includes the cognitive activities that engage users and impart knowledge to and familiarize them with the new system (Sharma and Yetton 2007). Much prior research has identified the importance of training in influencing user acceptance and system success (e.g., Compeau and Higgins 1995; Venkatesh 1999; Venkatesh and Bala 2008). By providing employees a feel for the features of the new ES, training can play an important role in influencing employee perceptions and attitudes toward the new ES.

Helpdesk support is provided by the organization in the form of onsite support personnel who address employee problems by answering questions face-to-face, through telephone calls or responding to emails (Gray and Durcikova 2006; Visser et al. 2009). Helpdesk support often represents the first line of support for employees (Halverson et al. 2004) and typically resolves problems that are straightforward and repetitive, such as help with password resets, or questions about using new system features. Helpdesks are staffed by the organization’s technical support employees (Gray and Durcikova 2006) and provide assistance to employees as and when they face challenges, usually during normal business hours, in working with the new system. However, being staffed by technical support personnel, helpdesk support may not be able to resolve user queries related to the new business processes introduced by a new ES (Sykes 2015; Sykes et al. 2009).

Online support refers to the support made available by an organization in the form of access to online information repositories and manuals about the new system being implemented. Employees can often access online support from any location with Internet connectivity. This support is likely to be particularly beneficial to employees working from a remote location and those working outside of normal business hours. For instance, when an employee working after normal business hours faces a problem with the system, he or she may not be able to receive a timely response from helpdesk support staff who typically work during the organization’s normal business hours. Being able to access an online information repository enables the employee to find answers to overcome the problem faced. Thus, online support makes it possible for employees to get access to support even after normal business work hours, enabling them to work effectively for longer durations and outside normal business hours, thus potentially increasing their productivity. Because much of the knowledge related to organizational processes and routines is difficult to codify (Millar and Choi 2009), it cannot be completely documented in online repositories and manuals. Therefore, a limitation of online support is that it provides little processrelated assistance to employees. Further, the information contained in online information repositories may be either too general or too detailed to be useful to employees looking for a solution.

Change management support is provided by consultants employed by the organization to optimize employee and organizational performance by identifying ways to manage the change process effectively (Worren et al. 1999). Often, change management consultants are supplied by the provider of the ES being implemented (Kumar et al. 2003). Given the large-scale nature of ES implementations and the business process expertise required to implement them, most organizations employ consulting firms to guide them through implementations that typically last several months. This support is typically provided onsite and relates primarily to the new business processes being implemented (Gallivan et al. 2005).

Informal Support Structures: ISS represent the selforganizing social ties that develop among employees in the organization over time as they interact with each other and share work-related information (Krackhardt and Kilduff 2002). A social network perspective is adopted to examine this form of support given it is based on interpersonal ties among employees. An employee’s work-related advice network represents the network of employees to whom the focal employee turns for advice, information, and support related to their work (see Sykes et al. 2009). Research based on the social network perspective has grown exponentially in the management literature and has become increasingly popular in the IS field. This paper is part of an ongoing program of research on social networks and information systems implementations. Sykes et al. (2009) established the importance of ISS in explaining system use. With the increased need to (1) move beyond technology use as an ultimate outcome in work settings and (2) contextualize ISS to large-scale systems implementations, Sykes et al. (2014) opened up the black box of advice networks by focusing on the role of get/give advice for both workflow knowledge and software knowledge and examining their effects on job performance. Given that ISS were shown to be more influential than FSS in explaining job outcomes (Sykes 2015), more recent research continued examining other approaches to study the effects of ISS, such as the source (i.e., whether advice is coming from a friend or acquaintance in the working setting) and content of advice (i.e., whether it is hindering or encouraging in nature) (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017). Advice network centrality represents the extent to which the individual is involved in exchanging assistance with coworkers and engaging in mutual problem solving (Sparrowe et al. 2001; Sykes 2015). An examination of employee advice networks in the context of an ES implementation has identified an employee’s advice network position as an important predictor of the information and support available to the employee from peers (see Davis et al. 2009; Sykes et al. 2009). Similarly, advice networks have been associated with greater IT use and higher job performance (Sykes and

Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014). An employee’s network position has also been linked to other employee job outcomes including satisfaction, promotion, power, influence and access to information (e.g., Baldwin et al. 1997; Burt 1992; Granovetter 1973; Ibarra and Andrews 1993). Position within the advice network plays an especially important role in providing access to information for two reasons. First, much of the organization’s information resides among its employees such that each employee specializes in one or two domains (Anand et al. 1998). Information about other domains is communicated between employees. Second, with users becoming increasingly technology savvy, employees can often help each other overcome problems faced with the new ES (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Davis et al. 2009). Similarly, Gallivan et al. (2005) observed that learning to use technology is not an individual phenomenon, but rather an outcome of skill acquisition and transfer at the group level. Based on the premise that individuals who are more central have greater access to social support and informational benefits (Cross et al. 2001), employees’ advice network centrality is expected to represent the level of informal support they receive.

It should be noted that social network ties can also convey negative affect in the workplace (Sparrowe et al. 2001). For example, if one is connected to others who dislike a new system, one would be less likely to use the new system themselves (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017). Ties can lead to positive or negative information transmittal and can have vastly different impacts on behavior and outcomes—for instance, Venkatesh et al. (2011) found a doctor’s ties to other doctors transmitted their negative affective reactions about the new system and the transmittal of these negative views were shown to be detrimental to patient satisfaction, whereas paraprofessional’s ties to paraprofessionals transmitted more positive information about the system and resulted in a positive effect on patient satisfaction. Focusing on positive and negative information being transmitted through a network requires a focus on either communication networks (Venkatesh et al. 2011) or hindrance networks (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017). However, this work examines employee work-related advice network ties. These ties are specifically seen as “instrumental” assets that aid in performing one’s work, and are less subject to affective homophilous influences found in friendship or hindrance networks (see Sykes and Venkatesh 2017).

## Narrative Description

This section details the unique contributions of the various manuscripts, the data, and any potential overlap, with the goal to clarify how the research program has evolved and how the various papers fit together.

The manuscripts listed represent a program of research on social networks and information systems implementations. The manuscripts leverage a large longitudinal dataset that has been collected over the past 12 years from a large multinational corporation, with data collected in Europe and in the United States. This narrative highlights how the manuscripts, ordered from MS1 to MS7, establish and continue to develop a body of knowledge on information systems implementations and social networks. The matrix that follows this narrative maps the variables that were used in each manuscript and additional characteristics of the studies that include the business unit, sample size, duration of the study, and a high-level description of the unique contributions.

MS1 established the importance of peer support in explaining system use. Hence, it builds on a long tradition of technology acceptance and use research, and moves beyond individual perceptions of technology and incorporate the role of peer advice and ultimately, improves our understanding of the drivers of technology use. Once the importance of peer support is established, it becomes important to open the black box of advice networks to (1) provide a nuanced view of advice networks in the context of large-scale information systems implementations and (2) incorporate more relevant outcomes beyond system use, such as job performance and more broadly, job outcomes. Against this backdrop, MS2 introduced the role of get- and give advice for both workflow knowledge and software knowledge, and examined their effects on job performance. MS3 brought formal support structures into the picture and compared their effects on job outcomes, which include job stress, job satisfaction, system satisfaction, and job satisfaction, to informal support structures or peer advice ties. MS4 sought to establish peer advice ties as a type of control belief, explaining the mechanisms by which peer advice is an effective lever to influence system use. MS5 continued developing a nuanced view of advice networks as in MS2, but with a special emphasis on (1) the source of advice in terms of whether the advice is coming from a friend or acquaintance in work settings, (2) content in terms of whether it is impeding from using the system or encouraging to use the system, and (3) deep structure use, which not only reveals a mediating mechanism that carries the effects of social networks in work settings, but also responds to calls by the information systems community to move beyond simplistic views of system use (e.g., measured in terms of frequency of use) and incorporate goals for using the system in relation to the job description. With the increased interest in the evolution of social networks in work settings, MS6 brings social network analytics into the picture to reveal patterns in (1) the evolution of social network ties over four phases of information systems implementation, (2) drivers of advice tie selection, and (3) technology use.

Building on MS3, MS7 provides a comparative view of informal and formal support structures, but with an emphasis on understanding the mechanisms through which both structures influence job outcomes. Specifically, a nuanced view of buffering and informational mechanisms extends our understanding of the role of peer advice in the context of large-scale information systems implementations in terms of its impact not only on job outcomes, but also in alleviating job demand and improving job control.

It is one of the most popularly examined outcomes in the job strain model (see Xie et al. 2008). Job stress is also becoming an important outcome studied in IS research. A review of the negative outcomes of technology suggests that due to the changing demands caused by new technologies, employees often experience stress (e.g., Tarafdar et al. 2010; Tarafdar et al. 2007). For instance, in an examination of stress caused by technology in organizations, Ragunathan et al. (2008) suggest that technologies that take several months to implement and provide constant connectivity to employees can lead to information fatigue and increase employee stress. In an examination of both ISS and FSS in work settings, Sykes (2015) suggested that ISS alleviate job stress significantly. Job satisfaction is defined as the extent of positive emotional response to the job resulting from an employee’s appraisal of the job as fulfilling or congruent with the individual’s values (Morris and Venkatesh 2010). Research has demonstrated that large-scale IT implementations in general, and ES implementations in particular, can have an impact on job satisfaction, whether from an individual level perspective (Morris and Venkatesh 2010) or social net works perspective (Sykes 2015). Job performance is a critical outcome in organizational behavior research (Luo et al. 2008). It is defined as the degree to which employees’ behaviors are effective in meeting organizational objectives (Beal et al. 2003; Grant and Wrzesniewski 2010). Largescale IT implementations in general and ES implementations in particular have been shown to have an effect on job performance (Sykes 2015; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014; Zhang and Venkatesh 2013).

In the rich body of literature focusing on job outcomes, it is understood that there are interrelationships among them. For example, an employee’s job stress negatively influences both job satisfaction and job performance (Judge et al. 2001; Riketta 2008), and job satisfaction has been shown to influence job performance (Judge et al. 2001). However, the job strain model does not explicitly focus on these relationships, and in order to remain faithful to the work on which this work builds (i.e., Sykes 2015), the dependent variables are tested simultaneously, rather than focusing on the interplay across these outcomes. Still another reason to focus on outcomes independently is that the literature is rich with mutually reinforcing relationships across stress, satisfaction, and performance that it would be difficult to disentangle the causal sequence here. Rather, by focusing on multiple related outcomes, independent impacts on the outcomes can be established.

## Model Development

Much research has focused on identifying the different ways in which job characteristics and social support influence job outcomes. Specifically, three effects have been identified (i.e., additive, mediation, and interaction) by which job characteristics and social support can impact job outcomes (e.g., Visweswaran et al. 1999), with three corresponding models being suggested (see Van der Doef and Maes 1999; Visweswaran et al. 1999). This work extends prior research in two ways. First, while noting that an employee receives both formal and informal support at work, as discussed earlier, a more nuanced view of social support that focuses on informal support received from coworkers is introduced, and this is examined in conjunction with job characteristics to examine the impact on job outcomes. Second, the effects suggested by three models from prior research are synthesized to create an integrated model of how job characteristics and social support influence job outcomes in the context of ES implementations.

## Additive Model

In the original job strain model, Karasek (1979) suggested that job control and job demand affect job outcomes, such as job stress, job satisfaction and job performance. Based on the basic premise of the job strain model, it is expected that job control and job demand will influence job outcomes. Although not a part of the initial job strain model, the role of social support in influencing job outcomes was later empirically tested (e.g., Cohen and Wills 1985). Reviews of prior studies on the job strain model found support for the direct (i.e., additive) effects model (e.g., Hausser et al. 2010; Visweswaran et al. 1999), wherein job characteristics and social support independently influence job outcomes. Building on these relationships between social support and job characteristics with job outcomes, the concepts of FSS and ISS are incorporated into the model.

## Mediation Model

Prior research has tested the mediation model where job control and job demand mediate the relationship between social support and job outcomes (e.g., Beehr and McGrath 1992; Visweswaran et al. 1999; Xanthopoulou et al. 2008). Results from testing the mediation model have been mixed. Although some researchers found evidence supporting the mediation model (e.g., Beehr and McGrath 1992; Xanthopoulou et al. 2008), others found little support for this model (e.g., Visweswaran et al. 1999). One possible reason for the inconsistent findings is the different conceptualizations of job outcomes. For instance, the test of the mediation model by Visweswaran et al. (1999) combined several types of job outcomes (i.e., job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and health symptoms) into a single construct. Because the focus of this work is on more granular conceptualizations of job outcomes and how these are affected by job control, job demand and support structures, it is expected that there will be mediation effects as suggested in prior research (e.g., Beehr and McGrath 1992).

## Interaction Model

It has also been suggested that social support interacts with job characteristics to influence job outcomes (Karasek and Theorell 1990). Specifically, social support alleviates the adverse effects of job characteristics and potentially protects employees from these adverse effects (e.g., Ganster et al. 1986; Kirmeyer and Dougherty 1988). Further, employees with greater access to social support are more likely to seek and find help to overcome challenges, thus improving their job outcomes (Bacharach and Bamberger 2007). Based on prior research (Hausser et al. 2010; Visweswaran et al. 1999), it is expected that FSS and ISS will interact with job characteristics to influence job outcomes.

Based on the three competing models discussed above, it is expected that job characteristics and support structures will influence job outcomes through additive, mediation, and interaction effects. An integrated model is shown in Figure 1.<sup>6</sup>

## Hypothesis Development

Role theory research and Karasek’s (1979) description of “active jobs” is used to explain the relationship between job control and job outcomes in the context of ES implementations. Jobs that allow high job control and also require a high level of employee involvement in job tasks are referred to as active jobs. Knowledge workers working with an ES that typically offers a large number of features (Boudreau and Robey 2005) are likely to have active jobs. Job control gives an employee the discretion to select, plan, and schedule work tasks (Dwyer and Ganster 1991; Karasek 1979). The perception of job control is associated with employee role orientation, defined as the extent to which employees own and feel responsible for work beyond their immediate operational tasks (Parker et al. 1997). An employee with a narrow role orientation views his or her job as comprising a small set of tasks and focuses on “doing what I am told” (Parker et al. 1997, p. 904). Employees with a broader role orientation view their jobs as being part of the overall organization’s functioning and are more willing to take on additional responsibilities (Parker 1998; Parker et al. 1997). Unlike traditional systems that typically offer low organizational visibility, the integration, interdependence, and access to information enabled by an ES increases employee visibility in terms of how their role fits within the functioning of the organization (Davenport 1998; Gattiker and Goodhue 2005). In the context of the shakedown phase of an ES implementation, high job control would enable employees to leverage the visibility afforded by the ES and to adopt broader role orientations compared to their roles and jobs pre-implementation, and thus feel more responsible for the outcomes of the tasks (Parker et al. 1997) than employees with low job control. In the shakedown phase, ESs introduce a wide range of new system features that users can adopt in their work tasks, many of which may also be new. Jobs with high job control and a large number of system features create an environment of active learning that increases employee engagement (see Spector 1986). This could motivate employees to learn several features and apply the ones that are most effective in their work. Therefore, employees with high job control will likely have better job performance. Having control over the job and being engaged with the job also increases the intrinsic motivation associated with the job (Spector 1986), thus increasing job satisfaction and reducing job stress associated with the job. Therefore, greater job control will be associated with more positive job outcomes. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H1a: Job control will positively influence job satisfaction and job performance and will negatively influence job stress in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

As noted earlier, job demand is a stressor that arises from the physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects of the job (Karasek 1979). Although one might expect that having to expend physical, cognitive, or emotional effort would lead to a decline in performance and satisfaction, interestingly, based on the Yerkes-Dodson Law (Ganster 2005; Gardner and Cummings 1988), job demand has an inverted-U shaped relationship with job performance, such that for low levels of job demand, an increase in job demand increases job performance. However, beyond a threshold, job performance deteriorates with an increase in job demand (Ganster 2005). An ES implementation introduces many changes in the organization (Devadoss and Pan 2007). In the shakedown phase of the implementation, employees are forced to learn to work with the new ES. The experience of having to give up the old way of working and learning to work with a new system can be challenging for employees (Devadoss and Pan 2007). Further, given that ESs typically introduce many features, users are often overwhelmed by their complexity (Boudreau and Robey 2005), especially in the shakedown phase. It is likely that users learning to use a new ES could become “stuck” in the process of trying to do their jobs in the new context, and could become frustrated and demoralized by their lack of knowledge that would help them become “unstuck” (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Venkatesh et al. 2010). Although job demand has a positive relationship with performance in certain cases, the positive relationship occurs due to the challenge associated with the task that requires cognitive resources to complete the task. When the challenge posed by the task is matched by the individual’s skill level, the individual experiences heightened concentration, creativity, satisfaction, and performs better on the task (Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Given that ES implementations, especially in the shakedown phase, have been identified by users as being painful (Robey et al. 2002), and often lead to users being frustrated and demoralized (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Venkatesh et al. 2010), it is unlikely that the challenge associated with learning to use the ES will be matched by users’ skills, especially particularly in the shakedown phase. Therefore, it is expected that high job demand, arising from having to use the new ES, will adversely affect job outcomes. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H1b: Job demand will negatively influence job satisfaction and job performance and will positively influence job stress in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

Informational Mechanism. The informational mechanism is the means by which employees using an ES are able to gain access to information necessary to complete their tasks effectively. The availability of information is beneficial in uncertain situations (Morris and Shin 2000) and when learning to use new systems (Sharma and Yetton 2007; Sykes et al. 2009). The shakedown phase of an ES implementation is one wherein employees are uncertain about using the system in their work (Markus and Tanis 2000). The changes in technology that occur during an ES implementation require the users to learn new features (Jasperson et al. 2005). ES interfaces are often inflexible with specific sequences of steps that must be followed to complete a transaction (Boudreau and Robey 2005). For instance, to complete a transaction, such as order entry, an employee would need to remember not only what needs to be done, but also when each step should be taken. There is thus a lot of information that the users must learn and use. In general, a single user is not likely to remember all necessary information when beginning to work with an ES, especially in the shakedown phase. In such cases, the em ployee would need to access information from external sources. There are two ways in which an employee can access information. First, the employee may access information using FSS, such as training, online guides, helpdesk agents and change management consultants, if available. Second, the employee may access information from ISS, such as those provided by an employee’s advice network. Given that all employees are working on the same ES, an employee’s coworkers can be expected to face similar challenges as those faced by the focal employee. Asking coworkers how they fixed a certain problem will thus be a quick and effective way to overcome the problem and continue with the task-at-hand.

Buffering Mechanism. The buffering mechanism is one that enables an employee who is central in the advice network to obtain social support from peers in the form of sympathy, empathy, and caring (Loewenstein and Small 2007). Employees working with a new ES often have to face several challenges that give rise to negative feelings toward the ES (Boudreau and Robey 2005; Morris and Venkatesh 2010); this is likely to be particularly prevalent in the shakedown phase. Prior research on ES implementations has shown that employees who are more central in the advice network interact more with peers and receive more support from peers (Sykes et al. 2009). Employees central in the advice network receive social support from advisors who can sympathize and/or empathize with the focal employee and such support can alleviate the adverse effects of work-related stressors on job outcomes. The buffering mechanism, therefore, represents the social support available to employees central in the advice network that alleviates their negative reactions toward the changes introduced by the new ES, particularly in the shakedown phase.

Direct Effects of Support Structures on Job Outcomes. FSS are expected to influence job outcomes through the informational mechanism. Consider the case of an employee who is new to an ES and is using it to perform a task. Lacking information needed to use the ES, this employee is likely to get stuck often in the shakedown phase and spend more time completing tasks than someone who has the right information. This will likely adversely impact his or her job performance and job satisfaction. Not only will the lack of information disrupt the employee’s progress on the task, but also will give rise to frustration about having to use a new system that is unfamiliar and difficult to use (Boudreau and Robey 2005). The frustration resulting from the lack of necessary knowledge will increase job stress (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005; Devadoss and Pan 2007; Tarafdar et al. 2010). When this employee has access to higher levels of formal support, based on the informational mechanism, he or she will be better able to easily access the necessary information from the FSS. Being able to access the information, this employee will be able to overcome the problem and complete the task. Thus, it is hypothesized:

## H1c: Formal support structures will positively influence job satisfaction and job performance and will negatively influence job stress in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

ISS are expected to influence job outcomes through both informational and buffering mechanisms. When an employee who is stuck has access to high level of informal support, other employees in his network can provide the information needed to get unstuck (Sykes et al. 2009), enabling the employee to continue working with the ES and to complete his or her work. In the shakedown phase of an ES implementation, users play a significant role in distributing information across the organization (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014). Employees receiving more informal support will likely have greater access to information from other users, enabling them to overcome problems faced when working with the new ES. In addition to the informational mechanism, ISS are expected to influence job outcomes through the buffering mechanism. Based on the buffering mechanism, employees who are more central in the advice network are more likely to receive support from their peers in the form of empathy. The empathy received from coworkers will, in turn, reduce the feelings of frustration with the new systems, which is likely to be high in the shakedown phase, reducing the wellconnected employee’s job stress and increasing job performance and job satisfaction. Thus, it is hypothesized:

## H1d: Informal support structures will positively influence job satisfaction and job performance and will negatively influence job stress in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

Mediation of the Effects of Support Structures by Job Characteristics. Based on the informational mechanism, FSS provide employees access to information about features of the new ES. For instance, training gives employees handson experience working with the ES. Training also increases the employee’s self-efficacy in working with the ES (Compeau and Higgins 1995), thus increasing the employee’s confidence in being able to work with the ES to complete work tasks. Employees with greater confidence in being able to complete tasks that are part of their jobs are expected to perceive a greater sense of control over the job. In the shakedown phase, the helpdesk support staff, online support, and change management support consultants provide information to the employee as and when the employee faces problems in working with the system (Gallivan et al. 2005; Markus and Tanis 2000). Access to information influences employee’s perceptions of job demand in two ways. First, because ESs introduce many features that users need to learn and apply to accomplish their tasks, having to learn these features leads to perceptions of information overload (Tarafdar et al. 2010), thus increasing job demand. Being able to gain access to information about the ES from FSS reduces the information burden on users, thus reducing their perception of job demand. Second, access to information from FSS enables employees to acquire new skills for working with the ES. When employees working with a new ES are faced with challenges in the form of complexity of the ES that is matched with their skills, employees will experience a state of intrinsic motivation (Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Working on tasks with high intrinsic motivation is associated with greater control (Csikszentmihalyi 1990). Given that the challenge is matched by the employee’s skill level, the employee will experience lower job demand. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H2a: Formal support structures will positively influence job control in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

H2b: Formal support structures will negatively influence job demand in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

Given the challenges involved in working with a new ES (Jasperson et al. 2005), especially in the shakedown phase, employees working with a new ES will spend more time to accomplish work tasks than they did with the old system (Boudreau and Robey 2005). Spending more time working on a particular task will reduce the time left for the remaining tasks, thus decreasing the decision latitude available to the employee. Organizations that implement ESs, often have employees working on both the old and new systems in parallel in the shakedown phase (Boudreau and Robey 2005). Working simultaneously with two systems would severely constrain the time available for other work tasks, thus increasing job demand. In general, the constraints of learning to apply new features and maintaining their productivity levels will lead to higher job demand for employees working with a new ES, particularly in the shakedown phase. However, based on the informational mechanism, when an employee receives more informal support, the employee is able to access information from a greater number of coworkers and is more likely to receive information to overcome problems when working with the system (Sykes et al. 2009)—a need that is likely to be frequent in the shakedown phase. When one employee learns how to use a new feature of the ES, this information gets quickly passed to other employees (Gallivan et al. 2005) through the advice network and an employee with higher advice network centrality is more likely to receive such information and more quickly than other less well-connected employees. In addition, employees receiving higher informal support can seek help from more coworkers who have faced similar problems to find answers to their problems in working with the system (Nelson and Cheney 1987; Sykes et al. 2009). This can save time and allow employees the latitude to plan and schedule other work tasks to be performed. Such quick and frequent advice will be critical especially in the shake down phase. Having access to information from coworkers will also reduce the time constraints on employees who occupy more central positions in the advice network. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H2c: Informal support structures will positively influence job control in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

H2d: Informal support structures will negatively influence job demand in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation.

Interaction Effects of Support Structures and Job Characteristics. Job control allows employees greater latitude in planning their work. Given that high job control leads to broader role orientation (Parker et al. 1997), it is expected that employees with high control over their jobs will typically have broader role perceptions and hence, will be more likely to adopt a wider set of work-related skills than those with lower job control (Morgeson et al. 2005). Job control is also associated with an increase in employees’ self-efficacy and work engagement (Morgeson et al. 2005; Parker 1998). These have been shown to beneficially influence employee job outcomes (Morgeson et al. 2005). Users with high levels of self-efficacy and work engagement will likely be more comfortable working with the ES—such confidence and comfort could be crucial in the shakedown phase when the employee will encounter many new processes, tasks, and features—and will have higher job performance, higher job satisfaction, and lower job stress. Although all employees with high job control are expected to perceive high selfefficacy and work engagement, those employees who can more easily access information pertaining to the ES’s features will be better equipped to translate their perception of selfefficacy into objective work output and will have higher job performance. Therefore, based on the informational mechanism, employees with higher formal support will be better equipped to translate their perceptions of self-efficacy and work engagement into higher job performance and job satisfaction, and will have lower job stress. Informal support will provide similar information benefits.

Based on the buffering mechanism, employees with high informal support will receive greater social support from coworkers. Social support in the form of empathy from coworkers acts as a reinforcement (Baumeister and Leary 1995), further increasing the focal employee’s perceptions of selfefficacy and work engagement (Gist and Mitchell 1992; Xanthopoulou et al. 2008). For instance, consider an employee with high job control working with a new ES. Given that the employee has high job control, the employee will have a high level of self-efficacy and believes he or she will be able to complete certain tasks independently. When this employee has high access to informal support that manifests as social support, the presence of other employees and the support received from interactions with them acts as encouragement for the focal employee and increases his or her selfefficacy about being able to complete even more complex and challenging tasks with the system. Therefore, while employees with greater job control are expected to have better job outcomes, based on the buffering mechanism, the coworker support and caring received by employees with high ISS is expected to further enhance this effect by increasing the self-efficacy of such employees, which, as noted earlier, is the mechanism by which job control affects outcomes. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H3a: Formal support structures will moderate the effect of job control on job outcomes in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation such that employees with high formal support will have greater job satisfaction and job performance and have lower job stress than those with low formal support.

H3b: Informal support structures will moderate the effect of job control on job outcomes in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation such that the employees with high informal support will have greater job satisfaction and job performance and have lower job stress than those with low informal support.

As discussed earlier, it is expected that job demand will be negatively related to job outcomes. An employee with high job demand, who must complete several tasks using the ES, is likely to come across several features that he or she cannot use on his or her own, leading to negative reactions toward the system and the work environment (Tarafdar et al. 2010). This is particularly likely in the shakedown phase of the implementation. Further, being unable to perform tasks that are part of the job, leads to the fear of “being bumped out” (Boudreau and Robey 2005, p. 12), thus adversely affecting job outcomes. However, if the employee receives higher formal support and consequently, is more easily able to access information about the system, the adverse effect of using a new system on his work performance is likely to be lower than that for an employee with lower formal support. Based on the informational mechanism, access to information will enable the employee to quickly continue with the task and will alleviate the negative effect that high job demand has on his or her job performance. Such support will be particularly relevant in the shakedown phase when problems and concerns are likely to be maximal and frequent. Informal support will have similar informational benefits.

Based on the buffering mechanism, when an employee has access to informal support by being connected to other employees and interacting with them, the focal employee will be able to receive sympathy, empathy, and caring from coworkers. Receiving social support from coworkers and the perception of belonging to a group decreases the stress experienced by the employee (Baumeister and Leary 1995) and reduces the adverse effects of job demand on job outcomes (Karasek and Theorell 1990). Recent research has found that support from coworkers makes the work environment more pleasant and rewarding (Van Yperen and Hagedoorn 2003). Therefore, even though it may not completely solve the problems that the employee is facing with the system, receiving emotional support from others and the feelings of not being isolated will likely reduce the negative effects that job demand has on job outcomes. Thus, it is hypothesized:

H3c: Formal support structures will moderate the effect of job demand on job outcomes in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation such that employees with high formal support will have greater job satisfaction and job performance and lower job stress than those with low formal support.

H3d: Informal support structures will moderate the effect of job demand on job outcomes in the shakedown phase of an ES implementation such that employees with high informal support will have greater job satisfaction and job performance and lower job stress than those with low informal support.

## Method

This study was conducted over one year in a business unit in a large multinational telecommunications company implementing an ES. Data were collected at three points in time: 5 months pre-implementation, 3 months post-implementation, and 6 months post-implementation. This data collection endeavor is part of a larger on-going research program on social networks and IS implementations over the past 12 years (Sykes 2015; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2009; Sykes et al. 2014). Given that the first publication in the program has extended a long tradition on technology acceptance and use research by incorporating the role of ISS in work settings (Sykes et al. 2009), subsequent data collection endeavors focused on additional perspectives to technology use and effects, such as job outcomes and job characteristics. As noted earlier, Table 1 provides the data transparency information. Data about employees’ perceptions of pre- and post-implementation job demand, job control, job stress, job satisfaction and satisfaction with support structures, as well as social network data for the employee get-advice network were obtained. Consistent with recent work (Sykes 2015; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014), employee job performance was obtained from archival annual supervisor ratings. The participants, enterprise system, measures, and data collection procedure are described next.

## Participants

The participants in the study were from one business unit (i.e., finance and accounting) of the organization. The organization provided a list of all the employees in the business unit, which formed the sampling frame for this study. One business unit of the organization was the focus of this study for two reasons. First, focusing on one business unit ensured that the sample comprised a set of employees with similar job descriptions, duties and tasks, thus limiting possible biases due to variations in job type. Second, employees in the business unit interacted regularly, thus enabling us to assess an employee’s advice network. Surveys were sent out to all 178 employees in the business unit. Of these, 152 employees provided usable responses at all points of measurement for a response rate of 85%, which is above the threshold of 80% for primary social network studies (Knoke and Yang 2008). Nonresponses were due to participants choosing not to participate, leaving the organization, or providing incomplete/unusable responses. Of the 152 respondents, 44 were women (29%). The average age of the respondents was 41.4 years, with a standard deviation of 8.5 years. The average organizational tenure of the respondents was approximately 5 years. The demographic profile of respondents matched that of the business unit.

## Enterprise System

The new ES module implemented was part of a large-scale ERP implementation at the organization and was specifically designed for the finance and accounting unit.<sup>7</sup> A consulting company customized a leading commercial ERP system to fit the needs of the organization in general and the business unit in particular. Prior to the implementation of the ES module, employees used a variety of off-the-shelf accounting and collections management software packages to manage information about accounts receivable and accounts payable, with some employees even using traditional methods, such as paper files and faxes. The old solutions did not support communication across different locations and led to occasional inaccuracies in the accounts. Hence, the organization implemented the ES to enable standardization of financial information across the organization, facilitate information sharing, and to increase accuracy. The ES module included a core database to centrally store and track financial and accounting data. This central database allowed the integration of financial information from multiple locations and supported multiple languages and currencies. The ES module was designed to provide access to financial information, thus enabling robust practices by streamlining receivable and collections management processes. This enabled the organization to easily and quickly resolve disputes and reduce the collections cycle. The ES module was customized over a period of 8 months, and introduced new software, a hardware platform, and business processes. The ES module enabled users to collaborate across locations in real-time and to report financial data more accurately. The implementation of ES modules, such as the finance module studied here, can substantially alter business processes and contribute significantly to organizational productivity (Tian and Xu 2015; Xu et al. 2008).

## Measurement

Wherever possible, previously validated and established scales were adapted. Seven-point Likert scales were used for all perceptual measures, with job performance being obtained from supervisor ratings of employee performance from the employees’ annual evaluations. Items used in this study are shown in Appendix B.

## Dependent Variables

Job stress was measured using the emotional exhaustion scale adapted from Maslach and Jackson (1986). This measure for job stress has been widely used in prior work (see Lam et al. 2010). Job satisfaction was measured using a version of the job diagnostic survey (JDS; Hackman and Oldham 1974) in which the reverse-coded items were replaced with items that had a positive wording. Job performance was obtained from archival records of supervisor-rated performance measured using the job effectiveness scale developed by Welbourne et al. (1998) that the organization used in its annual employee evaluations.

## Job Characteristics

The job characteristics, i.e., job control and job demand, were measured using an adapted version of the scales developed by Karasek (1979) that was adapted to the context of this study. The scale for job demand has been validated by Janssen (2001) and was recently used by Van Jaarsveld et al. (2010). The scale for job control was recently used and validated by Ilies et al. (2010). Other IS research has also used these scales (see Bala and Venkatesh 2013).

## Support Structures

The four types of FSS (i.e., training, helpdesk support, online support, and change management support) were measured in terms of the extent to which an employee felt that the support structures were adequate (Sykes 2015). The specific items used were adapted from the measurement of various satisfaction-related constructs in the IS literature (e.g., Brown et al. 2008). Satisfaction with training and change management support have been used by Magni et al. (2010). These items were adapted to the present context to measure satisfaction with all four types of FSS.

ISS, represented by employees’ advice network centrality, was operationalized as eigenvector centrality within the advice-seeking matrix (Bonacich 1973), which takes into account both direct and indirect sources of information from advisors (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2008). Eigenvector centrality quantifies how well-connected an individual is within the network compared to all others within the network, with quality of connection being taken into account in that the value of a connection is determined by how connected the connecting individual is compared to others within the network (Borgatti 2005). This measure takes into account the extent to which individuals in an employee’s network are themselves well-connected. Employees were given a roster that included the names of every other employee in the business unit. Advice network centrality for each employee was calculated from his or her responses to the question “Please indicate which of the following individuals are important sources of work-related advice or whom you approach if you have a work-related problem.” Responses ranged from never to hourly or more.

## Control Variables

Gender, age, organizational tenure, and organizational position are key control variables that have been shown to influence constructs related to technology adoption and job outcomes (Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014; Venkatesh and Morris 2000). Further, organizational tenure acts in some part as proxy for the amount of experience with organizational processes and/or as a baseline measure of business skill/knowledge in one’s job. Gender, age, and organizational tenure were measured using single item scales. Organizational position was coded as an ordinal variable based on employee grade used by the organization. Computer self-efficacy was a control variable in order to control for variance due to employees’ computer skills (see Venkatesh 2000) and was measured by adapting Compeau and Higgins’ (1995) scale. In addition to these variables, the influence of pre-implementation levels of job outcomes, job control and job demand were control variables given that prior research has shown that often the best predic tors for these constructs are earlier/prior measures of the same (Hunter and Hunter 1984; Morris and Venkatesh 2010). Controlling for the influence of pre-implementation outcomes also allows us to triangulate the influence of the implementation on said outcomes more fully as the pre-implementation values act as anchors, where post-implementation measures will show how the outcome variables adjusted after the implementation (see Shadish et al. 2002).

## Data Collection

As noted earlier, data were collected at three points in time. Pre-implementation, baseline data were collected five months prior to the system roll-out: employee perceptions of preimplementation job outcomes (i.e., job stress, job satisfaction, and job performance).

The organization provided training to all employees prior to the implementation. The training sessions were provided over a period of one month before the implementation and included 10 three-day sessions of which each employee was required to attend at least one session. Post-implementation data were collected three months after the implementation. In this wave, data were collected about the four FSS. Data about the employee advice network were also collected in this wave. Finally, six months after the roll-out of the system, data about post-implementation job stress and job satisfaction were collected from employees. During this wave, data from supervisor ratings of employee job performance were also collected from employees’ annual performance evaluations. Prior research has identified the strengths of such a data collection approach. First, the longitudinal design of the study, enables the causal flow to be tested and confirmed. Second, measuring job performance based on supervisor ratings alleviates potential bias introduced due to same source or rater as well as common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). Finally, measuring three dependent variables and also the longitudinal design of the study alleviates biases introduced by item demand characteristics and social desirability (Podsakoff et al. 2003).

## Results

## Data Analysis Strategy

UCINET 6.29 (Borgatti et al. 2002) was used to generate the get-advice network centrality scores for the employees. Partial least squares (PLS), specifically Smart-PLS 2.0 (Ringle et al. 2005), was used to analyze the data. It was appropriate for this work because it includes constructs measured using formative indicators and operationalized as second-order constructs. PLS is also suitable for analyzing the structural model with the three dependent variables (i.e., job stress, job satisfaction, and job performance) simultaneously.<sup>8</sup>

The measurement was assessed first. All constructs, except for FSS, were modeled using reflective indicators. Because training, helpdesk support, online support and change management support are expected to represent different forms of support provided to employees, and because the correlations between these are low (ranging from .14 to .24), FSS was modeled as a formative second-order construct, with training, change management support, online support and helpdesk support as the first-order constructs, consistent with the guidelines of Petter et al. (2007). Loadings and crossloadings of the pertinent variables are shown in Table A1 in Appendix 3. The results supported convergent and discriminant validity, with all loadings being greater than .70 and being greater than the cross-loadings, which is the criterion in PLS (for a recent example, see Setia et al. 2013). Table 2 presents the correlations, internal consistency reliabilities (ICRs), and the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for each of the constructs. As Table 2 shows, ICRs were greater than .70 for all constructs, thus supporting the reliability of the scales. Further, the square root of the AVEs were higher than the correlations across constructs, thus providing further support for convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell and Larcker 1981).

The results of the measurement model are shown in Table 2. The results shown relate to the most complete, integrated model. Latent variable values from the complete model (i.e., the integrated model shown in Figure 1) were used to estimate models that were a subset (i.e., control variables only and control variables with main effects only). The full model was first estimated with latent variable scores being saved and used for estimation of the subset of models because PLS reestimates latent variable scores each time a model with a new set of predictors is tested. All estimated models were tested by including all dependent variables simultaneously.

Dependent variables were predicted in three blocks. First, only control variables were added. In the second block of each DV, control variables and main effects from job characteristics and support structures were added. Each of the third blocks also included the interaction effects between job characteristics and support structures.

Overall, many of the hypotheses in the proposed model were fully or partially supported. The hypothesized direct effects of support structures and job characteristics were partially supported. Most of the hypothesized interaction effects were significant although the patterns were different from expected in some cases. ISS interacted with job characteristics to influence job outcomes. Job characteristics partially mediated the effects of support structures on job outcomes. The integrated model explained about 35% of the variance in the different job outcomes. This model addresses an important gap in the literature by identifying the effects of support structures on job outcomes.

The interactions that were examined in this work tell an interesting story that hopefully can be leveraged by managers to increase the likelihood of success in an ES implementation. An interesting finding, especially when considered in conjunction with the findings from Sykes (2015), is that FSS did not influence job control or any of the job outcomes. This supports Sykes that FSS (“traditional support structures”), except for change management support had no effects on the outcomes of interest. However, FSS in this work did directly influence job demand as well as interact with job demand to influence the job outcomes. One reason for this could be that FSS lowers the perceptions of job demand that would decrease job stress and potentially increase both job satisfaction and job performance. One reason for the lack of direct effects could be due to the majority of FSS being less useful to employees in an ES implementation context. The reasons for this are that— unlike ISS that is more likely to provide the right information at the right time and in the right context— much of the information provided by FSS during an ES implementation lack the right information, at the right time and in the right context (for a detailed explanation, see Sykes 2015).

## Contributions and Implications

This research makes important contributions to and has implications for research and practice. The importance of informal support received from coworkers and formal support provided by the organization is widely acknowledged in recent IS literature (e.g., Davis et al. 2009; Gray and Durcikova 2006; Sykes 2015; Sykes et al. 2009; Venkatesh and Bala 2008). In the recent past, research on IS implementations and social networks opened up the black box of ISS by (1) introducing a nuanced view of the content of ISS in terms of workflow and software knowledge (Sykes et al. 2014) and (2) examining the nature of ISS in terms of hindrance and advice from both friends and acquaintances in a work setting (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017). Although it has been established that ISS in general predict job outcomes (Sykes 2015), little is known about the process by which they influence job outcomes—and that is the void filled by the current paper.

Following Sykes (2015), this work presents one of the first studies to simultaneously examine the effects of these different and complementary forms of support and identifies the mechanisms by which different support structures influence job outcomes. It is interesting to note that employee advice networks, represented by ISS, directly influence all three job outcomes and also shape perceptions of job demand and job control. This underscores the importance of employee networks in large-scale organizational change events that affect employee job perceptions and job outcomes. This work also brings to the fore the two key pathways through which formal and ISS influence job outcomes, thus providing holistic insights into the role of support structures in organizations. Specifically, ISS affect job outcomes directly through the informational mechanism and also shield the employees from the negative effects of job characteristics through the buffering mechanism. The effect of ISS was examined in terms of an employee’s get-advice network that represents the advice received by the focal employee from coworkers. Giveadvice networks represent another important type of employee advice network and the effects of centrality in the give-advice network on job outcomes should be examined in future research (see Sykes et al. 2009).

This research addresses the calls to move beyond technology use as the ultimate dependent variable of interest (Markus and Robey 1988; Morris and Venkatesh 2010) and examines the impact of support structures and job characteristics on postimplementation job outcomes in the context of an ES implementation. Recent research has begun such examinations but has been restricted to one outcome in each study (see Morris and Venkatesh 2010; Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014). By examining three key job outcomes, this work extends the nomological network related to ES implementations. This paper also extends prior research on IS success by studying a more comprehensive set of individual outcomes of technology implementations (see DeLone and McLean 1992, 2003).

By applying the job strain model to study the relationships between support structures and job outcomes in the context of ES implementations, this work contributes to research on the job strain model in two ways. First, the effects of job control and job demand on job outcomes in the context of ES implementations was disentangled by providing insights in a specific context (see Johns 2006). Second, by identifying the effects of FSS and ISS on job outcomes, this work provides a more nuanced understanding of social support. Although the role of social support in the job strain model has been previously studied (see Visweswaran et al. 1999), prior research did not differentiate between the different forms of support that are available to employees. By studying the effects of FSS and ISS, a more complete understanding of the role of different types of social support in the job strain model is presented, and how these different forms of social support along with job demand and job control influence job outcomes is explained. This work extends prior research by demonstrating that in addition to the direct effects, employee advice networks have indirect effects on job outcomes that are mediated by job characteristics. An interesting finding of this research was that although job demand and FSS together improve job performance, they have an adverse effect on job stress and job satisfaction. This might be due to the time spent in receiving support from FSS, such as training and online manuals, interfering with and limiting the time employees have for their work. Future research is needed to validate this speculation and to examine the reasons underlying this pattern.

The results indicate that different forms of support influence job outcomes through different pathways. Although prior research on the job strain model has conceptualized and measured social support in terms of the support received by employees in general and this study builds on this thinking, future work must note the differences between different types of social support and conceptualize and measure social support in a more nuanced way (see Sykes et al. 2014). This study did not focus on differentiating between social support received from coworkers and managers. An examination of the different effects of support from coworkers and managers could provide additional understanding of the effect of social support on job outcomes. Further, other forms of support, such as support received from ties outside the organization, should be examined in future research.Finally, this work only examined the shakedown phase and future work should examine other/all phases of the IS implementation.

Although firms make large investments in FSS, they do little to promote ISS. From the findings of this study, it is clear that ISS play a more salient role in shaping postimplementation job outcomes than do FSS. Therefore, organizations should place greater emphasis on support from employee networks through initiatives, such as training superusers. Advice networks created among employees through such initiatives would not only provide access to information, but also shield employees against negative effects of the implementation. Additionally, managers should use other known methods of alleviating job demand and increasing job control at times of such large-scale implementations because even if support is provided, its effects are complex and not always sufficiently favorable.

## Conclusions

This paper is housed within a program of research on social networks and information systems implementations. This work opens the blackbox of the effects of support structures on job outcomes to enrich our understanding about how support structures, both formal and informal, influence important job outcomes through an interplay of effects on job demand and job control. This work thus contributes a richer nomological network regarding the effects of support structures on job outcomes. From a practical perspective, this work provides insights into key leverage points and pain points that deserve careful attention when an organization engages in such large-scale implementations.

## Loadings and Cross-Loadings

Table C1 shows the loading and cross-loadings from the full model. Loadings and cross-loadings from subset models (e.g., control variables only) showed similar patterns. The results support convergent and discriminant validity, with all loadings being greater than .70 and being greater than the cross-loadings, which is the criterion in PLS (for a recent example, see Setia et al. 2013). Three additional points are worthy of note related to these results. First, ISS is not shown on the table because it is a single-indicator variable and thus has an assumed loading of 1. Second, the four FSS shown were the first-order constructs that had reflective indicators and their loadings and cross-loadings are shown. These four first-order constructs formed the second-order latent variable of FSS and the weights of the first-order factors on the second-order factor are all significant and ranged from .43 to .51. Finally, it is worth noting that for variables that were measured both before and after the implementation, the cross-loadings are somewhat higher than other cross-loadings (e.g., the cross-loadings of pre-implementation stress on postimplementation stress) but this is to be expected given that the same variables measured at different points in time are often strongly correlated.
