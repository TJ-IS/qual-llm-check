## a b s t r a c t

Evidence based medicine is the modern standard for clinical decision making where the use of medical evidence combined with clinical expertise and research is leveraged for clinical decisions. Supporting evidence based medicine (EBM) and clinical decision making (CDM) requires access to accurate predictive models and a multidimensional patient view that is aggregated from multiple sources in a multitude of configurations. Data sharing in healthcare remains a challenge due to widespread privacy concerns. Despite abundant research in privacy preserving data mining, healthcare organizations are unwilling to release their medical data on account of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requires protecting the confidentiality and security of healthcare data. Further, sensitive data spanning multiple organizations result in not only the data syntax and semantic heterogeneity but also diverse privacy requirements, posing additional challenges to data sharing and integration. In overcoming these challenges, a multi-agent approach is a viable alternative. Despite its potential for addressing the aforementioned issues, little research has been conducted in integrating a multi-agent architecture with privacy preserving data mining in big healthcare data spanning multiple organizations. This research proposes a multi-agent architecture coupled with privacy preserving techniques to facilitate data sharing while preserving privacy. Results indicate that our design artifact is capable of overcoming the aforementioned challenges, thereby facilitating data sharing for knowledge discovery in healthcare and supporting evidence based medicine and clinical decision making via improving predictive models.

## 1. Introduction

Evidence based medicine (EBM) is becoming the de facto standard in modern medicine. EBM is the use of evidence when making clinical decisions and integrating clinical expertise with available external evidence and research [48]. New strategies are needed to support health information exchange [55]. A complete view of the patient is required to support EBM and clinical decision making. A partial picture, such as missing data attributes or instances prohibits a clinician from making an accurate decision. In diagnostic decisions, misdiagnosis is expensive and potentially life threatening. In the case of small local healthcare organizations (LHO), the patient samples may not be adequate to construct accurate predictive models, thereby making data integration a necessary aspect of EMB. Similarly, physicians and other clinical decision makers require access to multiple predictive models to aid in forming a diagnostic decision. In order to build accurate predictive models and support EBM in the clinical decision making process, data integration from multiple sources is oftentimes a requirement.

Data sharing and integration is a huge challenge given the fact that there is a great explosion in the amount of data being generated [8,12, 17]. If done successfully, data integration from multiple sources can lead to productivity gains and competitive advantage. Unfortunately, widespread privacy concerns aggravate the challenge of data sharing and integration for big data analytics [2]. Medical data is highly sensitive that the federal Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requires protecting the confidentiality and security of healthcare data [11]. Security of patient privacy in health information exchanges (HIE) has become a research priority [24]. Privacy preserving data mining (PPDM) has developed various approaches for protecting the privacy of individuals or groups within a dataset while maintaining the integrity of the knowledge contained within the data for knowledge discovery purposes. Despite abundant research in PPDM, healthcare organizations are concerned with potential breach of data privacy and thus unwilling to release their medical data. Further, sensitive data spanning multiple organizations result in not only data syntax and semantic heterogeneity but also diverse privacy requirements, posing additional challenges to data sharing and integration. Overcoming the challenges of data sharing and integration in healthcare calls for a broad framework that facilitates customized inter-organizational data exchange, integration, and mining, all based on organizational requirements while maintaining privacy via the de-identification of individual data to adhere to HIPAA standards.

Multi-agent systems perform tasks and take action on behalf of users [39]. The architecture of such multi-agent systems is inherently distributed since they employ multiple agents to perform distinct tasks and operations in different capacities or in different physical or logical locations. Individual agents could be customized to meet the privacy requirements of individual data sources before transmission to a central repository for knowledge discovery. A collection of such agents could facilitate data integration and maintain data privacy as well as the integrity of source data for EBM in the clinical decision making process. Despite its potential for addressing the privacy issues in distributed data, few studies have been conducted in integrating a multi-agent architecture with privacy preserving data mining in big healthcare data distributed over multiple organizations.

Our study aims to develop a multi-agent-based approach for preserving privacy at the data source while integrating multiple data sources for knowledge discovery for EBM. Clinicians are frequently required to use data aggregated from multiple sources to support the clinical decision making process [53]. Consider the following scenario: a healthcare organization has multiple disparate data sources and wishes to share them with other organizations or researchers via a shared clinical data warehouse. Data must be integrated at the source and properly formatted for the destination clinical data warehouse (CDW). Additionally, the integrity of individual privacy must be enforced at the local site prior to sharing the data via the shared CDW. In order to address the integration and privacy issues of this common scenario, this paper presents a design artifact, called Multi-Agent Privacy Preserving for Medical Data (MAPP4MD), which follows the design science research method [21,42]. Our design artifact, MAPP4MD, functions as a necessary precursor to intelligent clinical decision support and evidence based medicine. Specifically, MAPP4MD aids in the clinical decision making process by integrating data from multiple sources so clinicians have access to more accurate predictive models. The results show that MAPP4MD is capable of preserving data privacy at the source prior to integration into larger datasets, thereby overcoming the privacy concerns which accompany integrating medical data into a central data repository for knowledge discovery. Decisions are supported via augmenting their current data with additional attributes and instances to facilitate automatic generation of more accurate predictive models. The improved robustness of the predictive models arms the physician with otherwise unavailable information and models that support the decision and diagnostic process.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides the literature review; Section 3 discusses the conceptual architecture of MAPP4MD and the implementation details. Section 4 presents the evaluation of MAPP4MD followed by the discussion in Section 5. Finally, Section 6 offers conclusions and future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Privacy preservation and data integration

Data anonymization is a key aspect of privacy preservation, particularly in healthcare. Anonymization and Pseudonymity are two important privacy measures which are used while sharing sensitive data [16]. In healthcare, the data is typically horizontally distributed with different sites holding same set of attributes for different individuals. In this case, private information that directly identifies an individual is removed from other medical data. In anonymization, a certain degree of fuzziness is introduced for the attributes that hold high risk of re-identification to a given data item. A privacy criteria such as k-anonymity is popularly used in regards to quasi-identifiers wherein a data set is called k-anonymous if each data item cannot be distinguished as different from the other (k-1) data items [30]. Generalization and suppression are the two main techniques used for introducing fuzziness in the data for the purpose of anonymization. Generalization hierarchies are used to transform categorical and discrete numeric attri butes [10]. As an example, generalization of zip-codes can have 6 levels of hierarchies with level-0 showing all the digits of a zip-code while a level-5 would hide all the digits. Such generalization is called suppression.

Research in privacy preserving data mining [19,63] has also proposed several approaches, such as data perturbation, condensation [16], sanitation [61], data distortion, and cryptographic [63]. Data perturbation inserts ‘noise’ into records, allowing individual record confidentiality [7]. In the condensation approach, data is condensed into multiple groups with a predefined group size. Statistical information such as mean and correlations about each group is maintained. This information is sufficient to preserve the characteristics of the different records [16,37]. Sanitation technique involves removing or modifying items in a database to increase or reduce the support of some frequent item sets in order to hide sensitive patterns without greatly impacting the other patterns [61]. Data distortion modifies the original data so it becomes difficult to estimate the original value of the individual records. Cryptographic methods use the concepts of cryptography in the data mining process in order to facilitate computation of the output model without revealing details of the original input data [63].

While the aforementioned techniques have addressed the privacy concerns to some extent, they assume that data integration has already been done; thus, the fundamental problems underlying privacy preserving data integration have not been addressed. A broad framework that addresses these problems is all the more crucial within the medical domain where there is a great need for data integration to improve patient care and delivery of services.

## 2.2. Evidence based medicine and data integration

Evidence based medicine (EBM) is becoming common in today's healthcare delivery. Almost all branches of medicine practice EBM [38,41] and many examples can be found in the medical literature – rhinoplasty [33], facial plastic surgery [35], hand and upper extremity surgery [9], and neurotrauma [23]. EBM requires an integrated system where the clinician can gain access to not only the best practices and evidence in assessing the benefits and risks of a treatment, but also the patient information that might be scattered across the healthcare organization. Integration of this information is crucial in deciding on a particular treatment given the current condition of the patient as well as the patient history. Thus, clinical data integration is the need of the hour, which has been accomplished with very limited success [25]. A handful of systems have been reported in the literatures that provide decision support for EBM [1,27,65]. ADDIS provides access to aggregate clinical trial results, facilitates meta-analyses, and benefit–risk decision analysis [54]. Extracting evidence and integrating appropriate patient data is a major research challenge [50]. Hospitals are investing heavily in infrastructure where disparate systems can be seamlessly connected to facilitate data integration. For example, METEOR, an enterprise health informatics environment to support evidence based medicine, acquires data from internal and external sources and integrates them into an enterprise clinical and research data warehouse [45]. While this system has been shown to be useful, this solution is specific to Houston Methodist Hospital and might not be scalable and easily transferable to other healthcare organizations. Our proposed agent-based approach addresses this limitation since agents can be customized and a multi-agent system can be flexible and scalable.

## 2.3. Multi-agent approach in data mining

Consisting of multiple collaborating agents, a multi-agent system (MAS) performs tasks on behalf of users [39]. MAS has been applied to data mining (DM), such as classification [20], association rule mining [40], data clustering [29], and classifiers [5]. Applying multi-agent systems to data mining is further demonstrated by the Parallel Data Mining Agents (PADMA) architecture proposed in [28]. The JAM project provides Java agents with learning algorithms at a local site and a meta-learning agent to combine the models from the local site [52]. ADMI is an agent-based framework for knowledge discovery in a distributed environment where agents retrieve data on demand from multiple healthcare data repositories [62]. EMADS, extendible multi-agent data mining system developed in JADE, uses wrappers (instead of ontologies) for integration and the system is extendable by adding additional agents to the architecture [3]. Although prior research on MAS has advanced the state-of-the-art in data mining, they have not addressed the privacy issues in sharing and integrating data distributed over multiple organizations.

In general, an agent-based approach for data integration is effective because the agents can be customized to deal with the heterogeneity of data as well as organizational specific constraints and practices. However, the data integration problem is exacerbated in the healthcare domain because of the privacy requirements as well as the use of different standards in various departments within the healthcare organization. MAS has been used in integration of medical data [56] as well as distributed decision support [60]. Numerous mobile healthcare applications are being developed using intelligent agents to mask the complexity and data access and integration issues [64]. While there are some works reported in the literature regarding agent-based systems for managing healthcare data [34], much work needs to be done in terms of designing and implementing multi-agent systems for intra and inter-organizational healthcare data integration. Coupled with privacy preserving data mining, a multi-agent approach should be developed to take into account the varying characteristics of diverse data sources, thereby meeting specific needs for data acquisition, privacy protection, and integration in organizations [18]. Our research aims to fill this gap via a multi-layered structure where one layer deals with the data heterogeneity issues and the other layer deals with the privacy. An agent-based approach is flexible, scalable and can effectively deal with privacy and integration issues such as data accessibility, network issues, access control, and auditing, thereby supporting evidence based medicine and clinical decision support.

## 3. MAPP4MD

## 3.1. Requirements and viable alternative

Data exists in diverse formats in a distributed environment, forcing each healthcare organization to maintain requirements for data exchange while preserving privacy. Further, successfully executing privacy preserving data mining algorithms requires a significant amount of a priori knowledge about various privacy preservation and data mining techniques and their applicability to different scenarios. Moreover, the increasing amount of data being generated introduces the need for a scalable solution in processing big healthcare data. Possessing the properties of autonomy and social-ability [58], a multi-agent approach can meet the challenges of data sharing while preserving the data privacy in healthcare. ## 3.2. Architecture

Drawing upon the widely adopted layered agent architecture that represents the different levels of abstractions [43], MAPP4MD consists of three types of agents on three interacting layers or tiers. The different layers of this architecture help to coordinate various tasks required to satisfy diverse requirements. Fig. 1 details the architecture, consisting of local health organization agents residing in the local organization layer, the broker agent in the coordination layer, and repository agents in the storage layer. A local health organization agent (LHOA) is customized to meet the unique requirements of each healthcare organization and deployed to its respective organization site. The broker agent (BA) is responsible for communications between all agents and acts as a communication proxy. A repository agent transfers data to a clinical data warehouse for knowledge discovery. All the agents contained in the three layers cooperate and collaborate to facilitate data sharing for knowledge discovery while preserving the privacy.

## 3.2.1. Local healthcare organization agent (LHOA)

The core components of an LHOA are operations module, ontologybased integration module, anonymization module, and transmission module, as shown in Fig. 2. The operations module interfaces with the local data source monitoring records for insertions. The preferred data source is a dedicated database or table in which the local healthcare organization transfers relevant records via database triggers or similar methods of data insertion. When the number of insertions on its data source reaches a threshold, the ontology-based integration module performs data integration and then stores integrated data in its local ontology. The anonymization module performs anonymization of the data, and the transmission module transfers the anonymized data to the broker agent. When the broker agent receives the anonymized data, a success message is sent to the sending LHOA. Upon receipt of the message, the LHOA agent iterates the aforementioned process. (See Fig. 3.)

3.2.1.1. LHOA operations module. An agent launcher activates an LHOA by reading a configuration file. When activated, an LHOA queries the agent framework to locate the broker agent within the agent platform. If no broker agent is located, an LHOA continues to attempt to locate it until one becomes available or until the system execution is terminated. Once the broker agent is contacted and communication is established, an LHOA reads configuration parameters, such as data source polling interval and location, integration ontology file name and location, and the threshold to reach before processing data. The local agent then initiates a behavior to listen to communication from the broker agent and invokes the SQL connection subsystem which manages the connection and polling of an ODBC data source. At the configured interval, the module keeps track of new record insertions. Once the processing threshold set by the healthcare organization is reached, the records are extracted from the data source into a temporary table for processing and tracking. The records from the temporary table are inserted into a cached row set object which then becomes the input to the ontology based data integration. The system then flags those records as ‘processed’ and archives them from the temporary table.

3.2.1.2. LHOA ontology based data integration module. The data that are gathered from multi-sources are likely heterogeneous in the syntax as well as the semantics. LHOA employs a three-step ontology-based integration process to align the data. First, a lexical-based approach is applied to align the similar attributes. Second, an instance-based approach is used to map equivalent attributes and instances. Finally, the aligned attributes and instances as well as their data types are normalized according to the schema used in the destination clinical data warehouse.

3.2.1.2.1. Lexical-based integration. The attributes of datasets are compared for semantic equivalence. This is accomplished first via direct name matching as a string comparison followed by lexical-based methods using WordNet. WordNet is a lexical database of English words grouped into cognitive synsets [13,36]. WordNet is represented and utilized as an ontology [51]. Similarity between lexical entries in WordNet is calculated using a distance based algorithm, such as conceptual similarity [32] and scaled shortest path [59]. The aforementioned methods count the distance between two nodes or concepts in an ontology by counting the number of connections between the concepts or classes. The lower the number of connections between 2 concepts, the shorter the distance. Concepts with shorter distance are considered more similar than concepts with a greater distance. In the case of an RDF graph or OWL ontology, equivalent classes will have a distance of 0 which indicates that the classes are identical. A parent and child have a distance of 1, 1 step from child to parent, whereas siblings have a distance of 2, one step to parent and another to sibling. Based on this, children are more similar to parents than siblings. This most closely mimics biological evolution where children share more DNA with a parent than a sibling.

Relationships exist between concepts in the WordNet ontology. Examples include, but are not limited to, synonyms and hyponyms. Synonyms are equivalent classes or words that have identical semantic meanings. Hyponyms are parent classes, and hypernyms are child classes in an Is-A relationship (i.e. a tiger is a feline). A hypernym has a distance of 1 from its hyponym. A subset of the WordNet ontology is shown in Fig. 4. A doctor, physician, and MD are all equivalent classes, or synonyms, and have a distance of 0. A PCP is a physician which would have a distance of 1. Since primary care physician is the same as a PCP it also has a distance of 1 from physician. Similarly, since a GP is the same as a general practitioner it has a distance of 1 from physician and because physician is the same as an MD a GP has a distance of 1 from an MD. The lexical-based method operates on feature, attribute, or names by mapping them to the destination data source or CDW.

3.2.1.2.2. Instance-based integration. The second step is instancebased matching. In the case of attribute alignment, the common instances of two attributes are examined to measure their similarity. We chose Jaccard Similarity Coefficient (JSC) [45] to measure semantic similarity between two attributes. JSC is defined as the count of the intersection divided by the count of the union of the sample sets. Let set $S _ { 1 } = \left\{ { \sf A } , { \sf B } , { \sf C } \right\}$ and set $S _ { 2 } = \{ \mathtt { B } , \mathtt { C } , \mathtt { D } \}$ ; then JSC would be defined as: $|  S _ { 1 } \cap S _ { 2 } | / |  S _ { 1 } \cup S _ { 2 } | $ . In the example of $S _ { 1 }$ and $S _ { 2 , }$ JSC is the count of {B, C}) divided by the count of {A, B, C, D} or $2 / 4 ,$ which is 50%. It indicates that sets $S _ { 1 }$ and $S _ { 2 }$ are 50% similar. Based on this similarity, the sets may be aligned by either appending to sets $S _ { 1 }$ and $S _ { 2 }$ or creating a new set o $\mathrm { f } S _ { 3 } = \{ \mathrm { A } , \mathrm { B } , \mathrm { C } , \mathrm { D } \}$ . For our attribute alignment, JSC is calculated by the count of common instances of two attributes divided by the count of all unique instances of those two ones. If JSC exceeds a similarity threshold, the two attributes are denoted equivalent. In the case of instance alignment, the common attributes are examined to align and integrate the two instances after the primary key and the foreign keys are excluded. JSC is also used to calculate the similarity between two instances. In Fig. 5, there are five common attributes of two instances; JSC is 5 / 9. If this occurs in a configured percentage of the datasets, they are merged by appending “client\_id” and “Attrib3” to the Patient table, thereby integrating into a single instance.

3.2.1.2.3. Normalization. Normalization is a process for converting values measured on different scales, mapping semantically equivalent attributes, instances, data type conversion, and schema alignment. Oftentimes, data from different sources are rated on different scales. For example, there are multiple scales to measure pain such as the numerical rating scale (NRS), Faces Pain Scale Revised (FPSR), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) [57]. Normalization of the scales may be required as part of integration and can be achieved with standardized statistical methodologies such as z-score conversions, median and standard deviations, or using a percentage function to normalize every value from 0 to 1. This feature may be implemented on a case by case basis prior to integration in the central repository or globally by normalizing all data in the central repository. This can be customized based on the MAPP4MD implementation.

Additionally, the LHOA employs an ontology to provide knowledge about the structure, data types, and attributes of the central repository at the LHO site. This ontology is configurable at the LHOA level to allow maximum flexibility in LHOA operations. This permits the LHOA to manually map attributes that are not suited for the lexical and instance-based approaches. Additionally, the ontology is updated based on the results of the lexical and instance-based approaches to reduce the time for integration. Each attribute from the central repository is mapped to the corresponding semantically equivalent attribute from the LHOA, and metadata related to the data types, sizes, as well as structure are maintained so MAPP4MD can perform mapping, merging, and alignment.

3.2.1.3. LHOA anonymization module

3.2.1.3.1. Selection of best PPDM method. The anonymization module de-identifies the data with the most appropriate PPDM method. In selecting the best PPDM technique for the data, the module uses builtin intelligence and assesses knowledge attenuation (a.k.a. information loss) caused by a PPDM. This occurs by comparing the accuracy of data mining techniques at pre- and post-PPDM times. First, the target DM technique is applied to the raw data, and its accuracy is stored if it is below a pre-set tolerance. Otherwise, the agent waits for additional data before proceeding; the supplemental data likely increases the accuracy. When the acceptable DM accuracy is obtained, each eligible PPDM method is applied to the raw data in turn. The selection of eligible PPDM methods is described in the next section. The target DM technique is then reapplied to the anonymized data, and the knowledge attenuation is calculated and compared against a pre-determined tolerance. After all the eligible PPDM methods are applied, the module chooses the one that results in the least amount of knowledge attenuation within the acceptable threshold and sends its anonymized data to the transmission module. 3.2.1.5. LHOA transmission module. Data transmission begins with the receipt of a cached row set object (CachedRowSetImpl or CRSI) that stores rows and columns of data, which have been anonymized according to the local healthcare organization's policies. Prior to transmission, the CRSI object is serialized with base 64 encoding, which is necessary for network transmission of the data. Next, the broker agent is queried for availability and, if active, accepts the encoded, anonymized data from the data transmitter module. The module waits for acknowledgement from the broker agent that the data has been received and is able to be decoded, which protects against network failure or data corruption in the serialization process, transmission, or de-serialization of the data. Once it receives confirmation that a successful data transmission has occurred, the LHOA returns to the operations phase and continues to monitor the data source.

## 3.2.2. Broker agent

The broker agent in MAPP4MD is designed to receive data from LHOAs and pass it to appropriate repository agent(s) depending on the configuration. Specifically, the broker agent has six modules, as shown in Fig. 12: ping responder, data listener, data receiver, data verifier, acknowledger, and repository transmitter. The ping responder listens and responds to incoming requests from LHOAs locating a broker agent. This corresponds to the initialization of an LHOA when it attempts to contact a broker agent. The ping responder issues information about the broker agent to the LHOA. The data listener listens for an incoming dataset from an LHOA. Once the data listener detects a dataset, it receives and transfers the dataset to the data verifier. The data verifier inspects the dataset to ensure that it is readable, error-free, and serialized from the data transmission module of the LHOA. The acknowledger responds with a message indicating whether the data is successfully received, serialized, and suitable. The broker agent transfers the verified data to the repository transmitter where it is then sent to appropriate repositories. Exceptions and conflicts in the data trigger failure messages to the LHOA for error correction and data retransmission.

## 3.3. Repository agent

A repository agent receives the data from the broker agent and makes a backup copy before integrating it with the existing datasets. This prevents the added dataset from corrupting the existing ones in the repository. Corruption could occur due to incorrectly integrated data by an LHOA. In this event, the data can be restored from a backup operation. Once the data is verified, it is transferred to a data warehouse. Since the PPDM technique is employed at the source, there is no need to reapply additional methods at the central repository, allowing data mining to occur directly at the data warehouse.

## 4. Evaluation

We conducted five experiments for evaluating the utility of MAPP4MD. The metric used to assess the utility of MAPP4MD is classification accuracy (CA) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC). Classification accuracy is the percentage of correctly classified instances, which has been a primary measure of a classifier's effectiveness. ROC plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at various threshold points, illustrating the performance of a binary classifier system. Each of the experiments is briefly discussed below.

## 4.1. Experiment 1: PPDM integration scenario

The UCI Breast Cancer dataset was chosen for its relevance to the privacy of medical data. The dataset was modified to remove missing or incomplete data and insert random names (first and last) and zip-code for each instance reducing it to 689 instance and 14 features with a target variable. Based on the wide acceptance in healthcare and ease of interpretation [49], decision trees were selected as the primary classifier coupled with naïve Bayes. Decision stumps are simple but effective one-level decision trees [22] and C4.5 is a more full-featured decision tree algorithm [46]. Naïve Bayes classifiers are simple but effective classifiers [47] and have been extensively applied to medical data and diagnoses [31]. For comparison purposes, all three were used to evaluate the effect of the pre- and post-PPDM process on various sizes of dataset using the most widely used method, the k-anonymity [37].

## 4.3. Experiment 3: agent determining PPDM parameters scenario

An LHOA automatically determines which PPDM algorithm and parameters are best for a given situation. While the agent may be customized to support any PPDM algorithm, one such example is the level of strength, or k, in the k-anonymity algorithm. A higher k results in a greater PPDM strength because it is more difficult to identify any individual but raises the knowledge attenuation. In order to determine the best level of k, an LHOA applies a modified hill-climbing scenario to continue to increase k until the accuracy decreases. In this experiment, the UCI dataset is classified with a decision stump, C4.5 decision tree algorithm, and Naïve Bayes classifier. The classification accuracy and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) for all three classifiers are examined as the k value is incremented. As shown in Figs. 18 and 19, the results reveal that the accuracy is decreased once k-anonymity is applied but remains unchanged beyond k = 2. Due to this, ROC is also examined to analyze the effect of the k level on accuracy in more detail. Since ROC also remains the same beyond k = 2, an LHOA chooses the k level of 2 as the optimal k level for the dataset since each subsequent change does not alter the anonymized dataset but adds computational complexity.

## 4.4. Experiment 4: agent determining the optimal number of records to process

An LHOA determines an optimal number of records for which to apply PPDM. The process of determining whether to wait for records or to proceed begins by recording the classification accuracy of the classifiers. PPDM, i.e. k-anonymity, is then applied and the classification accuracy on the anonymized data is recorded. The difference in accuracy between the non-anonymized records and the anonymized records is the basis for the agent to make a decision. The larger the difference in the accuracy, the larger the effect of the PPDM technique, i.e. kanonymity, on the knowledge contained in the dataset. While some attenuation of the underlying knowledge will occur when applying any PPDM technique, an LHOA seeks to minimize the knowledge attenuation. A simple hill-climbing technique is followed whereas the system continues to check the difference in classification accuracy and waits until the difference no longer decreases. An LHOA begins its search for the optimal number at 100 records, and then waits for 200, 500, 1000, 10,000, and 20,000 records averaging the difference over classifiers. ## 4.5. Experiment 5: partitioned data scenario

In the case of smaller regional medical or research facilities, each facility may only have a subset of a larger dataset. Depending on the region, data may be horizontally partitioned, as would be the case with each medical facility maintaining data on their respective patients. Another scenario is where patients visit different independent medical facilities depending on their current needs, such as different specialists for different conditions. In this example, data would be vertically partitioned among providers with each provider having certain common data (e.g. demographics) as well as specific data related to their specialty. In the case of vertically partitioned data, there may be an overlap of common features, such as demographics and common clinical data (e.g. height, weight, blood pressure); however, data specific to conditions are unlikely to overlap. Oncology data, such as tumor type and size, will be tracked by an oncologist but not by a cardiologist unless a priori knowledge of the condition exists and the physicians coordinate patient care. Using the UCI Breast Cancer dataset (14 feature and 682 instances), the following experiments were conducted to examine the classification accuracy and false negatives of predictive models inducted from both horizontally and vertically partitioned data. Results coincide with results from [66] where authors demonstrated that having additional information improved the power of predictive models.

## 4.5.1. Horizontally partitioned data

More data aids in building a more accurate predictive model thereby supporting the decision making process as well as improving the quality of the decision. Suppose two local healthcare organizations each own 50% of the data horizontally partitioned. This would be the case when the healthcare organizations are in dispersed geographic regions or serving different patient populations. One key metric for comparing predictive models on clinical data is the rate of false negatives. A false negative indicates that a positive case has been classified as negative. Conversely, a false positive occurs when a negative case is classified as positive. In the case of false positive breast cancer, someone is misdiagnosed with breast cancer, additional tests would be performed, and eventually the correct diagnosis of negative would be reached. Consider a false negative where someone with breast cancer is misdiagnosed as cancer free—no additional testing is performed and the disease will continue to spread with the eventual mortality of the patient. A false positive is not life threatening but inconvenient, whereas a false negative is potentially life threatening. In order to examine the classification accuracy and false negatives of horizontal partitioned datasets, the C4.5 decision trees were constructed with the full dataset, the first horizontal partitioned dataset (50%, H1), and the second horizontal partitioned dataset (50%, H2). A decision tree inducted from the full dataset has a tree size of 37 with 19 leaves, while a decision tree on the first horizontal partition having a size of 11 with 6 leaves and a decision tree on the second partition having a size of 15 and 8.

## 4.5.2. Vertically partitioned data

A pivot point, such as a unique patient identifier, is required to integrate vertically partitioned data. If a unique pivot point is not available, a concatenated key can be generated via combining attributes into a unique key. In the case of horizontally partitioned data, a model built on one segment will have some generalizability to other data segments due to the overlapping data attributes. In contrast, models generated on vertically partitioned data with minimal overlapping attributes will not be generalizable. To illustrate this situation, the breast cancer dataset was vertically partitioned into two datasets each with nine attributes and 682 instances. The C4.5 algorithm was applied to determine how well the vertical partitioned datasets performed against the full dataset as well as each other. As shown in Table 8, neither vertically partitioned dataset had the ability to make predictions on the other due to a lack of sufficient overlapping attributes. In both cases, the respective confusion matrices showed that all instances were classified as positive. While false positives are not as dangerous as false negatives, classifying all patients as positive leads to additional and unnecessary testing, which taxes already constrained clinical resources and burdens the patients with unnecessary costs. The full dataset was generalizable in its ability to predict the first and second vertical partition with 95.6% and 91.5% classification accuracies coupled with a 1.6% and 2.3% false negative rate. Further, it is shown that V1 is unable to predict V2, and viceversa. This indicates that the model constructed from the full dataset (H1 and H2 horizontally integrated) is the viable option.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

Data sharing and integration are the foundation for evidence based medicine and clinical decision support. Despite abundant research in privacy preserving data mining, data sharing in healthcare remains a challenge due to widespread privacy concerns. Healthcare organizations are unwilling to release their medical data on account of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), which requires protecting the confidentiality and security of healthcare data. Further, sensitive data spanning multiple organizations results in not only the data syntax and semantic heterogeneity but also diverse privacy requirements, posing additional challenges to data sharing and integration.

Following the design science research method, we have developed a multi-agent framework, called MAPP4MD, to facilitate data sharing and integration from multiple healthcare organizations while maintaining privacy of the data to support evidence based medicine and clinical decision making process. The proposed multi-agent framework consists of a federation of agents that can be customized based on the privacy requirements of the individual healthcare organization as well as the specific characteristics of the data sources and the heterogeneity of data. The architecture of MAPP4MD facilitates the evolution of agents, i.e., their functionalities can be extended as the business processes, the type of data collected, and the privacy requirements of the healthcare organization change over time. The agents use ontologies and other semantic technologies to reason about the type of privacy preserving algorithm to use, how to perform integration tasks, when to transfer data, and to resolve conflicts during integration. The distributed architecture of MAPP4MD is flexible and the agents share the workload.

MAPP4MD is implemented using JADE and the efficacy of the system has been demonstrated in five different scenarios: 1) the anonymization effects on knowledge attenuation against the incrementally integrated data vs. the full data, 2) the effects of local vs. global anonymization on knowledge attenuation, 3) selecting appropriate PPDM algorithm and the corresponding parameters for a given situation, 4) varying size of the dataset, and 5) the accuracy of predictive models following integrating vertically and horizontally partitioned data. In all these evaluations, MAPP4MD has demonstrated its capability of integrating datasets from multiple sources by deploying local agents and applying privacy preserving techniques to healthcare data locally at the source as opposed to the destination, which is essential for evidence based medicine.

From a practical standpoint, this study provides insights into how to utilize privacy preserving techniques in distributed environments to facilitate data sharing for knowledge discovery. The results of MAPP4MD evaluations demonstrate that a distributed agent-based privacy preserving technique is quite feasible. The experimental results further show that the knowledge attenuation between the anonymized full dataset and the aggregation of anonymized subsets is insignificant. Considering the fact that the full dataset is not attainable, this finding has very important implications. This clearly indicates that the knowledge encapsulated in the anonymized data at local sites could easily be substituted for the anonymized full dataset. The proposed design artifact is a viable alternative to apply the customized PPDM to local datasets based on the unique privacy requirements of an organization, thereby facilitating data sharing and integration for supporting evidence based medicine and improving clinical decision support.

Improved data sharing facilitated by MAPP4MD can have a broader impact beyond EBM and CDSS to improve medical research, population-level analysis, and evaluation of population-level healthcare activities. For example, it can facilitate quality improvements and research in healthcare delivery and practice ranging from retrospective studies to clinical trials. It can also support systematic research into assessing and gaining a comprehensive understanding of the various aspects of population/community health through the integration of public health, patient-reported, geographic, cost, patients' socio-economic, and behavioral data. In addition, the open architecture of MAPP4MD will enable the integration of additional components to serve as a platform for carrying out big data analytics, natural language text processing, machine learning and predictive modeling. This type of effort can lead to macro level analysis of the healthcare activities and policy setting within a particular geographical region.

Data sharing, for purposes such as billing and joint ventures, is permissible under HIPAA regulations. To further protect patients, many healthcare organizations augment HIPAA with more rigorous standards. Healthcare data, such as an electronic medical record (EMR), are more valuable on the black market than a credit card or social security number making it a sought after commodity. Besides implications for patient privacy, a security breach has repercussions for healthcare providers such as diminished reputation, litigation, or imposed penalties. In addition to supporting evidence based medicine and clinical decision making, MAPP4MD's architecture and operations assuage the aforesaid risks associated with data sharing. Thus, a system such as MAPP4MD can encourage providers to share healthcare data and engage in dataintensive activities without having to be overly concerned about the risks related to hackers gaining access to medical data.

While MAPP4MD has performed well in our evaluation with limited datasets, it has to be evaluated using larger datasets from an actual healthcare organization to make definitive conclusions. Our future work includes refining the model and moving towards nascent design theory aimed at revealing constructs, methods, design principles and technological rules via expanding the design science contribution of our design artifact. Furthermore, minimizing the knowledge attenuation in multi-agent frameworks for PPDM and theoretical contributions to this area is an important next step into design theory. Integration features and capabilities will be expanded in addition to the PPDM aspects of the design artifact. Scalability will be addressed by improving capacity for larger and more dynamic datasets, developing a natural language processing module for qualitative data, and enhancing the computational complexity of the integration and anonymization modules. Additionally, applying different PPDM techniques at the local agent will have an effect on the required number of records to be processed prior to integration.
