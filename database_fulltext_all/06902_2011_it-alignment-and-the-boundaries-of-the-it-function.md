## Abstract

This study applies the concept of organizational boundary to examine how outsourcing and the management of external and internal boundaries of the information technology (IT) function impact IT alignment. The article argues that by decreasing the governance costs for the less strategic IT transactions, IT outsourcing enables the IT function, and IT management particularly, to focus more on aligning the IT activities and plans with business strategies and priorities, and thereby improve IT alignment. This article also suggests that organizations may improve IT alignment by effectively transmitting knowledge and supporting collaboration between IT and business functions through boundary spanning activities and with boundary objects. The concept of organizational boundaries and boundary management has not been explicitly addressed by the existing research on IT alignment. This study also contributes to the IT outsourcing literature by linking outsourcing to improved IT alignment.

## Introduction

he alignment of information technology (IT) and business functions is identified as an important enabler of effective use of IT and organizational performance (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993; Tallon et al., 2000; Chan et al., 2001; Grant, 2003; Kearns and Lederer, 2003; Pollalis, 2003). Organizations that have aligned their IT operations and systems with strategic plans and organizational processes are found to be able to leverage new information technologies more innovatively, optimize their IT spending, and achieve competitive advantage (Luftman et al., 1993; Peppard and Ward, 1999; Chan, 2002). On the other hand, owing to the misalignment of IT and business operations, organizations are expected to make costly, and even failed, IT investments and miss opportunities provided by new IT innovations (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999).

IT alignment has been a top priority for both IT executives as well as academics in the Management Information Systems (MIS) research discipline for decades. A recent bibliography of Chan and Reich (2007a) alone summarizes the findings of more than 150 scholarly articles on IT alignment from the past 20 years. The research on IT alignment covers topics such as the different dimensions and definitions of alignment, the challenges in attaining alignment, the performance impacts of IT alignment; with antecedents of effective IT alignment being one of the prevalent themes in the empirical studies (Chan and Reich, 2007a, b; Gregor et al., 2007; Moreno et al., 2009). However, despite the continuous managerial and scholarly interest in the sources of effective IT alignment, many organizations struggle to integrate the priorities and activities of their IT and business units.

In order to complete our understanding of IT alignment, Chan and Reich (2007a) call for greater use of well-established theories in IT alignment research. This study complies to Chan and Reich’s advice by exploring IT alignment through the concept of organizational boundary. Organizational boundaries are applied in the knowledge- and resource-based views of the firm, as well as in transaction cost economics, to examine how knowledge and resources are placed and transferred across organizational units. This article builds on the literature around organizational boundaries to understand how the transfer of knowledge and resources across the IT function and its environment impacts IT alignment.

## Alignment of IT and business

The alignment of IT and business means that the strategy, structures, technology, and processes of the IT organization are integrated to those of the business functions so that the IT personnel and their business partners are working toward the same organizational goals (Chan, 2002). Without alignment, organizations are more likely to build IT solutions that do not effectively support organizational activities and are more prone to miss business innovation opportunities provided by new information technologies.

Henderson and Venkatraman (1993) base their acclaimed model of strategic alignment on the building blocks of strategic fit and functional integration. Strategic fit links the business and IT strategies and reflects the organization’s external environment. Functional integration is more operational and internally focused, linking the organizational infrastructure and processes with the IT infrastructure and IT-related processes. One of the key contributions of Henderson’s and Venkatraman’s model is that it brings forward the external domain of IT, consisting of information technology scope, systemic competencies and IT governance, and strongly advocates the need to align the external IT domain with the internal one, including information systems architecture, IT-related processes, and IT-related skills. Thus, the model not only calls for the alignment of the business and IT domains, but it also highlights the requirement to integrate the different dimensions of IT management.

Chan (2002) has explored the alignment of IT and the rest of the organization from two perspectives, by differentiating strategic and structural alignment. Strategic alignment is defined to focus on the fit between the priorities and activities of the IT organization and those of the business units, involving both the content of the strategy and the strategy-making process. The structural alignment again examines the fit between the IT-related decision-making rights, reporting relationships, (de)centralization of IT services and infrastructure, and the deployment of IT personnel. Reich and Benbasat (2000) offer another kind of dichotomy for alignment by identifying intellectual and social dimensions. An alignment of strategies, structures and planning methodologies composes the intellectual dimension, whereas values, experiences and communications create the social dimension of alignment.

Despite the widely discussed and acknowledged benefits of such IT alignment, it seems to remain elusive for many organizations. A number of factors influencing the divergence of IT and business priorities have been identified by both practitioners as well as by academic research. First, if the governance mechanism for IT does not reflect the organizational characteristics, the organization are likely to have challenges to take full advantage of the business potential of IT (DeSanctis and Jackson, 1994; Sambamurthy and Zmud, 1999). The IT department and the rest of the organization can also be culturally apart (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Willcoxson and Chatham, 2004). The IT professionals may apply terminology and language that is not easily comprehended by the rest of the organization, they may be more inclined to solve technological rather than organizational issues, and they may have unique processes and policies that are foreign to other people in the organization. Likewise, the business executives and managers may in turn have limited understanding of technology-related issues, and they may lack skills to specifically articulate their needs and expectations for IT (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Chan, 2002). It has also been suggested that the traditional planning processes between IT and business functions do not assure the effective use of IT; rather a more dynamic approach to the collaboration is required (Grant, 2003).

In addition to the large number of factors that complicate the IT and business relationship, the existent research has identified the specific mechanisms and practices that organizations apply to improve the collaboration between the two domains. Organizations are known to have either centralized, decentralized or federated configurations to manage IT-issues, depending on a number of internal and external contingencies (Brown, 1999), to integrate their IT and business strategies (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993), and to appoint dedicated relationship managers to facilitate the collaboration between IT and business organizations (Iacono et al., 1995). Organizations have also been reported to support organizational integration with joint steering committees, cross-functional teams, high levels of communication and shared performance metrics between IT and business organizations (DeSanctis and Jackson, 1994; Van der Zee and de Jong, 1999; Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Tallon et al., 2000; Meyer, 2004).

Ward and Peppard (1996) symbolize the often-difficult relationship between IT and business functions as ‘a troubled marriage in need of guidance’ and offer a framework to manage the relationship (Peppard and Ward, 1999). This framework covers governance structures and processes, values and beliefs, leadership, roles, and service quality. Peppard and Ward underline that the effective exploitation of IT for business benefits is an organizationwide issue and cannot be left only to the IT organization’s responsibility. They also opine that the cultural differences between the IT and business functions are not the root cause for the ineffective working relationships between the two domains, but rather an outcome of poor alignment.

In addition to an often intense collaboration in many information system development projects, IT alignment requires a continuous effort to integrate the organization’s IT and business activities (Broadbent and Weill, 1993; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993). Alignment is an ongoing organizational process in which both IT and business managers participate in the exchange of knowledge (Kearns and Lederer, 2003). Successful alignment of the planning processes implies not only that the Chief Information Officer (CIO), or IT director, participates in business planning but also that the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) and organization’s other executives join in IT planning processes, too.

## Perspectives on organizational boundaries

Although the concept of an organizational boundary, referring to the demarcation line between the actors, social relations, and activities of an organizational unit and its environment (Scott, 2003; Heracleous, 2004), is central and ubiquitous in social sciences, it is also a complex and multifaceted construct with distinct definitions and theoretical underpinnings. In transaction cost economics boundaries determine where the organizational tasks are conducted. With hierarchical governance the organization operates and owns its resources, whereas with market governance the organization interacts with other organizations to acquire and operate resources. What can be referred to as the governmental boundary, defines the locus of resources as well as the power and control of decision-making regarding the allocation of the resources (Barney, 1999). Another technical perspective on organizational boundaries is provided by the property rights approach where boundaries determine the ownership of common assets (Grossman and Hart, 1986).

Boundaries are also considered more complex, socially constructed and shifting entities between ‘us’ and ‘them’ (Heracleous, 2004). Cultural boundaries refer to differences in occupational conventions and understanding as well as discrepancies in values, the important concerns and goals that are shares by most people that shape group performance, and behavioral norms, the common ways of acting found in a group (Ward and Peppard, 1996; Kellog et al., 2006). In a similar vein, social boundaries dissociate the identities of different organizational groups and providing distinct communities and the rules of exclusion to actors outside the boundaries (Kogut and Zander, 1996; Oliver and Montgomery, 2005).

A knowledge-based view on organizational boundary sees boundaries as markers of unique knowledge domains with syntactic, semantic, and political dimensions (Carlile, 2004; Oliver and Montgomery, 2005). As Tushman (1977) points out, different organizations as well as differentiated subunits are likely to cultivate contrasting languages and coding schemes. The syntactic boundary means that separate sides possess different information and apply different language so that even if knowledge is shared, the other side may not comprehend it accurately (Carlile, 2004). The semantic boundary suggests that even if a common language exists so that the syntactic boundaries can be crossed, interpretations of the same information often differ among organizational actors making communication and collaboration difficult (Brown and Duguid, 2001; Obstfeld, 2005; Kellog et al., 2006). Finally, a political boundary refers to the additional organizational effort required to develop common interests for the organizational actors to share and assess new knowledge. It has been suggested that instead of the common organizational goals, organizational managers tend to focus on the immediate goals of their specific organizational subunit (March and Simon, 1958; Lawrence and Lorsch, 1976). This position bias that creates the foundation for the political boundary can affect the willingness and ability for individuals to share and assess knowledge.

## Organizational boundaries and IT alignment

Organizations are known to conduct boundary management activities to cross the different dimensions of organizational boundaries. This study analyzes how three different boundary management strategies impact IT alignment. The three strategies are boundary decisions, boundary spanners, and boundary objects (see Table 1). IT outsourcing is a boundary decision where the organization chooses to shape its organizational boundaries by conducting specific activities outside the organization through market mechanisms. Through IT outsourcing the organization shapes the boundaries of its IT function so that IT management is able to focus more on coordinating business-specific issues instead of purely IT-related tasks. The main argument elucidated by the boundary decision strategy is that outsourcing decreases the amount of attention required by the IT management to govern the essentially technology-related activities, such as, for example, network and infrastructure management and application maintenance. At the same time, outsourcing enables IT management to direct more attention toward business critical issues and invest more time in coordinating the collaboration between IT and business functions. The other two boundary management strategies, boundary spanning and boundary objects, again, are essential in the transfer of knowledge and information across internal and external organizational boundaries. We will next discuss the different boundary management strategies in detail and derive the hypotheses to be tested in the analysis.

## Boundary decision

IT outsourcing is a boundary decision, where an organization chooses to turn over some of its IT services and assets, or even the entire IT function, to an external organization who in exchange provides and manages the assets and services for monetary returns over a contractual time period (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992b; Lacity and Hirscheim, 1993; Kern and Willcocks, 2000: 322). Outsourcing shapes the boundaries of the IT function and impacts the scope of IT governance in the organization. As the organization transfers its IT assets, processes, leases, licenses, and staff to an external IT vendor, the tasks of the internal IT function, and that of the IT management, are in many ways transformed. When IT management no longer needs to focus on the outsourced IT activities and assets, they can redirect their attention to other, more value adding tasks. This opportunity to outsource routine IT activities in order to focus on more strategic uses of IT has been found to be one of the main motivations for outsourcing (Grover et al., 1996). Other pursued benefits include the reduction and improved control of IT-related costs and an improved access to technical competences the organization does not possess in-house (McLellan et al., 1995; DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani, 1998; Salimath et al., 2008).

Organizations are known to outsource a variety of ITrelated services and assets, such as application development, application maintenance, systems operations, end-user support, data centers, and project management (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992a; Grover et al., 1996; Fish and Seydel, 2006). Parts of the IT activities are by nature continuous and more focused on keeping the IT infrastructure operational, such as support and maintenance services. On the other hand, other IT activities are conducted on a project basis to implement new solutions and new kinds of IT-support for business processes, such as application development. Whereas earlier research has reported that the success of outsourcing relates highly to the degree of outsourcing of ongoing systems operations and support, the outsourcing success has been found not to be related to application development (Grover et al., 1996). This finding is credited to the organization-specific nature of application development projects, which may increase the coordination costs of the project and at the same time reduce the economies of scale and scope for the external IT-service provider. In application development projects IT and business people jointly design and implement new organization-specific solutions. Although the external IT-service providers may provide required technological capabilities and needed extra resources, they may not possess, compared with the internal IT staff, sufficient understanding of the organization-specific issues and relationships.

Outsourcing the ongoing and more routinized IT services has been proposed to imply changes in the role of the in-house IT function and to enable an organization’s own IT management and staff to focus more on the critical internal activities with more value adding potential (Grover et al., 1996; Ward and Peppard, 1996; Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Jacobides, 2005). The review of the literature on IT outsourcing suggests that especially the outsourcing of the continuous and routinized IT services contributes to changes in the role of the internal IT personnel and eventually to better alignment between the IT and business functions. However, the relationship between the outsourcing of IT development projects and the alignment is not as evident, as the literature provides diverse arguments about the organizational outcomes of the outsourcing of IT development. Hence the following hypothesis is investigated:

Hypothesis 1: The degree of outsourcing of the information systems maintenance and support is positively related to the alignment between the information systems (IS) and business domains.

## Boundary spanners

Boundary spanners are members of an organization who coordinate the work among interdependent organizational units and monitor the activities in the external environment (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1976; Schwab et al., 1985; Carlile, 2004). Boundary spanners disseminate information inside and between organizations and act as liaison officers across organizational boundaries. The objective of their work is to link isolated sources of information and to facilitate and integrate interrelated tasks and processes in an organization.

Boundary spanners align interdependent domains that possess different sets of knowledge by transferring, translating, and transforming information inside and between organizations (Carlile, 2002; Carlile, 2004). ‘Transferring refers to the processing of information and includes the development of a common lexicon across boundaries. ‘Translation’ means creating common and shared meanings for domain-specific knowledge. Finally, the transformation process establishes common interests requiring a significant practical and political effort. To conduct these knowledgesharing activities, boundary spanners are known to engage in practices that help them know the organization and its members as well as knowing how to coordinate tasks and develop new capabilities (Orlikowski, 2002).

Based on the boundary-crossing activities they perform, the different roles of boundary spanners have been labeled as ‘ambassadors’, ‘task coordinators’, ‘scouts’, and ‘guards’ (Ancona and Caldwell, 1990, 1992). The ambassadors protect the team from outside pressure, persuade others to support the team, and lobby for resources. Task coordinators coordinate and negotiate with the members of the external domains. Scouts scan for external information about the competition, markets, and technologies to add to the expertise of the group. Guards have a more inward perspective, they avoid releasing team-related information to others in the organization in order to sustain the team’s profile. Concerning the effectiveness of these different boundary-spanning strategies, the ambassadorial activities are proposed to contribute to long-term performance (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). Too much scouting, on the other hand, seems to be related to low performance.

Three necessary conditions for effective boundary spanning have been identified (Levina and Vaast, 2005). First, boundary spanners need to become legitimate participants in the practices of their own units as well as those of the external unit(s), which requires sufficient competence and understanding of each practice. Legitimacy is also in part built on interpersonal trust that is acknowledged as an important antecedent for knowledge creation and sharing in an organization (Abrams et al., 2003). Second, boundary spanners should be legitimate negotiators on behalf of their own area and also seen by the others as capable of influencing practices in their own field. And finally, boundary spanners need to develop an inclination and motivation to span the boundaries of diverse fields.

Managers have been recognized as potential boundary spanners in organizations. Managers are believed to possess the required legitimacy across functions and also to have unique access to organizational and environmental information that needs to be transmitted across boundaries (Galbraith, 1977). The manager of the IT function, often referred to as the CIO, can therefore arguably be regarded as a potential liaison officer between the IT and business organizations (Thorogood et al., 2003).

As defined above, a critical condition for boundary spanners is the legitimacy of their position and person. The required legitimacy of the CIO to behave as an effective boundary spanner between the IT and business domains is reasserted by a strong relationship between the CIO and the CEO of the firm. It has been proposed that a regular access to the CEO increases the ability of the CIO to share knowledge about emerging IT-related opportunities so that collaboration between the two is strengthened (Kearns and Lederer, 2003). It has also been argued that when the CIO reports directly to the CEO it reflects the importance of IT in the organization and provides the CIO an access to senior executives, political support in decision making, and in general more responsibility (Cecere et al., 2005). Overall, effective collaboration between IT and business is suggested to require a good working relationship and a high level of communication between the CEO and CIO (Peppard and Ward, 1999; Reich and Benbasat, 2000).

The reporting relationship between the CIO and the CEO can be expected to strengthen and reflect the legitimacy of the CIO to act as a plausible boundary spanner between the IT and business functions. As the purpose of the boundary spanning activity was to align distinct but interrelated functions in the organization, the following hypothesis is investigated:

Hypothesis 2: The reporting relationship between the CIO and the CEO is positively related to the alignment between the IS and business domains.

## Boundary objects

Common models, lexicons, maps, tools, and drawings have been found to facilitate organizational knowledge transfer and sharing of information (Carlile, 2002, 2004; Pawlowski and Robey, 2004; Levina and Vaast, 2005; Kellog et al., 2006). These so called boundary objects are applied to establish a shared context and reference points between different but dependent problem solving contexts (Star, 1989). They provide organizational actors concrete means of representing and interpreting different functional interests and perspectives (Carlile, 2004). Examples of boundary objects in organizations include sketches and mock ups (Carlile, 2002), organization-wide information systems (Pawlowski and Robey, 2004), engineering tools (Carlile, 2004), and templates and documents (Levina and Vaast, 2005).

Carlile (2002) has identified three requirements for an object to serve as an effective tool for knowledge transformation and joint problem solving. First, the object needs to establish a shared syntax or language for individuals to represent their knowledge and ideas. Second, the object needs to provide actual and concrete means for individuals to specify and learn about the differences and dependencies across boundaries. Third, the object should enable people to jointly transform the knowledge and to draw on, alter, and manipulate the object to serve their specific needs for knowledge transfer and interpretation. In other words, boundary objects should be plastic and allow for multiple interpretations and uses by the different organizational actors (Star, 1989).

In the IT domain, enterprise architecture possesses many of the characteristics of boundary objects. Enterprise architecture refers to the ‘choices that define the portfolio of applications, the configuration of hardware, software and communication, and the data architecture that collectively define the technical architecture’ for the organization (Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993: 474). Enterprise architecture includes models of business operations, information systems, and technologies, as well as provides an organization ‘a blueprint for a technical platform which responds to present and future business needs’ (Zachman, 1987; Willcocks and Feeny, 2006: 51). It describes existing structures and relations as well as the IT-related decisions and guidelines for the entire organization or for a specific domain.

Enterprise architecture helps clarify and build an organizational consensus around technology, data, and process standards and conceptualize IT assets from many different perspectives (Sowa and Zachman, 1992; Ross and Westerman, 2004). It also provides IT and business professionals a framework for clarifying business concepts and relating them to IT-related issues, thus helping business and IT organizations to capture and communicate business and IT-related information and responsibilities in a comprehensible way (McDavid, 1999; Versteeg and Bouwman, 2006). Especially the organization-wide enterprise architectures are believed to be effective communications tools, as they provide a shared context for IT-related decisions in the entire organization (Armour et al., 1999a).

Enterprise architecture and IT policies help share knowledge between the IT and business functions and create a common syntax for people to represent their ideas and questions. Enterprise architecture has been compared to the classical architectures utilized, for example, when building a house (Zachman, 1987). In both cases, the architecture helps to articulate the needs, ideas, and solutions between different sides, such as the owner of the house, the architect, and the builder. With enterprise architecture, the typical users include business representatives – that is, the managers and other organizational actors outside the IT community – IT management, IT architects, and information system developers.

By providing a common language and simplified and comprehensible representations for IT-related issues in the organizations, enterprise architecture designs and models can be seen to serve as organizational boundary objects and thereby facilitate collaboration and alignment between IT and business organizations. Hence the following hypothesis is investigated:

Hypothesis 3: The application of enterprise architectures is positively related to the alignment between the IS and business domains.

## Method

## Data and sample

The data for this study was collected from the CIO’s and IT managers of Finland’s 500 largest companies during the summer of 2006. In 2005, the average turnover of Finland’s top 500 companies was 590 MEUR (million euros), ranging from over 30,000 MEUR to less than 100 MEUR. Based on a more detailed industry categorization of a Finnish business journal, Talousela¨ma¨, we classified the companies into four industries. Manufacturing was the largest group comprising 46% of the companies; followed by retail (28%), services (20%), and finance (6%) industries.

The distribution list was based on a commercial address list that was complemented with contact information received from the Finnish Information Processing Association and from an international IT consulting company. A total of 431 respondents were contacted by e-mail and asked to complete an online survey. In all 76 of the e-mails did not reach the respondents, thus indicating a fairly high turnover of IT professionals. From the 355 respondents that we were able to contact, we received 102 answers with a response rate of 37%. We contacted multiple respondents in some organizations, so the number of different organizations in our distribution list was 275. Duplicate answers were received from three organizations and used to create sum variables. For three other organizations we also received duplicate answers but from different business divisions. We decided to treat these responses separately because of the separate business focuses of the divisions. Our final sample thus consisted of 99 usable surveys from 96 different companies. 61% of the respondents were CIO’s or IT directors, 31% were IT managers, 5% IT specialists, and the remaining 2% had a managerial position in the line organizations.

As we were not able to collect contact information from all top 500 companies, we conducted a Mann–Whitney test to analyze potential biases in our distribution list. The results show that our list was somewhat biased for larger organizations. One cause for this could be that the contact information, for example the name and the e-mail address of the CIO, collected for commercial mailing lists may be more readily available for larger companies. No industry bias was found in the distribution list. We ran another Mann–Whitney test to evaluate potential non-response bias and found out that we had received proportionally more responses from larger organizations. Again, no industry effects were perceived. In our analysis, however, we found no correlation between size and the dependent variable; we also controlled for the size effect in the structural equation models. So, although our data is biased on larger companies we found no statistical reason not to generalize our findings for all Finnish top 500 companies.

We contacted CIO’s as we believed the IT management is best positioned to complete our questions on IT outsourcing and enterprise architecture and would be a reliable informant on the issues concerning the alignment between IT and business functions. Our decision to address CIO’s was supported by earlier empirical research that has shown that there are no significant differences in the perspectives of IT and business managers concerning IT alignment (Burn and Szeto, 2000).

The constructs for IT alignment and enterprise architecture were created by calculating the mean scores of the single indicators. ## Discussion

In his 1967 essay on management information systems, or more accurately management misinformation systems, Ackoff (1967) called for a closer collaboration between IT and business managers. After four decades, Ackoff ’s argument still remains highly topical. Concluding from the continuous attention given to the subject by both academics and practitioners, even if the discourse has become quite all-embracing, the alignment of IT and business objectives and operations seems to remain one of the most difficult organizational challenges for more efficient and effective use of IT.

This articles examines the alignment of IT and business from the perspective of organizational boundaries. Despite the extensive literature on organizational boundaries and boundary management in organizational theories, the concept of boundary seems to be largely omitted in the literature on IT alignment. In their recent, extensive review article on IT alignment, Chan and Reich (2007b) make no reference to organizational boundaries even if they widely discuss the challenges, antecedents, and success factors of IT alignment. Furthermore, in their annotated bibliography on IT alignment Chan and Reich (2007a) examine more than 150 alignment articles, yet they do not find studies that link the management of organizational boundaries with IT alignment, and only six articles where

IT outsourcing is addressed. As Chan and Reich (2007b) at the same time find most IT alignment research atheoretical and call for greater use of well-established theories in the IT alignment research, in can be argued that the approach and the foundation for this article is well warranted.

The key contribution of this study to the IT alignment literature is that the alignment of IT and business requires effective management of both internal and external boundaries. Outsourcing enables organizations to shape their external boundaries so that the focus of IT governance can be directed away from technology-specific issues and towards more value adding activities. In other words, when the CIO and the rest of the IT management need to control and coordinate extensive IT functions conducting a diverse set of ongoing IT support and maintenance activities, their ability to attend and concentrate on joint planning and collaboration with business management may be compromised. Naturally, outsourcing as such may not ensure that the IT management can turn their focus away from the outsourced activities. However, when the services provided by the external vendor are in line with the agreed service levels (see: Goo et al., 2008), the IT management can more readily turn their attention away from the production of IT services and towards integrating the IT services and operations more tightly with the organizational processes and goals.

Whereas most research on IT alignment focuses solely on the internal characteristics and requirements (see: Chan and Reich, 2007b), this study proposes that skillful management of the external boundary of the IT function can create a foundation for an improved IT alignment. With the growth of IT outsourcing as well as increasing information exchange and collaboration between organizations, the idea of a clear demarcation line between organizations may become less relevant. At the same time, the management of the external boundaries and coordination across organizations becomes more important. As technological expertise, IT systems, and even organizational data more often lie outside the borders of the organization, the antecedents of alignment can no longer be found exclusively within the organization. More focus on the external dimension of IT alignment, from both practitioners as well as from academics, is called for.

Concerning the management of the different dimensions of internal boundaries, this study examines the role of boundary spanners and boundary objects in IT alignment. The legitimacy of the CIO to act as a trusted liaison officer between the IT and business functions was operationalized by the reporting relationship between the CIO and the CEO. In our analysis, organizations where the CIO was reporting directly to the CEO were reporting better alignment scores. The reporting relationship between the CIO and the CEO signals the status of the CIO in the organization as well as the importance of IT for the organization. In addition, the direct reporting relationship implies an improved access to top management for the CIO and is also likely to drive CEO’s to pay more attention to IT specific issues and initiatives. Although there was no relation between the size of the organization and the alignment, the size of the organization was affecting negatively to the reporting relationship between the CIO and the CEO (Po0.05), thus suggesting that in large organizations the CIO was reporting to other members of the top management instead of the CEO. No relation was found between organizational size and reporting relationship between the CIO and the CFO.

Although this study focused on the role of the CIO, the boundary spanning activities can be naturally conducted by other members of the IT function as well. In addition to specific relationship managers (Iacono et al., 1995), middle-level managers, project managers, IT architects, and system developers are all potential knowledge brokers between IT and business organizations. As with IT management, outsourcing may enable these organizational actors to allocate more time to cooperating with the user organizations and to coordinate organizational initiatives and tasks across functions. To build legitimacy required for effective boundary spanning these IT professionals have to demonstrate their competence and actively participate in cross-functional activities (Tushman and Scanlan, 1981; Levina and Vaast, 2005).

Enterprise architecture was found to serve as a boundary object and be associated with improved IT alignment. Enterprise architecture help transmit decisions and plans across the entire organization in a way that is informative and comprehensible in both IT and business contexts. A typical enterprise architecture covers a number of dimensions of organizational activities and IT assets, such as business processes, information, systems, applications, and technological infrastructure. These different levels of an enterprise architecture are meant to be additive and complementary representations that have common interfaces and connections (Zachman, 1987; Armour et al., 1999b). This multi-dimensionality of an enterprise architecture can be assumed to further improve its applicability and flexibility for boundary crossing interpretations. Other potential boundary objects between IT and business functions may include different kinds of plans, roadmaps, mock ups, and even user interfaces of information systems.

Associating IT alignment with boundary management suggests that sustaining alignment is a continuous activity. Transmitting and translating knowledge across functions and organizations requires ongoing effort from boundary spanners. Likewise, the boundary objects need to be constantly updated as the organizational, and technological, characteristics evolve. For example, when organization’s processes or technological commitments change, the enterprise architecture needs to be updated respectively. In many organizations the shape of the external boundary may appear to be fairly stable. However, when the organizational boundaries become more ambiguous, for example with increasing outsourcing, the requirement for effective boundary management activities across organizational borders may also increase.

This study contributes to the literature on IT outsourcing by linking outsourcing to improved alignment of IT. In the spirit of transaction cost economics (TCE), a perspective widely applied in outsourcing studies, the findings of the study propose that organizations should set the external boundary of the IT function to a point where the IT management can most effectively coordinate the work of the external IT partners, internal IT professionals, as well as the collaboration between the IT and business functions. It is notably this internal integration of IT and business domains that seems to be omitted in the outsourcing studies that stress cost efficiencies, access to unique skills, and institutional pressures as the main drivers for outsourcing.

For practitioners the key finding of this study is that outsourcing of routinized IT operations, such as system operations, maintenance, and support, may enable the internal IT personnel to increasingly direct their attention to organization-specific business issues. Outsourcing can therefore be seen as an opportunity to ‘fix the troubled marriage’ between IT and business functions (see: Ward and Peppard, 1996), and to establish a new kind of collaboration mode between the technology and business experts. However, the designers of IT functions should also be aware of the potential downside of outsourcing. Although outsourcing may help organizations streamline predictable activities and focus on their core competences, high levels of formalization, for example in the form of outsourcing contract, may not be flexible enough for turbulent environments (Vaast and Levina, 2006). Also, the lack of evidence in this study concerning the relation between IT development outsourcing and IT alignment can be read as a reminder of the challenges related to IT projects and the requirement for intra-organizational collaboration around development initiatives.

The data also revealed that the bigger the organization, the less likely the CIO is to report directly to the CEO. However, considering the findings of this study it could be proposed that the reporting relations between the CIO and CEO could also be established, if only as a provisional solution, in large organizations to improve the alignment and collaboration between the IT and business functions. Reporting to the CEO may provide the CIO improved access to executive-level decision makers and project the importance and legitimacy of the IT function to the rest of the organization. The opportunity to utilize the reporting relation between the CIO and CEO as a transient solution is endorsed by earlier research that suggests that the greatest driver for the CIO to report to the CEO is the degree to which change is required (Cecere et al., 2005).

The cultural and knowledge barriers in organizations are known to be reinforced by differences in terminology and interpretations (Carlile, 2002). Enterprise architecture, including conceptualizations of business processes and digital systems, helps organizational actors cross these boundaries by establishing a shared language and joint practices for knowledge sharing. This study reminds practitioners that an enterprise architecture that covers both technological and business domains will facilitate the collaboration between the IT and business professionals and help them manage and develop increasingly large and complex information systems.

The study prompts interesting areas for future research. Concerning external boundaries, additional research is required to understand the actual shape of the boundary in terms of optimal IT alignment. In other words, what is the balance of internal and external IT work that contributes to a successful collaboration between IT and business functions in different contexts? Future work could also examine how boundary spanners and boundary objects help cross the demarcation line between two separate organizations. For example, how enterprise architecture, and other potential boundary objects, facilitate the collaboration between the client organization and their outsourcing partners? Future studies could also further analyze the nature and dimensions of boundaries between IT functions and their environment. One area of interest is how the co-operation between IT and business functions and the attitudes of the IT and business personnel are affected by outsourcing. The potential contextual bias of this study could be assessed with additional research in different environmental settings.

Our decision to focus on the CIO’s of the 500 largest companies in Finland makes the data country-specific, which may limit the generalizability of the results. For example, the maturity of the outsourcing market may vary from country to country. Also, the industry structure in Finland may be dissimilar to other countries. Another possible limitation is the use of a single informant instead of a larger group of respondents. Although we acknowledge that the use of multiple informants would have been preferable, we remain convinced that the CIO’s and other IT managers are well equipped to assess the IT-specific issues and the relations with the business functions in their organizations, as also suggested by earlier studies (Burn and Szeto, 2000). Finally, our study can be argued to suffer from potential measurement errors not only because of single respondent, but because of other survey-specific biases, too. To cope with the potential measurement error, we designed the survey instrument and applied statistical remedy techniques guided by existing literature.

## Conclusion

Along with the increasing growth of digital information systems, the alignment between IT and business functions remains a serious and ongoing challenge for many organizations. The study herein was conducted to improve our understanding of how the management of organizational boundaries, both external and internal, impacts IT alignment. The paper suggests that effective boundary management can help organizations to improve IT alignment. The three sources of alignment identified by this research, outsourcing of IT maintenance and support, the reporting relationship between the CIO and CEO, and the application of enterprise architecture have so far been neglected in empirical research. For the academic reader, the article proposes that the concept of organizational boundary and the practices for managing the boundaries may provide new insights for future research on IT alignment.
