# CONSTRUCTS AND INDICATORS: AN ONTOLOGICAL ANALYSIS<sup>1</sup>

Ron Weber

Faculty of Information Technology, Monash University, Melbourne AUSTRALIA, and School of Business, University of Queensland, Brisbane, AUSTRALIA {Ron.Weber@monash.edu}

Constructs and indicators are central to the efforts of many researchers who seek to build and test theories and articulate rich narratives about real-world phenomena. For this reason, an extensive discourse exists about their nature. Increasingly, this discourse has become fraught with controversy. Using Bunge’s (1977, 1979) ontology, I examine the nature of constructs and indicators as they are discussed in the extant literature. I define these concepts precisely, disentangle conceptual from measurement issues, and point to ways that discourse about them could better proceed. I show that unidimensional constructs, multidimensional constructs, dimensions, and indicators are all properties in general of a class of things. I also show that only three types of indicators exist—synonyms of the focal construct and succeeding or preceding properties in a pre-order of properties that includes the focal construct. I examine ontologically the notions of content validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and internal-consistency reliability and show their problematic nature. I introduce two new concepts, scope validity and the level of concomitance of indicators, that have rigorous ontological foundations. Together, they provide an improved foundation for assessing the construct validity of a set of indicators.

Keywords: Ontology, unidimensional constructs, multidimensional constructs, reflective indicators, formative indicators, convergent validity, discriminant validity, reliability, content validity, construct validity, scope validity, level of concomitance

“A final point worth noting is that formative and reflective measurement models have different ontological assumptions that rarely have been discussed in the literature, but nevertheless have important implications.” (MacKenzie et al. 2011, p. 303)

## Introduction

Constructs and indicators play central roles in the theorybuilding and theory-testing work of many researchers. Nonetheless, a longstanding, extensive discourse exists about their exact nature, purpose, and use (e.g., see early papers by Blalock 1964; Bollen 1984; Bollen and Lennox 1991; Chin 1998; Fornell and Bookstein 1982). Almost from the outset, this discourse has been fraught with controversies. Many issues still remain open and contested (e.g., Bollen and Diamantopoulos 2017a; Bollen and Diamantopoulos 2017b; Guyon 2018; Hardin 2017; Hardin and Chang 2013). Moreover, increasingly the debates that underpin the discourse have become entrenched, arcane, and fragmented. The first column of Table A1 in Appendix A shows the types of issues that have been canvassed.

I contend the reason for these outcomes is that we lack a rigorous ontology (a general theory about the nature of and characteristics of the main types of phenomena that exist in the real world). Such an ontology could be used to (1) clarify the nature of the concepts that are the focus of the debates, and (2) assist with our analysis of and use of these concepts. Absent such an ontology, I contend that protagonists in the debates are unlikely to resolve their differences. In this paper, therefore, I adopt an existing formal, rigorous ontology and show how it can be used to improve our understanding of constructs and indicators. I am responding to calls by several eminent researchers on the need for an ontology to inform existing discourse (e.g., Bagozzi 2011; Borsboom et al. 2004; Edwards 2011; MacKenzie et al.

2011). Overall, I hope to lay a foundation that will enable future discourse on constructs and indicators to proceed in clearer, more constructive ways.

Specifically, I seek to contribute to the prevailing discourse in four ways. First, I define precisely and clarify key concepts used in the discourse. Second, I disentangle theoretical from measurement issues—a problem that confounds much of the discourse (e.g., Coltman et al. 2008; Edwards and Bagozzi 2000). Third, I show why some existing discourse about certain types of construct measurement validity and reliability is problematic, and I provide ways to mitigate the problems. Fourth, I provide an ontologically based way of choosing a high-quality set of indicators to measure a construct.

The paper proceeds as follows. First, I discuss the ontological foundations I use to examine the extant discourse. Second, based on these foundations, I examine the nature of constructs. Third, I consider the nature of indicators. Fourth, I discuss the nature of unidimensional constructs and their measurement. Fifth, I examine the nature of multidimensional constructs. Sixth, I discuss some theoretical implications of my analyses. Seventh, I show how some of my analytical results can be applied during instrument design and validation. Finally, I canvass some limitations of my analyses, articulate some research opportunities, summarize my main findings, and present brief conclusions.

## Ontological Foundations

The ontology I use in my analysis of constructs and indicators is Bunge’s (1977, 1979). It is a “scientific ontology”—one that seeks “to analyse and to systematize the ontological categories and hypotheses germane to science” (Bunge 1977, p. 12). I have chosen his ontology for three reasons. First, it is exemplary in terms of its clarity and rigor, as well as the way in which its elements coherently build on each other. Second, many researchers have found that it assists their analyses of a wide range of physical, behavioral, and social phenomena (e.g., Gräbner and Kapeller 2015; Kaidesoja 2009; Lukyanenko et al. 2014; Mattessich 2014; Matthews 2019; Nevo and Wade 2010; Parsons 2011; Turvey 1992; Wan 2012; Weingartner and Dorn 1990). Third, it has been used extensively by information systems researchers—see reviews by Burton-Jones et al. (2017) and Recker et al. (2019). In my analyses below, I take Bunge’s ontology as given and seek to apply it faithfully.

## Thing and Property of a Thing

Fundamental to my analyses are the notions of “thing” and “property” of a thing. A useful, intuitive way to think about the notion of “thing” is that it is an object in the real world that has mass and/or energy. For instance, humans are realworld things because they have mass and energy, and photons are real-world things because they have energy but perhaps no mass.

Things in the real world possess properties (or characteristics or traits). For example, “humans” possess certain properties such as age, mass, and height. Some of these properties are used to distinguish humans from other mammals (such as the powerful language and cognitive capabilities that humans possess). In Bunge’s ontology, properties are real, even though our knowledge of them at a point in time may be imperfect (Bunge 1977, pp. 58-62).

## Scope of a Property, Property Pre-Order, and Property Precedence

An important concept for my analyses below is the scope of a property, which is the set of things that possess the property— the extension of the property (Goertz 2006, p. 10; Sartori 1970, pp. 1041-1044). For instance, the scope of the (complex) property “is human” is the set of all real-world things that possess it (a class of things called humans).

Properties may be more general or more specific. For instance, some humans are daily users of information technology (a more specific property because not all humans possess it). The scope of this property “uses information technology daily” is a subset of the scope of the more general property “is human.” Note that the elements of the scope of the more specific property in effect have two properties: (1) they are humans (the preceding property), and (2) they use information technology on a daily basis. The two properties combine to form a single complex property (formed by their conjunction).

Still a more specific property is “writes program code each day.” Its scope is a subset of the more general property “daily information technology users.” This subset is the scope of the property “writes program code each day.” Note, the elements in this subset in effect have three properties: (1) they are humans (preceding property), (2) they use information technology each day (preceding property), and (3) they write program code each day.

Figure 1 shows how the scopes of these three properties relate to one another. They manifest an order—one is a proper subset of a second set, which in turn is a proper subset of a third set (by transitivity, the first set is also a subset of the third set). Also, note that the scope of the property “is human” is a proper subset of the scope of the property “is a mammal,” but it is not a subset of the scope of the property “is a reptile.” More generally, the scopes bear only a pre-order relationship (reflexive and transitive) to one another.

In a property pre-order, note that a preceding property is more general than a succeeding property because more things possess it. Conversely, a succeeding property is more specific than a preceding property because fewer things possess it. A complex (succeeding) property can always be formed as a conjunction of preceding properties such that the scope of the complex property has one element (we have thereby uniquely identified a particular thing in the real world) (Bunge 1977, pp. 74-75). If the scope of a property is the null set (no thing possesses it), however, the property is not real (Bunge 1977, p. 76).

## Unarized, Dichotomic Properties and Values-Based Properties

The way in which I have represented the properties of things in Figure 1 is using a form that Bunge (1977, pp. 69-72) calls a unarized, dichotomic (singular, binary) form. The properties are expressed such that a thing either possesses or does not possess the property. Logically, we can express a predicate over the property that has a TRUE or FALSE answer—the thing that interests us either possesses the property (TRUE) or it does not (FALSE).

To build his theory of properties, Bunge (1977, pp. 57-109) uses the unarized, dichotomic form. This form considerably simplifies his analytical work. Nonetheless, he shows how a values-based form of properties (a form where a property can take on a range of values) can be transformed in a straightforward way to a unarized, dichotomic form (Bunge 1977, pp. 70-71). Thus, an important outcome is that the analytical results he obtains with a unarized, dichotomic form of properties generalize to a values-based form of properties.

To illustrate this transformation process, consider the example above of the humans who write program code each day. We might wish to classify them based on their level of competence as “novice,” “experienced,” or “expert” programmers. Using Bunge’s ontology, a “programmer competence” property function would be employed to map some things in a domain (humans) to one of three values in a co-domain—namely, “novice,” “experienced,” and “expert.” Note that this is a values-based form of the property.

A unarized, dichotomic representation of the programmer “competence” property that has three values would have each of the three values represented as three separate properties: a “novice competence” property, an “experienced competence” property, and an “expert competence” property. Things either possess or do not possess one of these three properties. If we wish to rate programmer competence on a finer scale (for example, a 7-point Likert scale), the same transformation process still applies—each point on the scale maps to a unarized, dichotomic property (seven different unarized, dichotomic properties).

While initially a unarized, dichotomic form appears to be an awkward and unwieldy way to represent properties and their values, it provides a powerful basis for analyzing how properties relate to one another. Importantly, “the unarization procedure can be generalized to cover not only mutual properties of n-tuples of individuals but all kinds of complex properties” (Bunge 1977, p. 70). Essentially, it is “a trick allowing one to speak, though at greater length, of any property of an individual as if it were intrinsic” (Bunge 1977, p. 70). By using the unarization procedure to conceptualize all properties as intrinsic and then examining how their scopes relate to one another, the ways in which properties are or are not (lawfully) related to each other can be determined clearly and precisely.

## Some Ontological Proscriptions

Bunge argues that certain types of phenomena do not exist in the real world. Thus, they are not elements of his ontology. Initially, they might appear counterintuitive and limiting for an analysis of the nature of constructs and indicators. Nonetheless, these proscribed phenomena are important elements of Bunge’s ontology and are significant for my analyses below.

First, Bunge (1977, pp. 60-63) argues that things do not possess negative properties. He comments (p. 60): “external reality wears only positive traits.” Thus, a person does not have the property “not a spreadsheet application user.” Nonetheless, Bunge also comments (p. 60): “We certainly need negation to understand reality and argue about it or anything else.” His approach is to use predicates as the basis for reasoning about properties. Thus, we might have a predicate “not a spreadsheet application user” that returns the value “TRUE.” In my analyses that follow, I rely on our ability to reason about properties of things using predicates to determine whether a thing is a member of a property’s scope.

Second, Bunge (1977, pp. 61-62) argues that things in the real world do not possess disjunctive properties. For instance, a person does not possess the property “an Android user or an iPhone user.” Instead, they possess the property “an Android user” or the property “an iPhone user” or the complex property “Android user and iPhone user.”

Third, Bunge (1977, pp. 98-99) argues that properties do not have properties. Thus, we can say a visual display has the property “can emit the color ‘red,’” but we cannot then say the color “red” has the property that it is “intense.” Rather, under Bunge’s ontology, the visual display has the property “can emit a color that is red and intense” (perhaps among other shades of red, all of which are succeeding properties of the property “can emit the color ‘red’”). Nonetheless, Bunge (1977, p. 99) acknowledges that predicates can have higher-order predicates (predicates over predicates). Using the example above, the first-order predicate might be: “The thing emits light waves that have a frequency within the ‘red’ spectrum.” The secondorder predicate might be: “The red is ‘intense’ in color.” Thus, we might describe, make propositions about, or reason about properties using higher-order predicates. In my analyses that follow, such reasoning underpins our ability to identify succeeding and preceding properties (e.g., “intense red” is a succeeding property of “red”).

## Nature of Constructs

The notion of a “construct” is fundamental to building semantically clear theories or models about phenomena. Given that researchers disagree or are unclear about the nature of constructs, it is unsurprising that discussions about the meaning and measurement of constructs are often at cross-purposes and contested (Bunge 1974a; Bunge 1974b; Goertz 2006; Goertz and Mahoney 2012; MacKenzie 2003; Osigweh 1989; Sartori 1970; Zhang et al. 2016). Table 2 shows constructs are often described in varied, arcane, and vague ways.

Bunge’s ontology has some important implications for the narratives in Table 2 about the nature of constructs. First, based on Bunge’s ontology, Weber (2012, p. 7) argues that the term “construct,” as used in theories or models in the social sciences, often is and indeed should be “an attribute in general of some class of things.” In short, constructs are not “ideas that unite phenomena” nor “things held in common.” Instead, they are “creations of the human mind” (Bunge 1977, p. 116) that represent (proxy for) properties in general of a class of things in the real world. In my analyses below, I use only this meaning of “construct.”

Second, contrary to some definitions and notions in Table 2, constructs as representations of properties of things do not have “distinct attributes” nor “dimensions” nor “facets” because, under Bunge’s ontology, “properties do not have properties.” I will return to this important matter below and offer some explanations based on Bunge’s ontology that might account for the ways these terms are often used.

Third, contrary to some definitions and notions in Table 2, constructs (properties) do not have “conceptual domains.” Under Bunge’s ontology, the conceptual domain of a property does not exist. I will also return to this important matter below and offer some explanations based on Bunge’s ontology that might account for the ways different notions of the “domain of a construct” have sometimes been used by researchers.

Fourth, unless constructs represent the fictitious “null property” (a property possessed by no thing in the real world, they are always real because they represent real properties of things in the world. Under Bunge’s ontology, whether they are real does not depend on the nature of the indicators (e.g., reflective versus formative) used to measure them, nor does it depend on the direction of causality hypothesized to exist between a construct and its indicators. Instead, providing a property has at least one thing in its scope, it is real.

Fifth, the meaning of constructs arises from their sense and reference (Bunge 1974b, p. xi). The sense of a construct is the “collection of its logical relatives.” In the case of theoretical constructs, the logical relatives are properties that are lawfully related to the construct (the focal property)—for instance, they are succeeding or preceding properties in the pre-order relation over the set of properties of which the focal construct is a member (Bunge 1977, pp. 80-85). The reference of a construct is the set of “individuals occurring in its domain of definition.” In the case of theoretical constructs, the referents are thus things in the construct’s scope (the set of things that possess the property represented by the construct).

## Nature of Indicators

Bunge (1975, p. 66) defines an indicator as “an observable trait of a thing (physical, biological, social, or other) that is rightly or wrongly assumed to point to the value of some other trait, usually an unobservable one, of either the same or a different thing.” In other words, like constructs, indicators are also properties (traits or characteristics) of a thing.

As Bunge notes, some indicators are used to measure (“point to”) values of other properties of the same thing. For instance, a website user may rate their perceptions of certain features of the website (these perceptions are properties of the user) as indicators of their perception of whether overall the website is easy to use (another property of the user) (van der Heijden 2004, p. 704). Alternatively, some indicators are used to measure the values of the properties of a different thing. For instance, certain properties of the images captured by an fMRI device are used as indicators of the strength of a user’s perceptions about whether particular features of the website are easy to use (properties of the fMRI device point to properties of a different thing, which is the website user) (Dimoka et al. 2011, pp. 695-696). Note, however, that the fMRI measure is a mutual property of the user—their ease-ofuse perception according to a particular fMRI device.

If indicators are to achieve high-quality construct measurement outcomes, they must satisfy several onerous criteria. First, they must be linked to the focal construct via a well-established theory (Bunge 1975, pp. 66-68; Bunge 2010, pp. 88-90)— sometimes called an “auxiliary theory” (Edwards 2011, p. 370). Second, the relational structure over the set of values that the indicator can assume must be isomorphic to the relational structure over the set of putative values the focal construct can assume (Borsboom 2008, pp. 32-38). Third, a high-quality measurement system (e.g., one that is stable, selective, noninvasive, and calibrated) must exist to determine the indicator’s values (Frigerio et al. 2010; Mari 2000).

## Unidimensional Constructs and Their Measurement

Unidimensional constructs that are latent (unobservable) constructs<sup>2</sup> are said to have only one “dimension” (e.g., Edwards 2001, p. 144; MacKenzie et al. 2011, p. 301; Polites et al. 2012, p. 22). The indicators used to measure them are observable (not themselves latent constructs) (Wong et al. 2008, p. 748). Moreover, the indicators measure homogeneous rather than heterogeneous “facets” of a unidimensional construct (Bollen and Lennox 1991, pp. 307-308). Using Bunge’s ontology, the problem with these conceptualizations of a unidimensional construct is that constructs (properties of things) do not have dimensions.

In the subsections below, I use Bunge’s ontology to examine the nature of unidimensional constructs and the ways they are measured. Note, I consider properties only from a unarized, dichotomic perspective (a thing either does or does not possess the property) and not a values-based perspective (a property can assume a range of values). My reasons are fourfold. First, adopting a unarized, dichotomic perspective of properties simplifies my analyses. Second, if my analyses do not hold for unarized, dichotomic properties, they are unlikely to hold under analyses that allow properties to assume a range of values. Third, as I have shown above, values-based properties can be transformed to unarized, dichotomic properties in a straightforward way. Fourth, I can treat measurements of mutual properties of different things (e.g., a person’s ease-ofuse perception measured by an fMRI machine) as an intrinsic property of the measured thing (see Bunge 1977, pp. 69-71; Mari 2003, p. 21). Nonetheless, my use of unarized, dichotomic properties as an expositional device does not mean I am advocating the use of single-item instruments nor items with only binary responses. I contend that my results generalize to multi-item instruments and multiple-value items.

## Unidimensional Constructs with Reflective Indicators

Reflective indicators are properties (attributes) of some thing whose value or change in value is somehow meant to reflect the value of or change in the value of another property (attribute) of the thing or another thing. Figure 2 shows a typical reflective-indicator model.

As the extant literature has evolved, more disputes have arisen about the nature of reflective (and formative) indicators and how they relate to their latent constructs (e.g., Bollen and Diamantopoulos 2017a; Borsboom et al. 2003). Nonetheless, the characteristics I present below are often found in the literature (e.g., Bollen 2011, p. 362; Henseler 2017, p. 180; Jarvis et al. 2003, p. 203; Petter et al. 2007, p. 626; Polites et al. 2012, p. 24):

Changes in the unidimensional construct “cause” changes in reflective indicators.<sup>3</sup>

• Reflective indicators “reflect” or “manifest” the unidimensional construct.

• Reflective indicators share a “common theme,” are interchangeable, and covary.

• Dropping an indicator should not change the “conceptual domain” of the construct.

• Reflective indicators have a common net of antecedents and consequents.

In the absence of an ontological theory of constructs and indicators, the meaning of these statements is unclear. Using Bunge’s (1977) ontology, I argue that reflective indicators can have three possible meanings: attributes/synonyms of the focal construct, succeeding properties of the focal construct, and preceding properties of the focal construct. In the subsections below, I discuss each meaning and use an example from the extant literature.

## Reflective Indicators as Synonyms for the Focal Construct

The first meaning that might be ascribed to a reflective indicator is that it is one of several attributes that proxy for the same property of a thing (the unidimensional construct). Thus, the indicators are simply syntactic variations developed to describe the construct directly—indicators that are elements of a synset (Miller 1995, p. 40).<sup>4</sup>

Multiple synonymic indicators of a focal construct are often used because sentient beings can interpret the meaning of the same syntax in different ways (different connotative meanings) (e.g., McGregor 2009, pp. 130-131). By using syntactic variants, each hopefully conveys more or less the same denotative meaning ascribed to the focal construct (see, also, Edwards 2011, p. 373). For example, the following items from Straub et al. (2004, p. 396) have been used to measure a user’s “intention to use” a particular software package:

• I am very likely to try out CHART-MASTER.

• I will very likely use CHART-MASTER.

• I will probably use CHART-MASTER.

• I intend to use CHART-MASTER.

All items are synonyms for the construct “intention to use,” which is a construct within Davis’s (1989) technology acceptance model (TAM) (see also Petter et al. 2012, p. 634).

If reflective indicators are synonyms, “causality” does not flow from the construct to its indicators. The indicators are simply attributes (syntactic variants) describing a single property. Thus, the reflective indicators’ scopes should have high overlap. The indicators are “interchangeable” in the sense they all describe the construct directly. Thus, one meaning that could be assigned to the construct’s “conceptual domain” is that it is the set of syntactic variants used to describe the construct. As such, the removal of one indicator will change the “conceptual domain” of the construct (an element of the synset is lost), but it will not affect the construct’s meaning. Moreover, the “antecedents” and “consequents” of the indicators will be the same as those of the unidimensional property.

## Reflective Indicators as Succeeding Properties

The second meaning that might be ascribed to a reflective indicator relies on Bunge’s notion of property precedence— specifically, a reflective indicator is a property that succeeds the construct. Thus, a reflective indicator’s scope is a subset of the construct’s scope. Often, this interpretation seems to underpin the idea of reflective indicators—the construct is the more general property manifested in reflective indicators that are the more specific properties.

For example, van der Heijden (2004, p. 704) measures the “perceived usefulness” of a hedonic information system (a movie website) using the following reflective indicators<sup>5</sup>:

• I can decide more quickly and more easily which movie I want to go see than in the past.

• I can better decide which movie I want to go see than in the past.

• I am better informed about new movies.

• I can decide more quickly and more easily whether I want to go see a particular movie or not.

• I can better decide whether I want to go see a particular movie or not.

Perceived usefulness is the more general (preceding) property. Users who perceive the hedonic information system to be useful may or may not perceive they can achieve each and every reflective indicator (succeeding property). For instance, users might be better able to decide which movie they want to see (the second indicator), but they might not be able to make this decision more easily and quickly (the first indicator). In other words, besides perceived usefulness, some other property must exist (e.g., ease of use) for users to be able to make decisions more easily and quickly. If users can achieve any of the indicators, however, they will perceive the hedonic information system to be useful.

Succeeding properties might be used as reflective indicators of the focal unidimensional construct for two reasons. First, the existence of any reflective indicator is deemed sufficient (in a logical sense) for the construct to exist. Second, by linking the construct to its succeeding properties, the meaning of the construct is deemed to be clearer because its sense will be more evident (see, e.g., Bunge 1974a; Speaks 2016).

If indicators are properties that succeed the focal construct, arguments about causality flowing from the construct to its indicators are problematic. Property precedence does not mean that causality flows from the preceding to the succeeding property. Instead, all that can be said is: (1) the existence of the preceding property (construct) is necessary for the existence of the succeeding property (indicator), and (2) the existence of the succeeding property (indicator) is sufficient for the existence of the preceding property (construct). Causality must be argued on other grounds (see, e.g., Edwards and Bagozzi 2000, pp. 157-159).

For instance, using the hedonic information system example above, it might be argued that users first must perceive the system to be useful before they can use it successfully for different tasks. Depending on whether other properties are then present, users may or may not be able to accomplish specific tasks. Thus, a sufficient-effect-and-necessary-cause conditional exists (Gomes 2009, pp. 378-380). For example, the causality argument might be: If I am better informed about new movies, then I already have access to a useful system. (Figure 3a).

If reflective properties are used as indicators, care must be taken to achieve two outcomes. First, a set of indicators must be chosen such that the union of their scopes equals the construct’s scope. Otherwise, a false-negative situation can arise—a thing (e.g., a person) might not possess any of the indicators but still possess the construct (Figure 4a). For instance, someone might answer “no” to all items in van der Heijden’s (2004, p. 704) instrument but still find the website to be useful. The reason might be that they are not interested in watching movies; rather, they are interested in tracing the histories of different actors, and the website might provide a useful search function.

Second, each reflective indicator used in the instrument must be a property that truly succeeds the construct. Otherwise, a falsepositive situation can arise—a thing (e.g., a person) possesses an indicator, but it does not possess the construct because the reflective indicator’s scope is not truly a subset of the construct’s scope (Figure 4b). For example, with van der Heijden’s (2004, p. 704) instrument, someone might respond “yes” to the indicator, “I am better informed about new movies,” and “no” to all other indicators. In this case, the reason might be that being better informed about new movies creates information overload and confusion for them and, as a result, indecision about which movies to see. However, overall, they would not perceive the movie website to be useful.

If reflective indicators are succeeding properties, the indicators are “interchangeable” in the sense the existence of one is sufficient for the existence of the more general property (the construct). From another perspective, however, the indicators are not interchangeable if the removal of one means the union of the scopes of the remaining indicators is a proper subset of the scope of the construct (because a false-negative situation can then arise).

Even if scope validity is retained when the indicator is removed, stakeholders’ sense of the meaning of the “perceived usefulness” construct might be undermined. Recall, Bunge’s theory of meaning is based on sense (how the construct is related to other properties) and reference (the individuals in the scope of the construct). If scope validity is preserved when the indicator is removed, the construct’s reference component of meaning is preserved. Respondents may have a better understanding of the meaning of the “perceived usefulness,” however, if all the indicators are retained (the construct’s sense component of meaning).

Finally, in a logical sense, discourse about the “antecedents” and “consequents” of a particular indicator might sometimes reflect a focus on properties that succeed and precede the indicator. The relationships among the construct and indicators are then components of a nomological net because the pre-order relation shows “laws” exist between the construct and indicators in the net (Bunge 1977, pp. 78-79; Cronbach and Meehl 1955, p. 290).

## Reflective Indicators as Preceding Properties

The third meaning that might be ascribed to a reflective indicator also relies on Bunge’s notion of property precedence—specifically, a reflective indicator might be a property that precedes the construct. For example, Saunders et al. (2011, p. A9) measure the “social presence” of Second Life using the following indicators:

• There is a sense of human contact in Second Life.

• There is a sense of personalness in Second Life.

• There is a sense of sociability in Second Life.

• There is a sense of warmth in Second Life.

• There is a sense of human sensitivity in Second Life.

They say (p. 1085), “We define social presence as the perception that there is personal, sociable, and sensitive human contact in the medium.” Thus, they imply that social presence is a complex property—one whose meaning is defined via the conjunction of the indicators they use (its scope equals the intersection of the scopes of its five indicators).

Preceding properties might be used as reflective indicators of the focal construct for two reasons. First, the existence of the indicator is deemed necessary (in a logical sense) for the construct to exist. Second, by linking the construct to its preceding properties, its meaning is deemed to be clearer because its sense is clearer (Bunge 1974a; Speaks 2016).

Using this third interpretation, no notion of “causality” exists between the construct and its reflective indicators (as with the second interpretation above). Rather, all that can be concluded is that a thing that possesses the construct will also possess the indicators (the sufficiency condition of property precedence). Nonetheless, perhaps this ontological interpretation underpins claims that causality “flows from” the construct to the indicators.

For instance, using the Second Life example above, a possible logical conditional statement is: If users have a sense of social presence in Second Life, then they will perceive that they have human contact. Causality might then be ascribed to this conditional statement by arguing that Second Life’s social presence leads its users to then feel they have a sense of human contact (sufficient cause, necessary effect) (Figure 3b).

If reflective indicators precede the construct, they may not be “interchangeable” because the scope of the unidimensional construct should be equal to the intersection of the scopes of the indicators. Thus, the removal of an indicator potentially changes the scope of the construct. Moreover, if the conceptual domain is defined as the set of properties (reflective indicators) that precede the construct, the removal of an indicator will change the construct’s “conceptual domain” and possibly its sense (meaning).

Use of a measurement instrument where the scope of the construct is a proper subset of the intersection of the scopes of the indicators can lead to false-positive conclusions (Figure 5a). The reason is that a thing could be in the intersection of the scopes of the indicators but not in the scope of the construct. For instance, using the Second Life example above, Second Life might need to possess another property, such as “a sense of helpfulness” (Gunawardena 1995), before it always gives its users a sense of social presence. To avoid false positives, the intersection of the indicators’ scopes must equal the construct’s scope.

The scope of the construct also must truly be a subset of the intersection of the scopes of the indicators chosen. Otherwise, a false-negative situation can arise—a thing is in the scope of the construct but not in the intersection of the scopes of the indicators (Figure 5b). For instance, a person might perceive that Second Life has a sense of social presence but they do not perceive that it manifests “human sensitivity.”

## Unidimensional Constructs with Formative Causal Indicators

A formative causal indicator is a property of some thing whose value or change in value is somehow meant to “form” the value of or change in value of another property. Figure 6 shows a typical formative causal indicator model.

Formative causal indicators supposedly have the following characteristics (e.g., Bollen 2011, p. 362; Diamantopoulos et al. 2008; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001; Henseler 2017, p. 180; Jarvis et al. 2003, p. 203; Petter et al. 2007, pp. 626-627):

• Changes in formative causal indicators “cause” changes in the construct.

• Formative causal indicators do not necessarily share a “common theme.”

• Formative indicators may not be interchangeable and may not covary.

• Dropping a formative indicator may change the “conceptual domain” of the construct and its meaning.

• Formative causal indicators have different antecedents and consequents.

In the absence of an ontological theory of constructs, the meaning of these statements is unclear. Using Bunge’s (1977) ontology, however, formative causal indicators seemingly have two possible meanings—they are (1) succeeding properties, or (2) preceding properties.

## Formative Causal Indicators as Succeeding Properties

The first meaning that might be ascribed to a formative causal indicator is that it is a property that succeeds the unidimensional construct. In other words, the indicator’s scope is a subset of the unidimensional construct’s scope.

For example, in online marketplaces, Pavlou and Gefen (2005, pp. 375-376) argue the following six indicators (buyer perceptions) are causally related to a construct they call “psychological contract violation” (PCV): (1) fraud/deception, (2) product misrepresentation, (3) contract default, (4) product delivery delay, (5) product guarantees, and (6) payment policy. They say that “any of these six seller behaviors can singlehandedly or in some combination cause the perception of PCV with an individual seller” (p. 376, my emphasis). Thus, the indicators are succeeding properties, and PCV is the preceding property—the existence of one indicator is sufficient for the existence of PCV (see, also, MacKenzie et al. 2011, p. 302).

In contrast to the hedonic information system example discussed above, note that causality with PCV is argued to flow from (one or more) of the succeeding indicators to the construct (Figure 3c) and not from the construct to the indicators (Figure 3a). Thus, Pavlou and Gefen (2005) imply that a sufficient-cause-and-necessary-effect conditional exists (e.g., if a buyer perceives they have a contract default, they will then perceive they have PCV).

If succeeding indicators are used as formative causal properties, care must be taken to achieve two outcomes. First, a set of indicators must be chosen such that the union of their scopes equals the construct’s scope. Otherwise, a falsenegative situation can arise (Figure 4a). For instance, using the PCV example above, concluding that a buyer does not have PCV because they have experienced none of the six seller behaviors listed in Pavlou and Gefen’s (2005) instrument might be wrong. The problem might be that a buyer has experienced other types of seller behavior that have led to PCV (e.g., harassment from a seller after a sale).

Second, each formal causal indicator used must be a property that truly succeeds the construct. Otherwise, a false-positive situation can arise (Figure 4b). For instance, some people might experience product delivery delays but not experience PCV. In other words, the existence of the indicator is not sufficient to conclude the PCV exists.

If succeeding properties are used as formative causal indicators of a construct, the following consequences arise:

It is difficult to ascribe meaning to statements such as “formative causal indicators do not necessarily share a ‘common theme’ and may not be interchangeable.” Based on Bunge’s (1977) ontology, properties do not have “themes.” Formative causal indicators are interchangeable, however, in the sense that the existence of one is sufficient to conclude the focal construct exists. Nonetheless, they are not interchangeable if the replacement of one indicator with another means that the scope of the focal construct is no longer equal to the union of the scopes of the indicators.

The removal of one indicator will change the “conceptual domain” of the construct if the conceptual domain is defined as the set of succeeding properties (represented by formative indicators). Moreover, removal of a formative causal indicator potentially changes the meaning of the construct if it affects the sense of the construct.

• Formative causal indicators have different nomological nets of antecedents and consequents if different sets of properties succeed and precede each indicator.

## Formative Causal Indicators as Preceding Properties

The second meaning that might be ascribed to a formative causal indicator is that it is a property that precedes the unidimensional construct—thus, its scope is a superset of the unidimensional construct’s scope.

For example, to study data warehousing (DW) success, Wixom and Watson (2001, p. 24) use a construct they call “team skills,” which “include[s] both technical and interpersonal abilities” (my emphasis). They measure it with two formative indicators (p. 29):

• “Members of the DW team (including consultants) had the right technical skills for DW.”

• “Members of the DW team had good interpersonal skills.”

Thus, the scope of the team-skills construct is a proper subset of the intersection of the scopes of the two indicators. In contrast to the Second Life example discussed above (reflective indicators as preceding properties), note that causality with team skills seemingly flows from the two indicators (preceding properties) to the construct (Figure 3d) and not from the construct to the indicators (succeeding properties) (Figure 3b). Thus, Wixom and Watson (2001) imply a sufficient-effect-and-necessary-cause conditional exists (e.g., if team skills are to emerge, then the team must first comprise individuals who have good interpersonal skills).

Use of a measurement instrument where the scope of the focal construct is potentially a proper subset of the intersection of the scopes of the formal causal indicators can lead to false-positive conclusions (Figure 5a). For instance, using the DW team skills example above, a team might be deemed to have team skills because team members possess appropriate technical skills and interpersonal skills. Teams might need to possess other properties, however, before they are regarded as skillful (e.g., goal clarity). Also, to avoid the possibility of false positives, the intersection of the scopes of the formal causal indicators must be equal to the scope of the focal construct.

Formal causal indicators whose scope truly precedes the scope of the focal construct also must be used. Otherwise, a false-negative situation can arise (Figure 5b). For instance, a team might have had good team skills even though its members do not have a high level of interpersonal skills. Perhaps for certain projects (e.g., those that can be broken up into self-contained, independent parts), team members’ interpersonal skills have little importance.

With one exception, the implications of this interpretation of formal causal indicators as preceding properties are the same as those when they are conceived as succeeding properties. Specifically, because the construct’s scope is a subset of the intersection of the indicators’ scopes, the removal of an indicator potentially changes the construct’s scope.

## Unidimensional Constructs with Formative Composite Indicators

With two important exceptions, the ways unidimensional constructs with formative composite indicators are described are generally similar to the ways that unidimensional constructs with formative causal indicators are described. The first is that formative composite indicators are not said to “cause” the latent construct; rather, they “make up the construct” (e.g., Henseler 2017, p. 180). The construct is sometimes deemed to be an “emergent variable” associated with an artifact—a product of design science research (e.g., Benitez et al. 2020, pp. 1-4).

The second is that a construct measured via formative composite indicators has no error term; the indicators “fully capture” or “fully determine” the construct and are assumed to be measured without error (e.g., Bollen 2011, p. 366; Grace and Bollen 2008). The construct is a linear combination of weighted indicators (Dijkstra and Henseler 2011; Henseler 2017, p. 181). Figure 7 shows a typical formative composite indicator model.

In a formative composite model, the scope of the construct is always a subset of the scope of each and every one of its indicators. The reason is that the construct’s value is some function of a particular combination of its indicators’ values. Thus, the construct is a more specific property (a complex property) of some thing than each property of the thing represented by the indicators. The indicators are therefore always preceding properties of the construct they are intended to measure (form)—the number of things that possess the construct is always less than or equal to the number of things that possess each indicator.

For example, Benitez et al. (2020) model the construct “social media capability” as a composite of the extent to which a “firm has deliberately used and leveraged” (p. 9) the following four platforms (indicators): Facebook, Twitter, corporate blog(s), and Linkedin. They measured “use and leverage” on a 5-point scale. Assume, however, that a unarized dichotomic “scale” is employed for each of the four indicators (a firm uses or does not use the platform) and the composite index has the predicate “true” if and only if the predicates over all four indicators are “true.” The union of the sets of firms that return “true” for any of the indicators will be a superset (most likely a proper superset) of the set of firms that return “true” for all four indicators (only some firms will possess all four indicators).

With formative composite indicators, an important issue is whether the constructs they form are real. For instance, according to Bollen (2011, p. 366), “with composite indicators and a composite variable, the goal might not be to measure a scientific concept, but to have a summary of the effects of several variables.” Also, given the nature of composite variables and their uncertain status as a representation of any real-world phenomenon, McDonald (1996) argues that composite variables should not be called “latent variables.” Similarly, Hardin and Marcoulides (2011, pp. 754-755) state: “we believe that researchers should avoid using the confusing convention of referring to composites as latent variables.”

If the scope of the unidimensional construct “formed from” composite indicators is not the null set, the property is real— at least one thing in the real world possesses it (Bunge 1977, pp. 71, 99). Lack of knowledge at a point in time may mean that researchers have difficulty understanding its nature. Nonetheless, the key issue theoretically is whether it is a property of at least one real-world thing, even if it understood imperfectly. If no thing in the world possesses the construct, the functional relationship between the construct and its formative indicators is spurious—it does not reflect any realworld “law” between the properties of a thing.

## Multidimensional Constructs and Their Measurement

Multidimensional constructs are said to have several distinct but related “attributes,” “dimensions,” or “facets” that (1) exist in “multidimensional domains,” and (2) are “conceptualized under an overall abstraction” (e.g., Edwards 2001, p. 144; Law et al. 1998, p. 741; MacKenzie et al. 2011, p. 301; Petter et al. 2007, pp. 627-668; Polites et al. 2012, p. 22). The attributes, dimensions, or facets are themselves deemed to be latent constructs. Thus, multidimensional constructs are often called “higher-order” constructs (norder, where ?? ≥ 2). Some dimensions of a multidimensional construct might be measured via reflective indicators; others might be measured via formative indicators (Diamantopoulos et al. 2008, pp. 1205-1208; Jarvis et al. 2003, pp. 204-205; Polites et al. 2012, pp. 27- 31). The dimensions in turn may have a multidimensional structure (Finn and Wang 2014).

Because the dimensions of a multidimensional construct are supposedly themselves (latent) constructs, what is the meaning of these dimensions? Insofar as they are constructs, in a Bungean sense they are therefore properties of some thing. Nonetheless, recall that Bunge’s ontology precludes a conclusion that the dimension of a construct must therefore be a property of the construct—a property of a property (Bunge 1977, pp. 92-94). Rather, the dimensions of a focal construct potentially are either succeeding or preceding properties in the pre-order of properties of some thing that includes the multidimensional construct.

Multidimensional constructs and their dimensions can take many different forms. Accordingly, several taxonomies have been proposed (Diamantopoulos et al. 2008; Edwards 2001; Jarvis et al. 2003; Law et al. 1998; Wong et al. 2008). Nonetheless, based upon their review of multidimensional constructs, Polites et al. (2012) conclude that four canonical forms exist: superordinate, aggregate, profile, and fit.<sup>6</sup> To illustrate how Bunge’s ontology might be applied to analyze the nature of and provide a rigorous foundation for these four forms, below I examine a few selected aspects of each.

## Superordinate Multidimensional Constructs

A superordinate multidimensional construct is said to have the following characteristics (e.g., Edwards 2001, pp. 145-146; Law et al. 1998, pp. 742-745; Polites et al. 2012, pp. 25-26):

The construct is a “higher-order,” “higher-rank,” or “deeper conceptual-level” abstraction that “lies behind” or is “manifested by” its dimensions.

The constructs and its dimensions are at different “relational levels” (because the construct is not some “algebraic function” of its dimensions).

The relationship between the construct and its dimensions is not causal; instead, it “flows from” the former to the latter (the dimensions are reflective of the construct).

• The dimensions are “simply different forms manifested by the construct.”

These ideas are analogous to some of those used to describe the nature of and associations between reflective indicators and unidimensional constructs. As with unidimensional constructs, in the absence of a rigorous underlying ontology, their meaning is sometimes unclear (e.g., what do “flow” and “form” mean?).

Polites et al. (2012, pp. 27-28) use the “cognitive absorption” construct employed by Agarwal and Karahanna (2000) to illustrate a superordinate multidimensional construct. The construct has five reflective dimensions: “temporal dissociation,” “focused immersion,” “heightened enjoyment,”

“control,” and “curiosity.” Polites et al. (2012) comment: “An individual who is cognitively absorbed in a particular software application will exhibit (to some extent) all five of these characteristics” (p. 27, my emphasis).

An ontological interpretation of this example of a superordinate construct is that the five dimensions are preceding (logically necessary) properties of the construct. The intersection of the scopes of the five dimensions equals the scope of “cognitive absorption.” Thus, an individual who has “cognitive absorption” will always have some level of all five dimensions. In this sense, a relationship “flows from” the construct to its five dimensions because the existence of the construct is sufficient for the existence of each dimension.

## Aggregate Multidimensional Constructs

An aggregate multidimensional construct is said to have the following characteristics (e.g., Edwards 2001, pp. 145-147; Polites et al. 2012, pp. 25-26; Wong et al. 2008, p. 747):

The dimensions are combined algebraically to form the construct. The construct is “a composite of its dimensions,” and the dimensions are “components” of the construct.

• The dimensions are not necessarily correlated. Nonetheless, changes in the dimensions cause changes in the construct.

Omitting a dimension affects the “conceptual domain” of the construct because the variance associated with the dimension is then lost.

The constructs and its dimensions are at the same “relational level” (because the construct is some “algebraic function” of its dimensions).

For the most part, these ideas are analogous to some of those used to describe the nature of and associations between formative indicators and unidimensional constructs.

Polites et al. (2012, p. 30) use the “IT infrastructure of supply chain management” construct employed by Rai et al. (2006) to illustrate an aggregate multidimensional construct. The construct has two dimensions: “data consistency” and “cross-functional supply chain management application integration.” Each value of the construct is a function of the particular values of its two dimensions. Polites et al. (2012, p. 30) observe: “These dimensions are also not interchangeable, that is, a firm cannot easily trade data consistency for cross-functional application integration.

Moreover, a change in data consistency does not imply a comparable change in cross-functional application integration.”

An ontological interpretation of this example of an aggregate construct is that the two dimensions are preceding (logically necessary) properties of the construct. In a unarized, dichotomic sense, only a subset of the total set of firms will possess those particular values of the two dimensions (i.e., firms in the scope of a particular property-value pair) that combine functionally to produce a particular value for the construct.

## Profile Multidimensional Constructs

Polites et al. (2012, p. 32) indicate that “profile constructs are theorized to exist at the same level as their dimensions (which may or may not be correlated), and to represent a combination of these dimensions” (see also Law et al. 1998, pp. 748-749; Wong et al. 2008, pp. 747-748). They use Sabherwal and Chan’s (2001) work, which is based on Venkatraman’s (1989) work, to show how three “theorybased strategy profiles”—namely, “defenders,” “analyzers,” and “projectors”—are based on a specific subset of combinations of three different levels of six different dimensions (Polites et al. 2012, pp. 32-33).

Using Bunge’s ontology, profile multidimensional constructs can be conceptualized as complex properties. Their scope is the intersection of the scopes of the dimensions (property-value pairs) on which they are based. Thus, the dimensions are properties that precede the complex property (in a property pre-order).

For instance, using Bunge’s ontology, each of the three levels (high, medium, low) of the six dimensions (“defensiveness,” “risk aversion,” “aggressiveness,” “proactiveness,” “analysis,” “futurity”) used by Sabherwal and Chan (2001) can be conceived as a unarized, dichotomic property (effectively 3 × 6 = 18 different properties exist). Each of these unarized, dichotomic properties has a scope. The three complex properties used to characterize “defenders,” “analyzers,” and “projectors” represent the intersection of the scopes of three particular subsets of these 18 unarized, dichotomic properties (see Table 3, Polites et al. 2012, p. 33; Table 1, Sabherwal and Chan 2001, p. 15). For instance, “Defenders” are members of the scope of the complex property whose scope is the intersection of the scopes of six unarized, dichotomized properties (propertyvalue pairs)—namely, “high defensiveness,” “high risk aversion,” “medium aggressiveness,” “low proactiveness,” “medium analysis,” and “low futurity.”

## Fit Multidimensional Constructs

Polites et al. (2012, pp. 33-35) consider a number of notions of “fit” used by researchers and conclude that three in essence are multidimensional constructs: covariation fit constructs, gestalt fit constructs, and matching fit constructs.

Covariation fit constructs conceptualize fit as “a pattern of covariation or internal consistency among a set of theoretically related variables” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). Thus, “the covariation perspective is no different from a reflective secondorder construct, where the dimensions covary” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). The requirement for the dimensions of a covariation fit construct to covary to achieve a particular fit outcome means the dimensions are preceding properties of the construct.

Gestalt fit constructs conceptualize fit “as the degree of internal coherence among a set of theoretical dimensions” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). They conclude, “fit as gestalt is conceptually equivalent to multidimensional profile constructs” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). Thus, the dimensions of a gestalt fit construct are preceding properties of the construct.

Matching fit constructs conceptualize fit “as a theoretically defined match between two related variables” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). They conclude, “fit as gestalt is conceptually equivalent to multidimensional profile constructs” (Polites et al. 2012, p. 34). Thus, the dimensions of a gestalt fit construct are preceding properties of the construct.

## Implications

My ontological analyses have a number of implications for how we conceive constructs, dimensions, and indicators and how we choose and evaluate the sets of indicators to be used to measure constructs. I discuss these implications in the subsections below.

## Nature of Constructs, Dimensions, and Indicators

Based on Bunge’s (1977, 1979) ontology, I have argued that unidimensional constructs, multidimensional constructs, the dimensions of multidimensional constructs, and indicators all have a common meaning—namely, they are properties in general of a class of things. Furthermore, all can be conceived as elements of a pre-order of properties possessed by a real-world thing. This pre-order can be tapped to provide the basis for the design of a set of indicators that has construct validity. Thus, Bunge’s ontology provides a single, unified, precise, coherent way to view constructs and indicators and how they are related.

## Types of Dimensions

When using Bunge’s (1977) ontology to analyze the nature of multidimensional constructs and their dimensions, my analyses show the four canonical forms identified by Polites et al. (2012) can be reduced to one canonical form—namely, all are variations of constructs that have been modeled with preceding properties as their dimensions. The variations in the canonical forms manifest different ways the preceding properties are related to the construct.

I see no reason, however, why dimensions could not be succeeding properties of a multidimensional construct. Polites et al. (2012, p. 24) point out: “Constructs are described as multidimensional when their indicators are themselves latent constructs.” Perhaps in some cases better measures of a multidimensional construct can be obtained through measuring its succeeding properties as latent constructs rather than its preceding properties.

## Types of Indicators

My analyses show that only three types of indicators exist: synonymic indicators (indicators drawn from a synset associated with the focal construct), succeeding indicators (properties that come after the focal construct in a pre-order of properties), and preceding indicators (properties that come before the focal construct in a pre-order of properties).

Note that my taxonomy does not include the traditional categories of “reflective” and “formative” indicators. These categories are often based on arguments about the direction of causality between constructs and indicators. My analyses show the direction of causality is a secondary issue—one that researchers might choose not to address perhaps because causality arguments are often fraught with philosophical and practical problems (Bunge 2009; Edwards and Bagozzi 2000; Petter et al. 2007). Thus, albeit for somewhat different reasons, my analyses support those of other researchers who eschew the value of the reflective-formative indicator distinction (e.g., Bagozzi 2011, p. 264; Hardin 2017, p. 598; Rigdon 2013, p. 24; Wilcox et al. 2008, p. 1220).

## Level of Concomitance of an Indicator

Constructs and indicators are concomitant to the extent they occur together as properties of the same thing. Their concomitance can be measured via their level of scope overlap.

Because synonymic indicators are intended to have the same meaning as the focal construct, they should be completely concomitant with the focal construct. In other words, the indicator and the construct should have equal scopes. For indicators that succeed or precede the focal construct, however, their level of concomitance might be partial.

For succeeding indicators, a measure of the level of concomitance, $L C _ { s c } ,$ is:

$$
L C _ {s c} = \frac {| \mathcal {S} (P _ {s}) |}{| \mathcal {S} (P _ {c}) |}\tag{1}
$$

which is the cardinality of the scope of the succeeding indicator, $P _ { s } ,$ divided by the cardinality of the scope of the focal construct, $P _ { c } .$ The measure is proportionate—a higher value (maximum of one) means the indicator and construct have a higher level of concomitance.

For preceding indicators, a measure of the level of concomitance, $L C _ { p c }$ , is:

$$
L C _ {p c} = \frac {| \mathcal {S} (P _ {c}) |}{| \mathcal {S} (P _ {p}) |}\tag{2}
$$

which is the cardinality of the scope of the focal construct, $P _ { c } ,$ divided by the cardinality of the scope of the preceding indicator, $P _ { c } .$ . The measure is proportionate—a higher value (maximum of one) means the indicator and construct have a higher level of concomitance.

## Content Validity versus Scope Validity

My ontological analyses show that researchers should be circumspect about some traditional narratives relating to a construct’s content validity. For instance, Straub et al. (2004, p. 424) give a representative definition: “The degree to which items in an instrument reflect the content universe to which the instrument will be generalized.” In the context of Bunge’s ontology, the meaning of a term such as “content universe” is unclear. Constructs have neither domains nor codomains from which “content” can be selected to develop a measurement instrument that “reflect[s] the content universe.” Nonetheless, my ontological analyses suggest alternative, more precise definitions of content validity that might be used.

If a construct is to be measured using synonymic indicators, content validity can be defined as the extent to which the synonyms chosen convey the same meaning (content) as the construct. The synonymic indicators should have the same succeeding and preceding properties as the focal construct (intension and thus sense). They should also have the same scope as the focal construct (extension and thus reference).

If a construct is to be measured using succeeding/preceding indicators, content validity can be assessed by evaluating whether the union/intersection of the scopes of the indicators chosen is equal to the scope of the construct. If this outcome does not occur, false-negative and false-positive situations can arise (Figures 4a, 4b, 5a, and 5b).

Note that none of the definitions of content validity that I have proposed deal with the content of a construct. Rather, they relate to whether the indicators cover the focal construct’s scope. Thus, they would be better called definitions of the scope validity of the set of indicators used to measure a construct.

## Convergent Validity and Discriminant Validity

The convergent and discriminant validities of a set of indicators used to measure a construct are based on the correlations that exist among the indicators themselves and the indicators used to measure other constructs. For reflective indicators, the goal is to have the correlations among the indicators themselves higher (converge) than the correlations they have with indicators used to measure other constructs (discriminate) (e.g., Straub et al. 2004, pp. 389-392).

Besides conventional measures of convergent and discriminant validity, the extent to which indicators converge on a construct or discriminate between constructs can also be examined using their level of concomitance:

$$
L C _ {i j} = \frac {| \mathcal {S} (P _ {i}) \cap \mathcal {S} (P _ {j}) |}{| \mathcal {S} (P _ {i}) \cup \mathcal {S} (P _ {j}) |}\tag{3}
$$

which is the cardinality of the intersection of the scopes of the two indicators, $P _ { i }$ and $P _ { j } { \mathrm { . } }$ divided by the cardinality of the union of their scopes. Their level of concomitance, $L C _ { i j }$ , is a proportionate measure (ranges between zero and one) such that a higher value means the two indicators have a higher level of concomitance (they share more elements in their scopes).

To examine how the indicators converge and discriminate, a Multitrait-Multimethod (MTMM) analysis can be done (Campbell and Fiske 1959). The usual correlations between two different indicators in an MTMM analysis are replaced by the levels of concomitance between them. Higher levels of concomitance indicate convergence, whereas lower levels of concomitance indicate discrimination.

Measures of convergent validity and discriminant validity are useful when the indicators used to measure the focal constructs are elements drawn from a synset. Ideally, the indicators evoke a singular meaning for the focal construct and do not allude to the meaning of other constructs. If this outcome occurs, the indicators will have equal scopes (level of concomitance of 1.00). In relation to discriminant validity, the scopes of indicators drawn from a synset will ideally have only a small amount of overlap with indicators chosen to measure other constructs (level of concomitance is low).

When the set of indicators chosen to measure the focal construct comprises properties that succeed the construct, the usefulness of measures of convergent validity is less clear. The levels of concomitance of each pair of indicators may be zero (they have no convergent validity because their scopes do not intersect), but the scope of each indicator may be a proper subset of the scope of construct. Thus, each indicator is valuable because its existence is sufficient for the existence of the construct. Alternatively, the level of concomitance between each pair of indicators in the set may be high, but the cardinality of the union of their scopes may be much less than the cardinality of the construct’s scope (convergent validity is high but scope validity is low, and thus the risk of false negatives is high) (Figure 8).

Similarly, the value of measures of discriminant validity is also unclear. The indicators might seemingly discriminate the focal construct from other constructs. Discriminant validity matters little, however, if the indicators’ scope validity is low and thus the focal construct is not measured accurately in the first place.

When the set of indicators chosen to measure the focal construct comprises properties that precede the construct, again, the value of measures of convergent validity is unclear. The levels of concomitance between pairs of indicators in the set used to measure the focal construct may be low but the intersection of their scopes might be equal to the scope of the construct (high scope validity). Alternatively, their levels of concomitance may be high but their scope validity may be low (Figure 9). Lack of scope validity with preceding indicators also undermines the usefulness of measures of discriminant validity.

For synonymic indicators, succeeding indicators, and preceding indicators, the usefulness of measures of discriminant validity is also contingent on the extent to which scope validity has first been achieved. Discriminant validity matters little if the indicators’ scope validity is low and thus the focal construct has not been measured accurately in the first place. Nonetheless, my analyses show that discriminant validity is more important than convergent validity because a set of indicators might achieve scope validity but not differentiate one construct from another construct.

## Internal-Consistency Reliability

Measures of internal-consistency reliability (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) are also a function of the extent to which the indicators used to measure a construct are correlated. In the context of my analyses above, internal-consistency reliability can be conceived as a function of the level of concomitance among a constructs’ indicators. Accordingly, when synonyms are used as indicators, traditional narratives that argue researchers should have reliability coefficients that are high but not too high (e.g., Straub et al. 2004, p. 401) are misplaced. Instead, the level of concomitance (as a measure of reliability) among the indicators should ideally be one.

Measures of internal-consistency reliability also become problematic when researchers use succeeding or preceding properties as indicators of a focal construct. The indicators might have high levels of concomitance but poor scope validity. Thus, they will have high reliability but poor construct validity. This problem has long been recognized (e.g., Burton-Jones and Lee 2017, p. 461). My ontological analyses show why it can occur.

## Application

To examine some ways in which my ontological analyses might impact researchers’ practice, below I consider some aspects (not all) of the first six steps in MacKenzie et al.’s (2011) helpful 10- step process for construct measurement and validation. I show how the outcomes of my analyses (1) clarify some of their narrative, (2) make precise some of their procedures, and (3) enhance the ways that some aspects of their steps are enacted. As an illustrative example, I use Serva et al.’s (2005) “perceived trustworthiness” instrument, which they employed to measure management’s and an information system development team’s perceptions about each other’s trustworthiness. I chose Serva et al.’s instrument because (1) MacKenzie et al. (2011) use it in Steps 1 and 3 of their process, and (2) it includes both unidimensional constructs and multidimensional constructs and thus allows the implications of my analyses for both types of constructs to be better illustrated.

Serva et al. (2005) based their instrument on Mayer et al.’s (1995) model of trust, which “posits that trustworthiness, in the form of beliefs about another party’s ability, benevolence, and integrity, are the predominant determinants of trust” (p. 626, my emphasis). Mayer et al. (1995, pp. 720-722)

acknowledge that “ability,” “benevolence,” and “integrity” can behave independently. Nonetheless, all three must be present at some level for “perceived trustworthiness” to be present (see, also, Mayer and Davis 1999, p. 127). Thus, they are preceding properties of “trustworthiness.” Moreover, in Serva et al.’s work, they are named as dimensions of “perceived trustworthiness” (Serva et al. 2005, p. 642). Each dimension is then measured via indicators that prima facie are preceding (reflective) properties (Mayer and Davis 1999, p. 127). For instance, the “able” dimension of “trustworthy” is measured via three indicators—namely, “capable,” skilled,” and “well-qualified.”

Figure 10 shows a representation of the slightly adapted property pre-order that appears to underlie Serva et al.’s (2005) model (properties to the left of and including “trustworthy”). For illustrative purposes below, the adaptation I have made is to add the properties “timely” and “efficient” as putative succeeding properties of “able” (shown with dotted lines). Thus, some, but not all, able teams are deemed to be timely and efficient, but timely and efficient teams are always deemed to be able. Note the subset symbol in Figure 10 represents the fact that the scope of a succeeding property is a subset of the scope of a preceding property. ## Step 1: Develop a Conceptual Definition of the Construct

MacKenzie et al. (2011, pp. 297-304) propose that researchers’ efforts to achieve a precise definition of a focal construct include two activities. The first is that researchers define “the conceptual domain of the construct” (p. 298), which comprises “the general type of property to which the construct refers” (p. 298) and “the entity to which it (the construct) applies” (p. 299). As per my analyses above, “the general type of property to which the construct refers” is a preceding property in the pre-order of properties that includes the focal construct. The entity to which the construct applies is the thing (more precisely, a class of things) that possesses the property (more precisely, a property in general) represented by the construct.

The second activity that MacKenzie et al. recommend (p. 300) that researchers undertake is to “clarify the intension of the focal construct by specifying its conceptual theme” (my emphasis). They define a construct’s “conceptual theme” as “the set of fundamental attributes/characteristics that are necessary and sufficient for something to be an exemplar of the construct” (p. 300, my emphasis).

Figure 10. Slight Adaptation of the Pre-Order of Properties that Appears to Underpin Serva et al.’s (2005) Trustworthiness Instrument

The attributes/characteristics are to be defined “as narrowly as possible” (p. 299, my emphasis). As per my analyses above, their “necessary and sufficient characteristics” would be those preceding properties such that the intersection of their scopes equals the scope of the focal construct (a complex property).

With the two activities that MacKenzie et al. recommend be used to articulate a construct’s conceptual domain and conceptual theme, my analyses above uncover three issues. The first is that they imply only one preceding property of the focal construct exists when they state that “the general type of property to which the construct refers” (p. 298, my emphasis). Clearly, more than one general property might precede the focal construct. For example, in Figure 10, “capable,” “skilled,” and “well-qualified,” are all preceding properties of “able.” Presumably MacKenzie et al.’s “conceptual theme” is intended to be a subset of all the preceding properties of a construct (the “conceptual domain”).

The second issue is that focusing only on the preceding properties of the focal construct when considering the construct’s intension is insufficient as a way of obtaining a deep understanding of the construct’s meaning. Instead, both preceding and succeeding properties must be considered because a property’s intension covers both types of properties. For example, consider Figure 10. In addition to “capable,” “skilled,” and “well-qualified,” some other preceding properties of “able” that researchers might propose are “disciplined,” “creative,” and “experienced.” Furthermore, besides “trustworthy,” some other succeeding properties of “able” that researchers might propose are “timely” and “efficient.”

The third issue is that seeking to identify the “necessary and sufficient characteristics” of the focal construct during the first step of an instrument development process is premature. This activity should be left to a later stage when the content (scope) validity of the items in an instrument is to be assessed (Step 3 below). During this first step, researchers should broadly explore the possible preceding and succeeding properties in a pre-order of properties that includes the focal construct so they can deepen their understanding of the meaning of (intension and extension) and thereby sharpen their definition of the construct.

## Step 2: Generate Items to Represent the Construct

Once a researcher has defined the focal construct, MacKenzie et al.’s (2011, p. 304) next step is to “generate a set of items that fully represents the conceptual domain of the construct. Recall, they define a construct’s conceptual domain as including both “the general type of property to which the construct refers” and “the entity to which it applies.” They also state (p. 304) that “the ultimate goal of the item generation process is to produce a set of items that fully captures all of the essential aspects of the domain of the focal construct.”

If, following my recommendations, researchers have carefully articulated in Step 1 a possible pre-order of properties that includes the focal construct, this second step of MacKenzie et al.’s 10-step process is superfluous. Researchers will have already generated items to represent the construct in the form of the putative preceding and succeeding properties. Moreover, by seeking to articulate a pre-order of properties, researchers have a structured way to think about items that might be used to represent the focal construct.

Nonetheless, this step provides an opportunity for researchers to consider possible synonyms for the focal property. In Step 6 below, I discuss how these synonyms can be used to validate the content (scope) validity of the items (both individually and collectively) that are chosen for inclusion in an instrument. For instance, in this step, researchers might choose “adept” and “competent” as possible synonyms for the “able” construct in Figure 10.

## Step 3: Assess the Content Validity of the Items

Once a researcher has generated items to represent the focal construct, MacKenzie et al. (2011, p. 304) state that the content validity of the items should be evaluated. They argue that “two related judgments must be made”: (1) individual items should be examined to determine whether they are “representative of an aspect” of the construct’s “content domain”; and (2) the set of items should be examined to determine whether its members collectively represent “the entire content domain.” Again, in the absence of an ontological theory to underpin concepts such as “represent,” “aspect,” “content domain,” “collectively represent,” and “entire content domain,” the meanings of these concepts are unclear. For instance, is “content domain” the same notion as “conceptual domain”?

To evaluate the content validity of the items, MacKenzie et al. (2011, pp. 304-306) recommend a procedure proposed by Hinkin and Tracey (1999) whereby a matrix is created with the “different aspects of the content domain” listed in the column headings and the different items listed in the row headings. The cells are populated with ratings given by knowledgeable stakeholders about how well an item represents an “aspect.”

In their Table 2, MacKenzie et al. (2011, p. 305) illustrate Hinkin and Tracey’s process using Serva et al.’s (2005) “perceived trustworthiness” instrument (Figure 10 above). The columns in this table, which are labeled “benevolence,” “ability,” and “integrity,” show the dimensions of “perceived trustworthiness.” The rows in this table show the nine items used to measure the dimensions (three items per dimension). Thus, in effect, the matrix shows a pre-order of properties: the items (rows) precede the dimensions (columns), which precede the focal construct (Figure 10). Hopefully, knowledgeable stakeholders will give the highest ratings to the three items chosen to measure a dimension.

The matrix provides a useful way of evaluating whether individual items are “representative of an aspect” of the construct’s “content domain” (the ratings in the cells). It is less useful as a way of evaluating whether the set of items represent “the entire content domain.” Moreover, even if the set of properties were deemed to “represent the entire content domain,” it also does not provide a way of deciding whether some subset of this set would still “represent the entire content domain.” Accordingly, below I provide a modified procedure to address these limitations in Hinkin and Tracey’s (1999) approach.

First, using the pre-order of properties prepared in Step 1, researchers should seek to identify (e.g., through feedback from focus groups) the subset of preceding properties of the focal construct that they believe to be salient—those properties whose conjunction forms the focal construct (complex property). Hopefully, this initial set will include the immediately preceding properties because it is these properties whose conjunction forms the focal construct. In this first stage when researchers are still discerning the salient properties, however, most likely the initial set will also include properties that closely precede but do not immediately precede the focal construct in the pre-order of properties.

For instance, two preceding properties of “able” information system development teams are that the members are “animals” and “alive.” Researchers are unlikely to include these properties in the set of putative salient properties because they (1) deem the scopes of “capable,” “skilled,” and “well-qualified” to be subsets of their scopes (succeeding properties of “animal” and “alive”) and therefore closer to the focal property in the pre-order, and (2) believe the scope of “able” is equal to or a subset of the intersection of the scopes of “capable,” “skilled,” and “well-qualified.”

Second, once a putative set of salient preceding properties has been identified, researchers can then explore whether these properties are likely to be members of the necessary and sufficient set. For instance, they could conduct an online survey of individuals who had experience with information system development teams, ask them to list each team with which they had experience, and for each team to then indicate whether the team possessed each of the putative salient properties. If the focal construct were “able,” Table 3 shows a worksheet that researchers might prepare based on the data they collect. The columns list the putative set of salient preceding properties, and the rows show responses provided by participants in the survey for each property. Table 3 shows that respondents to the survey deem “able” teams always possess the properties of “capable,” “experienced,” “skilled,” and “well-qualified” but not “disciplined” and “creative.”

Third, having identified the putative necessary and sufficient set of properties for the focal construct, researchers can then evaluate whether they believe the focal construct can and should be defined in terms of a subset of these properties. For instance, they might decide to discard “experienced” from the set because they believe (1) the scope of “capable” is equal to or a subset of the scope of “experienced,” or (2) the union of the scopes of “capable” and “skilled” is equal to or a subset of the scope of “experienced.”

A similar procedure could be used if succeeding properties rather than preceding properties were to be used to measure the focal construct. For instance, in an online survey, respondents could be asked to indicate whether a team that possessed a putative succeeding property was always an “able” team. ## Step 4: Formally Specify the Measurement Model

In this step, MacKenzie et al. (2011, pp. 306-310) state that researchers “must formally specify a measurement model”

that reflects the hypothesized relationships among indicators, dimensions, and constructs that informed the design of their instruments. My analyses have three implications for the different measurement models they examine.

First, in relation to formative indicators, they address the question of whether the “construct-level error term” should be fixed to zero. They comment: “an error term is necessary whenever the composite latent construct is in the endogenous position, or when the formative indicators do not fully represent the construct domain” (p. 307, my emphasis). Assuming that MacKenzie et al. mean a causal formative indicator model and not a composite formative indicator model, my analyses above underscore the importance of collecting data that will enable the scope validity of the formative indicators to be tested as a means of examining whether (1) missing indicators exist, (2) an error term is needed (see also Diamantopoulos 2006, p. 11; Henseler 2017, pp. 182-183), and (3) in due course the measurement model might have to be revised. I discuss the matter of how scope validity might be tested in my examination of MacKenzie et al.’s Step 6 below.

Second, to resolve identification issues with formative indicators (Bollen and Davis 2009), MacKenzie et al. (2011, p. 308) argue that “two global reflective indicators of the composite latent construct, along with the formative indicators” be used. While MacKenzie et al. focus on the statistical reasons for using global indicators, in my comments on their Step 6 below, I show why synonymic indicators assist researchers to empirically evaluate whether (1) a set of succeeding or preceding indicators deemed to be formative have scope validity, and (2) a putative pre-order of properties appears sound.

Third, MacKenzie et al. say little about specifying a measurement model for reflective indicators, perhaps because the extant literature deems the process to be straightforward. Nonetheless, even for reflective measurement models, Henseler (2017, p. 184) observes: “Concretely, researchers almost always find a significant discrepancy between the empirical correlation matrix and the model-implied correlation matrix, which reflects the pattern that should be observed if the world indeed functioned according to the researcher’s model” (see, also, Bollen 2007). My analyses suggest one possible source of the discrepancy that Henseler identifies—namely, the set of reflective indicators used lacks scope validity. Accordingly, as with formative indicators, to assess the validity of a measurement model with reflective indicators, I argue that global indicators should also be used (see Step 6 below).

## Step 5: Collect Data to Conduct Pretest

When researchers collect data from a sample of respondents to evaluate the psychometric properties of an instrument, MacKenzie et al. (2011, p. 311) point out the need to obtain data on measures besides those in the instrument so the instrument’s convergent, nomological, and discriminant validity can be assessed. As per my comments in Step 4 above and my analyses in Step 6 below, I recommend researchers also collect data using synonymic indicators so both the scope validity of succeeding or preceding indicators and the soundness of the pre-order used to design the instrument can be assessed (e.g., data on “adept” and “competent” as synonyms for the “able” construct in Figure 10).

## Step 6: Scale Purification and Refinement

In this step, MacKenzie et al. (2011, pp. 311-317) cover methods “widely discussed in the literature” for scale purification and refinement. Their narrative is dense, and they canvass many important issues. For illustrative purposes, I will focus on just three implications of my analyses above for the procedures that MacKenzie et al. discuss.

First, MacKenzie et al. support the use of widely advocated approaches for assessing the validity of a set of reflective indicators—for instance, assessing their convergent validity by calculating the average variance extracted (AVE) by the focal construct (p. 313). Recall, however, that I have shown the scope validity of a set of indicators needs to be considered first to determine how much weight to place on measures of convergent validity.

Second, for a set of formative indicators, MacKenzie et al. argue that measures of convergent validity are not relevant because the indicators may not be correlated. Instead, they recommend that the validity of the set be evaluated by examining the size of the construct’s error variance (smaller is better) or the adequacy coefficient (Edwards 2001, p. 163) (higher is better). For formative indicators that are preceding properties of the focal construct, my analyses support their recommendations. If the construct’s error variance is low and the adequacy coefficient is high, the intersection of the scopes of the preceding (formative) indicators must have a high level of concomitance with the focal construct’s scope. Furthermore, for formative indicators that are succeeding properties of the focal construct, a low construct error variance and high adequacy coefficient mean the union of the succeeding (formative) indicators substantially overlaps the focal construct’s scope.

Third, MacKenzie et al. (2011, p. 315) present a way in which “global reflective indicators” (synonymic indicators) can be used to evaluate the validity and reliability of individual formative indicators. In essence, the test examines “the indirect effect of each formative indicator on the global reflective indicator … by multiplying (1) the estimate of the impact of the formative indicator on the composite latent construct and (2) the estimate of the impact of the composite latent construct on the global reflective indicator” (p. 315). They stress (p. 315) that “this test of validity will only provide useful information to the extent that the global reflective indicators faithfully capture the conceptual domain of the construct.” In essence, they are arguing global reflective (synonymic) indicators must have scope validity.

My ontological analyses above suggest another way in which global reflective indicators might be used. Specifically, they can be used to evaluate (1) the validity of not only formative indicators but also reflective indicators (and indeed the global reflective indicators themselves), and (2) the soundness of the putative pre-order articulated in Step 1 above. Below I describe how the two types of evaluation can be enacted.

## Limitations and Some Future Research Directions

I see nine limitations of my ontological analysis of constructs and indicators and the conclusions I have reached. These limitations provide a basis for further research.

First, I have used only Bunge’s ontology to examine the nature and meaning of constructs and indicators. I encourage colleagues to use other ontologies (e.g., Chisholm 1996; Cocchiarella 1972; Guarino and Welty 2000; Guizzardi 2005; Guizzardi and Zamborlini 2014; Mattessich 2014; Searle 1995; Searle 2006; Sowa 2000) to determine whether, compared to mine, they reach similar or different conclusions.

Second, the quality of my analyses depends on how well I have interpreted Bunge and the extant literature and then applied Bunge to the extant literature. Even though I have attempted to “faithfully” interpret the texts, I am mindful that hermeneutics researchers have shown that texts take on meanings that differ from those ascribed to them by the original authors (e.g., Ricoeur 1991; Sarker and Lee 2006, pp. 134-135). If researchers dispute my interpretation of Bunge’s ontology and the extant literature or the validity of my analyses, I hope that further research will be done to sharpen the discourse.

Third, Bunge’s (1977, 1979) ontology relates properties to one another via their scopes. While properties are phenomena that exist in the real world, linguistic terms (attributes) must be used to name them. These terms may show “semantic similarity” because they have patterns of occurrence in a language (e.g., Arnulf et al. 2014). The extent to which these patterns undermine my analyses (and traditional analyses of validity and reliability) requires further research.

Fourth, I have shown only a few implications of my analyses for the nature of multidimensional constructs and their measurement. Many other issues could be addressed. For instance, Edwards (2001, pp. 148-152) canvasses five major controversies about multidimensional constructs that relate to their theoretical utility, levels of abstraction, reliability, construct validity, and criterion-related validity. Using two data sets, he examines these controversies empirically (pp. 165-183) by testing superordinate cause-and-effect and aggregate cause-and-effect models. An analysis of the property pre-orders implied in his models might shed additional light on some of his conclusions.

Fifth, to illustrate the practical usefulness of Bunge’s ontology and my analyses, I have examined only some aspects of only six of MacKenzie et al.’s (2011) 10-step process for scale development and validation. A more extensive analysis of all 10 steps could be done to tease out further implications of my analyses. My analyses could also be applied to other protocols that have been proposed for instrument development and validation (e.g., Bagozzi and Edwards 1998; Edwards and Bagozzi 2000; Petter et al. 2007; Polites et al. 2012; Rossiter 2002; Rossiter 2011; Straub et al. 2004; Zhang et al. 2016).

Sixth, I have not provided norms for the measures of scope validity I have proposed. I have simply stated that higher levels (maximum of one) are better. If data sets exist that contain responses for synonymic indicators as well as indicators that are succeeding and/or preceding properties of a focal construct, these data sets could be used to calculate values for measures of scope validity. The values obtained could then be employed to determine how well they predict the outcomes of tests of hypotheses done using the data sets. If significant relationships are found, they could be used to establish norms for scopevalidity measures.

Seventh, I have argued that conventional measures of convergent and discriminant validity are problematic unless scope validity is first achieved. If data sets are available that contain responses for synonymic as well as succeeding and/or preceding indicators, tests could be done to evaluate whether scope validity moderates the extent to which conventional convergent and discriminant validity measures underpin the outcomes of hypotheses tests.

Eighth, while I believe the changed procedures for instrument development and validation that I am proposing will be straightforward to use, I have no empirical evidence to this effect. Experiments or case studies could be conducted to examine whether the changes I propose produce better outcomes. I suspect that such research will be difficult to conduct rigorously, however; instead, my belief will be tested by how researchers “vote with their feet”— whether they stay with existing procedures or engage with those I have proposed.

Ninth, my analyses above imply that the measurement models tested in empirical work should be based on preorder relationships among properties. These models do not rely on causal, directional relationships among constructs and indicators. In my analyses above, I have skirted the statistical approaches that could be used to test the measurement models I am proposing. Minimally, they might be tested using nondirectional relationships in structural equation models (e.g., Hoyle 2011, p. 23). Future research might examine other approaches.

## Summary of Main Findings and Conclusions

In this paper, I have sought to show how Bunge’s ontology can be used to shed light on (1) the nature of constructs and indicators and how they are related, and (2) how the validity of indicators should be assessed. I stress that my analyses are not meant to debunk existing approaches to instrument development and validation. Rather, my goal has been to complement and improve them rather than replace them.

Based on my analyses, my major conclusions are the following (see also the third column of the Table A1 in the Appendix):

Unidimensional constructs, multidimensional constructs, dimensions, and indicators can be conceptualized using a single, coherent, precise ontological foundation. Specifically, they are all properties in general of classes of things in a pre-order of properties that is based on their scopes (the sets of things that possess them).

• The meaning of constructs and indicators derives from their sense (other properties to which they are related) and reference (their scope).

Reflective indicators are sometimes synonyms for the construct they are intended to measure. In a pre-order relation over a set of properties, sometimes they are also succeeding (more specific) or preceding (more general) properties of the construct.

• Formative causal indicators are never synonyms for the construct they are intended to measure. Instead, they are either succeeding properties or preceding properties.

• Formal composite indicators are always preceding properties that bear a functional relationship to the construct they measure. The construct is real if at least one thing in the real world possesses it.

• I recommend the terms “reflective indicators” and “formative indicators” be discarded and be replaced by the terms “synonymic indicators,” “succeeding indicators,” and “preceding indicators.”

• My analysis of multidimensional constructs above shows dimensions to be preceding properties of a multidimensional construct. I leave open the possibility that dimensions can also be succeeding properties of a multidimensional construct.

• Prevailing notions of content validity are problematic. Constructs do not have content. Instead, they have a scope. Content validity is better conceived as scope validity.

The convergent validity of a set of indicators can be assessed by the extent to which their scopes overlap. The discriminant validity of different sets of indicators can be assessed by the extent to which their scopes do not overlap.

• If a set of indicators does not first achieve scope validity, the usefulness of the indicators’ convergent and discriminant validities is undermined.

• Scope validity should have primacy over convergent and discriminant validities, and discriminant validity should have primacy over convergent validity.

The internal-consistency reliability of a set of indicators can be evaluated via the level of concomitance among the indicators. Ideally, the measure of internal-consistency reliability of a set of synonymic indicators should be one. Measures of internal-consistency reliability for succeeding and preceding indicators are problematic and sometimes have limited usefulness.

Researchers may or may not agree with my conclusions. Nonetheless, in the absence of an ontological theory, I predict that the current discourse on constructs and indicators has little chance of resolving several major matters that have remained contested for many years. Through an ontological analysis of these concepts, however, I believe the discourse can usefully progress. I encourage other researchers to scrutinize my analyses, point out and rectify any errors and weaknesses in them, and use of other ontologies to examine the nature of constructs, indicators, and measurement validity.
