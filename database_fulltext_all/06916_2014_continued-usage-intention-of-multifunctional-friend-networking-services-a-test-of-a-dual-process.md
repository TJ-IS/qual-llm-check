# Continued usage intention of multifunctional friend networking services: A test of a dual-process model using Facebook

Kholekile L. Gwebu, Jing Wang ⁎, Lin Guo

Peter T. Paul College of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire, NH, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 10 January 2013 Received in revised form 5 August 2014 Accepted 16 August 2014 Available online 23 August 2014

Keywords: Online friend networks IS continuance intention IS post-adoption Satisfaction Loyalty Habit

## a b s t r a c t

The success of multifunctional friend networking (MFN) sites such as Facebook directly relies on users continuous participation and content contribution. Information systems and marketing literature offers competing models and perspectives that explain users' development of continued usage intention of a technology. Drawing on the dual-process theories of decision making, this study theoretically integrates extant post-adoption continuance models, and examines the potential value for a dual-process model that combines the key components of the competing continuance models. The integrated model posits that three contrasting and interacting mechanisms. that is. analytic based mechanism. emotional based mechanism, and habit. automaticity, underlie the development of two MFN continuance intentions: continuance intention and usage comprehensiveness intention. Using responses from Facebook users, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study reveal evidence supporting the notion that an integrated model provides explanatory value. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also suggest that the three mechanisms simultaneously, yet differentially impact the two continuance intentions. Nevertheless, most respondents in this study have more than 2 years of experience with Facebook. It will be interesting for future research to focus on less experienced Facebook users for comparison purposes.

## 1. Introduction

Multifunctional friend networking (MFN) sites such as Facebook are becoming one of the fastest-growing platforms on the World Wide Web [64]. With an inordinate number of MFN sites clamoring for attention and the limited amount of time users have, many of such sites, including the famed MySpace have suffered from high abandonment rates [11]. Given the rapid growth of MFN sites and the alarmingly high abandonment rates, understanding the factors that increase users' continued usage after adoption is of strategic importance for the ultimate success of any MFN site.

Although a number of studies have begun to examine individuals' continued usage of a social networking site [31,33,43,70], research in this area is still in its infancy. The literature in information systems (IS) and marketing offers a variety of models that explain continued IS use in various contexts. Drawing on the dual-process theories of decision making, this study theoretically integrates extant postadoption continuance models and empirically explores the explanatory value of an integrated dual-process model in explaining two end-user MFN continuance intentions: continued usage intention and usage comprehensiveness intention. Doing so will facilitate the systematic identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of those managerially actionable factors that contribute to persistent user participation in MFN sites. Recently, <sup>fi</sup>rms have also become increasingly interested in making signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investments in leveraging online communities on MFN sites for marketing, new product development, and branding. Factors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study may be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in user retention on those online communities, thereby providing broader implications for organizations' initiatives in leveraging MFN online communities for competitive advantage.

We consider usage comprehensiveness intention as well as usage continuance intention, although previous post-adoption research largely focuses on the latter [7,13]. This is because MFN sites typically offer a broad array of services and there is a general trend towards incorporating even more services. Users can choose among many services, including posting or commenting on content, creating or responding to event requests, chatting to connections, updating their status, playing games, using apps and more. Clearly, the extent of MFN service usage varies. From the perspective of the MFN sites, encouraging users to utilize a wider array of services is critically important as many such sites are seeking to break out from their traditional business models (e.g., collecting and exploring user data) and expand into the new horizons such as online advertising and e-marketing [41,68]. Given the multifunctional nature of MFN sites and their desire to explore new business opportunities, studying factors that contribute to MFN usage comprehensiveness intention would have considerable merit for both research and practice.

From a broader perspective, this research contributes to the general IS literature on information technology (IT) usage continuance. In recent years, a variety of theoretical perspectives have been advanced to provide an understanding of post-adoption continuance intentions. By using the dual-process theories of decision making as a scheme for conceptual integration, this research is able to bring a broad range of theories and constructs in the realm of existing post-adoption continuance research under a common coherent framework, which shows how these seemingly disparate theories <sup>fi</sup>t together and interrelate to provide a more complete understanding of IT usage continuance.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Using the dualprocess theories of decision making as the theoretical foundation, we <sup>fi</sup>rst theoretically integrate the various perspectives of post-adoption continuance models offered in the IS and marketing literature. Next the methodology and the results of the empirical tests conducted in the study are described. Finally, the limitations of this research are noted and the managerial and research implications of its <sup>fi</sup>ndings are presented.

## 2. The dual-process theories and it continuance models

Researchers in cognitive psychology and neuroscience have long realized that two fundamentally different processes of thinking coexist in people's decision making, and have proposed various dual-process models to re<sup>fl</sup>ect this nature of the human mind. While different conceptualizations have been used, what these models have in common is the distinction between decision processes that are deliberative, analytic, slow, and conscious and those that are intuitive, automatic, rapid, and unconscious [22,23]. The assumption that there are exactly two distinct thinking systems is unrealistic and not implied by the dual-process theories [21]. Thus the distinction between the two cognitive systems can probably be best thought of as a continuum rather than a dichotomy, with unconscious intuition representing one end of the continuum and conscious analysis representing the other [17]. More often than not human cognition represents a form of imperfect reasoning characterized by a mixture of both intuition and analysis.

The dual-process theories also characterize IT continuance decision making, since people could carefully evaluate the target technology, or they could use a sort of “quick-and-dirty” intuitive approach, or they could use a mixture of both intuition and analysis. Although explicit linkages have not been made between the dual-process theories of decision making and IS post-adoption research, we demonstrate here that the existing IT continuance models implicitly represent pieces of the cognition continuum, and the dual-process theories of decision making indeed serve the purpose of conceptual integration, bringing the existing IS continuance models under a common umbrella.

Within the dual-process framework, we broadly classify the extant IS and marketing continuance models into the analytic based (AB) models, the emotion based (EB) models, and the habit/automaticity perspective (HAP). AB models, EB models, and HAP shed light on three interacting yet distinct processes underlying IT continuance decisions. AB models center on the role of reasoning and analysis and generally view continuance decisions as involving relatively slower, effortful, and deliberate evaluation. EB models and HAP recognize that most IT continuance decisions are probably handled in a quicker and more intuitive manner. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, HAP conceptualizes IT continuance decisions as being guided by spontaneous and automatic processes characterized by the over-trained and hardwired habit mechanism. EB models capture a partially automatic process that involves a mixture of affect heuristic and deliberative evaluation of emotion, thus falling in the central region of the cognitive continuum.

## 3. The analytic based models

Adapted from the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [24], the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is arguably one of the most dominant underlying frameworks used in explaining user acceptance of new IT [19]. TAM contends that adoption behavior of a new technology is an intentional behavior driven by conscious decisions to act [48]. Intention to use IT is a key variable in determining subsequent use behavior and is a function of two salient behavioral beliefs, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Additional cognitive beliefs have been integrated into TAM to explain users' technology acceptance in a particular context. The results from numerous studies suggest that the belief–intention–behavior framework is robust across a broad range of technologies, user populations, and settings [67].

Given its robustness, TAM has also been used as a theoretical backdrop in the investigation of continued IT use [35,55]. Within this framework, IT continuance is a conscious decision driven by cognitive beliefs [35,48]. Research on IT continuance has focused on the arguably more relevant post-adoption beliefs rather than pre-adoption beliefs [7,63] because users keep updating their perception towards the technology as they gain more experience from using it.

TAM depicts an effortful, deliberative, and analytic cognitive process that encompasses users' identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of key performance attributes (usefulness, ease of use, risks, and enjoyment etc.), and development of beliefs through effortful and analytical appraisal of the performance outcomes associated with the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed attributes. It is important to note that salient beliefs, once formed, can be stored in memory and automatically activated and retrieved to guide people's decisions [1]. Nevertheless, in the process of developing cognitive beliefs, each performance attribute may only be assessed in isolation rather than in relation to one another. Thus, even after the salient beliefs are automatically retrieved, users still need to weigh the relative importance of the attributes, consider the relationships between them, and make sense of any inconsistencies (e.g., high usefulness but high risks as well) before they are <sup>fi</sup>nally able to make the decision. This characterization of a slow, effortful, deliberate, and analytic cognitive process has led us to label TAM and its variations as the analytic perspective of IT acceptance or continuance.

When applying the analytic based framework to explain MFN continuance intentions, we posit that three post adoption beliefs are most likely to be salient factors in users' formation of continuance intentions: perceived usefulness, trusting beliefs, and perceived enjoyment (See Fig. 1). Perceived ease of use is not included here because although ease of use has been found to impact pre-adoption intentions, this effect decreases for post-adoption intention as users become familiar with the technology [55].

## 3.1. Perceived usefulness

Perceived usefulness is de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as the extent to which one believes that using services provided by an MFN site will meet his or her needs. There is strong empirical evidence supporting perceived usefulness, or similar constructs such as perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and consistent predictor of individuals' initial adoption intentions as well as continuance intentions in various contexts [7,55,63], including the social networking setting [31,33,71,72].

## 3.2. Trusting beliefs

Trust is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a psychological state comprising individuals' intention to accept vulnerability based upon positive expectations of the behaviors of an MFN site [57]. In the MFN environment, various uncertainties exist, including MFN sites' opportunistic behavior with the collection, disclosure, and use of personal information. Thus, trusting beliefs towards an MFN site could be an important mechanism through which users develop their MFN continuance intentions. An individual high on trust is more likely to evaluate the MFN site positively and intend to continue to use the services offered by the site. Empirical work in IS has repeatedly demonstrated that trusting beliefs positively impact various behavioral intentions in uncertain environments including MFN sites [30,43,50].

## 3.3. Perceived enjoyment

The IS literature has long recognized the differences between utilitarian and hedonic information systems [66]. While utilitarian systems aim to provide instrumental values to the user, hedonic systems aim to provide self-ful<sup>fi</sup>lling value, fun, and pleasure to the user [66]. MFN sites serve both purposes. Although individuals may utilize MFN services for instrumental objectives such as communicating with friends, they may also seek entertainment and experience fun and pleasure when using MFN services. In the IS literature, perceived enjoyment, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extent to which using an information system is perceived to be enjoyable and self-ful<sup>fi</sup>lling in its own right, has been documented as the dominant predictor for the adoption of hedonic information systems [43,66]. Similarly, we can expect that perceived enjoyment will play a critical role in the formation of MFN continuance intentions.

## 4. The emotion based perspective

Despite the appeal of the AB models, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to imagine that most post adoption decisions are made only in such an effortful and analytic manner. Everyday individuals face many decisions and their mental resources are limited. To overcome the limitation of their cognitive capacity, individuals will utilize automatic or partially automatic processes in making decisions. Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting this nature of the human mind, the dual-process theories of decision making stress the central role of intuition and heuristics in making judgments and decisions [22, 23]. Different mechanisms and processes underlie intuitive–automatic decision making, one of which is the affect–heuristic mechanism [22].

Recognizing the importance of emotion in decision making, various models of IT continuance and customer retention have identified affective constructs such as satisfaction and loyalty as the direct predictors of peoples' continuance intentions and by contrast, cognitive beliefs are expected to indirectly impact intentions through affects [7,8,13,37,40]. People's emotions often come to mind automatically to guide important decision making. Reliance on readily available emotion is quicker, easier, and more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in decision circumstances than applying logic analysis [22,23]. In essence, affect functions as a simplifying heuristic device as users consult their feeling states to their decision making. When making a decision, individuals may simply ask themselves how they feel at that moment, freeing themselves from the lengthy and effortful cognitive processing described by the analytic mode of decision making.

In the MFN context, we posit that an emotion-based model for MFN continuance intentions would center on two affective constructs, satisfaction and loyalty, given their consistently documented effects on intentions (See Fig. 2). Both satisfaction and loyalty are expected to directly impact the two MFN continuance intentions due to the expectation that people are more likely to rely on their sense of satisfaction and loyalty rather than systematic analysis when making MFN continuance decisions.

While some previous IS studies have highlighted the evaluative and cognitive aspects of the construct satisfaction, we recognize that affective reactions such as satisfaction can occur with or without extensive cognitive encoding. Recognizing the cognitive aspect of this construct does not con<sup>fl</sup>ict with the conjecture that users tend to apply their sense of satisfaction as a mental shortcut when making MFN continuance decisions. When induced by cognitive processing, satisfaction is a univalent construct that captures individuals' overall evaluative judgment of their emotion towards an MFN site. Similar to cognitive beliefs, once formed, people's evaluation of their emotions will be stored in memory and persist overtime. However, a univalent overall judgment will be easier to store in and retrieve from long-term memory than discrete attribute level assessments (usefulness and enjoyment etc.) [32]. Thus, the more easily available and retrievable overall judgments are more likely to be activated automatically without much conscious effort than the attribute level assessments to guide users' MFN continuance judgment.

## 4.1. Satisfaction

Satisfaction involves both affect and evaluation of affect against an experience [13,47]. Satisfaction is hence de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as a user's post-choice evaluative and affective responses to his or her overall experience with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c MFN site [47]. Satisfaction has been found to be a reliable predictor for repurchase and continuance intentions in various contexts [7,8,13], including the social networking setting [33, 44,70]. Individuals satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with a product or service are less likely to search for information on and/or switch to the alternatives [2]. Thus, we expect that a user's satisfaction towards a MFN site will increase the user's willingness to stay committed and repeatedly use the site as a result.

## 4.2. Loyalty

While earlier literature conceptualized loyalty as repetitive behavior (e.g., repetitive purchasing or usage behavior), this conceptualization has been criticized due to its inability to distinguish between loyalty and spurious loyalty [20,29,46]. Recently studies tend to emphasize both the affective and the behavioral dimensions of loyalty [12,46]. Following this development, loyalty is de<sup>fi</sup>ned here as individuals' deeply held affective commitment towards a MFN site. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition captures the affective aspect of the construct. The behavioral dimension of loyalty is not included here because it is captured by the dependent variable continued use intention.

Although the importance of loyalty is being increasingly recognized in the consumer behavior literature, this construct has not received the attention it deserves in the IS literature. Nevertheless, loyalty should be an important concept in the study of IT continuance because loyalty is relatively stable despite situational in<sup>fl</sup>uences, marketing efforts, and short-term changes in the performance of the product or service [34,46]. Further, despite the importance of satisfaction in postadoption intentions and behaviors, satis<sup>fi</sup>ed users do defect. In fact, the defection rate among satis<sup>fi</sup>ed customers can be as high as 90% [46]. Thus, marketing researchers have called for a shift in emphasis from the pursuit of customer satisfaction to maximizing both customer loyalty and satisfaction as strategic business goals [46]. Empirical studies have provided strong evidence supporting affective loyalty as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and consistent predictor of individuals' repurchasing and continuance decisions [12]. Similarly, we expect that a user's loyalty towards an MFN site will increase the user's willingness to stay committed and repeatedly use the site.

## 5. The habit/automaticity perspective (HAP)

Another mechanism that underlies the automatic mode of decision making is reinforcing-habit learning [49]. The emphasis on habit by the dual-process theories of decision making is also re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the IS post-adoption literature. Recently, researchers have realized that much of IT use is a habitual behavior that requires little conscious cognition and suggested that the AB models of IT continuance fall short in explaining habitual IT usage [36,48,71]. HAP places habit as the dominant and direct predictor of continued IT use. Fig. 3 depicts a HAP model of MFN continuance intentions.

In the literature, habitual IT use is conceptualized as learned behaviors automatically triggered, with little conscious effort, by environmental cues in a stable context [48]. Following this view, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne habit as the extent to which individuals tend to use a particular MFN site automatically because of learning. Empirical evidence supports habit as a direct predictor of future intentions to use IT [15,27,71] and actual IT use [35]. Habit may directly impact MFN continuance intentions because when a habit is well entrenched in a behavior, individuals tend to ignore any information on alternatives, and hence are less likely to be distracted by the incentives and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the alternative offers [40]. Second, as a user learns to successfully use the various MFN features, s/he tends to get better at using them. The associated feeling of increasing con<sup>fi</sup>dence of using these functions may motivate the user to continue using the same MFN site. The action of using an MFN site and the increasing con<sup>fi</sup>dence of using it may reinforce each other, so much so that the behavior of repeatedly using the same MFN site may be a guiding force in some habituated manner [40]. Once the habit of using a particular MFN site is developed, it can prevent the user from being tempted by other alternatives, leading to enhanced continuance intentions.

## 6. An integrated model

The dual-process theories of decision making serve as a useful framework not only for the categorization, but also for the integration of the various continuance models proposed in the literature. As a multi-process approach, the dual-process theories argue that human cognition is not governed by a single process, but by multiple processes and their interplay. The multi-system account of judgment provides the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for an integrated model of MFN continuance intentions that combines the three processes described by the AB model, EB model, and HAP. For the most part, these processes play cooperative roles in human cognition and act synergistically to determine behaviors. But occasionally, they produce con<sup>fl</sup>icting decision outcomes based on the same information and vie to guide behavior [23,60]. This competitive mode between systems provides an explanation for observed disassociations between seemingly closely related variables [60]. Thus, an integrative model has the advantage of explaining the possible disassociation between the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed predictors and the MFN intentions. For instance, it is highly probable that individuals' MFN continuance intentions are unconsciously in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their habit even though the intentions con<sup>fl</sup>ict with their conscious cognitive beliefs. Given its ability to deal with such disassociations, an integrative model is expected to add more explanatory value for MFN continuance intentions than each of the individual models. Fig. 4 depicts the integrated model.

## 6.1. The parallel and direct influence

Consistent with the multi-process account of decision making, the integrated model depicts parallel and direct links from the three categories of predictors to the MFN continuance intentions. Research in cognitive psychology has recognized motivation, cognitive style, and cognitive capacity as important factors that determine if one is more likely to engage in reasoning and analysis, or rely on heuristic cues, or both [21,60]. Therefore, in some circumstances, the three mechanisms (analysis, emotion, and habit) will operate simultaneously and have additive or offsetting effects on the two intention variables. But in other situations, individuals will resort to one mechanism depending on the contextual and individual differences.

## 6.2. The interplay between the processes

The multiple processes of thinking also work in an interactive manner. The integrated model captures such interactions through mediations and moderations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, most post-adoption continuance models assume the general pattern of cognitions–affect– intentions–behavior and there is no dearth of empirical evidence supporting this pattern [4,13,29,46]. Given that satisfaction and loyalty both involve affective reactions, the integrated model depicts an indirect path from the cognitive beliefs to the MFN continuance intentions through satisfaction and loyalty. Satisfaction and loyalty only partially mediate the effects of beliefs on MFN intentions due to the expectation that intentions can be directly controlled by cognitive beliefs. Based on prior research, loyalty is also expected to partially mediate the relationship between satisfaction and intentions [3,46,69].

Further, habit is expected to moderate the impacts of beliefs on continuance intentions [36,38,71]. The logic here is that as the same behavior is performed repeatedly overtime, the same set of cognition and evaluation tends to be formulated repetitively and becomes ingrained in individuals' mental representation. Automatic processing will then start to displace conscious processing and IT use will be automatically triggered [36]. Similarly, when MFN use becomes habitual, MFN continuance may become less evaluative. Habit may moderate the relationship between cognition and intention in such a way that the effects of cognitive beliefs on MFN continuance intentions will decrease as habit increases.

## 6.3. Differential effects on the two intentions

Despite the emphasis on the co-existence of multiple processes guiding decision making, the dual-process theories also recognize that the conditions conducive for a particular process will vary. For instance, repeatedly and routinely practiced behaviors and decisions will likely evoke spontaneous and automatic processing while unfamiliar and rarely practiced behaviors and decisions will likely evoke deliberative and effortful cognition [1,49]. Since not every user engages in an extensive range of activities on MFN sites, users are more likely to frequently face the continued usage decision (should I use the MFN site today?) than the usage comprehensiveness decision (should I use different MFN services today?). Stated differently, the continuance usage decision is more likely to be a routine decision to MFN users and hence will likely evoke automatic and spontaneous processing. By contrast, the usage comprehensiveness decision, which is expected to be less frequently made, will more likely be guided by the deliberative and analytic cognitive process.

## 7. Research method

A survey was used to test the proposed integrated model. Participants for the study were recruited using the Amazon crowdsourcing online marketplace, Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Given the research question of this study, we constrained potential respondents to Facebook users who are 18 years and older and live in the United States. A total of 345 Facebook users successfully completed the survey. The pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the respondents (see Table 1) closely resembles the pro<sup>fi</sup>le of Facebook users reported in a recent national survey by Pew Research [52]. The majority of respondents are female (59%), are below 50 years old with 47% falling in the 18–30 year old age range and 44% falling in the 31–50 year old age range. Most of the respondents have a 4-year college degree (41%) or a Master's, Doctoral or Professional Degree (21%). Finally, the majority of respondents report being a Facebook member for 2 or more years, having over 100 connections on Facebook, and visiting Facebook daily.

The items used in this study (see Appendix A) originated from validated scales employed in prior research. They were modi<sup>fi</sup>ed to <sup>fi</sup>t the context of this study. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the scales for perceived enjoyment and continued usage intention were derived from Lin and Bhattacherjee [39] while questions for habit came from work by Limayem et al. [38]. The questions used in the loyalty and trust scales came from Kim and Son [34] and Roberts et al. [56] respectively while the questions for perceived usefulness and those for satisfaction came from the work of Davis [19] and Bhattacherjee [7] respectively. For usage comprehensiveness intention we provided the respondents with a list of eight different activities available on Facebook and asked them to select the activities they intend to engage in the near future. The activities include posting content (text, videos or photos), commenting on or liking content, creating or RSVP events, chatting, posting status updates, playing games (e.g. FarmVille), using Facebook apps, viewing company/celebrity pages. With the exception of the usage comprehensiveness intention scale, 5-point Likert scales ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) were used.

## 8. Survey bias

We checked for two common sources of survey bias, non-response bias and common method bias. To evaluate the potential for nonresponse bias we followed the extrapolation procedure advocated by Boyer and Hult [10]. The responses were arranged into quartiles based on the time when they were submitted. The <sup>fi</sup>rst quartile was the early respondents while the last quartile was the late respondents and served as a proxy for non-respondents. Using a t-test for continuous variables and a Chi-square test for categorical variables, we compared the <sup>fi</sup>rst and last quartiles across a range of variables to determine if there were any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the early and late respondents. The results of the comparison tests did not reveal signi<sup>fi</sup> cant differences in terms of age (t = 0.301 p = 0.764), gender (X<sup>2</sup> = 0.193 p = 0.661), education level $( X ^ { 2 } = 5 . 1 6 3 ~ \mathrm { p } = 0 . 6 4 0 )$ ), frequency of visits $( X ^ { 2 } = 7 . 8 1 9 \ p = 0 . 3 4 9 )$ , number of connections $( X ^ { 2 } = 0 . 7 4 3$ p = 0.863), and membership length $( X ^ { 2 } = 1 . 6 9 3 ~ \mathrm { p } = 0 . 6 3 9 )$ . These results suggest that non-response bias is unlikely a major problem in the study.

Since the study data are self-reported, we used two approaches to ensure that common method bias is not a potential problem: Harmans Single Factor test and correlation matrix analysis. ## 9. Results

Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression was used to test the measurement and structural models. PLS has been extensively used in IS research and is chosen for this study because of its capability to handle the highly complex predictive models that incorporate formative constructs [14] such as the one considered in this study.

## 10. Measurement model

Composite reliability (CR) and Cronbachs alpha (CA) were examined to assess the reliability of the latent variables in the models. Suf<sup>fi</sup>cient reliability is demonstrated when CR and CA exceed a minimum threshold of 0.7 [45]. Table 3 indicates that CR ranges from 0.911 to 0.962 while CA ranges from 0.856 to 0.941, providing evidence for scale reliability. The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and the factor loadings were examined to evaluate convergent validity. Table 3 shows that AVE and factor loadings all exceed the recommended thresholds of 0.5 and 0.7 respectively [26], providing support for convergent validity in the scales. To evaluate discriminant validity we examined the square root of AVE values when placed on the diagonals of the latent variable correlation matrix and compared them to corresponding off diagonal values. The results in Table 2 provide support for discriminant validity of the scales since all the values on the diagonals are larger than their corresponding off diagonal values.

For usage comprehensiveness intention, a formative construct, we followed recommendations by Petter et al. [51] and examined the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the indicator weights for construct validity and the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors (VIF) of indicators for reliability. The t-statistics for four of the indicators (commenting on or liking content, posting status updates, using Facebook apps, viewing company/celebrity pages) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 5$ while the remaining four were not. However, in the interest of preserving content validity [51], the nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant items were retained in the model. Prior research has found that the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant items can help preserve content validity and do not necessarily bias the estimates of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indicators of the formative construct [9,42,51]. The VIF of the indicators ranged from 1.056 to 1.775, which is below the recommended VIF of 3.3 for formative indicators, signifying that multicollinearity is not an issue and that the indicators are reliable.

## 11. Structural model

The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the proposed relationships and the amount of variance explained in the dependent variables by each of the models are assessed through a standard bootstrapping procedure using 500 resamples [14]. To examine whether the improvement in variance explained in the dependent variables from AB, EB, or HAP models to the integrated model is statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, we compare the $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 }$ value of the integrated model with that of the AB, EB, or HAP models individually by conducting pairwise nested F-tests. The effect size $\textstyle ( \mathrm { f } ^ { 2 } )$ of incorporating additional variables into the integrated model was computed following Cohen [16]. Cohen [16] suggests that $\mathrm { f } ^ { 2 }$ values approaching 0.02, 0.15 and 0.35 be considered small, medium and large respectively. Table 4 presents the results of these analyses.

Following prior studies [28,62], two complementary analyses were conducted to test the speculated mediations: the nested model comparison approach and Sobel's test. Using the nested model comparison approach, we <sup>fi</sup>rst computed an $\mathrm { f } ^ { 2 }$ value to determine the effect size of the $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ differences between the nested and the full models. When models are nested, statistical comparison is possible using PLS [62]. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the $\mathrm { f } ^ { 2 }$ value was determined by computing a pseudo-F statistic. A non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant pseudo-F statistic indicates mediation, while signi<sup>fi</sup>cant one indicates that the model is not mediated. For the Sobel tests we computed z statistics [61] and their corresponding p-values. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant p-values suggest mediation because they indicate that the relationship between the independent variable and the dependent variable is reduced signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly when the mediator is incorporated.

## 13. Usage comprehensiveness intention

As can be seen from Table 4, the AB, EB, HAP models respectively explain 36.5%, 39.3%, and 38.7% of the variance in usage comprehensiveness intention. In the AB model, only enjoyment $( \beta = 0 . 4 5 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ is found to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence usage comprehensiveness intention. In the HAP model, habit exerts a positive impact on usage comprehensiveness intention $( \beta = 0 . 6 0 1 0 ; \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ .

The integrated model explains 46.5% of the variance in usage comprehensiveness intention, a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvement of 10.0%, 7.2% and 7.8% more variance in the dependent variable than the AB, EB, and HAP models respectively, with medium effect sizes for the $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ changes (see Table 4). Contrary to the expectation, habit does not reduce the effects of the three beliefs on usage comprehensiveness intention.

## 14. Supplementary analyses

Given the characteristics (with both re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative constructs) of the models, PLS was used in preference to the covariancebased structural equation modeling (CBSEM) method. Nevertheless, PLS lacks overall goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t measures that assess how well the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed model <sup>fi</sup>ts the observed data. We therefore conducted supplementary analyses using CBSEM of latent interactions via Lisrel 8.8 and observed the overall goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t measures on all the models [53]. A summated scale for the formative construct, comprehensiveness intention, was used in the analysis.

As can be seen from Appendix B, the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients from CBSEM and PLS are largely consistent. ## 15. Discussion, implications, and conclusion

Like all studies, this research is subject to limitations. First, this study focuses on one type of social networking site. The results should be tested further in other contexts such as professional networking sites (e.g. LinkedIn). Another related limitation lies in the sample and the country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of the study. Although the sample demographics in this research (age and gender) were consistent with those from a recent national survey conducted by Pew Research [52], most respondents in this study have more than 2 years of experience with Facebook. Further, users' behavior may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by cultural factors. Thus, before any broad generalizations can be made, future research using different samples may be required for comparison purposes. Second, intentions were used as opposed to measures of actual behavior. Despite the substantial empirical support for the intention-behavior link, future research may consider measuring the actual MFN continuance behavior and using longitudinal data to establish causal links. Third, this study is limited to the examination of two post-adoption intentions. Future research might consider enlarging the research agenda by investigating other intentions such as word-of-mouth and exploration of alternatives.

Despite these limitations, several important research implications emerged from the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this research. While the integrated model is only tested in the MFN context, the results indicate that the integrated model signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly surpasses the individual component models in explaining variance in both dependent variables. This result echoes some of the findings of previous studies conducted in different contexts [35,55,63] and suggests the need for an integrated model. In this sense, this research contributes to the literature in three important ways. First, drawing on the dual-process theories of cognition, the integrative model serves the purpose of conceptual integration by bringing the extant theoretical models of IT continuance under a common umbrella and thereby provides a more uni<sup>fi</sup>ed understanding of the different processes through which IT continuance intentions can be developed. The results from the integrated model show that beliefs, satisfaction and loyalty, and habit all contribute to users' MFN continuance intentions one way or another, suggesting that AB, EB, and HAP models present complementary perspectives in understanding the formation of MFN continuance intentions. Second, the integrated model allows for the modeling of possible mediation and moderation effects. Researchers have long suggested that the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of important mediators between predictors and outcomes is important for advancing theory because it helps uncover the underlying mechanisms through which predictors exert impacts on outcomes [5,18]. The integrated model allows for the exploration of the interrelationship between constructs derived from multiple referent theoretical perspectives, thereby bridging the gap between different domains of IT continuance research.

Finally, previous post-adoption studies largely focus on the role cognition plays in IT continuance intentions and behaviors [31,33,43, 44,70,72]. A few studies have only recently begun to explore the impacts of cognition and habit simultaneously and their interplay on IT continuance intentions and behaviors [38,71]. Nevertheless, explicit linkages have not been made between the dual-process theories of decision making and IS post-adoption research. Grounded in well-established dual process theories, the integrated model has the potential to open opportunities for new conceptual and empirical investigation as future studies have much to gain from the important insights that the rich research stream on the dual-process theories offers. For instance, dualprocess theorists have made considerable progress in understanding how human and task characteristics may impact decision makers' information processing strategies (intuition or analysis). Robust instruments have also been devised for the assessment of task characteristics and individual differences. IS researchers can draw on these conceptual insights as well as the robust instruments from this body of research and probe more deeply into how to design strategies well aligned with the decision maker and the task to yield desired intentions and behaviors.

Perhaps the most interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is the differential contribution of the three processes to the prediction of the two intentions. While continued MFN usage intention seems to be primarily guided by deliberative (beliefs) and partially automatic (affect) processes, MFN comprehensiveness intention is primarily under the control of automatic (habit) or partially automatic (affect) processes. This result questions the popular view that less frequently practiced decisions (comprehensiveness decision) are more likely to evoke careful deliberation, and routine decisions (continued usage decision) are primarily guided by automatic processing [1]. One explanation for this discrepancy is that other factors such as decision complexity and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, in addition to the routineness of the decision task, interact to in<sup>fl</sup>uence which processing mode will be activated [6,25]. Studies suggest that inconsequential/ insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant yet complex decisions may lead to heuristic and automatic processing simply because the decision makers are unable (the task is too complex) or unwilling (the task is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant) to extend their cognitive effort [6,25]. By contrast, one may be more motivated to apply deliberative processing when making simpler judgments because logic reasoning in this case is easily obtainable and analytical reasoning appears more rational. Although both continued use and comprehensiveness intentions are inconsequential decisions, it is reasonable to argue that the latter is a relatively more complex judgment task. With the comprehensiveness decisions, MFN users need to consider a greater number of alternatives, criteria, and increased amount of information. Therefore, users may be more likely to apply mental short cuts (automatic processing) when facing the comprehensiveness decision than the continued use decision.

Another explanation could be that individuals react differently to speci<sup>fi</sup>c versus general cases. Research suggests that individuals respond very differently to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c victim as opposed to a group of victims or a general statistics [59], or to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c politician as opposed to a group (Democrats or Republicans) [58]. This is because people tend to view speci<sup>fi</sup>c cases as more emotionally engaging and self-relevant and will be less apt to apply analytic rules and more likely to follow their spontaneous reaction. By contrast, the less engaging general judgments are more likely to call on rational rules [58,59]. It is reasonable to argue that continued use intention is a more general judgment case and thus calls on analytic processing, while usage comprehensiveness decision calls on spontaneous reaction because it is a more speci<sup>fi</sup>c and concrete judgment involving consideration of speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of an MFN site.

While related studies have offered valuable insights into the role habit and various cognitive and affective factors play in individuals' continuance decisions, there is limited research on when each category of factors will take control. The differential contribution of the three processes to the prediction of the two intentions found in this study calls for more research to delineate the boundary conditions under which each of the processes is likely to operate. Research of this nature will not only advance the theoretical knowledge on IT continuance, but also provide practitioners with valuable guidance on how continuance behaviors and intentions can be effectively cultivated by creating conducive conditions that favor the right mode of thinking. For instance, deliberative mode of reasoning is required to change any well-practiced habitual behavior [49]. When individuals are habitually not using an MFN site or not using a wide range of MFN services, an improved understanding of the boundary conditions for the different processes underlying MFN continuance decisions will help the formulation of effective strategies to evoke conscious and deliberative thinking and impede the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of established yet undesired habit.

Another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is the important role of loyalty in the development of continuance intentions. Although satisfaction, beliefs, and habit have been examined in various contexts [31,33,43,44, 70–72], the concept of loyalty and its effect on post-adoption intentions and behaviors have not received adequate attention in the IS literature [34]. The results show that trust and enjoyment positively impact continued use and comprehensiveness intentions respectively in the AB model, but once loyalty is taken into account, enjoyment and trust have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant residual impacts on comprehensiveness intention and continued use intention respectively in the integrated model and their impacts on the two intentions are fully mediated by loyalty. These results imply that loyalty may be a more important driver for post adoption intentions than the traditional constructs (i.e., beliefs) employed in IS research. Given these results and the fact that loyalty is a relatively stable factor that may lead to long-term behavioral outcomes, the merit of incorporating this construct into IS post-adoption research is certainly warranted.

The results also yield several important practical implications. First, usefulness positively impacts continued use intention, highlighting the role of service utility as a critical driver in MFN continuance decisions. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and negative coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the usefulness and habit interaction indicates that positive perception on usefulness is even more important for non-habitual MFN users. Hence, the provision of utilitarian features is particularly critical for the retention of light users. Further, maintaining positive affective experience is critical in forming MFN continuance intentions as satisfaction and loyalty directly impact continued use intention and fully mediate the impact of trust on continued use intention, and loyalty directly impacts comprehensiveness intention and fully mediates the impact of enjoyment on comprehensiveness intention. Implementing effective loyalty programs could also be very important for the success of MFN sites given the consistent and direct effects of loyalty on both intentions. Unlike satisfaction and beliefs which can change from time to time depending on users' experience with the site, loyalty remains relatively stable and thus effective loyalty programs could be essential to maintain long-term relationships with the site user. Lastly, the direct effect of habit on comprehensiveness intention and the moderating effect of habit on the relationship between usefulness and MFN continuance intention highlight the importance of nurturing habitual MFN use. Since habitual users are more likely to use a wide range of MFN services, providing stable supporting environment to facilitate the establishment of routine MFN use is critical in encouraging users to engage in the various features provided by the site.

Second, the results highlight that users' MFN continuance intentions can be strengthened through a multitude of approaches such as enhancing positive user perceptions, elevating user affect and emotion, and promoting habitual use of the MFN site. However, as the antecedents of the two intentions differ, strategies that promote continued use intention may not be effective in promoting comprehensiveness intention and vice versa. Finally, the results identify several important mediations: trust → loyalty and satisfaction → continued use intention, and enjoyment → loyalty → comprehensiveness intention. These results imply that comprehensive strategies that target beliefs, affect, and habit will be more effective than strategies that target each component in isolation. This is because different mechanisms are essential in user retention, and interventions that address each link on the mediation chain will be more successful.
