## 1. Introduction

The nascent digital economy, which trades information- and knowledge-based goods and services through computerized platforms, has been rewriting the nature of competition in the business world (Brynjolfsson and Kahin 2000). In particular, two aspects of digital market competition distinguish this particular type of competition from that of traditional markets. First, through various online venues (e.g., forums, social networking and product review sites, blogs, and other Web 2.0 platforms), consumers in digital markets often influence the decisions of other consumers by sharing their personal experiences (Dellarocas 2005). According to the Opinion Research Corporation, 84% of Americans reported that online reviews impacted their purchasing decisions.<sup>1</sup> Although these influences occur in traditional markets as well, the global reach of the Internet has facilitated a far-reaching and astonishingly rapid movement of digital word of mouth, thereby changing the buying patterns and knowledge-acquisition behaviors of an increasing number of consumers (Bampo et al. 2008, Dellarocas 2003). This consumer chain reaction has been defined as an influence network (Watts and Dodds 2007).

The second defining aspect of digital markets is their tendency to induce a collective competition model. Many strategic alliances (SAs)<sup>2</sup> that occur in digital markets are motivated by increased consumer traffic rather than by improved efficiencies (e.g., cost reduction). For example, digital markets encourage many horizontal alliances—cooperative arrangements between competitors within the same industry (Edwards 1955)—because of their structurally unpredictable and unstable operating environments (Kauffman and Wood 2000). Information technologies and digital systems<sup>3</sup> have enabled businesses to adopt collaborative strategies, such as joint marketing programs and cross-selling. In the face of hypercompetition (D’Aveni 1994, Smith et al. 2001), firms, through these arrangements, collaborate with one another to preempt competitive pressure and seize market opportunities (Gulati 1998). The online sponsored search advertising market provides one interesting context within which dynamic interorganizational partnering frequently occurs. For example, over the past several years Google has acquired DoubleClick and YouTube in multibillion-dollar deals and has formed a strategic partnership with MySpace, the world’s largest social networking site (110 million active members). In response, Microsoft acquired aQuantive in a cash deal valued at \$6 billion, and

Yahoo purchased Right Media for \$680 million.<sup>4</sup> Furthermore, in July 2009, Yahoo and Microsoft formed a technology and marketing alliance aimed at drastically challenging Google in online sponsored search advertising.<sup>5</sup>

In this paper, we investigate the outcome of SAs between two “runner-up” online search engine companies (SECs) in competition with a dominant market leader. This exploration involves settings where an advertiser’s decision making on the choice of a search advertising platform<sup>6</sup> is based on a combination of NI (e.g., an individual’s willingness to follow others’ decisions) and individual preference (IP). More specifically, we seek to answer the following key questions: Could a pairing of two smaller competitors overtake the dominant position of a market leader that maintains a larger share of the market than these two “underdogs” combined? And if so, when and how? Under what circumstances, with respect to the extent of NI, are the alliances with a size disadvantage most likely to succeed? Finally, to what extent does the combined market share at the time of alliance play a role in determining the likelihood of success?

Answering these questions poses major challenges. A multitude of factors, such as market characteristics, types of competitive actions, and changes in market environments, can potentially affect the outcome of such alliances. Moreover, because influence networks essentially come in virtual form, as well as on an astronomical scale in terms of the number of participants (Shapiro and Varian 1999), empirically identifying the specific typology of such networks is not practically feasible. Consequently, a flexible but powerful mechanism is needed to explore the dynamics inherent in influence networks and their effects on strategic outcomes. For this reason, this study employs an agent-based computer simulation model (Carley 2002) as its main analytical strategy to assess the outcomes of various competitive actions at a holistic level.

The findings of our study provide unique insights into the role of “size” (market share) in digital market competition. In conventional network effects theory, size is often assumed to be the determining factor to win in a market competition. The theory suggests that when a leader reaches a “safe zone” (i.e., over 60% of market share), the market builds itself and the winner eventually takes all of it (Katz and Shapiro 1994). In this study, however, we demonstrate that this may not always be the case in certain situations. Although important, size alone is not always a sufficient factor for maintaining market leadership. Our results indicate that with the presence of NI, IP, and cascading effects (e.g., a chain of actions), an alliance with “only” a 35%–40% combined market share could compete with a leader whose market share, at the time of an alliance, is 60%–65%. We discovered that by utilizing the combination of NI, IP, and global cascading, the pairing of two smaller competitors could disrupt the momentum of a leader’s dominance and possibly succeed in a “coup.” Another noteworthy finding is that a nonlinear association exists between NI and an alliance outcome; the combined runnersup have the best chance of success when the extent of NI is not too high or too low. Furthermore, our results suggest that NI substantially moderates the relationship between the combined market share at the time of an alliance and the likelihood of subsequent alliance success. Finally, we discuss our contributions to the existing literature on network science, as well as information systems and strategy, while providing the online sponsored SEC executives with managerial implications regarding the competitive nature of digital markets.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Several domains of behavioral research offer a fruitful lens to examine digital markets where NI and SAs play an important role in governing competitive positions. Social conformity literature (e.g., Deutsch and Gerard 1955, Cialdini and Trost 1998) suggests that individuals have a natural tendency to change their behavior to match the responses of others in order to “enhance, protect, or repair their self-esteems”

(Cialdini and Goldstein 2004, p. 611). This conformity behavior is also an underpinning for studies on herding (e.g., Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992). Banerjee (1992, p. 797) noted that “there are innumerable social and economic situations in which we are influenced in our decision making by what others around us are doing.” He further posited that in many decision-making situations “everyone is doing what everyone else is doing” (Banerjee 1992, p. 798). This herd or conformity behavior is particularly prevalent in the face of uncertain economic payoffs. Empirical finance literature shows that many investors, whether individuals or institutions, rely significantly on the decisions of others rather than on their own personal judgments (Lakonishok et al. 1992).

A plethora of motivations, including accuracy, affiliation, and efficiency, have been suggested as reasons that people conform in many decision-making situations (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). Deutsch and Gerard (1955) claim that people follow the decisions of others because they believe that others’ information more accurately represents the reality of the situation. In addition, the imitative behavior of an individual is motivated by the desire to obtain social approval from or affiliation with others. Apart from these motivations, many individuals simply mimic the behaviors of others as an “adaptive shortcut that maximizes the likelihood of effective action with minimal expense to one’s cognitive resources” (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004, p. 609). In other words, assimilation behavior is driven by efficiency as the goal, which may enable the decision makers to economize on costs.

The literature on customer switching is another research area relevant to our study. It has been shown that consumers tend to switch their service providers for numerous reasons, including quality and satisfaction (McDougall and Levesque 2000), value (Bansal and Taylor 1999), alternative attractiveness (Jones et al. 2000), low switching costs (Ping 1993), curiosity (Sheth and Raju 1974), social influences, and the propensity to seek variety (Bansal and Taylor 1999). Switching behavior is, therefore, stimulated by utilitarian objectives (e.g., value or satisfaction) and experiential or hedonic motives (e.g., curiosity, variety-seeking, or social influence) (Van Trijp et al. 1996). The brand alliance literature suggests that an alliance between large companies brings about a brand combination that significantly increases customer bases. For example, Spethmann and Benezra (1994) demonstrated that brand alliance increases market growth for the firms involved in such strategic actions by as much as 40%. Moreover, many researchers postulate that a brand alliance positively influences consumers’ attitudes and stimulates their curiosity about the combined entities (Park et al. 1996, Simonin and Ruth 1998). Switching behavior has also recently become the subject of increasing attention in information systems literature. For example, based on 299 samples, Zhang et al. (2009) discovered that satisfaction, sunk costs, and attractive alternatives are the main motives bloggers give for switching to services provided by major websites such as Google, Yahoo, and Microsoft. Similarly, using 1,408 survey responses, Kim et al. (2006) found that satisfaction, switching costs, and attractive alternatives are significantly associated with switching behavior for email services. Chen and Hitt (2002) investigated switching behavior with respect to online brokers. They found that of 1,249 online broker users, 146 (12%) changed their brokerage service providers and 269 (22%) discontinued their brokerage services within three months of choosing a new broker.

Finally, network diffusion and cascades are also important theoretical building blocks we used in the construction of our propositions. Abrahamson and Rosenkopf (1997) articulate the “bandwagon” effects by which innovation diffuses within the social fabric of an organizational network. They define a bandwagon process as “a positive feedback loop in which increases in the number of adopters create stronger bandwagon pressures, and stronger bandwagon pressures, in turn, cause increases in the number of adopters” (Abrahamson and Rosenkopf 1997, p. 289). Network-based diffusion literature also focuses on the impacts of network structures on the diffusion process and the outcomes. Several studies (e.g., Granovetter and Soong 1986) conclude that network structures significantly affect diffusion and cascading dynamics by demonstrating that a small variation in network structure can exert a major influence on the rate and extent to which network diffusion and cascades occur. Delre et al. (2007) found that the rate of diffusion is associated with the degree of randomness in the network. Oh and Jeon (2007) also demonstrated that scale-free networks are more resilient than random networks in preserving the stability of online community networks when responding to external forces.

## 3. NI and Competition in Online Advertising Markets

We chose an online sponsored advertising market as our study context for several reasons. First, our study focuses on the competition driven by digital systems in a triopoly market structure<sup>7</sup> (e.g., one lead firm and two runner-up firms) in which competitive dynamics, defined as a series of competitive actions and reactions among competitors (Ferrier et al. 1999), governs the strategic behavior of key players in the market. Online sponsored search advertising exemplifies one such market with the presence of strong competitive dynamics. Second, from a competition viewpoint, this market offers unique and dynamic settings. Agglomeration theory (Canina et al. 2005, McCann and Folta 2008) posits that competitors often colocate with one another to pursue collective benefits that occur through heightened demand. For example, multiple competing hotels and clusters of rival restaurants are colocated at the intersection of interstate highways. Similarly, online advertisers spatially and geographically colocate in the sponsored search engine space, which results in collective benefits as search users are encouraged to visit multiple stores that are colocated in online search space. Yet these stores also compete for a better location by attempting to be ranked highly on sponsored search pages. In fact, many advertisers deliberately choose keywords similar to ones used by their competitors to “steal” competitors’ customers.<sup>8</sup> In this respect, online sponsored search markets offer a novel setting in which advertisers not only compete, but also collaborate to reap agglomerative benefits.

Third, as pointed out earlier, the intense competition in this industry has triggered numerous user traffic-oriented horizontal SAs to increase market share (Edwards 1955) on the one hand and to deter potential competition (Steiner 1975) on the other. Moreover, this is one of the digital markets in which many large, public online forums exist for online advertisers that help each other through influence networks. For example, one online forum (http://forums.digitalpoint.com) has nearly 250,000 registered users, and more than 60,000 of them were active participants as of December 2008. Most of the participants in such online forums<sup>9</sup> are online advertisers that seek information, build bonds with each other, and share their experiences with major SECs. One important note is that similar to other online review sites, this forum site uses a “reputation system” through which members can rate the quality of information posted by other members.<sup>10</sup> Table 1 presents a typical conversation among those who look for experience sharing and who offer suggestions and recommendations.

## 3.1. NI, IP, and SEC Selections

Network influence (NI) like the one shown above could affect online advertisers’ selection of a particular SEC. Advertisers might be interested in learning which SECs their neighbors deal with and subsequently use that information when making decisions. The NI factor reflects the propensity of advertisers to follow what others choose.

There are several incentives that motivate online advertisers to pay attention to the decisions of other advertisers. Because search advertising is complicated, online advertisers, especially small retailers, might be inclined to accelerate their learning curve by emulating companies that have already done costbenefit analyses. Experimenting with different SECs could be costly, in terms of both time and resources. In addition, advertisers’ unfamiliarity with keywordbased auction mechanisms might induce them to adopt aggressive bidding strategies, resulting in economic loss (e.g., winner’s curse). Moreover, advertising, by its nature, is very time sensitive and requires prompt decision making. For these reasons, online advertisers can be better off basing their decisions on others’ choice.

However, although this behavioral adaptation is considered ecologically rational (Goldstein and Gigerenzer 2002), it might be more realistic to assume that decision makers also use their own IP when choosing an SEC. At the holistic level, these two factors influence each other; for example, increased IP value could entice an advertiser to switch, which, in turn, changes the proportional distribution with respect to the choice of SECs surrounding a focal advertiser.

## 3.3. NI, Cascading, and Alliance Success

Because NI has become one of the key factors affecting consumers’ purchasing decisions in digital markets, we further investigate its impact on the cascading and the alliance success. More specifically, we ask under what conditions with respect to the extent of NI (e.g., high wNI or low wNI)<sup>14</sup> are the alliances of the two smaller competitors mostly likely to succeed? To examine this issue, we draw on the ideas from the threshold model of collective behavior (Granovetter 1978, Watts 2002), which posits that global cascades rarely occur when networks are either too sparse or too dense. The rationale for this argument is that in a scattered network, nodes do not interact enough to influence one another, but in a highly connected network, inordinate influence may cause actors to become inactive. Granovetter (1978) asserts that global cascades are most likely to occur between these two extremes but remain contingent on other contextual factors (e.g., quality of network connection). Watts (2002) also contends that in random structures with arbitrary degree distribution, the propagation of global cascades is severely plagued by a lack of connections or local stability in the nodes themselves.

These findings suggest that the impact of NI on the SAs is optimal when an actor’s “willingness” to make decisions based on the actions of others (wNI) is neither too excessive nor too depleted. Building on the theoretical framework introduced by Granovetter (1978) and Watts (2002), we postulate that the dynamic interaction between NI and alliance success is governed largely by two forces: (1) the extent of influencing activities among actors and (2) the extent of collective unity among actors as manifested in homogeneous clusters.<sup>15</sup> In fact, these two forces are interrelated, which is illustrated below.

An optimal equilibrium with respect to the alliance success can be obtained between the two extremes of NI. In the presence of low NI, actors respect individual actions and rarely imitate another actor’s behavior. Thus, when wNI is relatively low (or wIP is high), actors make decisions primarily based on their IPs, regardless of the decisions of other actors. As a result, the extent of influencing and assimilation activities among actors will be low. Consequently, cascades are expected to occur slowly and only locally. Numerous fragmented clusters with heterogeneous choices will prevail among the advertisers in such an environment. Because of the low influencing activities and the presence of numerous small clusters, cascades will occur in a limited capacity. Conversely, when wNI is comparatively high, NI is the governing norm and actors tend to rely heavily on the decisions of other actors in that market. In markets with high NI, it is reciprocal influence that encourages actors to imitate their neighbors, which subsequently results in high homogeneity among local groups or clusters of networks (Moreland and Levine 1992). Advertisers in this market will not modify their choice, as the majority of their neighbors do not. In such highly “sociable markets,” this imitating behavior has been touted as ecologically rational because everyone follows the majority group’s behavioral patterns (Goldstein and Gigerenzer 2002). This collective unity that manifests in clusters encourages members to maintain their states rather than to switch. Consequently, the threshold model of collective behavior suggests that the cascades occur globally when NI is not too high or too low; therefore, the alliance success is most likely to occur under the moderate NI.

Proposition 2. Ceteris paribus, the probability that SAs formed by runner-up companies succeed, is optimal when the NI is medium (not too high and not too low).

## 3.4. NI and Timing of SAs

Timing is often thought to be one of the factors critical to the success of SAs (Bourgeois and Eisenhardt 1988, MacMillan et al. 1985). Yet little research is available regarding the impact of timing on SAs’ outcomes (Lavie et al. 2007). Network dynamics could provide one interesting theoretical lens through which to explore the complex relationship between the timing of SAs and their outcomes. We define timing in such a way that it represents the combined market share of the two allying runners up at the time of the alliance and investigate its impact on the likelihood of the SA’s success. From a network perspective, the size of the combined market share of the SA is an important element, although it alone may not guarantee the success, to hop on the positive feedback curve where “success begats more success and failure breeds failure” (Shapiro and Varian 1999, p. 174). The threshold rule in network research (e.g., Watts and Dodds 2007) suggests that individuals or firms will switch from one SEC (say, SEC\_1) to another (say, SEC\_2) only when a specified fraction of their neighbors have adopted SEC\_2. Therefore, the scale of the combined market share could significantly affect the outcome of the SA. In general network settings, the “bigger is better” principle holds true, which indicates that the larger the combined market share at the time of the alliance, the higher the chance of the combined SECs reaching the tipping point.

However, we posit that NI could moderate the effects of timing on the SAs’ outcome. When the alliance occurs, the combined market share of the runners up can potentially reach a tipping point faster when the NI is medium than when it is either too high or too low. As articulated earlier, high NI gives rise to the formation of large clusters that band the consumers of the lead firm together; low NI results in small, fragmented clusters because of limited influencing activities. Relatively large combined market shares are therefore necessary to penetrate the large clusters when high NI is present and to promote influencing activities in the case of low NI.

Proposition 3. Because of its impact on the tipping point, NI moderates the relationship between the timing and the outcome of SAs by runner-up SECs.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1. Network Simulation

To provide qualitative insights into our theoretical propositions, we developed an agent-based computer simulation model (e.g., Carley 2002) in which the market consists of a collection of autonomous decision-making entities called agents, each of which individually assesses its situation and makes decisions on the basis of a set of rules (Carley 1996). Simulation experiments are a powerful mechanism for solving identified problems largely because they have the capacity and capability to manipulate the high complexity of the market environment and perform a wide range of analyses under different settings. Although other approaches (e.g., game-theory models) can be also adopted, agent-based simulations are particularly useful for our research context, which deals with agent (advertisers) behavior in response to environmental changes (e.g., SAs).

In our simulation model, online advertisers serve as agents who choose SECs based on a common decision rule (described later), and we investigate how advertisers make decisions in response to the alliance formed by the runner-up SECs. To perform these tasks, in the simulation models we included the following components: (1) the construction of influence networks, (2) the switching mechanism of advertisers, (3) the formation of market structures, (4) the activation of alliances, and (5) the assessment of the outcome. In what follows, we describe each of these elements in detail. To closely reflect the current competitive structure of the online-sponsored search engine advertising market, we assumed the triopoly market structure (i.e., Google, Yahoo, and MSN) with one dominant leader and two smaller runners-up.

Construction of Influence Networks. We identify an influence relationship when the choice of advertiser i directly influences the choice of advertiser j in choosing a SEC. The collective set of such influence relationships between the online advertisers can be represented as an influence network as a whole. To generate such influence networks, we followed the existing network studies and constructed three general types of network: scale free, small world, and random. The structure of influence networks is important for diffusing influence (Newman 2003, Watts and Dodds 2007) and is, therefore, likely to moderate the impact of NI on the outcome of the alliance. Bampo et al. (2008), in their simulation-based study, use these three types of digital network structures to assess the reach performance of viral marketing. In general, a scale-free network is characterized by its hierarchical and centralized structure, with its node connections (or degree) unevenly distributed and concentrated on certain key nodes (Barabási and Albert 1999). In contrast, the nodes in a small-world network are locally connected to their neighbors, with the presence of a few short paths to the ones in a distance (Watts and Strogatz 1998); the node connections of a random network are most evenly distributed (Erdos and Renyi 1960, Kwon et al. 2007). Based on previous studies (Watts and Strogatz 1998, Bampo et al. 2008), we generated and used influence networks consisting of 3,000 nodes<sup>16</sup> and 9,000 influence links on average (note that the actual degree distributions differ depending on network type).

We operationalized the switching mechanism as follows. Initially, every advertiser is assigned weighting factors $\dot { w } _ { i } ^ { N I }$ and $w _ { i } ^ { I P } ~ ( = 1 - w _ { i } ^ { N I } )$ , where $w _ { i } ^ { N I }$ is assumed to be normally distributed around a given average value of wNI. In the absence of specific empirical data for this factor, we considered the full variations of wNI. Note that a market with high wNI indicates that advertisers tend to rely heavily on others’ choices, while low wNI means advertisers are not much influenced by others’ decisions. At each simulation time, advertisers calculate the NI levels by using the latest status of their influence environment. In contrast, for the IP levels, advertisers are supposed to manage their IP values $( I P _ { i } ^ { S } )$ for each of the three SECs. The IP values are initially $( t = 0 )$ , based on a uniform distribution ranging from 0 to 10. Note that the maximum value (10) we assigned can be replaced by any other nonnegative value, but the use of different values does not affect the results because the advertiser will use the relative IP values, not the absolute values in his decision. The IP values are updated when the market environment changes (see the next sections). Overall, at each simulation time, an advertiser will continuously check the status of its NI and IP and reevaluate the probabilities of each SEC $( p _ { i } ^ { S } )$ on which the decision to keep the existing choice or switch to another SEC is made stochastically.

Formation of Market Structure. As the goal of the simulation is to investigate the impact of the alliance formed by two smaller competitors in competition with a dominant leader, we created market structures in which the lead SEC has 60% market share and the two runners up combined have 40% market share. In this study, we measured the market share based on the proportion of the total number of advertisers that have chosen each SEC. Although we focused on real market share data at the time of this study, we investigated a wide range of other market share combinations. The results for these various settings are reported in the Results section.

To construct the market structures of our interest, we did not simply distribute the choices over the advertisers, because this approach cannot reflect the exact switching mechanism through which to reach such “unbalanced” market structures (e.g., 60:40 split). Instead, we allowed the advertisers to reassess their choices repeatedly for some sufficient time steps, based on the switching mechanism described earlier. Then we identified market structures satisfying the market conditions of our interest (e.g., 60:40 split). Compared to “arbitrary” network construction, this approach is more realistic and comprehensive because it can minimize biases arising from random shocks, even though it is more resource and time consuming than arbitrary network construction. Furthermore, following other network studies (Hill 1997, Lee et al. 2006, Lee and Wilde 1980, Shapiro and Varian 1999), the simulation is designed to reflect the “winnertakes-all” principle by which a company winning the stake will dominate the market until the next round of competition emerges. As noted by Shapiro and Varian (1999), “positive feedback,” a potent force in the network economy, will lead to a situation where a winner will eventually take all at the end of a round of competition. This “winner-takes-all” market principle is one of the key properties of the digital economy (Shapiro and Varian 1999), and online sponsored search advertising is known as a “tippy” market with strong network effects (Shuen 2008). Reflecting these market properties, at each simulation time the IP values of the advertisers increased slightly by a small amount (according to the uniform distribution ranging from 0 to 1) for each SEC in proportion to current market shares. This resulted in more advertisers with higher IP values for the lead SEC, which more realistically reflects IP distributions in unbalanced market structures.

Activation and Assessment of the Alliance. When the lead SEC and the runner-up SECs reached a market split of our interest (e.g., 60:40), the alliance of the runner-up SECs was invoked, which subsequently changed the market environment and affected advertisers’ decisions. More specifically, if a horizontal alliance is formed by the two runners up, two major changes will occur in the simulated market. First, all advertisers that have used one of the two runnerup SECs are now assumed to become the clients of the combined SEC, which results in environmental changes in terms of the NI distributions for the advertisers. Second, the advertisers, encouraged by various reasons, including the increased search traffic from the combination of two search engine sites, are expected to increase their IP values for the combined SEC. We incorporated this phenomenon into our simulation by increasing the IP values according to the uniform distribution with the average percentage of $d I P$ (in the simulation we varied the percentages by 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%). To maintain the consistency of the advertisers’ behavioral characteristics, the decision weights $( w _ { i } ^ { N I }$ and $w _ { i } ^ { I P } )$ are assumed to stay constant during the entire processes of a simulation run, including the construction process of the unbalanced market structure and the process following the activation of the alliance.

With the winner-takes-all principle, in the long run the winner will eventually take all and the losers will experience a disastrous failure. Thus, to determine the results of the horizontal alliance formed by runnerup SECs, we allowed the simulation to continue for numerous iterations following the alliance so that sufficient switches and cascades could occur. Then we compared the market share of the combined SECs after the alliance with the market share at the time of the alliance. If the combined market share increased, we considered it a success; otherwise, it was a failure. To statistically measure the success probability of the alliance, we experimented with 100 runs with different random number generations for the same simulation setting, and captured the success probability based on the proportion of successes among those 100 trials. For example, the success probability is 0.7 if the alliance won 70 of 100 trials. For a detailed illustration of the simulation designs and process, we provide simulation pseudocodes in Appendix A.<sup>18</sup>

## 5.1. Size and Alliance Success

To investigate the likelihood that an alliance of smaller SECs could surpass the leader that has a size advantage, we devised three experimental scenarios that differ in terms of the presence of switching forces (e.g., wNI,<sup>19</sup> dIP, and cascades). In the first scenario, we assume that advertisers’ decision making is not influenced by that of others (wNI 0) and that the alliance does not change individuals’ IP toward the combined SEC (dIP 0). This scenario reflects the situation in which the leader and the runners up compete based on “size” only. In the second scenario, advertisers’ decision making is assumed to be under the influence of others (wNI > 0), but as in the first scenario, the alliance does not change individuals’ IP toward the combined SEC (dIP <sub>=</sub> 0). Finally, in the third scenario, the values of both wNI and dIP are set as positive. In other words, in contrast to the first two scenarios, advertisers in this market do care about what their peer advertisers choose, and they incorporate such influences into their own decision making (e.g., wNI > 0). In addition, this scenario assumes that the alliance shifts advertisers’ preferences toward the combined SEC (dIP > 0). In these scenarios, we assume that a leader maintains the status quo and does not actively pursue SAs with a runner-up SEC. To examine whether the alliance wins or loses, simulation experiments were performed 100 times for each of the possible combined market shares, starting from 33% to 50%, with 1% increments. To generalize our findings, we employed a wide range of values with respect to wNI and dIP. However, for a simple illustration, we report the results for one specific setting, where wNI and dIP are set to 0.4 and 10%, respectively. In this setting, we found optimal outcomes with respect to alliance success. The results derived from other combinations will follow later.

## 5.2. NI and Alliance Success

Figure 5 presents the effect of NI on the outcomes of SAs formed by runner-up SECs. Proposition 2 asserts that an alliance by two “underdogs” will most likely succeed when the NI is medium. Reflecting real-world market shares, a 60:40 split was considered between the lead SEC and the combined runners up. In addition, the experiments considered all three different network structures. For each network structure, we employed a wide range of wNI, while setting dIP as 10% and 15%.<sup>22</sup>

Overall, the results strongly support Proposition 2; for scale-free networks, success probabilities exhibited nonlinear patterns (inverted U-shape) with maximum outcomes found when the NI is midrange (e.g., wNI <sub>=</sub> 03 <sub>−</sub> 05), rather than on the high or low end of the continuum. For small-world and random networks, optimal probabilities were observed between low and medium NIs (e.g., wNI  03). The findings related to these nonlinear patterns will be discussed in great depth in the next section. Regarding the effects of network structures, we found the highest success probabilities in the scale-free network and the lowest in the small-world network. Interestingly, in the presence of high NI (e.g., wNI > 07), success probabilities in the small-world and random networks become almost zero, whereas the scale-free network still shows moderate success probabilities (e.g., 10 20%). The difference can be explained by the presence of key influentials that seldom change their choices in the scale-free network (Oh and Jeon 2007). Among the three structures, the small-world network is least sensitive to changes in the NI, and the random structure is the most reactive to such changes. We found that cascading proceeds slowly and only locally in the small-world network. To validate insensitivity in the small-world network, we constructed various types of small-world networks that differ in terms of the proportion of link rewiring (10%–40%), but the results were consistent regardless of the degree of “small worldness.” One reason for this insensitivity is that small-world networks, by nature, consist of large homogeneous clusters choosing the same SECs, which tends to encourage the same states among the existing members.

## 5.3. Alliance Timing and Success Probabilities

We assessed how NI moderates the relationship between the two smaller competitors’ combined market share at the time of the alliance and success probabilities (see Figure 6). To focus on this relationship, we fixed dIP to a constant value (similar patterns were found despite the variations in dIP values). We show the results for Propositions 3 based only on the scale-free network structure because, as shown in Figure 1, the shape of influence networks appears to be of the scale-free structure. However, to ensure the generalizability of our findings, we conducted this experiment on other network structures as well, but obtained similar results. We found that the runner-up SECs could achieve the same level of success with a much smaller combined market share in the presence of the medium (wNI  04) NI. However, when the NI is high (wNI 08), the runner-up SECs would need much larger combined market share to reach the same success level. When NI is low (wNI 02), the likelihood of alliance success falls between the two other cases.

Interestingly, when NI is either low or medium, a small variation in the combined market share at the time of alliance could make a big difference for the likelihood of success. For example, when NI is medium $( w N I = 0 . 4 )$ , an alliance with a combined market share of 40% has a roughly 63% chance of success, but the likelihood increases rapidly to 83% with only 1% increase in the combined market share. In other words, 1% difference in the combined market share could result in as high as a 20% difference in the outcome. For this specific setting, where NI is medium $( w N I = 0 . 4 )$ , a combined market share of 43% at the time of alliance tends to ensure that the alliance will win and take all the market with 100% of success probability.

Following the existing literature (e.g., Shapiro and Varian 1999), we denote “tipping points” as the threshold points above which the combined SEC will surely become a winner. To further explore the tipping points, we conducted several comprehensive experiments. The goal was to identify tipping points (or the “minimum” combined market shares) in response to the diverse market conditions that result from variations in wNI, dIP, and network structures. Figure 7 depicts, for each network structure, surfaces rendered by a range of minimum combined market share, which is a function of wNI and dIP. These surfaces show collectively the effects of NI and IP while graphically displaying the minimum market share necessary to outcompete the dominant leader. Overall, the minimum tipping point is found in markets with medium wNI and high dIP. Yet tipping requires more market share as wNI increases and dIP decreases. In terms of network structures, the surface of the small-world network exhibits a flat pattern, whereas those of the scale-free and random networks show hills (highest tipping points) and valleys (lowest tipping points). Interestingly, when wNI is high and dIP is low, high tipping points (e.g., greater than 50%) are required in the random network, as reflected in the highest hills. In contrast, in the same market environment, much lower tipping points are necessary in the scale-free network.

## 6. Discussion and Implications

In markets where network effects govern competition, size (e.g., market share) is often cited as the attribute with the most crucial impact on winning. One of the findings of this study suggests that this “size rules” view may not always hold true in certain circumstances. Our results reveal that a pairing of two smaller competitors could overtake the dominance of a market leader that has a larger share of the market than these two “underdogs” combined. When fully utilizing “switching forces” in terms of materializing NI, amplifying IP, and eliciting global cascades, an alliance with a combined market share of 40% could effectively compete with a market leader that has a 60% market share. In this light, size is important, but it alone might not be sufficient to “build the market,” as suggested by the “vanilla” network effect theory.

However, nothing can be earned effortlessly in competitive markets. The union of smaller competitors does not in itself ensure success. Although size alone does not warrant success, the alliance might need a “minimum” threshold scale at the time of the union to utilize switching forces to its full capacity. For example, an alliance with a combined market share of less than 30% is unlikely to produce sufficient resources or capacity to capitalize on switching forces. Unless the alliance can substantially improve advertisers’ IPs toward the combined company and/or vigorously stimulate NI-driven switching, the combined effort to bring about a successful “coup” might not be easily rewarded. For example, as shown in Figure 4, although the NI/cascading combination $( d I P = 0 )$ can still empower the alliance with a smaller market share to disrupt the dominance of the leader, the absence of $d I P$ requires a much higher combined market share at the time of the alliance, compared with the case when all three switching forces are activated. Therefore, top executives involved in this type of alliance should constantly ascertain that all three switching forces are “up and running.”

One way to accomplish this task is to find a mechanism that triggers a virtuous cycle between switching forces themselves. For example, a substantial increase in IP toward the alliance due to its marketing or technological efforts (e.g., enhanced features and services, competitive pricing schemes, etc.)

could motivate many advertisers to switch to the combined SEC. Such switching behaviors might magnify NI and adjust the neighbors’ choice distribution. As a result of influencing, many other advertisers might also be tempted to switch to the alliance. These concerted efforts will further accelerate the distance and the speed of cascading, which, in turn, will give rise to more NI- and IP-initiated switching in favor of the alliance. However, if one of these switching components fails, switching might occur slowly and only locally. In fact, if the alliance does not provide users with incentives to switch, some of its existing users might migrate to the market leader. Therefore, to increase the likelihood of success, an alliance of smaller competitors should promptly consider undertaking aggressive strategies aimed at promoting a massive switching before it is too late. Influential targeting is one such strategy, which we will discuss later.

Another finding of this study shows that a nonlinear relationship exists between the NI (wNI) and an SA’s outcome; the combined runners up have the best chance of success when NI is medium. Regardless of network structures, success probabilities are almost negligible when NI is either high or low. This finding is noteworthy and might stimulate discussions among scholars in the field of network science. To gain additional insights into this pattern, we looked more closely at the simulated influence network. Figures 9(a) and 9(b) show the node distribution and the links of networks, prior to the alliance, for two different levels of NI (wNI 04 and wNI 08). The three tones of nodes each represent the users of the three SECs; the white nodes represent the users of the lead SEC, and the black and gray nodes correspond to the clients of the runner-up SECs. Note that the overall ratio between the number of white nodes (e.g., 60%) and the total number of black and gray nodes combined (e.g., 40%) is identical in Figures 9(a) and 9(b). When NI is high (see Figure 9(b)), online advertisers that are attached to the lead SEC tend to assimilate themselves, form in large clusters, and occupy the central locations in the network. Consequently, the users of the combined runner-up SECs cannot easily make changes in the NI environment throughout the network while experiencing difficulties in entering into this core network cluster (see the insets of the figures). This local stability, driven by the collective desire of the clients of the lead SEC, exists at the core spectra of the network and accounts for why the success probabilities for the conjoined SECs are suboptimal in the presence of high NI.

The findings of the experiment also suggest that the combined market share at the time of alliance formation is also important for success. Interestingly, this is more likely to be the case when the NI is either too high or too low. With a medium NI, however, a relatively low combined market share could possibly enable the combined SECs to reach the tipping point, on which virtuous cycles of cascades may then occur. Regarding the network structures’ moderating effects, it can be said that small-world networks, in general, require the largest combined market share to succeed because of the presence of high local clusters within these networks. If the influence network structure follows the small-world formation, it appears that managers of the alliance should first focus on the improvement of IP values rather than on leveraging NI.

One noteworthy observation is that according to our experiments in a triopoly market with medium NI, a combined market share below 35% is unlikely to generate global cascades. The crucial battle zone is therefore between 35% and 50%. Firms with market shares in this range are likely to face critical prosper-or-wither junctures, where even market share increases of 1% could make as much as a 20% difference in the likelihood of their success. In November 2005, Google signed a \$1 billion revenue-sharing alliance with Time Warner’s AOL when Google had a 47% market share. As a result of the alliance, AOL brought an additional 7–9% market share to Google. Experts note that this deal enabled Google to slant the competitive balance in this market. Since this alliance, Google has been able to harness the virtuous cycle and emerge as a winner that is now on the verge of taking it all. In fact, some industry experts estimate that this \$1 billion alliance was actually worth \$4.45 billion, asserting that the alliance was the turning point for Google’s success (Shuen 2008). How would the competitive landscape have changed if either Yahoo or Microsoft had teamed up with AOL? Although it is difficult to answer this question because of the uncertainties brought about by this turbulent market, it can be said that the online search market would likely have been more balanced than it is now.

Finally, despite the potential of winning, competing with a large company is always difficult. Furthermore, the leader in competition might not stand still and await its demise. Therefore, an alliance formed by smaller competitors should put aggressive strategies into action so that they can boost the likelihood of success. Influential targeting<sup>24</sup> is one such effective strategy, because influentials could play a vital role in mobilizing the switching forces. For example, influentials would significantly affect the influencing dynamics and switching behaviors if a central node surrounded by its neighbors relied heavily on the influentials’ decision relative to that of noninfluentials (Katz and Lazarsfeld 1955, Grewal et al. 2000). Moreover, the decisions made by influentials are likely to influence the IP of noninfluentials toward a particular company. Most importantly, influentials are typically connected with many nodes and occupy central locations in a network and, therefore, play a crucial role in the acceleration and diffusion of cascades.

Based on measures of centrality (Freeman 1991), we designed two influential targeting mechanisms and explored their impacts on the likelihood of alliance success. Degree-based targeting (DBT) recognizes influentials based on the number of ties that a particular node (advertiser) has, whereas betweenness-based targeting (BBT)<sup>25</sup> does this by counting the number of geodesic paths that pass through that node. Although both types of centrality measures denote the structural importance of a node within a network, the way that a node’s importance is conceptualized and measured differs. Experiments on influential targeting were performed with the focus on the effectiveness and efficiency of these two types of targeting approaches. Our results indicate that for runner-up SECs, influential targeting, whether based on the DBT or the BBT, is a viable strategy that can increase an SA’s likelihood of success (see Figure 10). For example, when 5% of the population is targeted as influentials, DBT and BBT can increase the combined SEC’s market share by 17% and 10%, respectively. However, DBT is more effective than BBT, because it has a greater capacity to elicit large cascades and increase alliance success. DBT is structurally more capable than BBT, because cascading itself arises between nodes that are directly connected. As a result, cascades will propagate rapidly and globally, thereby substantially increasing the likelihood of alliance success. In summary, our results suggest that an alliance with size disadvantage can still disrupt the dominance of the leader in certain situations without the use of influential targeting. However, influential targeting can help the alliance increase its chances of outcompeting the leader even further.

However, when efficiency, as measured by increased success probabilities divided by the total degree of the targeted nodes, is factored into the equation, BBT outperforms DBT (see Figure 10(b)). DBT would necessitate more resources than BBT, because the amount of resources typically needed to target individuals or firms is positively related to the sphere of influence these actors have in the network.

For instance, advertisers with high degree-centrality are those that have established knowledge, experience, and brand recognition. Therefore, they are barely influenced and rarely alter their preferences (Oh and Jeon 2007). The BBT strategy might be a more ideal option than the DBT strategy when resource constraints preclude firms from taking proactive strategic actions and environments are relatively stable with limited network effects. However, the DBT is an aggressive strategy that is deemed essential when the combined runners up are under time pressure because the market leader is scaling up rapidly. In fact, timing and targeting should be thought of as a continuum rather than as independent actions. When the combined market share is insufficient to reach the tipping point, managers should consider undertaking more aggressive targeting strategies.

## 7. Limitations and Future Research

Our study has limitations that can be overcome by future research. Despite our best efforts to simulate real-world situations, it can be said that simulated worlds cannot be the same as the real world. Our choice of simulation variables is far from exhaustive. Like that of other simulation studies, the goal of this study was to better understand qualitatively the nature of competition as shaped by digital systems. Therefore, an empirical study is necessary to validate our results and to provide more accurate assessments for some of the variables used in the simulation model. For example, how do consumers balance between NI and IP when making economic decisions? Which markets exhibit high NI? In addition, we must make an effort to identify the shape of influence networks, even though a full-scale empirical study might not be feasible (Watts and Dodds 2007). Survey questionnaires could provide detailed insight into the extent of NI and the formation of network structures.

It should also be noted that our assessments are based purely on the network perspective; we do not claim that our approach is the most comprehensive. Understanding other applicable theoretical frameworks may help us better comprehend the nature of digital competition. For example, a game theoretic approach might offer more dynamic insights into competitive actions and market equilibrium in the digital market. Moreover, the outcome of SAs can be determined by many other factors, including organizational fit, costs, and postalliance management. Therefore, a study that identifies critical success factors for SAs among Internet rivals could provide a more pluralistic view regarding the performance of competitive actions in the digital market. Finally, our study can be further extended by investigating the effects of NI in more competitive market settings than the triopoly structure focused on in this study. For example, a similar study could be conducted with the absence of a dominant market leader and the presence of a large number of competitors in the market.

## 8. Conclusion

It is widely known that competition is a multipart, complex phenomenon. Yet it is becoming increasingly complex in the digital market in which influence networks and traffic-oriented alliances have become an integral part of the ecosystem. Building on the general notion that economic decision making is often influenced by the social context in which actors are embedded, we sought to investigate, using agentbased experiments, the outcome of such alliances between two smaller SECs in competition with a dominant market leader in settings where advertisers’ decision making is based on a combination of NI and IP. Although we focused primarily on the online advertising market, our results have broader implications for other industries wherein consumers’ economic actions are influenced by others’ actions and similar competitive market structures exist.

The experiments produce several noteworthy findings. Contrary to the conventional view, our results suggest that despite size disadvantage, an alliance of two smaller competitors could disrupt a leader’s dominance and possibly result in success. Although important, size alone may not define the nature of digital competition. Yet nothing can be rewarded effortlessly in competitive markets. Such alliances should ascertain that they utilize various switching forces to their full capacity. Apart from investigating the role of size in digital competition, we also examined when the alliance could result in optimal outcomes, in terms of the extent of NI and the combined market share at the time of formation. In contrast to the conventional view, nonlinearity exists between the extent of NI and the likelihood of alliance success. An extremely high or low level influencing decision makers appears to penalize the alliance of smaller competitors. Moreover, NI was also found to moderate the relationship between the minimum combined market shares and likelihood of alliance success. These findings provide relevant implications for executives whose businesses are “under the influence” of the Internet and Web 2.0 platforms. Although considerable work remains to be carried out, we hope that conceptual underpinnings and methodological trajectories presented in this paper will enhance our understanding of complex digital competition and lay foundations for future research in this field.
