# The Impact of Capabilities and Prior Investments on Online Channel Commitment and Performance

JOHN HULLAND, MICHAEL R. WADE, AND KERSI D. ANTIA

JOHN HULLAND is an Associate Professor joined the faculty of the Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh in 2001. Prior to that, he was a faculty member in the Richard Ivey School of Business at the University of Western Ontario in Canada (1989–2001). Dr. Hulland received his Ph.D. in Marketing from MIT in 1990. His research focuses on three broad areas: (1) understanding and managing the relationship between marketing resources and firm performance, (2) improving the effectiveness of marketing and sales group interactions within firms, and (3) applying causal modeling techniques in strategic contexts. Dr. Hulland’s research has been published in a wide range of top journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Marketing, Marketing Science, Strategic Management Journal, and Organization Science.

He received his Ph.D. from the Ivey Business School at the University of Western Ontario. His current research focuses on the strategic use of information systems for sustainable competitive advantage. His research has appeared in journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Strategic Management Journal, and Communications of the ACM. Professor Wade is coauthor of the textbook Information Systems Today: Canadian Edition, and has coauthored two e-commerce casebooks.

KERSI D. ANTIA is an Assistant Professor of Marketing at the University of Wisconsin– Madison. He has also taught at the University of Western Ontario, Washington University in St. Louis, the University of Southern California, and the Oral School for the Deaf in Calcutta. He holds an M.S. in management and information systems from Clarkson University, and a Ph.D. in Marketing from the University of Southern California. Dr. Antia’s research focuses on the governance and impact of Web-based technologies on interfirm relationships (alliances, franchise agreements, distribution channels). His research has appeared in Journal of Marketing, Strategic Management Journal, and MIT Sloan Management Review.

ABSTRACT: Attracted by the promise of greater market exposure and increased revenues, firms across a wide variety of industries have undertaken significant investments in online channels. However, while some firms’ entire business models revolve around this initiative, others have made only limited commitments to online channel ventures. What accounts for these marked differences in commitment to online initiatives, and do firms reap the performance benefits of increased levels of commitment? Furthermore, how do firms’ internal and external capabilities affect their propensity to establish and succeed with online channel ventures? Drawing on marketing, innovation, and information systems perspectives, along with insights from the resource-based view of the firm, we propose an integrative conceptual framework that helps answer these questions. We ground our hypotheses in the context of retailers’ online channel development efforts, and test our conceptual framework with data collected via a Web-based survey of 550 retailers. We find evidence of significant positive returns to investments in online channels. Furthermore, we observe the divergent effects of different sets of capabilities on commitment and performance. Importantly, although we find that the direct effect of firms’ information systems capabilities on online performance appears to be negative, the indirect effect (mediated by commitment) is positive. Our study also examines the impact of firms’ established distribution channels on levels of commitment to, and performance of, the online channel. We find that firms’ established distribution channels act as double-edged swords, with divergent effects on commitment and performance. We also find evidence of diminishing returns to commitment as a function of established distribution presence, thereby suggesting that the rewards of commitment do not accrue equally to all firms.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: B2C e-commerce, commitment, distribution channel, e-tail, IT capabilities, online firm performance, resource-based view, retailing.

IN TODAY’S FAST-MOVING COMPETITIVE ENVIRONMENT, firms are under constant pressure to increase their market exposure and revenue-earning potential. Partly in response to such pressures, firms are experimenting increasingly with a wide variety of online channel formats,<sup>1</sup> with the objective of providing customers with more contact points. The stakes are high: while success of the online channel—defined as a firms’ use of the Internet as a means of connecting with the market—may lead to greater sales, profits, and innovation, mistakes can lead to the complete breakdown of a firm’s carefully crafted business strategy. Thus, it becomes important for firms to understand the drivers of online channel performance in order to maximize the effectiveness and efficiency of their online investments [91].

There has been strong interest in the information systems (IS) literature on the vital issue of how information technologies (ITs) contribute to the success of online channel ventures, both at the level of the firm [80] as well as the online initiative [94]. Two streams of research predominate. The first adopts a tactical view of online commerce at the individual level of analysis [1]. This stream is focused on issues such as attracting and retaining visitors to a Web site, buyer behavior, and consumer satisfaction [56, 57, 75]. The second stream of research explores the role of IT investments on the performance of online initiatives [28, 107, 109]. A close review of these perspectives reveals a recognition for the critical role of the interaction between IT resources and other firm assets and capabilities [82, 92]. Exploratory research has found evidence for the complementary value of IT and non-IT resources in achieving firm-level benefits [93, 108], and enhancing business value in the context of online commerce [106]. This emerging literature argues that firms must manage the relationship between IT and non-IT resources, along with their commitments to each, if they are to realize performance gains that result from integrating “bricks-and-mortar” channels<sup>2</sup> with emergent online channels [60, 96].

The evolving literature on fostering innovation [17, 38] would suggest that the performance of an online channel is likely to hinge on the firm’s commitment to that channel—that is, the proportion of the firm’s tangible resources devoted to building and maintaining that channel. However, firms display significant heterogeneity in their commitments to online channel formats [18, 44]. While some firms implement their entire business models using the online channel alone (e.g., Amazon.com), others choose to maintain little more than a rudimentary presence with the online channel format. In the face of such wide variations in commitment, three key questions arise.

First, does a firm’s commitment to the online channel impact the performance thereof? As pointed out by Ghosh [40], it is difficult for most companies to estimate accurately the returns on any investment they may make in online channels. Although intuitively appealing, the link between resource commitment and online channel performance has never been examined, to the best of our knowledge. Yet, as was made abundantly clear in the bursting of the “Internet bubble,” misallocated resources can even threaten firm survival.

Second, what are the drivers of commitment and channel performance? We will demonstrate that the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm provides a powerful lens through which to examine this question. Firm resources have been found to be strong drivers of superior firm performance (e.g., [16, 64, 107]). Differences in performance arise from heterogeneity in firms’ resource portfolios, and their commitment of these resources to strategic implementation. The literature in IS has identified the potential of IT resources to support firm performance, but it is not known whether this effect extends to online channel ventures [80, 109]. Furthermore, more research is called for regarding the relative effects of IT versus non-IT resources [96, 106, 108].

A third as yet underexplored question is whether firms’ prior investments in traditional channel formats help or hinder their levels of commitment and online channel performance. Although much has been made of the potential synergies arising from seamless integration of online and traditional channel formats [52], examples of such synergies are few and far between. Instead, the prospect of dysfunctional channel conflict and customer confusion looms far larger, as the online channel format can cannibalize sales and profits from traditional distribution channels. According to one survey, 66 percent of business managers cited potential channel conflict as the biggest cause for concern with respect to their online channel development efforts [41]. In a similar vein, Geyskens et al. concluded that “any firm that sets up an Internet channel should expect to lose at least some of the goodwill of its entrenched channels” [37, p. 116]. Gaining a better understanding of this issue is crucial, as very few firms have the ability to design their go-to-market strategy starting with a “blank slate.” More typically, firms deploying online channels do so in addition to traditional channel formats, and must wrestle with the contentious issue of integrating legacy systems with the online channels [20, 101].

In response to the above questions, this study sets out to accomplish three objectives. First, we examine the relationship between firms’ commitment to online channels and consequent online channel performance. Second, we provide a conceptual framework linking firms’ resource deployments with both their commitments to, and performance of, their online channel undertakings. A key component of this objective is to examine the relationship between IT and non-IT capabilities, along with their direct and indirect effects on online performance. Finally, we examine the vital issue of how the legacy of an established distribution network may help or hinder firms’ commitment to online distribution channels, as well as the performance outcomes achieved by these online channels. In doing so, this research advances the IS field by emphasizing the role of motivation (as reflected in the firms’ commitment to their online channels) in addition to ability inherent in firms’ resources and capabilities. Absent the former, the latter may even result in dysfunctional channel outcomes.

We ground and test our conceptual framework in the context of retailing, specifically retailers’ recent and ongoing efforts to develop online channel formats. Accounting for \$1.1 trillion [14] and 15 million jobs [15], the retail sector is a vital contributor to the North American economy. The retailing industry can be characterized as brutally competitive and fast moving. In such a daunting operating environment, the search for a new source of competitive advantage is an ongoing quest, driven in large part by investments in new technologies. For example, many retailers have recently seen online commerce as a means through which to facilitate enhanced service levels, garner greater efficiencies, and increase market exposure and revenues [13, 110]. Perhaps most important, considerable variation exists in the extent to which retailers have made use of traditional “bricks-and-mortar” outlets. While some retailers maintain vast networks of physical retail outlets, others have attempted to exploit the possibilities afforded by technology, instead choosing to adopt a “pure play” online presence. For all these reasons, the retail sector provides an appropriate empirical setting in which to test our proposed conceptual framework.

## Theoretical Background

## Definitions

THE TERM ONLINE CHANNEL REFERS TO FIRMS’ use of the Internet as a means of connecting with the market. Such use may be limited to brochureware, at one extreme, to fullblown transactional capabilities, at the other. The performance of the online channel refers to the extent to which the firm’s stated objectives are met by the online format [36]. The present study focuses on the gains to a firm specifically attributable to the introduction of an online channel. By commitment, we refer to the proportion of the firm’s tangible resources devoted to building and maintaining the online channel [38]. Resources, in turn, comprise the assets and capabilities that are useful to the firm in responding to and/or anticipating changes in the competitive environment.

Although assets and capabilities together define the set of resources available to a firm, there is growing recognition that competitive advantage often depends on the firm’s superior deployment of capabilities [19, 25, 26, 32, 65, 70, 87, 92]. Capabilities are typically accumulated slowly over time, are strictly idiosyncratic to the individual firm, and are often very difficult for other firms to imitate [7, 29]. While assets (such as financial instruments, patents, infrastructure, personnel) can serve as inputs to a process or as the outputs of a process [90, 92], capabilities transform inputs into outputs of greater worth [19, 31, 83].<sup>3</sup>

Some scholars have acknowledged the difficulty of identifying resources that are relevant to a particular empirical context, but that are also generalizable across diverse settings (e.g., [45, 96]). One solution to this problem is to define resources at a relatively high level of abstraction. Another is to define relevant resources in context-specific terms. In this paper, we attempt to do both. The discussion of relevant capabilities that follows is set in the specific context of retailers’ online channel development efforts and the performance of online retail channels.<sup>4</sup> In doing so, we are able to explicate the capabilities relevant to online channel development and performance, even while providing insights applicable beyond the immediate retail context to online channels in general. Furthermore, we describe capabilities defined at a relatively high level of abstraction (e.g., we talk about a general IT technical skills capability rather than a capability in dealing with Java programming, a legacy systems capability, etc.).

## Research Model

THE IS LITERATURE HAS RECOGNIZED the importance of the interaction between IT and non-IT capabilities to support the online initiative [106]. To shed additional light on this critical issue, we propose a conceptual framework (represented in Figure 1) that synthesizes relevant insights from the rapidly evolving literature on innovation and the RBV. Specifically, the literature on innovation helps identify resource commitment to the online channel as a critical driver of online channel performance; we then use the RBV literature to identify specific capabilities relevant to both online commitment and online performance.

The RBV argues that firms possess resources, a subset of which enable them to achieve competitive advantage, and a subset of those that lead to superior long-term performance [7]. Resources that are valuable and rare, and whose benefits can be appropriated by the owning (or controlling) firm provide it with a temporary competitive advantage. That advantage can be sustained over longer time periods to the extent that the firm is able to protect against resource imitation, transfer, or substitution [96]. Empirical studies using the RBV have found that the effects of firm resources on performance can be significant (e.g., [9, 67, 68, 103]).

## Hypotheses

## Online Commitment and Online Performance

INVESTMENTS IN TECHNOLOGICAL INNOVATIONS represent one important means through which firms can achieve competitive advantage [69]. However, the extent to which firms commit themselves to such investments often varies. For example, Grewal et al. note that firms’ commitments can range from spending “concerted effort and resources to streamline their electronic market operations” to carrying out “virtually no business in the electronic market” [44, p. 19]. Ghemawat [38] emphasizes the central role played by commitment in determining the performance of organizations. The single-minded focus of the firm on developing the innovation lends a sense of importance and value [88], leading to the success of the innovation [23, 100, 102]. A key part of this commitment is reflected in the extent to which the firm’s tangible resources are devoted to the development and later maintenance of these electronic channels.

Preliminary work on the link between online commitment and online performance suggests that it is likely to be strongly positive. For example, Morgan-Thomas and Bridgewater [71] found that greater financial investment in Internet development and implementation resulted in superior firm performance in an exporting context. Furthermore, the addition of an online channel has been found to both enhance customer loyalty [98] and increase customer purchase frequency [95]. Wallace et al. note that “multiple points of contact make it easier for a multiple channel customer’s satisfaction to manifest itself in the form of loyalty” [98, p. 257]. Greater loyalty, coupled with increased purchase frequency, should be reflected in superior performance. Furthermore, the addition of an online channel provides broader market coverage than established channels, leading to the likely addition of new customers. Because the number of contact points (both with existing and new customers) will increase in proportion to the commitment the firm makes to its online channel, it follows that

Hypothesis 1: The greater a firm’s commitment to online channels, the higher its online channel performance.

## Capabilities

The RBV argues that firms possess capabilities that enable them to achieve competitive advantage, in turn leading to superior long-term performance [7, 10, 50, 76, 99]. Empirical studies of firm performance using the RBV have found differences not only between firms in the same industry [47] but also within the narrower confines of groups within industries [22]. This suggests that the effects of individual, firm-specific capabilities on performance can be significant [64]. Drawing on RBV theory, IS researchers have started to examine how IT resources—either alone or in conjunction with non-IT resources—affect firm performance (e.g., [6, 81, 84]).

The capabilities possessed by a firm can be thought of as belonging to one of two sets—those that primarily influence or respond to market demand and those that reflect the firm’s internal operations [31]. Externally focused capabilities place an emphasis on anticipating market requirements and creating durable customer relationships, and are thus more closely associated with market demand. In contrast, internally focused capabilities emphasize IT infrastructure, information management, and operational efficiencies. This distinction between externally and internally focused capabilities is not new. For example, Day [25] has suggested that highly successful firms tend to possess (and maintain a balance between) both internally and externally focused capabilities [95]. In a similar vein, Slotegraaf et al. [87] demonstrated that different financial returns result from the deployment of externally versus internally focused resources. Finally, studies have shown that some firms prefer to emphasize exploitation of existing knowledge and abilities (through use of internally focused capabilities) while others pay more attention to the exploration of new opportunities and ideas (via externally oriented capabilities; e.g., see [39, 61, 62, 63, 74]).

In this paper, we focus on three critical, externally oriented capabilities—market sensing, brand management, and customer service. The ability to gather, interpret, and use market information (i.e., market sensing); the ability to manage brands; and the ability to deliver superior customer service are all important externally oriented success factors in the retailing business. Within the rubric of internally focused capabilities, we look specifically at IT skills and IT-enabled cost efficiency as important determinants of online commitment and performance. In the context of online channel development, the existing IT skill capability is likely to play a significant role. Moreover, retail firms will be competitively more successful when able to efficiently manage costs. Although grounded here in the retail context, these same capabilities have been identified as applicable to nonretailing contexts as well (e.g., [3, 9, 16, 25, 90]).

Before turning to a more detailed discussion of individual firm capabilities, it is important to recognize that they can—and, in fact, are likely to—have different effects on online commitment and online performance. A firm’s assessment of its own capabilities will guide the extent to which it commits to (or steers away from committing to) an online presence. In general, capabilities that are more fungible (i.e., they can be transferred across different markets or channels with relative ease) should play a bigger role than other capabilities in affecting a firm’s online commitment, because they can be expected by the firm to more quickly contribute to firm success [54]. For example, Anand and Delios [4] found that technological expertise (which is internally focused) is highly fungible across national borders, whereas marketing expertise (externally focused) is not, due to the fact that the latter requires significant tailoring to the new market.

One implication of this is that firms with superior internally focused capabilities may prefer to enter new markets quickly, relative to those possessing superior externally focused capabilities, because the former are likely to be more fungible than the latter. Thus, we expect internally focused and externally focused capabilities to have distinct effects on online commitment, with strong internally focused capabilities tending to positively impact the firm’s commitment to online operations.

In contrast, once a given level of commitment has been made, the capabilities possessed or controlled by the firm will then help (or hinder) in translating this commitment into online success. In this case, the deployment and use of capabilities is critical in determining success versus failure. For example, Slotegraaf et al. [87] found that when a firm is better able to deploy its resources, performance is enhanced. In a different context, Capron and Hulland [16] noted a relationship between superior redeployment of marketing resources and firm performance following merger and acquisition activity. We now hypothesize the likely impact of each specific capability on online commitment and online performance.

## Market Sensing

Day proposes that market sensing involves the “processes for gathering, interpreting, and using market information,” and that firms exhibiting a superior market-sensing capability are “more systematic, thoughtful, and anticipatory than . . . other firms” [25, p. 43]. In this paper, two facets of market sensing are emphasized—environmental scanning and market responsiveness. Environmental scanning focuses on the ability of the firm to learn about customers, competitors, and channel members, and to thereby recognize external opportunities and threats. Market responsiveness involves the dissemination of a firm’s intelligence across departments, the organization’s response to that learning [55], and the ability to react quickly to market changes [9, 103].

It is important to recognize that a firm with a superior market-sensing capability will not necessarily be any more or less likely than other firms to make a commitment to online operations. While it may do a better job of monitoring and responding to the market, such a firm will only invest in an online presence if the market is perceived to be ready for such an initiative. Indeed, if the marketplace is seen to resist a shift to online distribution, firms with superior market sensing are likely to be slower than their less sensitive counterparts in making an online commitment. Thus, we predict no relationship between market sensing and online commitment.

In contrast, once the decision to go online has been made, for a given level of commitment the firm with a superior market-sensing capability will be better able to adapt its online offering to changing customer needs, and will be more responsive to new competitor initiatives. This greater adaptability should in turn translate into higher online performance:

Hypothesis 2: Firms’ superior market-sensing capability will be associated with higher online performance.

## Brand Management

Brand management is defined as the capability to develop, support, and maintain strong brands. While brand assets can lead to superior performance (e.g., [3, 16, 51, 53, 90]), the ability to manage brands over time is also important. Brand resources represent both an historical and ongoing relationship with external customers.

It is not at all clear that firms with well-honed brand management capabilities will necessarily be more likely to exhibit greater levels of commitment to the development of an online channel. On one hand, firms that have a superior ability to manage brands should be able with greater ease to deploy their brands across both traditional and online channels. Furthermore, strong brands—characterized by high consumer awareness and well-established and attractive brand images—make it easier for firms to encourage existing consumers to make online purchases. However, the introduction of an online presence also creates the potential for new customers to encounter the brand for the first time. These fragile relationships must be carefully monitored and strengthened by the firm if it wants to establish the same sort of strong ties it has had with its more traditional customers. This means that firms must be careful to integrate their brand management activities across a wider range of channels in order to avoid developing inconsistent images for different customer groups.

On balance, then, while a superior brand management capability may encourage some firms to make a strong commitment to online operations, others will prefer to focus on deepening their presence in traditional distribution channels. As a result, we do not predict a relationship between brand management capability and online commitment.

For a given level of online commitment, however, firms that have a superior ability to manage brands should be able with greater ease to deploy their brands across both traditional and innovative channels. Ceteris paribus, the stronger the firm’s brand management capability, the less a firm will have to invest to develop online brand awareness and brand image. This translates into lower costs associated with the introduction of online operations for firms with superior brands. The combination of greater revenue-garnering potential and lower costs would suggest that firms that are most able to effectively deploy their brand assets—that is, those with a superior brand management capability—should be able to achieve superior online performance versus their major competitors. Thus,

Hypothesis 3: Firms’ superior brand management capability will be associated with higher online performance.

## Customer Service

The ability to provide superior customer service has been found to have a strong link to increased overall firm performance (e.g., [5, 11]). In the online context, for any given level of online commitment we would expect a superior ability to deliver desired customer service to result in higher online performance. This can come about, for example, as a result of being able to provide enhanced value to customers (via unique product–service combinations), through the deepening of customer relationships, and by gaining customer insights during service interactions [49, 95, 98].

However, the impact of a superior customer service capability on a firm’s commitment to online operations is less clear. On one hand, adding an online arm could increase consumers’ interactions with the firm [48], allowing it to deepen already strong relationships with its customers through continued superior service delivery. Furthermore, customers satisfied with the firm’s delivery in the physical world may exert pressure on the firm to add online operations. On the other hand, customer needs and demands are often quite different via the Internet than in a face-to-face context. Furthermore, the ability to deliver superior service in-store is not easy to transfer or apply to the online context. These latter considerations are likely to diminish the attractiveness of going online for many firms with a superior customer service capability. Thus,

Hypothesis 4: Firms’ superior customer service capability will be associated with higher online performance.

So far, our discussion of externally focused capabilities has emphasized their positive impact on online performance, as well as the notion that such capabilities are not expected to have any significant effect on firms’ commitment to online channels. In contrast, we expect firms’ internally focused capabilities to impact both their levels of commitment to, and performance of, their online channel. The rationale for these dual effects is provided below.

## IT Technical Skills

By its nature, online commerce places heavy demands on a company’s IT capabilities. IT technical skills are a result of the appropriate, updated technology skills—relating to both systems hardware and software—that are held by the IS/IT employees of the firm [9, 82]. Such skills not only include current technical knowledge but also the ability to deploy, use, and manage those skills. Although the relative mobility of IS/IT personnel tends to be high [66], some intangible IT skills—such as corporate-level knowledge assets [9] and technology integration skills [33]—are typically quite immobile, and thus difficult for firms to obtain, trade, imitate, or substitute. Firms that possess a superior IT skills capability, then, are likely to be more able than competitors to establish and maintain an online operation, and to do so more efficiently. Thus, ceteris paribus, firms with greater IT technical skills should be more likely to make a significant online commitment [56]. This prediction is consistent with the empirical findings of both Srinivasan et al. [89], who found that radical technology adoption is positively related to the presence of complementary technological assets, and Grewal et al. [44], who confirmed that IT capabilities are positively related to firm participation in electronic markets.

Furthermore, it seems reasonable to expect that the ability to manage IT skills should have a positive impact on online performance for any given level of online commitment, because firms possessing this capability will be able to more efficiently and effectively deploy their IT assets to support superior online performance [28, 109]. Existing studies within the IS/IT field using the RBV support such a perspective (e.g., [9, 78]). Thus,

Hypothesis 5: Firms’ superior IT technical skills capability will be associated with (a) higher online commitment, and (b) higher online performance.

## Cost Efficiency

This resource encompasses the ability to provide efficient and cost-effective operations on an ongoing basis. Firms with greater IT-enabled efficiency can develop a long-term competitive advantage by using this capability to reduce costs and develop a cost leadership position in their industry [7, 27, 50, 77, 97].

Having a superior cost-efficiency capability is likely to dissuade firms from making a commitment to online operations. Although going online may lead to reductions in transactions costs, large initial costs are incurred (or at least are perceived to be incurred) to set up an online channel format. Moreover, the skills required to cut costs in the online environment are likely to be different than those developed in the traditional, physical distribution world. Firms may believe that by waiting before making an online commitment, they will be able to reduce these fixed costs or develop new cost-cutting abilities more relevant to online operations.

Once the decision to commit to online channels is made, however, a firm with a superior cost efficiency capability should be able to reduce its variable costs of operation, leading to higher online performance. A number of studies have shown that a superior ability to efficiently manage costs allows a firm to achieve superior performance [9, 33, 82]. Thus, we predict the following relationships:

Hypothesis 6: A superior cost efficiency capability (a) will have a negative association with online commitment, but (b) will be associated with higher online performance.

## Established Distribution

Up to this point, our discussion has focused exclusively on capabilities underlying firms’ online commitment and online performance levels. However, one of the questions raised in the introduction has not yet been addressed: Does a firm’s established distribution structure impact its online commitment and online performance? The literature on innovation would suggest a rather gloomy prognosis in this respect, thanks to two underlying forces—the escalation of commitment [12, 38] and inertia [46]. Specifically, firms with an established distribution infrastructure are often loathe to forsake these investments in favor of an online channel [8]. Given a choice of continuing to invest in established channels versus allocating significant resources to the development of an online channel, firms are likely to “place less emphasis on the new compared with the old course of action” [18, p. 3]. In addition, previous research [72] has documented the propensity for firms to persist with the status quo, rather than engage in untested, relatively less well-understood ventures. Thus, we expect that

Hypothesis 7a: The greater the firm’s established distribution infrastructure, the lower the firm’s online commitment.

There are a number of reasons to believe, however, that for a given level of commitment, established distribution infrastructure actually facilitates the achievement of higher levels of performance of the online channel. First, the higher market presence afforded by established distribution infrastructure translates into a higher level of familiarity with the brand, in turn leading to a greater willingness to purchase via the online channel. Second, the “real-world” presence offered by established physical distribution creates trust in the mind of the consumer as well as allowing for customers’ personal interaction service needs to be met. As pointed out by one retail banking analyst, “The Internet is great for a lot of self-service kinds of things . . . but when you drill beyond that and it gets to the point of wanting more directed advice, that’s when the branch and the physical content becomes important” [34, p. A23]. Perhaps most importantly, the marriage of physical and online channels allows for seamless contact with customers. Thus, customers may use one channel for obtaining information on the retailer’s product offering, and undertake the purchase in another, or pay for the purchase online and pick up the item at the nearest physical outlet. Thus,

Hypothesis 7b: The greater the firm’s established distribution infrastructure, the greater the firm’s online performance.

The preceding hypothesis traces out the expected main effect of established distribution infrastructure on firms’ commitment to, and performance of, its online channel. There is some evidence to suggest that, in addition to the main effects above, established distribution levels may moderate the positive relationship between online commitment and online performance. Specifically, recent research would suggest that the performance gains from adding an online channel do not accrue equally across firms (e.g., [37]). When the established distribution infrastructure is minimal, the rewards from committing resources to the online channel accrue, for the most part, to that channel because customer demand for the retailer’s offering must be satisfied via the online format. However, as retailers’ physical outlets increase in number, customers begin to have choices in the format they use. Ironically, the very interchangeability of channel formats that makes for strong online performance (see H7b) also leads to a greater potential for conflict between the established and online channel formats. This potential for conflict, arising from increased levels of physical distribution outlets, is realized as consumers “free ride” on the information, market development, and demand stimulation efforts provided by the online channel, but purchase from the retailer’s established channels.<sup>5</sup> Thus, we expect:

Hypothesis 8: The greater the established distribution presence, the weaker the positive relation between online commitment and online performance.

## Controls

Both firm age and firm size might be expected to be associated with resistance to change, and thus a lower commitment to online operations [2]. The potential effects of firm age and size on online performance are less clear. Nonetheless, both variables are included as potentially important control variables in our study because they are often included in studies of firm performance.

## Empirical Study

DATA FOR THIS STUDY WERE COLLECTED through an online survey of North American, publicly traded retail firms listed in EDGAR—an online database maintained by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)—and SEDAR, the Canadian equivalent. We also used private financial databases such as COMPUSTAT, Hoover’s Masterlist of Major U.S. Companies, the Bloomberg Academic Financial Directory, LexisNexis, and The Blue Book of Canadian Business online. We used multiple sources to create the sampling frame, so as to be as inclusive as possible. Early versions of the instrument were reviewed by a panel of five domain experts and researchers, and then iteratively tested on 31 randomly selected key informants from the sampling frame (not included in the final sample). Items were added, dropped, or modified at each stage as warranted. For each firm in the sample, we also obtained contact information (telephone number and e-mail address) for the relevant personnel.<sup>6</sup>

In the interests of minimizing the time and expense incurred, we sent e-mail messages to 3,316 potential key informants, soliciting their participation in an online survey, and giving instructions on how to do so (along with a password and user name). We administered the finalized survey instrument following guidelines for online survey methodologies set out by Cook et al. [21] and Schaefer and Dillman [85]. Appendix A describes in greater detail both the suitability and efficacy of Web-based surveys.

Of the messages sent, 593 (17.9 percent) messages “bounced,” leaving 2,713 potential respondents. Reminders were sent to verified nonrespondents two weeks following initial contact [21]. A total of 625 surveys were returned, representing a response rate of 23 percent. This figure is in line with response rates achieved in other online surveys (e.g., [24, 85]). Thirty surveys were found to be incomplete and were dropped from the total response pool, leaving 595 responses. Further investigation of the responses indicated that 100 of them had come from multiple respondents within organizations. In total, there were 45 firms for which two or more respondents had submitted completed surveys. For each of these firms, the supplied information was compared across respondents. No significant differences were noted. In order to avoid biasing subsequent analyses in favor of these multiple respondent firms, the responses within each firm were averaged, leaving a final sample size of 550.

Responding firms were generally large, with a majority having total annual sales greater than \$100 million (54 percent) and more than 500 employees (70 percent). All firms in the sample conducted some degree of online commerce. A majority of the firms (54 percent) had online sales between \$10 million and \$100 million, while the rest had smaller online operations (41 percent).

## Measures

Whenever possible, we used measurement scales from previous research, modified to fit our setting. If preexisting scales were not available, measures were developed on the basis of the conceptual definitions of the constructs and our prestudy interviews. A process of scale validation was conducted on all the measures. Table 1 reports the correlation matrix and descriptive statistics for the final measures. The specific items used in our study are presented in Appendix B. Each set of measures is also described in more detail below.

In order to minimize the potential for social desirability bias, we did not ask key informants to directly assess their firms’ levels of online commitment. Instead, we used two proxy ratio variables to create a composite measure of online commitment: (1) the proportion of the firm’s employees devoted to online sales (i.e., online employees/total employees), and (2) the proportion of the firm’s sales generated through online operations (i.e., online sales/total sales). Recall that we define online commitment as the proportion of a firm’s tangible resources devoted to online operations. Both of these ratios are reflections of the firm’s underlying commitment to online operations, and can therefore be thought of as indicators of online commitment. The process used to combine these two ratio measures is described in more detail in Appendix C.<sup>7</sup>

The employee ratio reflects the firm’s investment of a specific, tangible asset (employees) to its online operations, and therefore represents a focused measure of online commitment. In contrast, the sales ratio assesses the extent to which the company’s total operations have been devoted to achieving online—relative to total—sales, and as such, this ratio reflects a broader set of resource investments than employees alone. Note that this latter ratio is not necessarily associated with superior performance. For example, consider Amazon.com. Amazon’s sales ratio is 1.0, yet this does not mean that Amazon is necessarily pleased with its performance. Conversely, another company might achieve only a small proportion of its total sales from online operations, yet be highly satisfied with its online performance.<sup>8</sup>

Online performance is viewed here as a reflective construct that gives rise to a variety of different measurement indicators. Our study includes indicators that assess overall online performance (based on items developed by Lederer and Mirani [58] and Mc-Grath et al. [67]), online performance compared to the firm’s original objectives [67], and online performance compared to that of the firm’s main competitors [59, 73]. All items were measured using five-point item scales (see Appendix B). Taken together, these items exhibit a high degree of composite reliability (alpha = 0.98).

The survey contained questions relating to the five internally and externally focused capabilities. In each case, the capability was first defined, and the respondent was then asked to identify (1) the firm’s overall level of competence in that capability, (2) the importance of that capability to the firm, and (3) the amount of time and effort invested by the firm in developing and maintaining that capability [67]. For the market-sensing capability, separate questions relating to the construct’s environmental scanning and market responsiveness facets were included.

## Discussion

THE PRESENT STUDY NOTES THE INCREASING PROPENSITY for firms to employ traditionally unutilized channel formats as part of their “go-to-market” strategies. To better understand this trend, we focus on retailers’ online offerings with a view to (1) shed light on the relationship between commitment to these online channels and their subsequent performance, and (2) identify the underlying resource-based drivers of online channel commitment and performance.

## Research Implications

Across a variety of different retail sectors, we find a robust and positive relationship between the extent to which firms commit to online commerce and the performance of their online undertakings. At its most basic level, this finding can be summarized as “the more you commit to online commerce, the better your online performance.” While prior studies have hinted that commitments to online commerce may positively affect firm performance (e.g., [37, 43, 98, 107]), ours is the first study that we are aware of to provide large-sample evidence of this positive link.

This study also identifies and examines the impact of key firm capabilities on both online commitment and online performance. Specifically, we find that firms’ levels of commitment to online commerce and their online performance are associated with markedly different sets of capabilities. In the retailing context, there is a strong link between IT skills and the extent to which firms invest in online operations. For given levels of commitment, however, it is the attention paid to developing and deploying externally focused capabilities such as market sensing, brand management, and customer service that determines the relative success of the online undertaking. Furthermore, our findings suggest that continued reliance on internally focused capabilities (e.g., IT-based capabilities) is associated with decreased online performance. These results emphasize the need to have an outwardly focused perspective once the internal decision to commit resources has been made, and are consistent with prior literature showing the importance of striking an appropriate balance between an internally and externally focused perspective (e.g., [25, 39]).

Our finding that internally focused capabilities are negatively associated with online performance is perhaps surprising, given the centrality of these capabilities to online endeavors. For instance, cost efficiency was negatively associated with both commitment and online performance. One possible explanation for this result may be that the online performance measurement scale consists of items that assess non-cost-related perceptions of outcomes such as user satisfaction, reliability, and quality. Firms that underspend on such outcomes may be cost-efficient, but also underperformers versus their competition. Conversely, investments in such outcomes could lead respondents to conclude that the firm is not cost-focused, even if IT costs are carefully controlled. By contrast, the possession of strong IT skills is positively linked to the decision to commit to the online channel, but is negatively linked to online performance. Thus, the IT skills capability has a negative direct path, and a positive indirect path to online performance through its effect on commitment. The justification of these findings may lie in an overreliance on internal capabilities, particularly those based on IT. This explanation is consistent with findings that emerged from a more in-depth study we conducted with a small number of retailers similar to those studied here. Firms that possessed strong IT capabilities were more likely than others to pay excessive attention to getting IT issues “right,” often to the exclusion of other internal and external demands.

The present study also contributes to the evolving literature looking at how firms deal with the multiple channel management issues that arise when traditional modes of distribution are complemented by online channels. Present opinion is divided as to the consequences of augmenting traditional with electronic channels. Findings from our study suggest a negative relationship between firms’ levels of physical distribution and their propensity to commit to an online presence. This finding is consistent with results obtained in the innovation context, in that increasing commitments to incumbent modes of doing business dissuades firms from investing in new, as yet untested, innovations (e.g., [18]).

Interestingly, we find strong evidence for a positive relation between firms’ physical distribution infrastructure and their online performance. This finding supports the argument that the presence of physical stores together with an online presence enables the firm to realize synergies from the multiple contact points afforded as a result [98]. Seamless transitions between traditional and online distribution appear to result in positive online performance outcomes. The divergent effects of physical presence on firms’ levels of online commitment and online performance are particularly noteworthy given the widely prevalent fears of dysfunctional channel relationship outcomes (e.g., [41]).

At the same time, however, our results also show a negative interaction between physical presence and online commitment. We interpret this negative interaction to suggest that increasing the level of online commitment provides the biggest “bang for buck” for online performance when the level of physical presence is low. With growing numbers of physical outlets, increasing levels of online commitment presumably translate into greater intrachannel competition [35], in turn resulting in lower online performance levels. For those retailers with a large physical presence, the benefit of online commitment appears to be marginal. Taken together, our study results provide useful, if preliminary, insights on the intricate distribution-relevant issues inherent in any online commerce undertaking.

A final research implication worth noting is the impact of firm characteristics such as age and size. Not surprisingly, we find firms’ levels of online commitment to vary negatively with age and size. Older, larger retailers appear to be more circumspect about new retail formats, and appear to take a more measured approach to online commerce. Interestingly, however, there appears to be no age- or size-specific handicap with respect to online performance. For given levels of commitment, smaller, less well-established firms do not seem to reap any particular “nimbleness” premium. This finding complements the results reported by Geyskens et al. [37], who noted that firms of all sizes in the publishing business were able to successfully add Internet-based channels.

## Managerial Implications

The present study provides several implications for managers grappling with the challenges and opportunities of online commerce. First, it is comforting to note that the higher the level of commitment to online commerce, the better the firm’s online performance. This result should be of particular interest to those who champion the cause of firms’ online ventures, and could serve as a warning for those entrenched in traditional business models.

Given this result, the natural question that arises is how does one engender commitment to online commerce? Our study provides a twofold solution to this question. Specifically, we find the presence of relevant capabilities (in the present context, IT skills) to be positively associated with greater online commitment. Firms that have access to well-developed IT skills are likely to display higher levels of commitment to online commerce. The existing skill set is a natural fit with the demands of the new opportunity, in turn leading to the decision to commit. The relevance of the particular resource to the new undertaking is all the more important given our finding that prior investments in traditional business operations actually reduce the firm’s commitment to online commerce. Within the present context, the presence of a significant number of traditional “bricks-and-mortar” retail outlets seems to dissuade firms from adopting greater levels of commitment to the online retail format. Not surprisingly, retailers are reluctant to jump into a retail format that has the potential to disrupt their traditional distribution infrastructure and channel relationships.

A second point for managers to note is that online performance is a function not only of high levels of commitment but also of a well-developed portfolio of ancillary capabilities. In addition to the directly relevant resource set discussed above, market sensing, brand management, and customer service capabilities are also seen to play a vital role in translating online commitment into superior online performance. Just as in traditional business operations, the ability to anticipate and read environmental trends, nurture and enhance brand value, and provide customers with a unique service experience serve the firm well in the online context.

Although having a good sense of one’s internal capabilities facilitates commitment, it is important to note that an overemphasis on internally focused capabilities, such as IT skills, may actually hurt rather than help online performance. As noted earlier, it appears that firms focusing attention primarily on strong internally oriented capabilities may lose sight of external market demands, and as a result, fail to properly implement a successful online launch. It may more generally be inferred that organizational benefits accrue from the use of IT when it is wielded with intelligence and skill, and guided by experience, more so that when it is regarded as a tool. It is the technology-supported work practice that makes the difference, not the technology alone.

To managers interested in coming to grips with their established retail format, our study provides some relevant insights. In particular, the greater the number of traditional “bricks-and-mortar” outlets, the lesser the propensity to commit resources to an online undertaking. Yet the very same distribution outlets seem to provide a positive boost to online performance. Our finding in this regard is consistent with the notion of synergies afforded by the careful integration of traditional and online retail formats. Such synergies, however, seem to accrue to the firm only at lower levels of physical distribution presence. As the number of traditional retail outlets increases, the potential for cannibalization and dysfunctional channel conflict increases. While the preceding result is no doubt heartening to smaller retailers who do not have a large number of physical outlets, it suggests that larger, more established retailers must proceed with caution when undertaking their online ventures in order to be sure to strike the proper balance between greater operational synergies and manageable channel conflict.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

THE PRESENT STUDY REPRESENTS AN INITIAL ATTEMPT to understand the resources underlying firms’ online channel undertakings. As such, our study has some limitations that we must acknowledge. One of the challenges confronting RBV-informed empirical work is the lack of guidance on identification of the appropriate resources underlying firms operating strategies [79]. By their very nature, resources are usually sector-specific and frequently firm-specific, thus making it very difficult to identify those that are universally relevant. We attempted to get around this problem in the present context by grounding the present study in the retail sector, and by adopting the internally and externally focused resource taxonomy of resources relied on by a growing body of RBV-informed studies. Moreover, we attempted to confirm the appropriateness of the resource portfolio we examined by conducting a series of extensive interviews with personnel from diverse operating niches within the retail sector. Nevertheless, we do not purport to provide a comprehensive listing of resources relevant across all operating contexts. Future research on developing such a portfolio of relevant resources would be welcome.

A related issue pertains to measurement. Although most of our measures yielded satisfactory psychometric properties, lack of significance on some items (i.e., low weights for some formative items) necessitated their removal. Our ability to obtain statistically significant findings despite measurement error supports the relevance of these resources in the current context. Furthermore, while it seems possible that our respondents are not familiar with all aspects of their firms’ operations, leading to the possibility of response bias, careful examination of the multiple responses obtained for 45 of our sample firms shows that this is likely not a concern here. Nonetheless, we strongly encourage efforts to find improved resource measures. As with all crosssectional work, we can only claim associations and correlations among variables of interest. Further work, perhaps through experimental research, could explore whether these links represent causal relationships.

In this paper, we focused exclusively on the commitment to, and performance of, the focal firm’s online channel, in order to shed some light on an unexplored area of channels research. Future research might extend our focus past the online channel to the performance of the firm as a whole, or add the perspectives of dyadic channel partners. Doing so would help to further enhance our understanding of the benefits and limitations of going to market through online channels of distribution.

The present study hypothesized a rather complex relationship between established and online channels, and found evidence to support these hypothesized links. Overall, we observed significant positive returns to investments in online channels within the context of the retail sector. These returns were affected by commitment to the online channel, and were further influenced by an important set of firm capabilities. We found divergent effects of the impact of firms’ established distribution channels on levels of commitment to, and performance of, the online channel. Finally, we found evidence of diminishing returns to commitment as a function of established distribution presence, thereby suggesting that the rewards of commitment do not accrue equally to all firms.

## Appendix A

## Web-Based Survey Methodology

DESPITE THE RELATIVE NEWNESS OF THE INTERNET and the Web, online survey methodologies have been extensively researched in the social sciences (e.g., [30, 94]). Benefits of online surveys include quick response times, respondent accuracies equivalent to or better than other survey methodologies, interactive capabilities, distribution flexibility, low incremental cost of administration, and fewer data entry errors [24, 86].

Two main areas of concern exist when the Web is used for data collection—coverage bias and nonresponse bias. Coverage bias concerns the fact that certain members of the population of interest have no chance of being sampled. In the case of online surveys, those with Internet access will be oversampled, while all others are ignored. For this reason, mail and phone surveys are used more often in survey research, despite the advantages of online methods. For the current study, an assumption was made that respondents would have access to both e-mail and the Web, and that using this medium for distribution of the questionnaire would not result in excessive coverage bias. This assumption was based on the fact that target respondents were senior retail managers working in firms with online retail operations. However, as a precaution, respondents were given the option of receiving the survey instrument by regular mail. Empirical studies have demonstrated that a combination of Web-based and regular mail-based questionnaires produce no errors in estimates [94]. In any event, none of our respondents requested the survey be sent to them by mail.

Nonresponse bias is concerned with accounting for possible systematic (nonrandom) differences that may exist between respondents and nonrespondents. Because online surveys typically have lower response rates than mail or telephone surveys, nonresponse bias can be a cause for concern [86]. Most studies that have explicitly examined this issue have concluded that response rates for e-mail and Web-based surveys in the 20 percent to 30 percent range or above are not likely to suffer from severe nonresponse bias [24, 85, 86]. The response rate of 23 percent attained in our study fails within this acceptable range, suggesting that nonresponse is not a major problem in this case.

## Development of Online Commitment Measure

TO ASSESS THE EXTENT OF FIRMS’ COMMITMENTS to online operations, we used two distinct sets of self-report measures for the most recent fiscal year completed when the survey was administered. One set focuses on the extent to which a firm’s sales (in millions of dollars) are transacted online (versus all channels), while the second focuses on the extent to which the firm’s employees are devoted to online operations (versus all operations). For each set, two separate categorical variables were used, leading to the Tables C1 and C2.

The entries in both of these tables reflect the extent to which individual firms are committed to online operations. For example, 32 respondent firms have less than \$1 million in both total and online sales, 84 firms have less than \$1 million in online sales but \$1–\$10 million in total sales, and so on. Firms falling on the diagonals of these tables can be thought of as maximally committed to the online world, whereas firms above the diagonal are less committed. Furthermore, the further away from the diagonal a firm is, the lower its commitment. Note that Table C1 reflects the extent to which a firm is reliant on sales from the online channel, whereas Table C2 reflects the extent to which the firm has committed employee resources to its online operations. Thus, they reflect (respectively) the firm’s online commitment from both an output and an input perspective.

We do this for two reasons. First, the two ratios are significantly correlated with one another $( r = 0 . 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , suggesting a unidimensional construct. Because the ratios separately reflect the resource inputs and the outputs of the firm, using both measures together provides a more complete view of the firm’s true level of commitment to online operations. Second, when either ratio is used on its own, we obtain substantively similar results, but the effects are weaker; combining the two yields a more powerful test of our hypotheses.
