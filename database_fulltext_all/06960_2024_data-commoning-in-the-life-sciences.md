# DATA COMMONING IN THE LIFE SCIENCES<sup>1</sup>

Laia Pujol Priego<sup>2</sup> and Jonathan Wareham Ramon Llull University, ESADE Business School, Barcelona, SPAIN {Laia.pujol@esade.edu} {Jonathan.wareham@esade.edu}

Datafication is driving organizations to invest in data commons, not only to share the costs of data generation, analysis, and curation but, more importantly, to realize synergies in precompetitive research collaborations where private and public motives interact (i.e., semicommons). The fanfare surrounding datafication often hails the sophisticated algorithms used to develop large quantities of data toward greater insight, naively assuming that more data equals better data. Yet for datafication in general and precompetitive research specifically, less attention is awarded to what actually constitutes data and evidence in the first place—that is, to its genesis, construction, and interpretation by heterogeneous scientific and commercial entities. We present the case of Open Targets, a precompetitive collaboration in the life sciences, where publicly funded research, nonprofit foundations, and for-profit pharma collaborate to generate and share data in genomics, proteomics, and bioinformatics. We theorize about the process of data commoning, a political activity in the semicommons where data are created, evidential value is assembled, and scientific meaning converges as data travels, or journeys, across creators, validators, and users. Our findings highlight the effects of relational dynamics and the political nature of data journeys: why these dynamics form, how they are manifested in a precompetitive semicommons, and what implications this can have for the mobility of data as a shared, public good.

Keywords: Data commons, data journeys, commoning, semicommons, drug discovery, life science

## Introduction

Computationally intensive industries such as life sciences, chemical, energy, and advanced manufacturing are discovering that collaborative data generation and analysis can be mutually beneficial. Diverse—often competing—entities can bridge complementary capabilities and resources to build precompetitive research collaborations that increase the speed and efficacy of computation while sharing the economic costs. Both public and private sectors are developing precompetitive research collaborations called data commons, aimed at generating, pooling, and linking vast data sources across disciplines and topics to facilitate their repurposing in computationally intensive domains (Jarvenpaa & Markus, 2018; Rothe et al., 2019). The term “commons” describes the “institutionalized sharing of resources among members of a community” (Madison et al., 2009, p. 841). Data commons have now been developed across fields including drug discovery and life sciences (Vassilakopoulou & Aanestad, 2019), advanced manufacturing (European Commission, 2022), energy (Parmiggiani et al., 2015), and environmental science (Edwards, 2019) by members representing commercial, scientific, and public health interests. However, expectations and fanfare about the increasing datafication of our societies often portray data as a fixed, transactional, and contextindependent entity that is transportable across organizations, disciplines, and applications (Cukier & Mayer-Schönberger, 2014). The spotlight often falls on the novel algorithms used to generate insights as given (Markus, 2017), while discounting the innate complexity of constructing data and evidence (Leonelli, 2020). We refer to evidence as “the specific arrangement and formatting of data, which is taken to corroborate (provide reasons to believe in) a particular claim” (Leonelli, 2018, p. 23). In the growing tendency of building data commons in precompetitive research across many computationally intensive domains, questions of how evidence is constructed are often black-boxed, with somewhat naive depictions of scientific data as neutral, agnostic, and generalizable across multiple research and development activities (Chang, 2012).

The premise of our research is that what counts as data—the processes in the making and mobilization of data as evidence— cannot be assumed or ignored (Leonelli, 2020). Additionally, we build on the tradition that data are constructed through nonlinear, layered, and innately relational and political processes (Edwards, 2010; Monteiro, 2022; Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019), where data’s evidential value is derived through strategic negotiations and actions by actors with divergent interests (Chang, 2012; Leonelli, 2016). In that respect, we build on the notion of the data journey as an analytical construct to theorize about the evolution of data and evidence building, where data journeys are defined “as designating the movement of data from their production site to many other sites in which they are processed, mobilised and repurposed” (Leonelli, 2020, p. 9).

In the specific context of precompetitive research, we consider how scientific data are generated in data commons where participants have competing motives across public and private objectives. We describe data commons as an activity—using the verb commoning—to express the situated interdependence among the common’s stakeholders and the resources on which commoning depends (Linebaugh, 2009). This relational perspective prioritizes “process over substance, relation over separateness, and activity over passivity” (Guillaume, 2007, p. 741). When commoning processes take place in collectives that have attributes of both private and common property, they are often referred to as semicommons (Laakmann, 2015). Semicommons are described as hybrid regimes where private and common uses of the resource interact dynamically (Heverly, 2003). Commons have been studied extensively, but analyses of semicommons and their unique dynamics are less frequent (Laakmann, 2015), with research largely ignoring how semicommons function when data is the “resource,” how the nature of data affects commoning dynamics, and how such dynamics affect data (Vassilakopoulou et al., 2019). This is particularly relevant because a “data-centric perspective is lacking” (Jarvenpaa & Markus, 2018, p. 4582) in research on how communal data are generated and shared (Vassilakopoulou & Aanestad, 2019), especially in computationally intensive, scientific settings. Accordingly, our main research question is:

How are data commons generated and transformed by heterogeneous scientific and commercial entities?

To answer this, we study Open Targets (OT), a large-scale data collaboration created by an ever-growing group of companies and leading organizations in bioinformatics, genomics, and pharmaceuticals dedicated to collaboratively generating scientific evidence on genetic targets and disease development. As a celebrated public-private partnership, OT began in 2014 and is the result of the collaboration of (1) the European Bioinformatics Institute unit of the European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL-EBI), Europe’s flagship research infrastructure for life science funded by EU member states; (2) the Wellcome Sanger Institute, a nonprofit research foundation funded by the Wellcome Trust; and seven of the world’s most highly capitalized, for-profit life science companies, namely (3) GSK, (4) Biogen, (5) Takeda, (6) Celgene/Bristol Meyers Squibb, (7) Sanofi, (8) Pfizer, and (9) Genentech.<sup>3</sup>

We open the “black box” of data commoning at OT by studying the data journey through which data are constructed and awarded evidential value through their making. Our case provides a vivid illustration of how the different institutional mandates of OT members manifest themselves—often contentiously—across scientific advancement, public welfare, and commercial interests in their scientific practice.

Our analysis provides three main contributions. First, building upon the notion that data are political (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019), we offer a rich and granular empirical explanation of how scientific data are constructed and eventually awarded evidential value (Leonelli, 2016). We delineate specific relational dynamics of the data-making process and describe its journey from genesis to public release. In so doing, we emphasize relational frictions and nonlinearity, demonstrating not only that data-making is politically charged (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019), but also how it is politically charged, by specifying data construction as patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding. We further describe the specific political forces and actions that datamaking actors take throughout the course of the data journey.

Second, we demonstrate these dynamics in the context of a semicommons in precompetitive research that is growing in prominence across many domains. We show how the heterogeneity and divergent interests of semicommons members directly influence scientific data-making and evidential value.

Third, our description of a data journey in life sciences demonstrates that scientific data are neither neutral nor agnostic—not simply that they are not so, but why and how they are not so. Despite substantial investments in FAIR<sup>4</sup> and OpenDATA<sup>5</sup> movements (Lee, 2015; Lee et al. 2016; Wilkinson et al., 2016) that aspire to make data available, reusable, and generalizable, our analysis demonstrates that those who participate in the construction of data are best equipped to build evidential value from it due to its relational nature. Actors more distant from the data generation may be substantially disadvantaged in appropriating value from these large investments. Hence, our operational explanation can be useful for the many public, academic, and private organizations that are addressing the growing costs of data production and analysis with data commons and wish to maximize their overall socioeconomic value.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we review the debates around data ontology from the philosophy and sociology of science and IS literatures to introduce our theoretical premise of data as a relational process. We further problematize our phenomenon by describing the diverse nature of the actors that constitute members of the OT semicommons and describe the scientific and commercial practices of their work. This is followed by a discussion of the research context and methods employed for conducting the study. We then draw on our theoretical constructs in presenting the results of our analysis of OT. Finally, we conclude by discussing the implications of this study, the contributions it makes to the literature on data ontology and data commoning in semicommons, and directions for future research.

## Theoretical Underpinnings

## Perspectives on Data: Data Journeys

The tension between viewing data as either objective descriptors of the world or something more subjective and human-made has been at the heart of sociological and philosophical controversies since the scientific revolution (Bowker, 2014). Current practices that aggregate data as an economic resource are premised on data as “raw” objects that can be accumulated and transacted (Lycett, 2013). This tradition stands in contrast with the idea of data being a “process” that reflects specific points of view, interests, individual goals, and systems of knowledge (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Leonelli, 2016; Monteiro and Parmiggiani, 2019).

Under a representational perspective of data, studies have typically assumed that data are reasonable representations of external facts (Burton-Jones et al., 2017; Jones, 2019) that capture and represent specific aspects of the world and that data work takes place within typified functional operations and capabilities. Under these premises, the actual conditions and practices through which data are derived and transformed tend to be discounted.

Departing from viewing data as a static representation of reality, scholars have progressively sought a more subtle and processual view of data, proposing a dual ontology of data as being both an object and a process (Aaltonen et al., 2021). The nature of data is thus ambivalent: as an object, manifested as concrete entities at any one place and time but inherently editable, recombinable, and subject to repurposing (Alaimo et al., 2020), and as a process, “continuously transformed as they travel across research sites and media” (Leonelli, 2016, p. 83). Although both the representational and processual views of data share the understanding that data are the result of complex interactions between data constructors and the world—and that measurement instruments and observational techniques play an important role (Rheinberger, 2011; Vincent, 2022)—the processual view emphasizes the whole cycle of data production, data mobility, and the implications of how the evolution of data both affects and is affected by how we act in the world (Leonelli, 2016).

Yet despite a growing convergence among many scholars that data is a process, there is divergence concerning its nature as a relational process (Howlett & Morgan, 2010; Leonelli, 2015, 2016; Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019), or rather an object-centric process—describing a transformation from raw data to data commodities (Aaltonen et al., 2021; Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2022). Under a relational perspective, there is no such thing as raw data as an abstract category, nor are there intrinsic properties of data or a fixed evidential value in and of itself. Data can only be assessed in relation to specific expectations, aims, knowledge, and social and economic contexts of data use. In the words of Gitelman (2013), data “are always already ‘cooked’ and never entirely ‘raw’” (p. 3). Such a relational perspective forcefully highlights the relevance of the journey of data; that is, how data travel and evolve in response to their environment and relations between data constructors and users. This implies explicit acts of data manipulation and “packaging strategies” that facilitate data’s movement to new epistemological contexts and actors (Leonelli 2015). It suggests that data are often choreographed or regulated toward divergent goals (Leonelli, 2020). In this vein, Morgan (2020) argued that data are best viewed as mutable vehicles to understand how facts move and land in diverse epistemic spaces: “The more they travel, the more they shift shape to suit their new circumstances” (p. 107).

Studies of data construction from the relational perspective have often drawn from scientific and innovation settings (Borgman, 2015). This tradition considers the complex interactions embedded in epistemic work, metrology, and evidence building as a process among heterogeneous actors (Bowker, 2014; Edwards 2010; Leonelli, 2015; Vincent, 2022). For example, Bowker and Star (2000) delved into the politics of healthcare data classification, such as the International Classification of Diseases, and showed that defining diseases is not solely scientific but swayed by societal, cultural, and political forces. Thus, data are not innately an objective foundation for knowledge; rather, the way data are worked on—and with (Rheinberger, 2011; Vincent, 2022)—determines their meaning (Faraj et al., 2020). Such studies are often situated in empirical contexts with substantial operational and organizational complexity. For example, Monteiro and Parmiggiani (2019) described the data work politics in the marine Arctic by an oil and gas company, reflecting on how certain types of knowledge, held by certain actors, may be privileged. In such studies, data are considered a process of construction by actors with divergent goals and resource endowments that further define their political relationships.

We position our research within this relational perspective in order to understand the precompetitive collaboration among pharmaceutical companies and research labs in the life sciences. We chose this setting because it represents a distinctly rich milieu of actors with various expectations, objectives, and capacities for data use. As per the relational perspective, data here should not be seen as static artifacts but rather as dynamic, evolving entities that acquire their meaning and value in relation to the aims, knowledge, and the specific social and economic context of these actors (Leonelli, 2020; Morgan, 2020). Each actor’s interplay critically influences how data is shaped, repackaged, transferred, and interpreted, and, consequently, how scientific evidence is built and refined (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019).

While theoretical arguments supporting a relational vision of data are emerging, they have not commensurately benefited from empirical insight into data journeys and the granular, epistemic work in the everyday parcourse of data. Given the growing movements in datafication, open and FAIR data, precompetitive research, and computational and collaborative science, we believe empirical attention to how data are created and cultivated via a pragmatic analysis of routine practices can offer greater insight into the actual triggers and actions that actors take in a relational process (Dewey, 1938). By taking this standpoint, we aim to empirically unravel how actors’ interactions shape data across its evolution and, ultimately, how these data are accorded the status of evidence within these collaborative endeavors.

## Data Commoning

Commons systems (Ostrom, 1990) describe the “institutionalized sharing of resources among members of a community” (Madison et al., 2009, p. 841). A common pool resource (CPR) is defined as a “natural or man-made resource system” (Ostrom, 1990, p. 30) that “may be owned by national, regional, or local governments, by communal groups, by private individuals or corporations, or used as open-access resources by whomever can gain access” (Hess & Ostrom, 2003. p. 120). In commons systems, the participants (i.e., commoners) are “anyone who actually constructs, repairs, or takes actions that ensure the long-term sustenance of a resource systems itself” (Ostrom, 1990, p. 31). For a data commons, this would be anyone who generates, curates, integrates, disseminates, or cultivates data across its evolution. For data resources that are eventually publicly shared, their status as a social good is premised on usage on a “nondiscrimatory basis, regardless of the users’ identity or intended use” (Frischmann, 2012, p. 7). One of the first treatments of data commons as a CPR came from Hess and Ostrom (2003), who criticized the fact that data-centric resources are often treated as standardized, homogeneous objects transacted among participants and thus irrelevant to or black-boxed away in any analysis of commons governance. This may be a legacy of traditional commons studies where physical resources are assumed to be homogeneous and constant in essence (although depletable, see Hess & Ostrom, 2007). This assumption of resource homogeneity is problematic if we consider: (1) the relational dynamics that constitute the process of making data as a CPR, and (2) the heterogeneity of actors that can potentially extract value from data with disparate, complementary resource endowments (Leonelli, 2020). Consequently, we believe it is more accurate to consider commons as a process and thus conceptualize data construction and use with the verb data commoning. The term “commoning” was popularized by historian Peter Linebaugh in his book The Magna Carta Manifesto (2009). Commoning expresses the relationship between the community and resources, which “negotiates, imperfectly, the various limits (needs) and possibilities (capacities) that arise in particular contexts” (Bresniham, 2015, p. 105).

The need to understand data commoning is particularly acute in the case of commons systems where both commercial and public entities collaborate to produce data resources that represent both private and common property (i.e., semicommons, see Heverly, 2003). One of the main challenges is that in semicommons, data are collectively generated and shared with the ambition of serving some social objective or public good (Klievink et al., 2016); yet there is a simultaneous need to maintain the option for for-profit participants to commercialize the data (Jarvenpaa & Markus, 2018; Rothe et al., 2019). Hence, the divergent incentives, strategies, goals, and practices carry natural frictions in data commoning (Edwards & King, 2021), which can make these processes fragile (Heverly, 2003). In the case of life sciences, the strong economic rationale of for-profit pharma companies tempers their willingness to contribute to science as a public good, given the industrial norms of an IP<sup>6</sup>-intensive sector accustomed to high levels of secrecy (Allarakhia, 2014; Mittleman et al., 2013). Considering that precompetitive semicommons are now appearing in genetics (Vassilakopoulou & Aanestad, 2019; Vassilakopoulou et al., 2019), biodata (Rothe et al., 2019), and energy (van den Broek & Veenstra, 2015), a granular understanding of the generation, curation, and sharing of data under the auspices of both public and private goods can inform the design and management of these regimes as they continue to expand into other industries.

## Case Context

A great deal of research in genetics is focused on the genes that encode the 22 fundamental amino acids, of which 20 develop into complex structures that make up proteins. Proteins are responsible for almost all functions in human physiology and thus command significant attention from life science researchers. Most pharmacology research focuses on the role of individual proteins in physiological processes and pathologies and how these can be moderated by molecular or biological compounds. In traditional drug discovery, cell biologists and pharmacologists seek to: (1) identify the function of a specific protein (target), (2) determine the protein’s 3-dimensional structure, and (3) identify a molecule (lead) that can bind to the protein in a specific pocket or crevasse on the protein’s surface to provoke an agonizing or antagonizing effect on the protein’s function (i.e., increasing or decreasing a function that is determinative of a symptom or cause of pathology). Historically, this one protein-one molecule strategy has been the dominant method of smallmolecule drug discovery. However, it has limitations that contribute to the perpetually increasing cost of bringing a drug to market. Life scientists are increasingly aware that proteins do not operate in isolation; that is, a large number of proteins can interact to regulate a given physiological process. This recognition has spurred an increasingly holistic perspective of cell biology that departs from a reductionist study of simple protein structure and function toward a science of systems biology, where proteins interact through extensive signal and metabolic pathways to regulate physiological processes.

Hence, three factors—(1) an increasingly holistic approach to cell biology, (2) the acutely growing costs of drug development, and (3) a deluge of biological data from the sequencing of the human genome in 2003 (Collins et al., 2003)—have led pharmaceutical and life science companies to develop new collaborative approaches to scale research and innovation capabilities through precompetitive data commons (Contreras & Vertinsky, 2016; Vertinsky, 2014). Large investments have been made in the initial phases of pharmacological research that were previously siloed in individual companies and warily guarded as trade secrets (Chaguturu et al., 2014). However, where public and private research collaborations have functioned well in the discovery and development of uncommon diseases of little interest to the private sector, their success in disease areas with major economic markets is limited (Mittleman et al., 2013). Big pharma is notorious for its secrecy and aggressive enforcement of IP, traits that are often at odds with contributing to the public good of science (Allarakhia, 2014).

A compelling example of data commons is Open Targets (OT), which originated in March 2014 when EMBL-EBI, the Wellcome Sanger Institute, and GSK came together at the Wellcome Trust Genome Campus near Cambridge, U.K., to discuss how organizations could pool their data and capabilities to accelerate target identification, prioritization, and validation—ultimately identifying therapeutic targets for new and repurposed drugs. As one pharma participant expressed: “We did not want to invest a lot but [wanted] to work with the ones that had the expertise. We are better off cooperating with them than investing ourselves” (CM1).

In February 2016, Biogen joined OT and contributed its data and knowledge on therapies for neurodegenerative diseases, hematologic conditions, and autoimmune disorders. Progressively, other companies joined; namely Takeda (December 2017), bringing its resources in oncology, gastroenterology, and central nervous system therapeutics; Celgene (May 2018 [acquired by Bristol Myers Squibb in 2019]), also pooling resources on oncology and immuneinflammatory conditions; Sanofi (October 2018), expanding the resources on immunology, oncology, neurosciences, and diabetes; Pfizer (February 2022), adding expertise in immunology, oncology, cardiology, endocrinology, and neurology; and, finally, Genentech (November 2022), adding expertise in oncology, neurosciences, immunology, and infectious diseases.

OT participants pool the resources necessary to run genomic experiments to generate data and computational techniques that identify and validate causality among targets, pathways, and diseases that are beyond the resources of any single organization. Even though the path to full drug development from this stage remains long, expensive, and risky, the practice of precompetitive data collaboration is relatively new in this industry, which is accustomed to high levels of confidentiality. To support the generation and sharing of experimental data, OT participants have developed a data infrastructure capable of searching, assessing, and integrating a vast quantity of public and proprietary genetic and biological data. Additionally, participants have developed new technologies in gene editing, single-cell sequencing, and cell models to inform drug target selection (Behan et al., 2019). Most importantly, one of the main objectives of OT is the generation of new experimental data. Where computational approaches have increased the power of experimental methods and the resulting insights, their predictive power is limited by a major bottleneck that one research scientist described as: “A lack of data of sufficient size, variety and biological relevance to train computational approaches” (RS-NPA1). In response, OT participants have collectively invested £77M through member subscriptions. Each OT member gets one vote and one veto on the OT board in deliberations about new experimental foci.

OT participants have generated a data infrastructure that allows varying degrees and layers of data access: namely, (1) a public layer, (2) a prepublication layer that is private to all OT participants, and (3) a private-project layer that is limited to single project contributors within OT. The public layer of OT infrastructure can be accessed through a web user interface, an API, and data downloads. The infrastructure interface works as an open access “Google”—a type of search engine that extensively searches, assesses, and integrates the vast quantity of genetic and biological data that is publicly available, including data collaboratively generated by OT— supporting two main paths: target-centric and disease-centric inquiries. The output is a visualized summary of the genetic targets associated with the disease interest and the underlying evidence available (Koscielny et al., 2017). The codebases of the OT public layer are open source, and the license allows third-party organizations to create self-hosted private instances of the OT public layer with custom data. The prepublication layer integrates, transforms, and processes all OT data generated through experimental projects exclusively for OT participants until the data are ready to be released in the public layer. A third layer of data access is provided more informally through a private-project layer, where OT participants in single experimental projects access data prior to elevation to the prepublication layer. Each OT participant works both individually and collectively to build individual lines of evidence that provide reasons to believe in a particular target and disease association. In other words, “lines of evidence” refers to the various independent sources of data that collectively contribute to the understanding or validation of a particular biological hypothesis, therapeutic concept, or drug target (Leonelli, 2018). For example, participants can combine genetic data with cellular and molecular studies, biophysical and structural data, patient-derived data, and other sources to build a comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic approaches. Since its inception in 2014, OT has achieved the identification of more than 62,678 drug targets, 6,960,486 genetic associations, 22,274 diseases, and 22 data sources (Open Targets, 2022). ## Research Methods

## Access to Case

This paper presents a longitudinal case study of OT from December 2016 through November 2022. We gained access to OT through EMBL-EBI, the flagship life science research infrastructure in Europe. In 2016, we had our first discussions with EMBL-EBI about critical industrial collaborations with the research infrastructure. EMBL-EBI put us in contact with the OT managerial team, which gave us access to the OT partners. The pharmaceutical sector has traditionally been apprehensive about sharing information. As a result, our access to OT and its partner companies was the result of a continuous, prolonged engagement, building trust over time with progressively more access to teams, data, and information.

After an extensive period focused on a technical understanding of the operations of OT, we sought greater granularity in the analysis. Consequently, we were offered access to a specific experiment and directly mapped a data journey. BIO1 (pseudonym) is an “in-the-making” large-scale experiment in OT that seeks to uncover how genes interact with each other to produce functions in cells that develop in (1) triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and (2) colon cancer (KRAS-mutant CRC). BIO1 is the continuation of a related OT data experiment called SCORE (Dwane et al., 2020) and aims to identify potential therapies by showing how genes modulate other molecules in these types of cancers. This is particularly relevant when the patient no longer responds to a specific treatment; furthermore, it is a reason why new therapeutic strategies have advanced from a one target-one molecule strategy to focusing on the interactions of different targets simultaneously. By doing so, these combination therapies can help overcome primary resistances and target different cell types or functions in synchrony. As a company manager explained: “There’s now some clinical examples where we treat patients with more than one drug simultaneously and that leads to more effective treatments for those patients. But predicting which drugs to combine and in what cancers is really difficult” (CM).

## Data Collection

Our data collection proceeded in four phases from 2017 through 2022. In the initial phase, we embarked on an exploratory investigation into the organizing processes of OT. This allowed us to grasp a fundamental understanding of the actors, their capabilities and motivations, and their ways of interacting. In the second phase, we tailored our data gathering to examine the comprehensive lifecycle of OT’s research processes, specifically focusing on the generation, production, and dissemination of data. Through the phases, our data gathering focused on the specific data work across OT to understand the diverse organizational processes determinative of the evolution of the data. During the fourth phase, we narrowed our focus to an in-depth analysis of BIO1.

Our study’s primary data consisted of 46 semi-structured, open-ended interviews performed with selected key personnel at OT, along with numerous direct observations and informal conversations. Next, through the mediation of the leading managerial team at OT, we gained access to selected OT participants, including the companies, scientific and nonprofit organizations in OT as well as the operational personnel in OT that oversee and support the processes across members (i.e., data and operations managers). The interviews were conducted both online and during study visits to the Wellcome Trust Genome Campus in OT workspaces.

Adopting an iterative approach, we progressively refined our interview protocol, allowing insights gleaned from initial transcripts to guide the conceptual development and thematic exploration in subsequent interviews. Initially, the questions were guided by three key issues: (1) OT organizing processes and infrastructure (technical components and structure); (2) data-making activities among OT organizations to generate, produce, and release data; and (3) how the data potentially create value for upstream and downstream development for OT organizations. In the final stage, to increase the technical granularity and nuance, questions evolved to uncover the specific circumstances and actions in BIO1 data-making processes and their evolution. Table 1 provides details on data collection and uses in the analysis.

Our direct observations were from a three-day study visit at the Wellcome Trust Genome Campus for the OT Integration Days (June 2019); Integration Day meetings, which were held online due to the COVID pandemic situation (June 2021); online observation of the development of BIO1 data (May-November 2022); and a visit to the Wellcome Trust Genome Campus again in November 2022. The Integration Days were organized around a set of discussions and activities aiming to share emerging ideas and methods for new experimental projects, as well as interim outputs of ongoing projects.

While the first author was primarily responsible for the majority of the interviews, the second author focused on reviewing the substantial written documentation acquired, validating the initial interpretations, refining the codes, and conducting research through secondary sources on domainspecific topics such as drug discovery, proteomics, and precompetitive research. For example, as OT’s work has been widely publicized in media outlets, it was possible to collect substantial secondary data from published sources (see Table 1). We combined these data with formal information from OT, together with tutorials about how to use OT data, blog posts, release notes, webinars, workshop presentations, and OT information contained on partners websites and partners’ annual reports. We were also granted access to internal documents about data policy, organizing rules, and specific project-level documentation.

Secondly, given that neither author had formal training in pharmacology, rigorous cross-checks with scientific experts were considered vital to the correct interpretation of the findings. Here, the second author focused on periodic conversations with bioinformaticians and cell biologists to gain a sufficient level of domain-knowledge understanding of cell biology. Additionally, peer-reviewed publications on the topic of target identification and validation in the drug discovery process in genetics, cell biology, and pharmacology were consulted to acquire sufficient subject domain knowledge to inform interview questions and interpretations (Lok & de Rond, 2012).

## Data Analysis

Over the course of this study, we immersed ourselves in the specific ways in which scientists from the pharmaceutical companies in OT and academic labs collectively worked, discussed, debated, negotiated, argued, and reached compromises during the generation and transformation of OT data. We focused on the actions through which data are engineered, aggregated, disaggregated, reconfigured, and considered evidence such that academic labs make scientific claims and pharmaceutical companies base investment decisions on new drug development. Guided by established recommendations for interpretative case study research (Klein & Myers, 1999; Walsham, 2006), we endeavored to embrace the complexity of the phenomenon while simultaneously striving for analytical clarity.

During our initial analytical stage, the first round of coding was open ended. We obtained an overview of the relevant actors, dynamics, and emerging concerns in the data collaboration. As initial themes began to emerge, we noticed the patterns of constant layering or juxtaposing of the private data with commons data, continuous negotiations in the configuration of data, and compromises about how to structure it. ## Findings

Our analysis of the BIO1 journey reveals the political nature of the data journey through the strategies, controversies, and negotiations that have characterized BIO1 from its genesis to maturity. The data journey we disentangle here is far from linear, consisting of various detours and fluctuations occurring across three dynamics; namely patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding (Figure 1). In addition, we reveal heterogeneity in the internal structure of the semicommons, with subgroups forming around common interests that subsequently disbanded to form new collaborations. This dynamic is characterized by strategic decisions to adopt more—or less— transparent postures with respect to other OT members, representing behaviors of selectively sharing and concealing specific elements of the data that directly affected the evolution of BIO1. We qualify this tactical use of opacity and transparency in data commoning as veiling and unveiling. These practices make the dynamics of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding more politically charged— that is, they introduce strategic behaviors where participants often intentionally limit transparency when constructing and evaluating evidence.

In the following section, we briefly describe the journey of BIO1 in a sequence of three broad phases. It is important to qualify, however, that the actual data journey was often nonlinear and disordered; it progressed and regressed, stagnated, swirled, and then, at times, flowed forward. Following this, we delve into the dynamics of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding, reflecting on the deep interrelations among these processes. As a constitutive part of the three dynamics, we reflect on the veiling and unveiling practices that emerged. This further conditioned how data are valued in the construction of evidence; more specifically, what is made available to whom and under which conditions.

## A Data Commoning Journey: The Making of BIO1

BIO1 can be delineated into three broad phases: (1) BIO1 origins, (2) BIO1 building experimental data, and (3) BIO1 dissemination.

## BIO1 Origins

BIO1 originated during discussions between a reduced number of OT pharma and scientific participants on important therapeutic areas. Starting with the most current scientific knowledge, intense internal negotiations in BIO1 led to a joint agreement to focus on target associations for two cancers (i.e., TNBC and KRAS-mutant CRC).

The challenge for the company participants was to influence the collective research focus on KRAS-mutant CRC and TNBC without revealing substantial insights into the confidential research conducted within their own drug development portfolios. Discussions were held about how the

BIO1 data would be built, with participants seeking consensus on certain foundational datasets; namely “anchors” and “data libraries.” The anchors were datasets embodying wellestablished, empirically validated genetic sequences representing the most promising associations with the disease of interest. These formed the bedrock of the KRAS-mutant CRC and TNBC research, providing robust, stable foundations upon which the BIO1 data were built. On the other hand, the data libraries constituted datasets that encapsulate potential genetic associations. While these associations exhibited promising characteristics, their connections to diseases were less substantiated compared to the anchors. Given this structure, the data libraries were expected to evolve as research in BIO1 progressed and new evidence was acquired. Deliberations to define anchors and data libraries were protracted and iterative. Participants found themselves in the challenging position of advocating for certain anchor datasets, all while cautiously avoiding revealing too much about their private research and commercial objectives. This delicate balancing act highlighted the complexity of the negotiations, underscoring the strategic maneuvering that occurred in the development of BIO1. As an OT data manager explained:

The selection of the genes was a big portion of this project. A lot of meetings with the partners. So especially for the selection of the anchors, everybody was like saying, okay, these genes are important for us and we would like them to be included. And that was the consensus about a final list of data anchors. (OT-DM1)

An OT manager expanded: “The anchor genes are created by people … because once we know the anchors, then based on that, we try to increase our chances and we try to add data on the most likely things that we believe” (OT-OM1).

With a more variable component (i.e., the data libraries), the overarching BIO1 dataset became more reflective of the participants’ motives and the evolving state of knowledge. The discussions and negotiations in BIO1 were iterative, frequently revisited, and vivid. As a company researcher described: “Meetings [were] happening quite frequently and updating about list[s] of the genes (anchors), and we are going over them and then some say: I like this or exclude this, include that, things like that” (RS-C1).

The entire process exemplified the interplay between data management, participant negotiation, and scientific understanding in the formation of BIO1.

From this point, the construction of the BIO1 dataset shifted to a more experimental phase, where gene-editing technologies were applied. A common method was the use of genetic editing tools (e.g., CRISPR-CAS9) to inactivate certain gene sequences to disable (i.e., knock out) their function on both normal and diseased cells, which gives researchers the opportunity to partition the effects of specific genes by examining a cell’s behavior with and without the gene of interest. In the context of BIO1, this procedure was carried out on a grand scale; the team conducted 900 different examinations across 50 unique groups of human cancer cell lines. The process was both resource intensive and timeconsuming. As one OT research scientist explained: “We are sequencing this for the last one and a half years and we are almost halfway to it” (RS-NPA2).

## BIO1 Building Experimental Data

From this phase onward, discussions on how BIO1 was progressing intensified. These deliberations were far from neutral; participants engaged in vigorous discussions concerning the optimal methods for classifying, grouping, and restructuring BIO1 to prioritize certain combinations of interest. This process involved imposing an ordered structure on the data, with each participant attempting to align the data structure with their respective therapeutic pipelines. As one company participant described: “There was then also an opportunity for the pharmaceutical partners to ensure that the strategically important things were represented” (CM2). Another pharma participant stated: “There’s a lot of industry level criteria that need to be included” (CM1).

The process of classifying, ordering, and restructuring data to highlight certain elements revealed the priorities and influence of those involved. The pharmaceutical members’ economic resources and wider access to data sources positioned them to advocate more strongly for certain “anchor” datasets. Their voices became louder in the chorus of negotiation and their commercial interests became increasingly salient: “We try to account for everything on the downstream,” explained a company manager (CM2).

On the other hand, scientific participants from the labs, with their unique and specialized knowledge, also exerted influence in shaping the decisions of BIO1. They shed light on overlooked areas and offered approaches to data categorization, effectively swaying the group’s decisions. Their “power” resided in their unique scientific expertise.

The final outcome of these deliberations was a heuristic algorithm that encapsulates the agreed-upon categorizations and criteria. The algorithm determines the least number of cell lines—referred to as “m”—from a specific cancer type in which a combination of genes is predicted to exert a significant effect. This is more than a mere data-crunching tool. It embodies an integrated approach, synergizing multiple strands of evidence to attribute a priority score to each gene combination. This score, which can range anywhere between 0 to 100, provides a metric of relative importance, thus aiding in the production of a prioritized roster of combination candidates for each individual type of cancer. The algorithm is far from absolute in a scientific sense; as expressed by an OT data manager, it is very much a reflection of the negotiations: “There is no ideal scenario, that’s not realistic. So, then it’s really a case of before prioritizing targets, actually we need prioritizing priorities in our categorizations” (OT-DM1).

BIO1 dissemination: Over the evolution of BIO1, project participants engaged in intensive discussions about the maturity of the data, its evidential value, filtering choices, and how to repackage and share it with other partners within OT. When participants agreed that some data had reached a degree of stability—meaning they believed it could be inspected from different angles and consistently produce an accurate prioritization of disease-target associations—the next step was to permit the BIO1 data to travel to the remaining OT partners. Prior to this, however, the data need to be filtered (e.g., removal of negative results, different sets of categorizations with alternative prioritizations, beta versions of the developed computational model, and other messiness from prior stages). After an additional period (18 months minimum), the data generated within OT experimental projects will then be allowed outside of OT into the public domain. The BIO1 data traveling process described here is muddled because: “Everything is being done in a parallel manner to make it as efficient as possible … The computational things are going on, the data production and accuracy part, the labs with other batches, etc.” (OT-DM2). Moreover, the BIO1 data journey does not end there. BIO1 can accommodate additional orthogonal validation rounds with constant pharma pressure to increase its alignment with the drug discovery pipeline.

## Relational Dynamics in Data Commoning

OT data journeys involve many operations that are critical to determining what will be considered data with evidential value. Across the BIO1 journey, we identified three dynamics, which we qualified as patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding as relational forces. While we present them as distinct, they should be considered to be interwoven and complementary.

## Patching

Patching describes the act of establishing relations to or across other data. In the BIO1 case, Patching involved importing additional data sources to BIO1 and aligning the internal datasets with the larger aggregations of data (i.e., internally generated, private, public). Patching highlighted an important aspect in the relational dimensions of data; that is, the evidential value required data to be positioned relative to other lines of evidence from both private and external sources. Patching sought alignment with parallel data sources that were often not disclosed and of variable consistency. This made the distinction between evidence and non-evidence highly contingent on participants’ work with complementary data in a dynamic of constant and collective data transformation that built tight connections across data sources through layering, configuring, and integrating to build meaning in BIO1 (see Figure 1).

First, the layering of diverse data sources was performed by juxtaposing the BIO1 data with other private sources to support the same disease and therapeutic hypothesis. For example, while an initial CRISPR knockout screen may identify n genes whose knockout inhibits cancer cell growth, layering additional data from patient tumor samples, proteomics data, or drug response data can help identify x out of the total genes identified that encode proteins that are potential targets for existing drugs. As expressed by a company research scientist: “If I want to create this combination therapy where I target two genes, it might be more economical and easier if one of the targets is already known and well developed (by us)” (RS-C2).

Second, patching involved configuring heterogeneous data sources. More specifically, data with dissimilar provenance were transformed to make the datasets comparable, commensurate, and integrable as evidence of target and disease associations. As one data manager stated: “There is a normalization and scaling process for the data ... so we can compare different cell lines with each other. So, you run the libraries separately, but we want to make them comparable and then merge them” (OT-DM1).

Finally, patching involved integrating diverse and complementary sources of data to inform the decisions about categories for regrouping target pairs. Examples include the Cancer Genome Atlas, Genotype-Tissue Expression, and clinical data: We also looked at patient information. Data must be aligned following the participants’ need to produce not only “good” data (technically) but also their need to transition from “these data are good” to “these data work for me.”

Patching transpired across the stages of the BIO1 journey differently. In the first phase (i.e., BIO1 origins), extant scientific knowledge and private data sources guided the initial hypothesis development. Hence, patching reflected the provenance of data in an established field of inquiry and illustrated how all aspects of evidence building are connected: “We look for data that adds additional evidence, so we want to generate data related to our working hypothesis and it is not obvious!” (OT-DM1)

The problem is that internal evidence privy to BIO1 participants diverged, and members were not transparent about what data they had: “So, then you sort of start to understand that space without really explicitly anyone saying anything [laugh], you kind of try to figure out, what the interest is and there might be five different opinions obviously” (RS-C2).

Hence, patching was often realized in the constant political negotiations between members who were, at best, vague about the private data they would patch in BIO1. Ultimately, the disputes and compromises made between BIO1 participants regarding which genes to knock out reached a compromise. Specifically, 40 anchor datasets were defined and used with selected data libraries to explore 120,000 possible gene combinations in 50 human cancer cell lines. Deliberations were often heated and political: “The 40 genes are selected based on what they want … It’s really a question of what are you gonna do with it? What is that, what is the strategy after that?” (OT-DM2). Participants also recognized the need to compromise: “We try to understand what is a deal breaker and where is it that we have a bit of a gray zone” (RS-C2).

In the second phase, the patching dynamic involved using alternative parallel computational methods, both within BIO1 and inside the member organizations, to confirm identical targets and disease associations. This patching dynamic utilized primary and orthogonal methods simultaneously, with each member independently employing distinct techniques to validate the same hypothesis. For example, participants used machine learning algorithms to analyze large datasets, such as genomic and proteomic data to identify potential targets. But in addition, they conducted tests using data libraries of chemical compounds on disease model cells (e.g., organoids, organ-on-chip) to determine if any of them exhibited desirable effects. As one company scientist described:

We have different data freezes and in each data freeze, we can do something slightly different. This is based on our understanding of the data up to that point. If we find something else in the data, then we modify the methods and the things based on what we find in the data. (RS-C3)

In the third phase of the BIO1 journey (i.e., BIO1 dissemination), when data was stabilized and prepared for release to other OT participants, participants leveraged a wide range of external and proprietary data for quality control, effectively guiding the data toward interesting downstream development paths. As one pharma participant described: “There’s a lot of room for there to be errors or things that were missed or things that need to be looked into or exciting things that we need to take a deeper dive into” (RS-C2).

In sum, patching describes the dynamics among OT participants as they assessed experimental data and supplemented it with parallel and private data sources to achieve their institutional objectives (i.e., commercial, public health, scientific, or otherwise). Here, the distinction between evidence and non-evidence across the journey remained vague, as various interests contributed to the evidential meaning conferred on the data.

## Deconstructing

Deconstructing involved engaging with the relationships inherent within the produced data, with the objective of attaining a degree of semiotic stability. Data were far from singular in meaning and required a dynamic process of superimposing categories and substructures to comprehend the relationships between pairs of targets and diseases. This dynamic transpired across the stages of the BIO1 journey differently and involved continually decomposing data to build meaning, superimposing categories based on different individual criteria and interests, and rebuilding the data over time to generate meaning for prioritization.

First, decomposing consisted of disaggregating data to produce meaning. In decomposing data, participants dissected the dataset into more manageable and analyzable units segmented in a manner conducive to answering the diverse queries from BIO1 participants: “We’re making more of a complex decision, where [the target] doesn’t just need to be a high performer but needs to be a high performer that also does a, b, c, d, e criteria” (RS-C2).

Second, superimposing categories is a process involving discussions that occurred within the BIO1 group, as well as in bilateral conversations where pharma representatives sought to have greater influence. The nature and priority of the categories that constrained the data were not always agreed upon. As one participant stated when describing the categorizations: “There’s a lot of opinions right now in that space” (OT-DM1). Next, we describe some of the categories superimposed onto the data that reflect the political nature of the dynamic.

Toxicity was the first category superimposed onto the data by most pharma members. The priority of company participants was not only to understand why a target is highly associated with a disease but also to develop a therapy for a nontoxic target. Thus, although toxic targets may be potentially relevant from a scientific perspective, they are not necessarily considered admissible from a commercial point of view. Therefore, these targets were identified and eliminated.

Tractability, the second category, refers to the ability of a molecular compound to bind to a protein to achieve some therapeutic effect. Participants engaged in discussions about structuring gene knockout effect data based on a target’s tractability for drug development. Although it was relevant for commercial participants developing drugs to identify specific targets that are tractable, other participants were interested in having an overview of all scientifically relevant targets. As a result, each gene was assessed using 1 of 10 tractability buckets (with 1 indicating the highest tractability; see Figure 2). Data were then further crossreferenced and categorized into three categories: Tractability Group 1, comprising targets with approved anticancer drugs or compounds in clinical or preclinical development; Tractability Group 2, containing priority targets without drugs in clinical development, but supported by evidence of target tractability; and Tractability Group 3, including priority targets that lack support or sufficient information to determine their tractability. As one participant explained:

That’s quite important, so we [include] things that are tractable and also some of the genes that are [of] interest [to] the partner companies. These might be inhibitors that are being developed by those companies or in progress of development. (OT-OM1)

The third categorization concerns targets with preclinical cancer compounds, whereby the data were prioritized for targets with either approved or preclinical cancer compounds (see Figure 3). The economic rationale behind this categorization was company efforts to repurpose existing preclinical cancer compounds for newly identified targets.

Non-essentiality, the fourth category, aimed to distinguish core fitness genes from context-specific fitness genes. The non-essentiality category adheres to the same logic as the tractability criteria; that is, pharma participants were interested in genes with strong associations with diseases, but they prioritized those that can be treated effectively and safely. If certain targets are essential for the functioning of our cells, we run the risk of damaging important biological processes when targeting associated proteins with therapeutic molecules. Logically, commercial companies showed less interest in targets of this nature:

If you have a gene that when you perturb it, it kills cells and it kills cells every single time, every single cell line in every single context, then it’s likely that if you…were to inhibit that in a patient, that would be really toxic to every tissue. So you don’t want that. (RS-C2)

Finally, novelty represents a fifth category that is particularly important for academic scientists. Novelty refers to the percentage of newly identified genes that were previously undefined as genes known to be involved in essential cellular processes related to specific diseases.

In the BIO1 process described here, clustering data via these superimposed categories was often contentious. Moreover, grouping and ranking the optimal pairing of targets was often entwined and convoluted and diverse visual representations were thus employed to support deliberations.<sup>7</sup> Interest in the evidence of a specific target or medical compound may also vary dramatically throughout a data journey. Perceptions of a drug’s commercial value of a drug can change as alternative therapeutics arrive, patents expire, and international reference pricing evolves (Frank et al., 2022). In our case, this led to shifting decisions, where each data categorization validated certain interests while silencing others. As one participant stated: “What happens is that they go back and, and they [referring to an industry participant] say, okay, well maybe I’m interested in this category now” (OT-DM2). In the words of another pharma participant: “This is a strategic decision [referring to categories] and it is not a static decision. It’s very dynamic” (RS-C4).

Within deconstructing, rebuilding data describes a process of reconfiguring data structures to discern the relationships between the disease and target pairs. At this point, despite potential consensus that the data were sufficiently stable for public release, additional validation tasks needed to be performed with iterative cycles of fragmentation and rebuilding. As complementary evidence crystallized with more stable relationships, increasingly singular interpretations emerged, only to be deconstructed again in a constant cycle of decomposing, superimposing, and rebuilding.

Similar to patching, a nuanced grouping of participants materialized during this decomposing dynamic, signifying a heterogeneous network that transcended the traditional insideoutside demarcations associated with BIO1. This dynamic allowed for a diverse amalgamation of perspectives and capabilities, where participants withheld information about the motivation behind some of the categories superimposed onto deconstructing, which effectively reshaped the fabric of BIO1’s construction.

## Scaffolding

Scaffolding entails the formal efforts and deliberate organization of data that express the relations within data, with a particular focus on ensuring formal consistency to facilitate data traveling. This is a dynamic that occurred throughout the data journey phases (i.e., starting within BIO1, then to the remaining OT participants, and finally, into the public domain). In our case, scaffolding involved formatting, labeling, and scoping data to increase its portability; that is, data were repackaged so that they could more effectively travel to other use contexts (Leonelli 2015).

First, formatting describes a process that seeks to standardize data. In the very first phases of data generation, scaffolding started with political negotiations about how the data would be specified. The data structure was designed and agreed upon by individual participants, using blind (empty) datasets to test internal integration problems at an early stage: “We never get the whole, the full set of data. And sometimes we only get dummy data, which is fine because we only need to see the structure of the data” (RS-C1).

This was relevant for participants because format changes or amendments affected the ways in which data could be analyzed, consequently impacting its meaning and evidential value. As one data manager explained:

They do need our help and say, OK I have my private instance, now how do I inject my private data? But they do not want to share their data with us, so we talk about it in the context; we talk about the structure of their data, so to some extent it is blinded. (OT-DM2)

Second, labeling included discussions around relevant annotations in the metadata, bio-ontologies, procedural information, and other data descriptors. Scaffolding through filtering, assembling, structuring, annotating, and labeling data assemblies was central to building evidence. Yet, as the scaffolding enlarged, it became commensurately fragile. Although bioinformatics is a highly organized domain with specialist data standards and bio-ontologies, scaffolding can become complex when controlling access to specific data. As one participant described:

We have like two sets of data. One set is for the partners [referring to selected participants in BIO1]. They can get the results on all the cell lines, even the ones that we eliminated through the QC [quality control] process, but there’s also the OT preview platform [the internal platform for all OT participants]. So that’s a separate platform where they incorporate all the data together. For that one, we only give significant results from our gene interactions, and we filter out any kind of data that has any inconsistency, we eliminate them here. (OT-DM2)

Third, scaffolding describes a dynamic of scoping the data and continuously determining whether it qualifies as evidence to be shared with other participants both within and outside of OT. In this project, scoping decisions were far from neutral. As one data manager clearly described, when preparing data to be shared among OT partners (i.e., beyond BIO1 participants):

We filter what we do not consider evidence. We filter basically what we consider as neutral information. For example, when you knock out two genes, if the effect you observe is the same as when you knock out the genes independently, that is something we have decided to not consider as evidence because if our goal is to look for interactions between genes, when there is a synergistic or antagonistic effect of both genes, then we have considered evidence only when the data shows cooperative effects between genes. (OT-DM2)

As the data matured over time, a subset of the data was prepared for public release: “at that point, all these data that you’re seeing, they start getting more filtered” (OT-DM2). This process required intense work from OT data managers to adapt the data format for integration into the public layer of the OT platform. It involved increasing BIO1 discoverability and adding metadata related to the BIO1 experiment. The scaffolding of data for public release was a very purposeful repackaging (Leonelli 2015):

So there’s like the sequencing file ... There’s going to be a section on spatial imaging so like images of the tissues and how it was affected, there’s going to be a summary file and a file on how they did their analysis, there’s also going to be information on like the molecule itself that they are utilizing for the CRISPR analysis itself. (OT-DM2)

Unfortunately, even with useful and transparent labeling and descriptors provided, there are no guarantees that the data can travel far as a finished “fact”: “Within these large companies like ours, sometimes that data is disseminated quite broadly and inevitably that can mean that to some extent, that knowledge that we as a project team have, isn’t perpetuated throughout the whole organization” (RS-C1).

Inevitably, scaffolding serves to maintain the integrity of data during its travel, especially when it is shared with unknown audiences, whether within or outside OT organizations.

## Confluence of Patching, Deconstructing, and Scaffolding

We identified three dynamics of the data commons journey as patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding as distinct yet interwoven dynamics of the production and distribution of BIO1 data.

Patching acted as the foundation upon which the relationships between diverse data sources were built. This involved layering, configuring, and integrating disparate data sources into a coherent whole. This patchwork was not static, but rather fueled by the constant influx and integration of new data.

Deconstructing extended patching, as it involved breaking down the relationships within the produced data, aiming to stabilize its meaning. Deconstruction led to a deeper understanding of the relationships that patching built in an iterative process to reconstruct new meanings and insights. It was through these iterations that patching and deconstructing were synergized: Patching offered an initial structure of data, whereas deconstructing explored, broke down, and reinterpreted these relationships, enhancing their progression and evolution.

Scaffolding prepared the data for final passage, enabling external actors to interact effectively with the data, both within BIO1 and, eventually, outside OT. It standardized data descriptors through metadata, labels, and classifiers, thus enhancing its portability by providing an external structure.

Through the complementary amalgamation of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding, the political nature of the data work emerged. Cooperation can eventually increase the depth and breadth of data travel, yet it is fragile. Two forces—cooperation and competition—are intricately woven into the fabric of the data journey, through the deliberate veiling and unveiling of information in the datamaking, creating a delicate yet compelling tapestry of interactions (Table 2). Indeed, as one pharma participant commented:

We try to help them, but they need to read between the lines, if you know what I mean. I can tell them: “Don’t follow this path, it is going to die,” but I can’t tell you how I know it. I also can tell them that that is not the best way to test it, but I can’t say how we test targets in X [the organization], even if we eventually publish about that. (RS-C4)

## Discussion

Through our analysis of data commoning, we empirically examine the equivocal nature of data as raw and neutral products of research (McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012). By contrast, our study supports the notion that processes in the making of data are not negligible (Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2022) but highly relational (Howlett & Morgan, 2010; Leonelli, 2015, 2016) and intertwined with political acts (Edwards, 2010; Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). We extend this perspective by substantiating the dynamics of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding that define how data are transformed throughout the course of a journey. Specifically, our analysis offers three main contributions.

First, we provide a detailed analysis of how life science data are collaboratively constructed and awarded evidential value. We further highlight the political and relational dynamics that define the data-making process in its journey. In so doing, we emphasize relational frictions and nonlinear diversions to empirically demonstrate not only that datamaking is politically charged but also how the meaning ascribed to data is contingent on its provenance—the motivations, strategic behaviors, methods, and instruments used by the various contributors to its journey.

Second, we demonstrate these dynamics in a semicommons context of precompetitive research where private and public interests are simultaneously served, intensifying political strains. Moreover, we show how the boundaries and substructures of the semicommons are quite porous. Consequently, we operationalize the concept of commoning by describing the patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding as contingent on who is contributing under what premises.

Third, we enrich the research tradition that emphasizes that scientific data are neither neutral nor agnostic by describing why and how they are not so. Despite substantial investments in FAIR and OpenDATA movements intended to make data reusable and generalizable, our detailed analysis of the data journey demonstrates the complicated conditions for data mobility that determine the evidential value for those both close and more distant to its production.

We elaborate on these findings and further consider implications for data-centric work and data commons within and beyond the life sciences, addressing both public and commercial agendas.

## The Political Relationship Between Data and Evidence Value

First, our findings affirm the view of data as a perpetually editable, ontological, and epistemologically unstable process (Aaltonen et al., 2021; Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2022; Kallinikos & Constantiou, 2015). We further nuance this description by empirically reflecting on the relational aspect of data (Leonelli, 2015) and its political nature (Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). We bridge two perspectives on data: We embrace the idea of data being continually unstable, contingent, and subject to continuous manipulation (Kallinikos et al., 2013) due to inherent epistemic uncertainty (Aaltonen et al., 2021). We also empirically show how such instability is, to a large extent, a result of the relational and political forces behind the data construction (Leonelli, 2015; Monteiro & Parmiggiani, 2019). Consistent with a view of data fluidity and editability (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Alaimo & Kallinikos, 2022), we interpret the challenges in a political light, where data does not have an inherent meaning but is influenced and interpreted by a range of social and economic factors.

We delineate three dynamics—patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding—that occur as relational actions within and across different sources of data, materials, instruments, computational manipulations, and external actors. Hence, we demonstrate the relational nature of data by showing how data evolve through negotiations and decision points both on the generation and interpretation of data and also illuminate the “packaging strategies” or scaffolding of data that enable its portability, reuse, validation, and valuation (Leonelli 2015, Morgan 2020). We empirically describe how the heterogeneity of actors’ interests and their relations are determinative: Choices of where and how to generate and manipulate and repackage data are influenced by scientific or commercial priorities and—importantly—further tempered by their relative competitive positions. In this respect, it should be noted that the ability to realize commercial value in pharma is often not only a function of understanding how a molecule binds to a protein but also relies on expertise in designing a molecule to optimize its efficacy and minimize its toxicity, the resources to push it through regulatory approval, and access to commercial channels to reach an adequate target market—capabilities that few companies are able to master in their entirety. Consequently, companies with strengths in one aspect or another should carefully consider their collaborative posture when negotiating experimental foci, the criteria superimposed in data categorizations, or the data structure in the context of a semicommons. Here, our research echoes Bowker and Star (2000), who describe data complexity as the “depths of interdependence of technical networks and standards, on the one hand, and the real work of politics and knowledge production on the other” (p. 34). By extension, our analysis focuses analytical attention on the data themselves and reflects on the implications that expectations and material conditions have on data’s evolution.

We further enrich the relational perspective on data by examining the connection between data and evidential value (Borgman, 2015; Leonelli, 2018). We track data movements to unpack how data’s relational capacity affects the everyday epistemic work, from genesis to public release, and how this continues iteratively. While philosophy has a long tradition of understanding the nature of evidence (e.g., Achinstein, 2001; Reiss, 2015; Sismondo, 1993), what emerges prominently in our study is the complex relation between data and evidence as a politically negotiated outcome (Leonelli, 2016): Aggregated data are considered evidence when they are negotiated and agreed upon as having such value. These negotiations are not discrete conversations but rather processes of constant layering, configuring, integrating, decomposing, superimposing, rebuilding, formatting, labeling, and scoping that constitute the ongoing practices of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding. Hence, consistent with Sismondo (1993), we argue that scientific research is more about evidence construction than discovery through an account of how evidence is constructed in the life sciences. However, we do not mean to suggest that the evidence generated in OT is arbitrary, unreal, or inconsequential: While our study clearly shows the prominence of scientific expertise in life science research, we argue that its formulation and acceptance depend on specific social processes and practices.

## Data Commoning in Semicommons

Research on commons calls for greater focus on the nature of data in commons systems (Jarvenpaa & Markus, 2018). To better understand how data commons are fabricated (Vassilakopoulou et al., 2019), we describe a commoning process where divergent interests with strong public and private motivations are manifest as semicommons. We empirically articulate the process of data and evidence making as commoning, characterizing the social practices and political dynamics that encompass and (re)produce commons. This perspective differs from traditions that commoditize data commons as a homogeneous or easily transactable resource. In contrast, we explain the heterogeneity of semicommons data resulting from the specific actions of commoning where data are the common pool resource (Hess & Ostrom, 2003).

By opening the box of data and its evolution toward evidence building (Leonelli, 2018), we award greater protagonism to actors who assign evidential value to data—purposeful actors who are often abstracted away in commons research (Mindel et al., 2018; Ostrom, 1990). Actors’ heterogeneity plays an important role; that is, although it is contradictory to the public rationale for precompetitive research, permissible veiling in a semicommons enables the bounded protection of private interests at specific times and contexts and thereby— counterintuitively—increases the data’s mobility and evolution. This insight accords with research suggesting that openness-closedness is not a binary but rather a continuum (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016), where discretionary levels of veiling can fluidly regulate the balance of individual and collective gains (Vassilakopoulou et al., 2019).

Finally, by considering commoning as a process, we reflect on how data commons are something fundamentally different in composition and essence from what is traditionally described in physical commons (Hess & Ostrom, 2003) and address more recent studies on digital or intangible commons (Mindel et al., 2018). Extensive negotiations and subtle collaborative tactics in OT were needed to position and generate data in the context of an explicit causal hypothesis, proprietary analytical techniques, and the discretionary sharing of complementary insights. Collectively, such dynamics determine how data endogenously evolve in a contentious yet formative process, supporting the cumulative nature of data as both a common pool and a private resource.

## Conditions for Data Movement

The growing phenomenon of data commons and precompetitive research infrastructures speaks to the elevated status that data has attained. Scientific data now represent their own scholarly object, with dedicated journals such as Nature-Scientific Data. The assumption is that data can be shared and, to some reasonable degree, mined, aggregated, and recombined with other data for novel findings across other contexts (Borgman, 2012). Most literature on scientific data sharing strongly attests to the need for normative requirements to include metadata, protocols, and other relevant contextual information in data descriptors (Borgman, 2012; Edwards et al., 2011; Pasquetto et al., 2017). Here, FAIR principles are likely the most recognized set of recommendations to increase the likelihood that data can be easily and effectively discovered and reused (Wilkinson et al., 2016). However, studies examining the utility of data sharing are ambiguous. Research suggests that scientific data travel poorly across disciplines (Edwards et al., 2011) and even within disciplines, as differences in instrumentation and computational methods can limit the mobility and reuse of data, particularly in largescale research infrastructures that have unique attributes or locations that make similar data types innately incomparable (Pujol Priego et al., 2022; Wallis et al., 2013). Finally, there is a tendency in some scientific communities to discount data reuse and replication—or conversely, a naive, build-and-theywill-come premise guiding much data commons investment (Leonelli, 2020).

Our analysis reiterates how scientific data are neither neutral nor agnostic (Leonelli, 2016). Those who play a pivotal role in the construction of data are best equipped to build evidential value from them and benefit from their reuse (Edwards et al., 2011; Howlett & Morgan, 2010). We further argue that it is through the experience of actively contributing to the data journey that actors shape data, embedding their interests and theoretical preferences within the data and identifying their prospective uses. Through the scaffolding dynamic, our findings also call attention to the practices of data standardization, formatting, and repackaging, which are relevant for the mobility of data and their treatment as evidence, particularly for use in the public domain as a social good.

## Implications for Policy and Management

There are few domains where the uncomfortable convergence of profit seeking, public health, and public goods is as extreme as it is in life sciences and big pharma (Buranyi, 2020)—a tension exacerbated by the perpetually increasing costs of drug discovery. Recent experience with the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine development, production, and distribution testifies to the need to orchestrate subtle and powerful levers to motivate capital-intensive pharma to strive toward the greater objectives of public health (Fegan & Cheah, 2021).

With some of the world’s most accomplished scientific institutions and life science corporations, OT represents a case in which the ethos of scientific knowledge as a public good intertwines with that of a profit-seeking business investment. While it may not be reasonable to expect that all data commons will operate with such acute opposition between public and private interests, most data commoning will likely have divergent or competing objectives in some form (Contreras & Vertinsky, 2016). A leitmotif in our analysis is that the general increase in novel data-intensive computational methods is a tendency we expect to observe across other scientifically intensive industries such as chemical, energy, and engineering industries (European Commission, 2022). It can be expected that the movement to develop data commons to share data and capabilities in research and innovation activities will increase (Beck et al., 2020). We suggest that a critical analysis that scrutinizes how data are built, by whom, and why, will be relevant when such data enter the public cannon of scientific evidence.

## Limitations and Future Research

Although we believe our findings have important implications for data commoning, we also recognize that exploring the unique aspects of our context opens possibilities for future research. For one, we study an extreme case of a highly heterogeneous composition of participants in the context of the life science and pharma industries. In this regard, data commoning with more homogeneous resources and actors may have relatively moderate political dynamics and therefore reveal the dynamics of patching, deconstructing, and scaffolding with less intensity. Future research can extend our findings by testing them in contexts that do not have the same level of heterogeneity in the engagement of corporate and public organizations, as well as organizations less sensitive to commercial loss in downstream research and innovation. Second, our study presupposes a degree of openness and willingness to share data among all involved parties. However, this is not always the case in real-world scenarios, where various economic, legal, and sociopolitical barriers often obstruct seamless data commoning. Therefore, our findings may not accurately reflect situations where such constraints are more pronounced.

Further research on the relational notion of data commoning and its political dynamics is an especially fascinating avenue for scholarship given the nascent scholarship on data ontology and collaborative and distributed data (see European Commission, 2022). Relatedly, recent research on deep learning methods in artificial intelligence (AI) applications has highlighted the determinative role of training data in shaping the emergent properties of generative large language models (Bubeck et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022). Such cases clearly emphasize a potential link between the political dynamics of data commoning, which, if used as training data in AI, could manifest as ethical, economic, scientific, or technical bias in prediction models. Finally, longitudinal studies could shed light on the temporal aspects of data commoning and how it evolves as a result of political negotiations and shifts in power dynamics. This could contribute to a more nuanced understanding of data commoning as a dynamic, evolving process.

## Conclusion

As computationally intensive research and development methods pervade other industries, we can expect a commensurate increase in required investment levels, rendering them prohibitive to individual organizations. As semicommons emerge in response, our theoretical contribution lies in delineating the relational and political dynamics of data-making and how these forces are manifested in the context of a semicommons where both public and private interests coexist and are entangled. Our empirical findings offer insight into how and why scientific data and evidence are relationally and politically defined, and further inform policy on how to accommodate these effects to maximize the public value of commons-based research collaborations.
