# Too Busy to Be Manipulated: How Multitasking with Technology Improves Deception Detection in Collaborative Teamwork

Nathan W. Twyman<sup>a</sup>, Je<sup>f</sup>rey G. Proudfoot<sup>b</sup>, Ann-Frances Cameron<sup>c</sup>, Eric Case<sup>d,e</sup>, Judee K. Burgoon <sup>f</sup>, and Douglas P. Twitchell <sup>g</sup>

## ABSTRACT

Deception is an unfortunate staple in group work. Guarding against team members’ deceptive tactics and alternative agendas is di<sup>fi</sup>cult and may seem even more di<sup>fi</sup>cult in technology-driven business environments that have made multitasking during teamwork increasingly commonplace. This research develops a foundation for a nuanced theoretical understanding of deception detection under these conditions. The intersection of information technology multitasking and deception detection theories is shown to produce various and sometimes competing ideas about how this type of multitasking might a<sup>f</sup>ect truthfulness assessments in real-time teamwork. A laboratory study involving a collaborative game helped evaluate the di<sup>f</sup>erent ideas using manipulated deception and multitasking behaviors in a real-time, virtual group environment. The results provide evidence that information multitasking can actually improve deception detection, likely because multitaskers engage less in the team conversation, making themselves less manipulable. As understanding of multitasking bene<sup>fi</sup>ts increases, managers and designers can incorporate e<sup>f</sup>ective multitasking into collaborative processes.

KEYWORDS Deception detection; multitasking; multicommunicating; group work; performance; StrikeCOM; credibility assessment

## Introduction

Deception is an unfortunate staple of group work [6], and computer-mediated environments exacerbate the problem [30]. As if critical and creative thinking were not challenging enough for collaborative projects, team members must gauge the truthfulness of other members generally to guard against the deceptive tactics that collaborators may use to carry out hidden agendas. Failing at this task can result in faulty decision making that undermines project objectives [30] or betrays organizational values. Much evidence suggests that even with plenty of material to work with, accurately gauging truthfulness is di<sup>fi</sup>cult [13] even for experienced personnel [15]. To make matters worse, the computer-mediated collaboration systems that enable dispersed interactions unintentionally create more deception [5], even though they can lead to more information sharing [55].

Gauging truthfulness may be becoming even further complicated by the current ITdriven workplace: There is a growing trend of employee multitasking, probably due to the increased number and mobility of communication technologies, and greater expectations of productivity and accessibility [75, 77, 78]. This increase in multitasking has become commonplace in group meetings and group collaborative work [19, 25]. Such multitasking behavior seems likely to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact a group member’s ability to assess the veracity of the information that is being received. For example, how well can the employee who is responding to texts while in a virtual work meeting detect that a colleague is lying about their ability to complete a critical project? Or, how well can the remote investor who is distracted by a phone call detect that his <sup>fi</sup>nancial team member is purposely overstating expected growth? Endless questions of this nature could be asked, and the answers translate into performance gains or losses. But little is known about how the new technology-enabled multitasking phenomenon a<sup>f</sup>ects deception detection.

The purpose of the present research is to investigate the impact of multitasking on deception detection. Given the sometimes contradictory or competing outcomes in multitasking research, this study seeks to provide a foundation or a starting point for a nuanced theoretical model of deception detection when multitasking, by empirically evaluating truthfulness assessments in a multitasking scenario. This research <sup>fi</sup>rst reviews relevant literature related to deception and multitasking and contextualizes it within the problem space. Multiple possible mechanisms are proposed, including at least one that is unique to the intersection of the two research domains. A laboratory experiment is then described, which involved 129 participants (in groups of three) completing a collaborative seek-anddestroy game. Analyses and results of hypothesis testing are reported. Finally, the implications of this research are discussed.

## Literature Review

As long as humans have been able to communicate, there has been the opportunity to engage in deceptive communication. Many studies have reported on (1) the low accuracy rates of unaided human veracity assessments [13, 26, 38], and many other studies have investigated (2) methods or tools that can be used to augment, and thus improve, humans deception detection abilities [42-44, 59, 64-66, 83, 84]. Research on deceptive communication in information and communication technologies-based group work has received scant attention. This section reviews key studies relevant to deception detection in team environments and information technology multitasking costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts as a foundation for the hypotheses that will ultimately be presented.

## Deception Detection in Team Environments

While some prior research has investigated using groups to detect deception external to the group (e.g., [54, 82]), little empirical research has directly investigated deception detection within the team itself: there is evidence that deception diminishes team performance [29], and that more deception is present in virtual versus co-located groups [30, 50]. To glean more insight relevant to the task at hand, we must look to the deception detection domain more generally.

Deception detection research commonly cites deception theories that argue that liars exhibit behavioral or psychophysiological indicators that can be used to identify their deception. These indicators are posited to come from underlying cognition or emotion “leaking” out even if liars try to appear truthful [27] or in some cases because they try to appear truthful [81, 88]. Much information systems research in this domain has investigated automated solutions (e.g., [40, 64, 66, 81]), partly for e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains (e.g., [59, 71, 83]) but in large measure because ample empirical evidence shows that people are generally not good at detecting deception [26, 38].

However, most of the studies reporting poor detection results are based on binary choice scenarios (i.e., deceptive or truthful) that use one-way interactions or third-person observations (e.g., [28]). The lion’s share of human deception detection accuracy research leverages experiments in which participants identify deception in highly-controlled environments (e.g., a participant sits in a lab and watches a video of an interaction and is then asked to judge whether deception has taken place) [26]. Detecting deception in team settings is di<sup>f</sup>erent. In collaborative settings, truthfulness estimations are not commonly binary — more often, deceptive collaborators lie on a scale. Except in cases of obvious and continuous deception, team members implicitly gauge just how truthful a team member is rather than labeling him or her fully deceptive or completely honest. That truthfulness estimation naturally in<sup>fl</sup>uences how much a team member will rely on or discount another team member’s input.

Real-time team interactions provide another important di<sup>f</sup>erence from much of deception detection research, in that these truthfulness estimations are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by dynamic, unstructured conversation rather than mostly one-way interrogations or third-person observations. Interpersonal Deception Theory proposes that this dynamic nature of interactions heavily impacts both deception and its detection: the deceiver adjusts her communication in response to perceived indicators of suspicion, and the receiver adjusts her communication in response to perceived indicators of deception [18, 49]. Given that most people struggle with identifying deception and tend to show a bias toward presuming truthfulness [47], this back-and-forth process over time seems to favor the deceiver’s success in evading detection [16].

## Information Technology Multitasking Costs and Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts

Multitasking has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the engagement of an individual in multiple goalindependent tasks that are performed concurrently [8, 10], often involving a “shift in the focus of attention when tasks are swapped” [85, p. 2]. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition of multitasking encompasses a wide range of scenarios, from cooking dinner while talking with a family member to texting while driving. Of particular interest to management information systems is the multitasking that occurs during information processes that may impact the value proposition [58], such as the multitasking during collaborative teamwork made possible by information and communication technologies.

Such information technology multitasking is increasingly prevalent due to a number of factors, including the ubiquity of information technologies (e.g., [68]) and the move toward organizational structures that require employees to work on multiple, sometimes distributed teams [22]. Multitasking with technology (hereafter shortened to multitasking) is described in the literature using a variety of terms, including computer-based multitasking, technological multitasking, mobile multitasking, and digital multitasking, and it is closely related to (and can draw on the research of) behaviors such as media multitasking and multicommunicating.

Making use of brief but unavoidable wait time between objectives is one potential bene<sup>fi</sup>t of multitasking [73]. Multitasking may help improve productivity when it takes advantage of this wait time before the primary objective can resume [2]. This is probably why workers who strategically manage multiple team memberships in an organizational setting can enhance productivity [60]. However, multitasking is common even when tasks have little or no such wait time e<sup>fi</sup>ciencies to leverage. Most multitasking research investigates these types of scenarios and reports a drop in performance (e.g., [1, 53, 74]) or an increase in errors [1, 4, 19, 23, 32, 56]. For instance, one study reported that multitasking among online customer service agents creates a reduced rate of problem resolution, resulting in decreased customer satisfaction [35].

Cognitive theories and models of rapid task switching and dual-task interference [56, 62, 86] provide insight into why performance decreases: multitasking usually involves resource interference: the goal-independent tasks simultaneously require the same cognitive resources. This resource allocation problem necessitates either sharing the resource between the tasks or switching the resource back and forth between tasks [67]. The reorienting of cognitive attention when switching tasks introduces performance costs seen in prior studies, such as latency [2, 24], confusion [2, 19], inability to maintain attention or manage information [3], and increased stress and frustration [4, 51]. The negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of increased latency, confusion, poor information management, stress, and frustration may be ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed to the extent that the tasks are dissimilar [33, 57]. When there are no e<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains for multitasking, the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these switching costs become more apparent.

A variety of human factors further a<sup>f</sup>ect a person’s ability to perform e<sup>f</sup>ectively when multitasking, including mental ability [87], emotional state [52], and the person’s polychronicity disposition (i.e., the tendency to favor engaging in multiple tasks vs. a single task at a time [37, 46]). With so many costs and challenges stemming from multitasking, it is not surprising that most multitasking studies identify performance detriments. However, a select few have proposed performance bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of multitasking beyond eliminating downtime. While a majority of extant research on multitasking implies or proposes an inverse linear relationship between multitasking and performance [1, 2, 67], some recent studies suggest that the relationship between multitasking and performance may be more complex [1, 2, 72]. In one study, a moderate amount of multitasking resulted in more errors but better productivity compared to a low amount of multitasking. It was suggested that task juggling triggers an increase in cognitive arousal to match cognitive demand [1]. In addition to increased cognitive arousal, knowledge or skills gained from one task may transfer to another [21], and in some cases unique properties among two tasks can create synergistic e<sup>f</sup>ects when combined [9, 48].

## Truthfulness Assessments When Multitasking

The existing theories and research on multitasking and deception o<sup>f</sup>er di<sup>f</sup>erent and potentially con<sup>fl</sup>icting perspectives on how multitasking during real-time collaboration teamwork may in<sup>fl</sup>uence assessments of truthfulness. A negative relationship between multitasking and performance has been found in a large body of literature from a variety of contexts [41, 61, 76]. Drawing on the oft-cited theories of dual-task interference and rapid task switching [56, 86], we propose a negative relationship between multitasking and deception detection performance. More particularly, we suggest that signi<sup>fi</sup>cant multitasking while engaging in teamwork will create interference, leaving fewer attentional resources available to allocate toward interpersonal communication. The switching costs that will occur as a result may cause lack of attention toward some verbal and non-verbal communication from other team members. In this way, we expect multitasking to diminish a team member’s interpersonal communication performance. We further propose that this lack of conversational attention means less awareness of potential indicators of deception. Truthfulness assessments may su<sup>f</sup>er as a result. Conversely, no multitasking should lead to better communication performance, which in turn increases deception detection due to increased perception of potential deception indicators.

Hypothesis 1a (H1a): Multitasking decreases communication performance.

Hypothesis 1b (H1b): A deception target’s communication performance increases his or her deception detection performance.

However, the aforementioned logic implies that when an individual is completing multiple tasks simultaneously, attention allocation is a zero-sum game. This may not be an appropriate assumption for many multitasking scenarios. In some scenarios, increased task juggling may trigger “increase[d] levels of arousal and mobilize cognitive resources to handle increased levels of load” [1, p. 159]. Thus, it may be that multitasking increases cognitive efort, meaning the total amount of cognitive resources employed [45]. A more cognitively active mind may increase vigilance to the tasks at hand (as opposed to low cognitive activation, where distractions are more tempting), leading to a deeper or more careful processing of the deception cues that are seen and heard. In other words, increased mental stimulation may increase focus and attention given to behavioral cues relevant to assessing truthfulness.

Hypothesis 2a (H2a): Multitasking increases cognitive efort.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): A deception target’s cognitive efort increases his or her deception detection performance.

The above hypotheses investigate the di<sup>f</sup>ering views we extrapolated from existing multitasking research, as we propose they apply to deception detection in teamwork. However, we must also consider the dynamic nature of interpersonal deception [14], which involves sending and receiving communicated deception and suspicion. When the target of deceptive communication is experiencing switching costs, the target’s focus is somewhere other than the team communication.

H1b considers the target’s diminished ability to perceive deception cues as a result, but there is another factor to consider that may counterbalance or outweigh this detriment. We suggest that the target’s attention is hindered; the target’s ability to process deception tactics su<sup>f</sup>ers. As a result, deception that requires a lot of explanation may be too complex for a less-engaged team-member, who will be less susceptible to nuanced manipulation simply because he or she does not deeply process that manipulation.

Perhaps more impactful is the diminished feedback sent back to the deceiver. Deceivers theoretically rely on their target’s feedback to reveal how well their deception is working, and throughout an interaction, the deceiver will adjust his or her own behaviors based on this verbal and nonverbal feedback [16]. If a deceiver’s targets are not engaging well in the conversation, the deceiver may have a corresponding decrease in indicators that allow him or her to e<sup>f</sup>ectively adjust. If these deceiver limitations play a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role than a receiver’s diminished ability to recognize deception cues, we could expect an opposite result to that hypothesized in H1b:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): A deception target’s communication performance decreases his or her deception detection performance.

That these are competing hypotheses is not surprising given that these mechanisms have not previously been explored in this context or in the same study. When theories predict di<sup>f</sup>ering results, competing hypotheses are an appropriate approach to begin evaluating the competing ideas [34, 69]. Because both ideas predict directional e<sup>f</sup>ects, competing hypotheses are di<sup>f</sup>erent from an exploratory approach where no theory is evaluated. Di<sup>f</sup>erent members of the research team favored competing outcomes to the research prior to data collection, which made for an interesting project. While the outcomes of this single study cannot de<sup>fi</sup>nitively support or reject either idea, they can provide initial support for one over the other for this context, and they can provide a foundation for even more nuanced theoretical development in the future.

## Methodology

A laboratory experiment was conducted using ad-hoc, three-person teams. Participants (N = 129) were recruited from the student population of a large university in the southwestern United States. Prior to participation, each participant <sup>fi</sup>lled out a survey that captured demographic and personality data.

## Experiment Description

Each team played a collaborative game called StrikeCOM, which has been previously used for team-related research to measure a variety of phenomena, including group dynamics, communication quality, task performance, mutuality, trustworthiness, and deception [12, 17, 29, 39, 80]. It is one of a growing number of collaborative games used for this type of research application (e.g., [79]). In StrikeCOM, the team’s objective is to identify the location of an uncertain number of enemy targets (e.g., hidden enemy bases) by allocating investigative resources to selected areas on a map grid.

Each member of the team is given a di<sup>f</sup>erent investigative resource, each with varying reliability. For instance, a team member assigned to the intelligence commander role has the option of using a “message interceptor” asset to search three vertically consecutive cells on the grid. The information returned has a lower probability of being correct compared to information obtained from a human spy resource that allows a more restricted search space. The <sup>fi</sup>nal phase of the game is the “strike” phase, at which point the team must decide which grid cells on the map to strike. Team members are not shown the information gathered by other team members, so collaboration is necessary for optimal performance.

## Experiment Procedures

Each member of the three-person teams was randomly assigned to one of three roles: air commander, space commander, or intelligence commander. The three team members were stationed in three di<sup>f</sup>erent rooms to ensure that each participant could not see the other participants’ screens. The team members communicated with one another via audioconference. All three team members watched an instructional video showing them how to play the game and completed a practice round to ensure understanding. They were then given their speci<sup>fi</sup>c mission objective, which was to <sup>fi</sup>nd hidden enemy bases. Participants <sup>fi</sup>lling the intelligence commander role (i.e., deception condition) were given an additional instruction: they were told to try to undermine the team’s mission by doing all that they could to keep the hidden bases from being hit.

The air and space commanders also received additional instructions just before beginning the game. They were told that there was an anonymous tip that someone on the team might be trying to undermine the mission but that it might be a false lead. This had the e<sup>f</sup>ect of producing a team comprised of one deceiver and two truth-tellers who were somewhat primed to look for deception. Past research suggests that warning participants in this manner may by itself improve deception detection performance [11], possibly due to a mitigated truth bias [31]. This feature means caution should be exercised when extending results to teams who implicitly trust one another or similar situations where deception detection is not a conscious subtask in team decision making. We felt the slight priming was a necessary limitation, to counter the possible perception among participants that no deception would occur in a sanctioned laboratory experiment.

The air commander (i.e., multitasking condition) was also given an additional instruction, namely to complete an additional task while playing the game. This task was to answer periodic questions from the “Administrative Leader” using a chat window. During gameplay, this team member typed answers to questions that appeared in the chat window about every 90 seconds by a research assistant. The questions were open-ended, required re<sup>fl</sup>ective thought, and were irrelevant to the game (e.g., “What is the most di<sup>fi</sup>cult activity you have done this year?”). The inclusion of this cognitively engaging but irrelevant task ensured that the air commander was engaged in multiple technology-enabled, goal-oriented tasks that were completed concurrently [8, 10], within a simulated virtual team collaboration environment. This audio and text type of multitasking is among the most common types of multitasking for this context [78].

Data for nine of the participants were excluded because they were unable to complete the game due to technical or scheduling issues. Though monitoring research assistants reported no instances where intelligence commanders failed to deceive, these <sup>fi</sup>ve teams were excluded out of an abundance of caution. For two participants, a survey error prevented collection of truthfulness judgments of the deceiver. This left 35 teams (two of them partial) comprising a total of 103 participants available for analysis.

## Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the impact of multitasking on team members ability to detect deception. Expanding on past research, multiple hypotheses were proposed, two of which were directly competing. The large experiment was designed to help identify the e<sup>f</sup>ect and elucidate which mechanism(s) might be driving the e<sup>f</sup>ect. Table 5 summarizes the empirical support for the proposed hypotheses.

The results show that on average, deception detection improved among those team members who were multitasking — performing an additional disparate communication task. The e<sup>f</sup>ect was present after controlling for personality-based performance e<sup>f</sup>ects and con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels. The same e<sup>f</sup>ect was not seen in truthfulness estimates of truthful team members. That multitasking would improve deception detection in real-time collaborative decision making may seem counterintuitive, because there is little time e<sup>fi</sup>ciency advantage to multitasking.

We anticipated that perhaps the increased cognitive arousal created by multitasking could create this improvement. But that hypothesis was not supported. Multitasking appears to have indeed increased cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort, but that increase does not appear to be the driver of more accurate truthfulness perceptions. Some deception detection literature questions whether the average person has signi<sup>fi</sup>cant ability to detect deception through interpersonal behavioral cues [26, 38]. If such ability is truly minimal, then increased cognition may have minimal impact at best. If individuals do have greater than minimal ability, the performance boost from increased cognition would need to be greater than the performance costs from task switching. That di<sup>f</sup>erence was not present in this case, or at a minimum, was not strong enough to be detected.

Strangely enough, it is the multitasker’s lack of conversational engagement that appears to have improved deception detection. Deceivers morph their tactics over the course of interactions, modifying their deception based on verbal and nonverbal feedback from team members [18, 49]. It follows, then, that when team members provide fewer points of feedback for the deceiver due to being occupied by multiple tasks, the deception may not be delivered or disguised as e<sup>f</sup>ectively, since the lack of feedback inhibits tailoring the manipulations to the target. In short, multitasking may starve the deceiver of feedback, thereby reducing his or her ability to dynamically manipulate the targets of the deception.

Multitasking may also diminish the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of complex deception tactics. The cognitive switching costs associated with multitasking diminish the attention that can be allocated to the deceiver’s attempts to allay suspicion. When deception involves complexity, multitasking receivers may not have time to try and make sense of it, leaving a level of suspicion instead of reasoning it away.

## Implications for Research

The results of this study make important contributions to the general multitasking literature and add to a growing stream of relevant literature within the information systems community [2, 22, 68]. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding that multitasking can have a positive task performance outcome (increased deception detection) contrasts with much of the multitasking literature, which almost uniformly <sup>fi</sup>nds a negative relationship between multitasking and performance. This highlights the importance of studying multitasking in di<sup>f</sup>erent contexts with di<sup>f</sup>erent outcome variables. Furthermore, this study supplies deception theories with insight into how competing tasks a<sup>f</sup>ect the dynamic deception process. To the extent that the receiver’s conversational distraction inhibits the deceiver, alternate methods of detection that similarly limit receiver feedback may improve detection.

Theory involving multitasking during collaborative decision making is surprisingly sparse in management and systems research domains. Given the di<sup>f</sup>ering results among various multitasking research that is extant, it seems likely that properties of the tasks (and the multitaskers) heavily in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relationship between multitasking and performance. We are careful to contextualize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study in terms of the properties of the task that are theoretically relevant to the proposed mechanisms: this was a real-time interactive, team-based decision-making task, paired with a dissimilar synchronous task. The real-time nature of the task is an important constraint, as it precludes the performance gains that would come from eliminating downtime. The interactive nature of the task was also necessary for the performance gains — such gains would theoretically not be expected in one-way information delivery. Future research can be more impactful in this area if it identi<sup>fi</sup>es more key task properties that mediate or moderate the multitasking-performance relationship.

## Implications for Practice

This study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate how multitasking impacts a team member’s ability to detect deception, and it begins to reveal the mechanisms underlying this impact. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the results suggest that in team-based interactive settings, deception detection performance may be increased when detectors multitask and that an increase from performance likely stems from a disruption of manipulative communication rather than from an increase in detective skill. This knowledge has straightforward implications for managing teamwork and designing e<sup>f</sup>ective collaborative teamwork systems, especially in high-stakes decision making.

Particularly in cross-functional team interactions, di<sup>f</sup>erent roles and di<sup>f</sup>erent persuasions can drive overpromising, misrepresentation, or outright fabrication. In attempts to secure outcomes most favorable to themselves, a development team member may overpromise software functionality or a sales team member may misrepresent a customer’s <sup>fl</sup>exibility. When team members are motivated by disparate agendas, deception can often occur. This same dynamic can be applied and adapted more broadly to countless organizational contexts, as deception is present in most interpersonal communication [70]. The overarching practical contribution of this research is the discovery that the growing prevalence of multitasking in organizations today may actually restrict the ability of deceivers to manipulate their targets and thereby yield higher detection accuracy.

A call to encourage this type of multitasking would be an over-prescription because multitasking has many e<sup>f</sup>ects, which were not all analyzed simultaneously in this study. Yet as new types of group support systems, collaboration tools, and communication platforms are developed to help promote the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of organizational teams, the lessons learned from this research should be considered.

## Future Research

The somewhat counterintuitive nature of the results certainly demands follow-up research to verify both the e<sup>f</sup>ect and the apparent mechanism. While a norm in specifying future research is to suggest examining the same phenomenon in di<sup>f</sup>erent cultures and socioeconomic groups, more impactful future research on this topic might further identify how the properties of a multitasking experience a<sup>f</sup>ect performance costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Inhibited communication performance was a result of resource interference, meaning some of the same resources were required for both tasks (e.g., attention allocation, computer use). This interference prevented completely simultaneous operation and introduced switching costs.

However, to the extent that the tasks involved do not share resources, such as listening to music while working, interference and switching costs approach zero. In such cases the e<sup>fi</sup>ciency bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and interaction e<sup>f</sup>ects may come at an extremely low cost. Whether deception detection performance would improve during zero-interference multitasking or other types of multitasking beyond the scope of this study is far from certain. Additional research could build an increasingly comprehensive model. A more complete vision of multitasking costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for various interactive tasks would help managers and experience engineers design collaborative e<sup>f</sup>orts, piggybacking one type of task onto another so as to leverage the unique bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of multitasking involving those activities.

## Conclusion

Team-based decision making necessarily involves truthfulness assessments, and it is increasingly done in the context of multitasking. Prior to this study, research in this area pointed to multiple possible mechanisms. This study also integrated both multitasking and deception detection theories to propose a unique possibility and provided empirical evidence supporting that outcome. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study showed that multitasking can improve truthfulness assessments of deceivers by diminishing the deceiver’s ability to manipulate the multitasking target. These groundbreaking insights call for further examination to create a more nuanced theoretical understanding of multitasking when manipulation is present. They also point to a broader area of opportunity for research and practice, namely, purposefully designing multitasking tools, systems and platforms to produce performance gains and minimize losses. To make this possible, future research needs to identify crucial mediating experience factors and moderating task properties, such as those identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study, which boost or diminish outcomes when those factors and properties are combined.
