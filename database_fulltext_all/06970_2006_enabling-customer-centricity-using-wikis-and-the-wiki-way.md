# Enabling Customer-Centricity Using Wikis and the Wiki Way

CHRISTIAN WAGNER AND ANN MAJCHRZAK

ABSTRACT: Customer-centric business makes the needs and resources of individual customers the starting point for planning new products and services or improving existing ones. While customer-centricity has received recent attention in the marketing literature, technologies to enable customer-centricity have been largely ignored in research and theory development. In this paper, we describe one enabling technology—wikis. Wiki is a Web-based collaboration technology designed to allow anyone to update any information posted to a wiki-based Web site. As such, wikis can be used to enable customers to not only access but also change the organization’s Web presence, creating previously unheard of opportunities for joint content development and “peer production” of Web content. At the same time, such openness may make the organization vulnerable to Web site defacing, destruction of intellectual property, and general chaos. In this zone of tension—between opportunity and possible failure—an increasing number of organizations are experimenting with the use of wikis and the wiki way to engage customers. Three cases of organizations using wikis to foster customer-centricity are described, with each case representing an ever-increasing level of customer engagement. An examination of the three cases reveals six characteristics that affect customer engagement—community custodianship, goal alignment among contributors, value-adding processes, emerging layers of participation, critical mass of management and monitoring activity, and technologies in which features are matched to assumptions about how the community collaborates. Parallels between our findings and those evolving in studies of the open source software movement are drawn.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: customer-centricity, knowledge creation, knowledge management, open source, wiki.

COMPANIES ARE STRUGGLING WITH CHANGES in customer behavior [25]. Instead of seeking information from corporate sources, the product producers or service providers, customers increasingly turn to other customers or customer-centric, third-party Web sites, such as Epinions, eLance, or Citysearch [12]. In an attempt not to have their marketing functions disintermediated, some organizations have begun to embrace the new Web media and to create an open dialog with customers. Others have attempted to engage customers even more thoroughly in a joint informational production process [34], from product development to after-sales (help desk) service.

Discussion forums or blogs, for instance, have become a medium for organizations to facilitate a customer-centric, joint-production process with their “customer communities.” This approach encourages discussion, but frequently limits customers to comment on an organization’s published information content, without being able to change it. In contrast, wiki technology [24, 41] is a platform where anyone—inside or outside the organization—can write or edit other authors’ writings, thus giving customers the opportunity to publicly edit an organization’s public Web presence. Gartner, Wall Street Journal, and BusinessWeek have recently identified wiki technology as an up-and-coming technology to engage customers [2, 15, 22, 36]. The assumption is that those organizations that can truly capture the technology’s potential will experience dramatic improvements in the degree to which customers are engaged with the organization.

Despite this increasing awareness of wikis within the popular press, there has been no research attention given to how wikis are used to enable customer engagement. As with any technology adoption, organizational changes are undoubtedly required (cf. [10]). The unique characteristics of wiki technology are likely to require more structural changes to the organization than other technologies—in large part because wikis can blur the boundary between an organization and its customers (cf. [16]). Consequently, changes may be needed both in the organization serving the customers and in the network of participants who coalesce in the wiki space. In this paper, we examine three case studies of organizations at early stages of using wikis to enhance customer engagement, resulting in a model of successful characteristics to focus future research.

## Customer-Centricity in the Internet Era

## Customer-Centricity Defined

IN RECENT YEARS, INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) has brought considerable customer relationship capability to organizations by capturing and managing customer profiles seamlessly across various touch points. This capability, however, is used to keep the organization “in the center” of the relationship, allowing only “quasi-customized” relationships with each of its customers [33, 34]. Truly individualized, customer-centric relationships have been almost impossible for organizations to maintain because of the vast number of customers with which the organization must interact (cf. [7]). Organizations therefore often respond by predefining touch points and ignoring inputs from customers that are outside of those touch points. For example, during the product development process, lead customers may be invited to join focus group meetings, but many others may be ignored. Empowered by the Internet and their ability to share knowledge and information with others, customers may thus turn to other channels to share their knowledge and feedback about an organization’s products or services. Online user groups discuss issues ranging from corporate strategy to new product development and after-sales service, such as a customer’s newsgroup posting about the faulty Intel Pentium floating point unit [9]. Increasingly organizations find themselves in the position of having to respond to such customer comments (e.g., [20]). Customers are even redefining the meaning of brands as they share their knowledge about product usage, thus reducing the effectiveness of traditional positioning strategies [13].

In this new world of customers sharing their knowledge and reactions openly with others, the notion of customer-centric business is becoming redefined. Customercentricity accepts, as a starting point, the assumption that customers have specific needs and wants that only they can articulate. This articulation occurs not just in communications with representatives of the organization but also in discussions with other customers. These needs are articulated not simply through discussions, but through a cocreation process. Thus, knowledge creation with customers becomes the focus of the customer-centric processes [34].

Customer engagement and cocreation hence become metrics for the success of customer-centric processes. Customer engagement becomes defined as the intensity of customer participation with both representatives of the organization and with other customers in a collaborative knowledge exchange process.

## Technologies to Support Customer-Centricity

Customer relationship management (CRM) systems, while satisfying a considerable need to better understand the customer, fall short of enabling this more acute form of customer-centricity. CRM systems are unprecedented in their ability to capture operational customer information and enable companies to manage and use that information across a range of customer touch points. Yet they capture the information predominantly in ways defined by the organization (and the CRM system’s data structures), rather than by the customer. Consequently, customer insights and information sharing are rarely captured in CRM systems [6].

Technologies to support customer-centricity that complement CRM functionalities and enable customers to cocreate have recently gained popularity. These technologies, when sponsored by the organization, enable customers to quickly, easily, and securely share their ideas with other members of the community, giving customers a relatively free and flexible medium for sharing, while allowing some control and management of the interaction by the sponsoring organization. The three technologies receiving the most recent attention as a medium for such customer engagement are discussion forums, weblogs, and wikis [2]. Common among these technologies is that they are “lightweight” [7] because they do not require the extensive technology and support infrastructure that other applications such as enterprise software demand. Consequently, implementation is often observed by user departments at the “grassroots” level. The language of interaction is simple, necessitating little or no “programming” (due to the use of simple codes or a word processor–like editor, instead of HTML). New content is visible immediately after its posting.

Despite the similarities, there are differences between the three technologies. Weblogs promote a model of first-person storytelling and commenting, whereas forums engage customers in a process of question-and-answer (discussion). In both weblogs and forums, the content is organized chronologically rather than by topic. The wiki way, on the other hand, is intended as a medium for collaborative, topical writing and editing. Customers or organizational representatives can post an idea whereupon others, without special authorization, can modify the text to reflect their own thinking. Over time, with multiple edits, the resulting idea may be of higher quality because it has been exposed to reactions of both customers and organization insiders.

At the most essential level, wiki design and use are founded on a set of 11 principles [24] that jointly define a vision, process, and structure for free and open idea exchange (see the Appendix). These principles, when followed, are intended to lead to open and incremental contributions, as well as serendipitous knowledge exchange. For example, one user may ask a question and in so doing create a hyperlink to an as yet undefined term while another user—unaware of the question—may inadvertently answer the question by independently defining the term. As such, wikis enable idea exchange in surprising and collaboration-promoting ways, described by some as the “wiki magic.”<sup>1</sup> Furthermore, unlike forums or weblogs, where new content is appended to the old, wikis let new information update and overwrite the old while preserving previous versions in a temporal database, together with a record of the change history. In a wiki context, then, customer engagement may be encouraged by customers and organizational representatives contributing knowledge to the wiki, both by posting linked wiki pages and by coediting wiki pages created by others.

While wikis may foster customer engagement, they may also create new problems for the organization. The open engagement of customers in a coediting process makes the organization vulnerable to Web site defacing, destruction of intellectual property, and general chaos. Ideally then, customer-centricity involves not simply higher levels of customer engagement, but higher levels of “constructive” customer engagement. Therefore, our research question is: How do organizations use wiki technology and the wiki way to facilitate higher levels of constructive customer engagement? Our intent was to develop a theoretical model describing factors leading to constructive customer engagement when using wikis.

## Existing Literature on Possible Factors Facilitating Customer Participation

SCHOLARLY RESEARCH ON WIKI USE IS IN ITS INFANCY [41]. Nevertheless, wikis are an outgrowth of an open source community, with the tenets of the “wiki way” following the tenets of open source projects [24]. Although wikis are used for many other tasks than software projects [27], the similarities of the tenets between the wiki way and open source communities suggest that the scholarly literature on open source communities might provide some guidance in understanding how higher levels of constructive customer engagement might be facilitated with wikis. From that literature, five factors have been identified as important for facilitating participation in open source communities (cf. [4, 28, 31, 39]):

• Value proposition. Open source software projects usually build upon an initial core artifact whose value is recognized by potential contributors (such as the original Linux kernel).

• Community expertise (“collective wisdom”). In open source software projects, skill is a significant determinant of an individual’s role in the community, with core team roles usually being attainable only by those possessing significant systems development expertise.

• Governance of the community. Existence of a lightweight structure, but with operational, collective choice, is important in open source projects.

• Processes for cocreation. In open source projects, communities rely on distributed, parallel development, low-participation overhead, and version management.

• Technology. The ability to rapidly create, share, test, and revise open source outputs is facilitated by the use of collaborative technologies for knowledge sharing. In fact, many open source projects use wikis as the collaborative tool for their collaborative efforts. Therefore, having a simple, easy-to-use technology is an important enabler of the engagement process.

Given these five factors, then, we undertook a set of qualitative case studies to explore the role of these factors in encouraging customer engagement when using wikis.

## Research Design

YIN [42] SUGGESTS THAT CASE STUDY RESEARCH DESIGNS are most likely to be appropriate for “how” and “why” questions. Consequently, because our research question focused on how organizations use wiki technology, we adopted a case study research methodology. Moreover, we believed that a case study approach was appropriate because we were interested in studying how customers were engaged in the wiki technology in a natural setting to ensure that the success characteristics we identified would be generalizable to actual customer engagement processes.

While we used the five factors as guideposts in our case study approach, the dearth of existing research theory on the topic led us to adopt an exploratory case study strategy [3]. That is, instead of examining specific propositions for each of the five factors in the case research, we reasoned that a more open-ended approach to our research was required. As such, we approached the case study looking for specific ways in which wikis were used, rather than specific theories for explaining how wikis might be used. We employed a multiple-case research design to identify patterns across cases. Moreover, we carefully selected the cases to represent a range along our dependent variable of customer engagement. In this way, characteristics favorable for customer engagement should be more present in the more successful cases and less evident in the less successful cases. Thus, the multiple cases serve as “replication” logic for our results [42].

To ensure construct validity, we used multiple sources of evidence in our cases. We interviewed as key informants those who either started the organization’s wiki or continued to monitor the wiki’s use. Key informants in all cases included the creators or cocreators of the wiki site. We also obtained statistics about each wiki site. In addition, we examined the wiki sites for access, comments, changes, and other contributions. Finally, for each case, we consulted blogs or Web sites describing opinions about each of the organizations’ wikis. Using these multiple sources of evidence, we were able to develop case studies describing how wikis were used to engage customers.

Finally, to ensure internal validity, we followed a process in which we first developed a description of each case that focused on ensuring that the facts of the case were appropriately depicted. The description was reviewed and approved by the wiki champion for the site. Then, for each case, we examined the description for a list of characteristics associated with wiki use that were present in the case. We did this for all three cases. We then looked for similarities and differences between the three cases, clarifying terms and constructs. The final set of enabling characteristics confirmed that the most successful case had more success characteristics than the less successful cases. A similar approach to multiple-case research has been used in Majchrzak et al. [26].

The three cases of wiki use we developed were the “Boomtown Times” (a pseudonym) wiki editorial experiment, Novell’s Cool Solutions wiki, and the Wikimedia Foundation’s Wikipedia (see Table 1 for an overview of cases). While all three cases represent some level of customer engagement, they vary in the degree of success. The Boomtown Times wiki editorial experiment was the first attempt by a large, mainstream media organization to engage customers in jointly preparing an editorial. The experiment proved that customers were interested in participating in such a venture; however, the experiment was discontinued after repeated site defacings. Novell’s Cool Solutions wiki has not experienced any vandalism since its inception in March 2005. It has achieved a reasonably wide participation rate; however, to date, much of the participation has been limited to FAQs, rather than cocreation. Finally, the Wikimedia Foundation’s Wikipedia has achieved a wide readership, wide authorship, and significant cocreation, sufficient to alter the business model under which encyclopedias operate today.

## Boomtown Times Customer-Written Wikitorial

In June of 2005, Rich, an editorial manager, and Martin, an IT staff person at the Boomtown Times, decided to start an experiment in customer-centric news reporting. The wiki editorial experiment was launched for the purpose of allowing customers— readership of the Boomtown Times—to cocreate well-argued and well-informed opinion pieces.

The premise we had is that . . . there is an aggregate greater value to have many people look at a problem. We had been interested in trying new experiments in the world of opinion because we think opinion is going online and how it would relate to what newspapers already do. (Rich)

As a newspaper, we wanted to be able to engage readers in as many ways as possible. This was another way to do that. Instead of a letter to the editor that is fixed by the editor, this [format] eliminates the middleman. (Martin)

The wiki-based editorial was set up on a single wiki page. The page was seeded with an editorial on Iraq, a topic chosen because of the wealth of information available about it and the level of likely interest among the readership. Announcements in the Boomtown Times then invited visitors to participate in rewriting the editorial.

The process of becoming a contributor was uncomplicated and fast. In general, registrations on wiki sites can be handled in one of three ways—no registration at all; a simple, automatic registration with instant approval where any unique name and password can be used (identification/authentication); or a more complex customer registration process in which registrants must complete forms that are checked for false information before registration is granted (authorization). At Boomtown Times, only simple identification/authentication was required. Therefore, anyone could easily post anything and contribute to the editorial. This arrangement created considerable freedom for all participants, but lacked the establishment of roles and responsibilities within the just-emerging participant group.

Martin, the IT specialist, had the only formally assigned role. He was asked to check the wiki page every few minutes for the first couple of days, and stay in close contact with Rich, the editorial manager. Few technology tools were in place to help Martin or the community of users to monitor site postings or rapidly manage destructive postings.

Snapshots of the wiki taken at several points during the experiment appear to confirm Martin’s and Rich’s views that for the first 24 hours, contributions were constructive, facilitating cocreation among contributing users. For example, one contributing user wrote: “My suggestion is that instead of branching out into alternate op-eds, one option is to hyperlink each sentence.” This comment was followed by other suggestions for organizing the many contributions. The contributions were not completely constructive, however. Some users tried to completely delete the editorial, or replace or vandalize paragraphs with inflammatory comments, These unconstructive contributions were quickly responded to by contributing users, rolling back a deleted editorial to its previous state, deleting contributions unrelated to the editorial, and rewording unconstructive phrasing in a way that would allow the inclusion of multiple opinions in less inflammatory ways. In all, about 20 versions of the editorial were cocreated in this manner during the first 30 hours.

After about 30 hours, one participant, who previously had completed a few rollbacks to counter vandalism, split the editorial into pro and con pages. He explained:

It seems impossible for someone who disagrees with the central theme of the original editorial to both respect the intentions of the authors, and to have a voice. So I’m proposing this [split] page as an alternative to what is otherwise inevitable, which is extensive editing of the original to make it neutral . . . which would be fine for Wikipedia, but would not be an editorial.

With the split, about another 20 versions of the editorial were created by various authors. The original editorial page grew to considerable length, about 2,400 words, with links to off-pages, and thematic photo content. The “counterpoint” page had less support, containing only 300 words, and drawing 40 contributions overall, including numerous defacings that were removed through rollbacks.

By the second day, the popularity of the site had doubled, and with it the attention of unconstructive contributors. Obscene images appeared on the site. About the same time, Slashdot (slashdot.org), a large alternative media site, posted a commentary about the wiki editorial. Slashdot is infamous for the effect it can produce on other sites [1]. Its many readers, if advised to visit another site, create so much traffic that the effect can equal a denial-of-service attack (thus called the “Slashdot effect”). According to some Internet sources, Slashdot’s commentary may have raised awareness and interest in destabilizing the wiki editorial. Regardless of the precipitating factors, the editorial staff at Boomtown Times, as well as customers genuinely interested in cocreating an editorial, were now facing writers who were generally inflammatory in their comments, changed others’ comments, and vandalized the site in ways that seemed intended to destabilize it. Furthermore, for every self-elected Web site “custodian” among the customers, there were many others willing to challenge this role and engage in arguments. Compounding this problem was the number of contributions, which overwhelmed the monitoring capabilities of both Martin and the small number of customers interested in maintaining the site. One user wrote on LAVoice.org,

I made a small edit, but as of a few minutes later, it still has not appeared. This tells me that human editors are monitoring this closely, and filtering the edits to ensure no one makes a complete mockery of it by surrounding the text with BLINK tags. Five minutes later, ah, there it is. Turns out my edit was overwritten by someone else filing an edit at the same time.

Interestingly enough, the user quoted above immediately attributed the delay in his posting to editorial prerogative, a role that Boomtown Times management had explicitly indicated they never played, and which was not enabled by the Mediawiki software. The culprit, in fact, was a Mediawiki feature, namely, not locking a Web page during an edit. With only a few wiki pages available (two editorials and a few discussion pages), concurrent edits and editing conflicts became inevitable. These edit conflicts only compounded the already existing vandalism problems. Hence, without a large enough group policing the site, and few moderation mechanisms in place, the site could not be monitored and maintained at the rate at which changes were made.

The edits were coming so fast and furious that we locked the site down at 1:30 A.M. at which point Martin went to sleep. (Rich)

Within an hour, a hacker unlocked the wiki and a pornographic picture was posted.

A call came into the switchboard at 4 A.M. about the pornography and we decided we didn’t want our organization to be connected to porn. So I took it down. (Rich)

The experiment ended three days after it began.

Despite the editorial’s short life, Boomtown Times’ editors considered the experiment highly valuable, offering them many insights into the challenges of communitybased editing, and convincing them that there is a strong and ardent community interested in cocreating editorials.

## Novell’s Cool Solutions Wiki

In early 2005, Novell created a customer-focused Web site called the Cool Solutions Wiki. Years earlier, Novell had decided to partner with a segment of its customer base using a discussion forum (coolsolutions.com). This segment consisted of technology administrators in schools who were seeking more support for the Novell software they had implemented. At that time, schools were using multiple Novell products in their solutions, and, hence, no single Novell product group “owned” them or supported their needs sufficiently. Sam, Senior Knowledge Manager at Novell, clarifies, “over one hundred products fit into different solutions. Therefore several product experts have to come together when making a sale, plus service, plus consulting, plus hosting, and so on.”

School technology administrators had made known their interest in communicating and sharing with Novell by e-mailing questions, tips, and similar information. “The community was already there,” explained Sharon, the initiator of the discussion forum and wiki site, “we just gave them a place to play.”

A community discussion forum was created in October 2002 using standard bulletin board software. By 2005, the site had 2,700 registered users (including about 1,200 who did not contribute a comment or article but read the posted material). The discussion forum became not just a “place to play” but also a source of valuable information for Novell. Customers and Novell engineers alike could make their postings, usually messages of a few sentences’ length, organized as question-andanswer threads. Full threads sometimes grew four pages long, thus becoming confusing to search. Furthermore, because of the chronological ordering in the forum, newer contributions tended to obscure older ones; this made it difficult to find information, leading people to repeat the same questions (cf. [29]).

The wiki was intended to address these limitations and to become the source for a coedited magazine of articles written by both employees and customers. Novell was already publishing a magazine (originally titled GroupWise magazine) with articles produced by both employees and customers via a typical editorial review process. The wiki was intended to serve a role similar to the magazine but without editorial intervention.

Unedited wiki content on the site created several challenges. Site visitors needed to be informed that wiki-based articles were works in progress, not Novell endorsed, and thus needed to be treated accordingly. Community members, in the beginning, were reluctant to participate either in writing or editing others’ content. According to Sharon, this reluctance stemmed in part from nonnative English speakers lacking confidence in their English writing skills, as well as from a more general concern about changing someone else’s work (despite the code of the wiki way). Nevertheless, more than 1,300 wiki pages were created and modified (by about 1,900 registered users) in the first 11 months of the wiki’s existence, with an increase over time in the number of pages edited by multiple authors. Popular pages have received over 10,000 views each (over 330,000 page views for the site in total). According to Sharon, some Novell marketing managers were initially uncomfortable with the idea that a Novell site might contain content that was not entirely positive, or identified some potential quality issues with Novell products. Sharon explained her response to these marketing managers:

Our view is if [the product] isn’t good, then fix it. Don’t suppress the opinion. In a way, it’s a quality control problem. [Moreover,] if something negative is written and stays on the site, so that the site is not completely positive, customers trust the information more, and feel better about the company. It’s the open source idea. Only it’s open source for information.

Because the site is still in its infancy, it is monitored daily by Novell staff with an expectation to transition to weekly monitoring cycles. Sharon comments on the future of the site:

We hope to get to that point where we feel we can leave it completely to the community to manage the site. A success measure for us will be when the community can take over the wiki.

Participation has been distributed across a variety of customers. Some have written 20-page white papers while others are primarily lurkers. Novell does not mind lurkers but, following proven practice, also intends to give customers different rights and responsibilities according to their involvement. In the initial Novell Cool Solutions discussion forum, used before the wiki was created, organizers identified the most active individuals and elevated them to advisory board members, led by a chairperson. This, in itself, created another reward, a formal recognition of their participation.

The advisory board concept is planned for the wiki site as well. As Novell’s Sam explains: “at the end of the day you don’t depend on a hierarchy, but on the individual. . . . It’s a meritocracy.”

## Wikimedia Foundation’s Wikipedia

Wikipedia was created in the year 2001. Wikipedia emerged from an unsuccessful attempt to create an open encyclopedia written and rigorously edited by a group of subject experts. That attempt (called Nupedia) yielded only 20 articles in 18 months. Hence, Wikipedia’s cofounders were looking for a new process and a new participation model. The plan was to use a wiki and let any volunteer contribute, then use the resulting material as the base from which to “feed” Nupedia. Within six months of going online, a small group of “regulars” and other contributors created almost 6,000 articles. Furthermore, the articles were of sufficient quality to discard the idea of a separate Nupedia with its high-effort editorial process. The fast growth of the early months led Wikipedia’s “chief editor” to believe that it might achieve 78,000 articles by 2008<sup>2</sup> and thus become a viable alternative to commercial encyclopedias. The milestone of 78,000 articles was actually reached six years earlier than expected—in 2002—with Wikipedia doubling in size each year since. As of May 2006, the site contained over 900,000 English-language articles.

Although considered by some a “free-for-all,” without editorial norms or quality assurance, Jimmy Wales points out that there is a process.

It is not true that Wikipedia has no editorial process. It is a new and completely different editorial process, empowered by the technology. It is much faster. Wikipedia [also] has many standards. Most of them have emerged over time from people’s discussions and consensus. There is little formal voting. For example, many people will agree on what might be a useful structure for the bio of a president or the description of a city. These are broad guidelines and easy to follow for people of goodwill.

Asked about the growth of Wikipedia and the incentives for people to participate, Wales talks about small contributions, the “wiki magic,” and the culture among Wikipedians:

Someone may write a three-sentence article in the evening. And then something wonderful happens because in the next morning it may have grown to a seven paragraph article. This creates enthusiasm and involvement in the community. A big part of the magic of Wikipedia is its low barrier to entry; that’s why people are enjoying it.

Wales also points to the overarching interest to contribute to a common good:

Wikipedia is a gift culture, a sharing culture. In fact at a recent meeting with Wikipedians in Germany, there was the discussion about the gift culture issue. Someone likened the gift culture approach to people sharing MP3 files openly over the Internet. But then many people spoke up—and I was happy about that—that sharing MP3 files was actually not at all what Wikipedia culture was about. Wikipedia has a creator culture, not a “cut-and-paste” culture. There is definitely pride of ownership.

One surprising phenomenon about Wikipedia has been the relative lack of vandalism, even though the site is wide open and modifiable by anyone, whether a registered user or not.

There is really very little vandalism, only the occasional vandal. Sometimes people come by and blank pages. But this is very easy to revert. Their IP addresses are logged and if they are found to make unreasonable changes, their IP addresses will be barred and nobody really worries much about it. But if someone is a registered user and logged in, that user can barely be blocked. Maybe for 24 hours. If a user needs to be blocked longer than that, the case goes in front of the arbitration committee. That is a very small number of cases, but takes lots of effort. People get upset, so these cases are evaluated carefully. Not many people want to do this type of administrative work. Many just want to write articles.

Although most people participate in Wikipedia with the goal to write articles, several roles have emerged. Nevertheless, the hierarchy has been rather flat and largely based on contribution and respect in the community.

The core group of Wikipedians is the “sysops.” They become sysops by informal vote. Someone has to vouch for them. However, their rights, when it comes to articles, are no different than those of other users. So a new user can make a change, even if they are not registered, to an article, and there is no “pulling of rank” in principle. Article contribution goes by expertise, not by rank.

## Interpretation of Cases

TO ANALYZE THE THREE CASES, WE IDENTIFIED in each case the five factors from the open source literature that affect participation—value, expertise, governance, processes, and technology. While we found some obvious similarities (e.g., the ease of technology use is important to both open source and customer participation as exemplified by the use of wikis in both contexts; and, in both contexts, value is created through a shared artifact), we found some key differences. The importance of an individual’s expertise in contributing to an open source solution, for example, was not a requirement for customer participation in wikis. Instead, our cases illustrated the reliance on communal wisdom (cf. [35]); that is, one does not have to be an expert to contribute, only has to have a willingness to share an idea that others can build on. Moreover, we found in applying these factors that they did not help us to convey the richness of how customer engagement was fostered with wikis. Through several iterations of analyzing the cases, we realized that the characteristics in these cases that enabled customer engagement were not found within each of the five factors but across the five factors. That is, the enabling characteristics we found in the cases were those that cut across multiple open source factors in new ways to fit the special opportunities provided by wiki-supported customer engagement. From this new perspective on the cases, then, we identified six characteristics that we believe more closely describe how customer engagement is enabled. Because the three cases were aligned from low engagement (Boomtown Times) to high engagement (Wikipedia), our confidence in our characterizations was increased when the highest engagement case (Wikipedia) had more of the six enabling characteristics than the case with moderate levels of customer engagement (Novell), and both Novell and Wikipedia had more of the enabling characteristics than the case with the least engagement (Boomtown Times). Clearly, with only a sample of three, the enabling characteristics we have identified can only serve as propositions, rather than conclusions. For that reason, in the paragraphs below we describe each characteristic and then offer a proposition for further research. The six enabling characteristics are:

• Community custodianship, generating different forms of responsible self-help.

• Goal alignment, creating and maintaining alignable, multiple purposes among participants.

• Value-adding processes with methods and norms that make the process of contributing explicit.

• Allowing for and enabling the emergence of layers of participation within the community.

• Critical level of management and monitoring effort.

• Technologies enabling collaboration and engagement, not just communication.

## Community Custodianship Generating Different Forms of Responsible Self-Help

Comparing the three cases, we noticed that the three differed in their level of community custodianship. In the two cases achieving a higher level of customer engagement— Novell and Wikipedia—particular attention was paid to the notion that customers can and should take on leadership roles both in governance and in maintaining the community, what we call custodians. Both Novell and Wikipedia had customers taking responsibility for content development and monitoring, with Wikipedia, the most successful case, also having customers assume administrative, promotion, measurement, and asset protection responsibility. As Novell’s Sharon noted, “you have to respect the community,” meaning that with a wiki, the community decides what gets posted, not Novell. The Wikipedia case demonstrated that customers can play a variety of different custodianship roles, from creating new content to reinstatement of defaced Web pages. In contrast to Novell and Wikipedia, in the Boomtown Times case, a few customers seemingly tried to exert some custodianship, but the community was still at too early a group development stage (i.e., “forming” or “storming” [37]) to allow this.

Proposition 1: Allowing customers to exert more custodianship over the wiki site leads to greater constructive customer engagement.

## Multiple Purposes Alignable Among Participants

Customer-centric wiki sites exist in the inherent tension between customers who want the highest value experience for themselves, and a company that seeks the highest revenue for the value it provides. Even among customers, the motivations for engaging with the wiki site differ. Some participants may wish to help others, some may seek notoriety by publishing their knowledge, and some may simply try to obtain free advice. Nevertheless, all parties must realize the mutual benefit in this mixed-motive situation, where helping others might help oneself as well.

Examining the three cases indicated that goal alignment was an issue given the most attention by the most successful customer engagement case (Wikipedia), and the least attention by the least successful case. In Wikipedia, those participants who seek notoriety have the opportunity to achieve that goal by being elected to “sysop” roles—a high honor in the Wikipedia community. Those participants who want to help others can do so either through posting articles on a topic of personal interest (and then examining the statistics to see how many people have viewed the page) or by helping others contribute to Wikipedia (by posting suggestions for improving the Wikipedia process). Those participants who want simply to obtain free advice can do so by simply reading the pages; lurkers are welcome (and increase ever important page view statistics).

The Novell case aligned goals as well, providing a mechanism by which a lessattended-to customer segment could be supported. Support came from both Novell individuals who were interested either in the specific customer segment, or in the products the customers were using. Support also came from customers who shared their success stories. While the goals of the Novell users were not as diverse as the goals of Wikipedia users, and thus alignment not as difficult, concern about dissatisfied customers venting their frustrations and directly challenging corporate goals is clearly a concern. Therefore, corporate sites are more likely to be more closely monitored than a neutral site such as Wikipedia.

Finally, in the Boomtown Times case, we observed little attempt to align goals; moreover, the desired goals in the user community not only were diverse but possibly incompatible (even if customers had wanted to align their goals, time was too short to achieve this). There appeared to be users (from Slashdot, for example) that wanted to challenge the strategic decision of a large media organization to enter into a wiki experiment—a goal that obviously conflicted with Boomtown Times’ goal. There were participants who saw the editorial as a way to single-mindedly promote their own worldview—a goal obviously incompatible with the wiki way notion of striving for a neutral point of view. These individuals did not align themselves with the overall goal of creating the most informative editorial possible, regardless of political direction. Our second proposition therefore states:

Proposition 2: Increased alignment among wiki site participants leads to greater constructive customer engagement.

## Value-Adding Activities That Make the Process of Contributing Explicit

The cumulative experiences of Wikipedia participants over time brought about the evolution of rules and processes for contributions. These rules were intended to ensure that artifacts are created and maintained that offer the most value to readers. The rules explain, for instance, that an intervention by the sysops is triggered after three rounds of “tit-for-tat” (where contributors repeatedly delete each other’s contributions through rollbacks). Another example of a rule is that lengthy contributions developed quickly by a single individual without integration into the rest of the wiki (nicknamed “walled gardens”) are frowned upon and targeted for removal. In contrast to Wikipedia, where there are pages and pages of rules, in the Boomtown Times case, such rules did not exist. As a result, for example, participants found themselves engaged in tit-for-tat situations without resolution, exhausting time and goodwill. Novell, at the midpoint on customer engagement, is also at the midpoint on the use of rules: the site posts etiquette guidelines, covering at a minimum that participants are reminded not to attack each other. These demonstrations of the role of the process suggest a third proposition:

Proposition 3: Explicit processes for contributing to the wiki site leads to greater constructive customer engagement.

## Emergent Layers of Participation

Participants in wiki-based communities will exhibit varying levels or intensity of participation. Some will participate actively and extensively, while others will prefer to primarily lurk. All three cases exhibited these varying participation levels. Moreover, in all three cases, the level of participation was not predefined for a specific group, but was allowed to emerge. For example, in the Boomtown Times case, the key informants were asked why they had not hand-selected a set of “editors” or “trusted authors.” The response from both informants was that they did not want to predefine who would be interested in contributing to an editorial; moreover, they did not want to reserve the editorial for an elite few, but instead to “let the man on the street speak”

if he was interested. Similarly, in the Novell case, lurkers were expected and viewed positively. Helping them to learn from the site content was considered valuable. The ratio of lurkers to contributors for Wikipedia is in the range of 1,000:1, with 50,000+ contributors, about a third of them being active contributors, and 4 percent being very active contributors, indicating that several layers of participation are feasible (see Wikipedia statistics, stats.wikimedia.org/EN/TablesWikipediaEN.htm).

In Wikipedia, the most successful of our cases, the layers were most pronounced and defined. That is, there were predefined roles for participants, with different roles having different expectations. Participants could choose among a variety of roles—read only; make minor edits; make significant edits; organize or refactor articles substantially; encourage others to contribute to articles; or become a sysop, an elected member of the administrator group; they were allowed to move into these various layers, and to recommend others for the layers. Ward Cunningham, creator of the oldest, and still active wiki, describes a multistage process for progressing to contributor layers, beginning first with reading the site’s existing material to understanding it (“lurking”), then commenting on others’ materials, then engaging in one’s own writing, then editing to help others explain their ideas, then becoming an organizer who refactors content for better structure.<sup>3</sup> A contributor may also lose interest and fade away, using the site mostly in reference mode. The key, though, is that each layer is propagated: organizers as well as lurkers exist. This suggests our fourth proposition:

Proposition 4: Allowing multiple layers of participation to emerge and be maintained leads to greater constructive customer engagement.

## Management and Monitoring Activity

Being in the open Internet, widely accessible, and generally under the custodianship of customers, customer-centric wiki sites need mechanisms (process, technology, objectives, and community attention) for performance monitoring and management. The challenge is to set up a management system that is “lightweight,” so that the responsibility can be borne by volunteers, and at the same time is capable and robust enough to drive the ongoing success of the site and protect the system from destruction [8]. Such a management process thrives on increased levels of activity but also requires a critical (minimum) level of community activity related to purposeful management and monitoring. Part of the management system is a monitoring function that indicates the current level of contributions to the site and any exceptions, such as a spike in contributions or rollbacks. In all of our cases, we observed the use of monitoring functions that enabled review of version histories, participation, and management functions such as rollbacks. Wikipedia included sophisticated reporting of site statistics, plus a well-developed mechanism to manage disputes, identify new user contributions, and handle questionable items (targets for deletion). In addition to the use of sophisticated monitoring mechanisms, the size and scope of Wikipedia increases the possibility of a critical level of participant activity to review contributions and maintain the social infrastructure. Mistakes, whether caused by deliberate introduction of misinformation or by human error, are usually detected and remedied quickly by the “many eyeballs” (“Linus’ Law”; see [31]) of the community. While there are exceptions with factual errors remaining for a considerable time, evidence suggests that the process is overall surprisingly robust and effective [17]. Moreover, processes have been put into place to maintain critical levels of high-quality participation. For example, selection of a new sysop requires that the person has both made many contributions (based on logged counts of contributions) as well as made high-quality contributions (based on recommendations, endorsements, and the lack of significant objections from others). As another example, new contributors to Wikipedia are, according to cofounder Wales, more closely monitored than those with a track record. New processes related to targeted revision (“projects”) and quality assurance have recently been added to take quality even further.

Novell’s monitoring and management system is not nearly as sophisticated as Wikipedia’s. In part, this is because the amount of traffic to Novell’s site is much lower than Wikipedia’s. Moreover, the concern with Novell’s site is less about ensuring the quality of a contribution than in ensuring adequate customer participation. Thus, Novell’s monitoring and management system consists primarily of close monitoring of recent activity to identify ways to augment contributions so that other contributors are encouraged.

Finally, in contrast to Novell and, especially, Wikipedia, where monitoring was community based and technologically supported, Boomtown Times had put in place only the most rudimentary form of management and monitoring: a single corporate individual (Martin) whose only monitoring role was to remove clearly inappropriate language and images. These practices suggest our fifth proposition:

Proposition 5: Management and monitoring of system activity by the community leads to greater constructive customer engagement.

## Use Technologies That Help Monitor and Manage How Customers Collaborate

All three cases used the same basic wiki technology (Mediawiki), providing a control on the technology features available. Features available for use include the following:

• statistics on site usage (who used each page, how many used each page, trends over time);

• registration;

• statistics on contribution history (who made what contributions);

• version history (what changes were made to the pages by whom and when);

• ability to require different levels of user access rights for log in;

• ability to identify user/contributors by name or IP address;

• ability to automatically identify and block postings that contain unacceptable language;

• ability to automatically block specific IP addresses (e.g., those who violated site posting policies, for example);

• ability to block content by type (e.g., no images, no attachments larger than x kB) to reduce probability of inappropriate postings;

• ability to automatically evaluate dynamic interactions and take action (e.g., when there are more than three “tit-for-tats,” the sysop is notified); and

• ability to institute a “write-lock” to avoid unintentional overwrites.

Despite the range of technology features available, our examination of the actual ones used in each case suggested that each had selectively implemented features depending on their assumptions about collaborative cocreation. Boomtown Times assumed that collaborators would demonstrate goodwill, would be concerned that their comments would be edited by the newspaper, and wanted to move toward a common understanding and shared knowledge about Iraq. Consequently, Boomtown Times installed the most rudimentary registration process, with no content or contributor blocking. Novell assumed that collaborators would demonstrate goodwill, would only contribute if they saw immediate value, and would have no experience contributing in a wiki way. Consequently, just as the Boomtown Times, Novell installed only a rudimentary registration process with no content or contributor blocking, but used summary statistics and version history to carefully track contributions and contributors, and actively augment contributions to provide value. Finally, Wikipedia operates under the assumption that collaboration is a complex, emergent process in which large numbers of collaborators well-informed about how the community is behaving will engage in activities that foster higher-quality products. Therefore, Wikipedia requires no registration process for the editing of article pages (although administrative pages and page creation are restricted) to encourage broad participation but couples this broad participation not only with detailed processes (as described above), but also detailed metrics. Wikipedia volunteers have the capability to obtain rich sets of summary statistics on pages, such as how many read a page, how many made different types of changes, what words have been changed the most, or what has been the trajectory of changes over time.

The success of Wikipedia and partial success of Novell in engaging their customers suggest that their assumptions about how their constituency collaborates are more accurate than the assumptions made by the Boomtown Times case. Therefore, we offer our sixth proposition:

Proposition 6: Using increasing levels of technology that help monitor and manage how customers collaborate promotes greater constructive customer engagement.

## Fit of Task and Technology

Considering the different task environments in our study, it is worthwhile to reflect on the potentially different levels of fit between task and technology [18] in our three cases. Wiki technology, which is essentially a shared, centralized database, works best when there is little opportunity for updates to conflict with each other. This would be easiest to achieve in a single-contributor environment, where only one individual can make changes. However, the benefit of customer-centric systems results from the contributions of many, and all our tasks were intended to benefit from multiuser inputs. This approach is different from the traditional “ask the expert” sites, where a single expert answers all “audience” questions. With multiple contributors, ideally the ratio of wiki pages to authors is high, or authors perform updates asynchronously (not concurrently) [30]. This is true for Wikipedia and the Cool Solutions Wiki, but it was not for the wiki editorial. Furthermore, from a task perspective, it appears that customer-centric sites work best when they harness the information and knowledge possessed by customers instead of collecting and aggregating preferences or opinions (as done by opinion-sharing sites). Wikipedia’s “neutral point of view” captured this principle, and so did the Cool Solutions wiki, although it did invite both information and opinion, with negative opinions being seen as adding credibility. The Boomtown Times editorial was highly opinion based and thus the most difficult to manage via a customer-centric Web site, especially when compounded by a lack of goal alignment and wiki technology characteristics. We therefore offer the following three task-technology related recommendations to companies seeking to engage customers with wiki webs:

• Choose tasks that require the collective wisdom of many, not tasks that are the responsibility or domain of a single expert.

• Select wiki technology to collect and aggregate knowledge and information with a neutral point of view, instead of opinions, especially unaligned opinions.

• Seed the wiki web with a sufficient number of possibly short “starter pages,” or encourage participants to create their own new pages, avoiding an imbalance between a large number of authors and a small number of pages (e.g., target a ratio of one page per author).

## Limitations

Given the exploratory case study research design, there are limitations to the implications of this study. Perhaps the most important limitation of our design is that alternative interpretations of the case studies cannot be ruled out [14, 23]. For example, factors other than wiki use could determine successful customer engagement, such as market conditions, nature of the discussion, nature of the organization hosting the system, or project management principles. None of these were explicitly studied.

We limited our interviews to informants from the site-hosting organizations in order to identify their objectives and activities because their objectives and activities could not be obtained from public Web sites. We obtained views of users by examining postings to each site, as well as blogs and discussion forums referencing each site. For example, Wikipedia participants frequently explain in their user pages “why I write.” This analysis of users’ motives and activities is therefore neither representative nor complete. Future research should couple primary data collection from both users and site-hosting champions.

## Implications for Future Research

Additional research is needed to test the propositions on a large sample of companies where wikis are employed for customer-centricity. Primary data collection from both users and champions would ensure that the propositions represent multiple perspectives. Such a study should consider the important role of potential moderating variables—a role that has been ignored because of the small sample size. For instance, was the lack of a neutral point of view a stronger contributing factor to the Boomtown Times’ lack of success than any of the six dimensions? Are Wikipedia’s nonprofit orientation and virtuality significant contributing factors to its success, in addition to the six enabling characteristics? Research that either controls for these and related potential confounds or explicitly examines their role is needed.

Moreover, we need to explore whether all six enabling characteristics must be present for constructive customer engagement. Is there a complementarity to these conditions, that is, do they interact or substitute for each other in subtle ways? For example, is wiki technology really necessary (we believe it is), or could organizations, for instance, modify their existing discussion forums to achieve similar results? After all, Novell’s Cool Solutions had successfully operated as a discussion forum for several years. And yet, the decision was made to move toward a wiki-based solution (according to Novell’s Sharon, the wiki “would give customers a sense of a solid output”). Our argument would be that the wiki technology enables value-added processes and lowers the “barriers to entry” for cocreation in ways that other technologies cannot. Also, is the type of artifact a significant success factor? Is an editorial (Boomtown Times) more difficult to successfully create than a knowledge site (Novell)? We attributed the difficulties to successful editorial creation largely to a lack of goal alignment, but other explanations are certainly possible.

Other complementarity conditions are worth exploring as well. For example, suppose that wiki participation included not only customers but also participants from competing companies. Competitors would have their own self-interested motives for participating, such as identifying and launching competing products. Is it possible in such an environment that the presence of different levels of responsible self-help among the participants, coupled with close monitoring of proper usage of wiki content, could compensate for the lack of goal alignment?

Additional research is also needed to test the limits of the six propositions. Just as too much information leads to overload [32] and too much filtering leads to balkanization rather than collaboration [38], the six propositions taken to their extreme may lead to less knowledge creation rather than more. While aligning goals may help to keep participants focused on a single task, too much alignment may reduce the elicitation of divergent ideas. While responsible self-help may facilitate peer pressure and feedback, self-help may lead to participants’ reorganizing content, making it more difficult for others to find [11]. While monitoring in real time may be necessary to ensure conformance to norms, some degree of nonconformance may be needed for knowledge creation [26]. Research examining the progress of wiki sites over time is needed to address this issue of the right balance among the enabling characteristics.

Recent research on knowledge collaboration and innovation [19, 21, 39, 40] has begun to examine the innovation model posed by the open source software development community (e.g., [4, 5, 31, 39]). Our research has focused exclusively on customer engagement. The presumption is that greater levels of customer engagement will lead to greater innovation. This is a presumption that very much needs testing. Is it possible that the differences we saw between our six characteristics enabling successful customer engagement and the five open source factors is due to the innovation expected and required in the open source community? Does the reliance on collective wisdom in the wiki context (versus reliance on individual expertise in the open source context) lead to less innovation from wiki participants than open source participants? Not necessarily. The wiki notion that no artifact (e.g., an article) is ever finished and thus can always be augmented may lead to greater innovation than would be observed from the formal release-based structure of open source. Moreover, the layers of participation and governance are more fluid in the use of wikis than in open source. While this fluidity creates a greater reliance on entire communities to monitor and manage contributions compared to the hierarchical structures found in open source communities, such fluidity may also generate opportunities for more divergent knowledge sharing and thus more innovation. Thus, wikis may offer a new model of innovation that extends beyond the model offered by the open source community.

## Conclusion

OUR MULTIPLE-CASE STUDY LEADS US TO SUGGEST that wikis and the wiki way can enable the creation of successful customer-centric Web sites, as long as certain enabling characteristics are in place. Moreover, wikis may be used in a way that defines a paradigm shift in the cocreation of knowledge between companies and their customers.

While the old paradigm demands that customer-facing information systems need to be locked down to remain functional, wiki sites (as long as enabling characteristics are followed) thrive on openness. While the old paradigm demands that knowledge be captured from a few experts through intense processes, wikis thrive on the small contributions from typical customers, who by and large are not experts.

The six enabling characteristics emerging from our research may help to suggest why some efforts at customer-centricity fail while others succeed. Moreover, the dynamism implied by these six characteristics underscores the effects this technology and the wiki way can create in a world with widely distributed expertise and volunteer mentality. Wikipedia has already altered the business model under which encyclopedias operate today, and wikis are ideally suited to allow further dynamic evolution of responsibilities, specializations, participation layers, norms, and goal alignment. As such, wikis present a new model for customer participation in the evolving valuecreation process that was once proprietary to the firm and offer a variety of new research opportunities to explore knowledge sharing and innovation.

Appendix. Wiki Principles [24]

<table><tr><td>Principle</td><td>Explanation</td></tr><tr><td>Open</td><td>If a page is found to be incomplete or poorly organized, any reader can edit it as he or she sees fit.</td></tr><tr><td>Incremental</td><td>Pages can cite other pages, including pages that have not been written yet.</td></tr><tr><td>Organic</td><td>The structure and text content of the site is open to editing and evolution.</td></tr><tr><td>Mundane</td><td>A small number of text conventions will provide access to the most useful page markup.</td></tr><tr><td>Universal</td><td>The mechanisms of editing and organizing are the same as those of writing so that any writer is automatically an editor and organizer.</td></tr><tr><td>Overt</td><td>The formatted output will suggest the input required to reproduce it (e.g., location of the page).</td></tr><tr><td>Unified</td><td>Page names will be drawn from a flat space so that no additional context is required to interpret them.</td></tr><tr><td>Precise</td><td>Pages will be titled with sufficient precision to avoid most name clashes, typically by forming noun phrases.</td></tr><tr><td>Tolerant</td><td>Interpretable—even if undesirable—behavior is preferred to error messages.</td></tr><tr><td>Observable</td><td>Activity within the site can be watched and reviewed by any other visitor to the site.</td></tr><tr><td>Convergent</td><td>Duplication can be discouraged or removed by finding and citing similar or related content.</td></tr></table>
