# The Coevolution of Objects and Boundaries over Time: Materiality, Affordances, and Boundary Salience

Paul M. Leonardi,<sup>a</sup> Diane E. Bailey,<sup>b</sup> Casey S. Pierce<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Building on recent work suggesting that objects and boundaries coevolve in practice, this paper theorizes the mechanisms for such coevolution. We argue that users in organizations choose objects to use for crossboundary collaboration, because they believe that they will afford certain types of communication. Over time, use of the object changes collaboration, which alters the constitution of the boundary at which the object was originally used. When the object’s materiality makes it difficult to achieve collaboration goals, users change objects. New objects provide new affordances for communication, which help to transfer new knowledge across the boundary. As knowledge changes people’s work in practice, the boundary that separates them changes, often requiring the use of a new object with new affordances useful for communicating across the new boundary. We illustrate our emerging theoretical perspective that highlights the role of materiality and affordances in the coevolution of objects and boundaries through a lon gitudinal study of a global product development organization. We discuss how our findings can lead to novel theorizing about the coevolution of objects and boundaries, the role of affordances as the mechanism for this coevolution, and the use of objects in communicating knowledge across boundaries.

## Introduction

Work within organizations is increasingly accomplished by people who must communicate across boundaries. These boundaries may separate people according to differences in, for example, language (Neeley 2012), geographic location (Hinds and Bailey 2003), organizational function (Pinto et al. 1993), ethical standpoints (Lynch and Kline 2000), or knowledge (Knorr-Cetina 1999). Boundaries come to matter in the context of work when they inhibit communication between people based on such differences between them. For this reason, an increasing number of studies have explored how to improve communication across boundaries. The research shows that objects—both digital and physical—provide one promising way to help overcome the communication problems that arise due to the boundaries that separate people. Objects, such as drawings, sketches, databases, graphs, charts, computer models, or physical parts, can facilitate communication in ways that help people to work across boundaries productively (Carlile 2002, Bechky 2003, Kellogg et al. 2006, Ewenstein and Whyte 2009, Nicolini et al. 2012, Metiu and Rothbard 2013).

Objects facilitate communication across boundaries in various ways. As research shows, they may help people to transcend knowledge differences by enabling individuals to assign different meanings to them, thus facilitating coordination without prompting understanding of collaborators’ goals (Star and Griesemer 1989, Galison 1997, Sapsed and Salter 2004 Leonardi 2011a, Majchrzak et al. 2012, Nicolini et al. 2012). Alternatively, objects can aid people in transforming their knowledge so that collaborators can understand it, thus establishing common ground (Bechky 2003, Carlile 2004, Kellogg et al. 2006). Also, objects can enable collaborators to coconstruct new knowledge that becomes the basis of future joint interaction (Henderson 1999, Ewenstein and Whyte 2009, Zeiss and Groenewegen 2009).

Regardless of the ways that objects are used at boundaries, the findings of existing research suggest that the mechanism that makes it possible for objects to facilitate otherwise impeded communication is that the meaning of objects is malleable rather than fixed (Nicolini et al. 2012). When individuals from different sides of a boundary come together to communicate, they negotiate the meaning of an object in ways that enable them to transcend, transform, or coconstruct their knowledge. That an object’s meaning is not static but emerges in the practice of use makes objects useful for facilitating communication (Barley et al. 2012). Yet, although most studies today recognize the plasticity of an object’s meaning at boundaries, they typically take but a snapshot of object use at boundaries at one point in time. Consequently, the majority of studies of object use at boundaries show (inadvertently, most likely) flexible object meaning in the context of stable boundaries.

A rich literature on boundaries has shown that boundaries are neither fixed nor stable. Boundaries emerge and are sustained in practice as individuals negotiate the differences that matter between them by developing processes and routines that have meaning within a community and appear foreign to those who do not engage in similar practice (Lamont and Molnar 2002). If the meanings of objects can change as individuals work across boundaries, and boundaries themselves are changing, it would stand to reason that boundaries and objects are mutually constitutive (Levina and Vaast 2005, 2008). Yet, studies that have acknowledged the possibility of coevolution have not theorized how such an evolutionary process unfolds (Levina and Vaast 2013). Understanding the mechanisms that drive the coevolution of objects and boundaries seems important for both theoretical and practical reasons. From a theoretical standpoint, it is clear that not all objects are useful for communication across all boundaries (Carlile 2004). Furthermore, an object that is useful at one point for crossing a boundary may not be useful later (Galison 1997). Building theory about the coevolution of objects and boundaries may help to explain how and when objects are useful for facilitating communication across boundaries and, importantly, might help to illuminate what happens to boundaries after they are successfully crossed. From a practical standpoint, new technologies offer the ever-increasing promise of crossing boundaries of various types—especially boundaries that separate functions, geographies, and knowledge communities (Leonardi and Vaast 2017). More robust theory about how objects and boundaries coevolve can lead to better strategies for using objects and anticipating some of the hitherto unanticipated consequences that arise when employees work to cross boundaries.

To move toward such theorization of how and why objects and boundaries coevolve, we conduct a longitudinal study of a global product development firm. Our findings show that engineers were separated initially by geographic and knowledge boundaries: the decision to offshore work from engineers in the United States and Mexico to engineers in India enacted a geographic boundary, whereas the difference in levels of product development knowledge across those countries (higher in the United States and Mexico than in India) enacted a knowledge boundary. After an initial attempt with an object that failed to cross either boundary and then, an attempt with an object that crossed the geographic boundary but not the knowledge boundary, the engineers turned to a third object with material properties that combined with social practices in ways that afforded them the ability to communicate across both boundaries. Over time, communication afforded by this third object dissolved the knowledge boundary as Indian engineers’ knowledge changed. At that point, with the materiality of the third object no longer providing the affordances that it once did, the engineers reverted to the first two objects, in which materiality now offered new affordances to engineers that they could not previously appreciate. We conclude our paper by discussing how our findings lead to novel theorizing about the processes through which the coevolution of objects and boundaries occur, the role of materiality and affordances in the context of changing boundaries, and knowledge sharing in organizations. To frame our inquiry, we discuss what is known about objects at boundaries—in particular, how objects meanings are enacted in practice.

## Theoretical Background Boundaries and Objects

Boundaries are the result of differences between people. Boundaries inhibit communication when those differences make it difficult for people to share whatever communicative matter (e.g., knowledge, emotion, interests, values) they wish to share. For example, a boundary arising from differences in knowledge divided workers in the study by Carlile (2002) of new product development, a geographic boundary divided a team in the study by Metiu and Rothbard (2013) of software engineers in the United States and India, and both knowledge and geography divided managers in the study by Koppman et al. (2016) of managers in the United States who worked together with information technology (IT) professionals in India.

All boundaries are enactments that occur in practice. Drawing on the work of Bourdieu and Wacquant (1992), Levina and Vaast (2005, 2008, 2013) argued that, as people pursue joint interests, they inadvertently differentiate themselves from others who do not share those interests, which then creates a boundary demarcating who are insiders and who are outsiders. They suggested that people (“agents” in their terminology) create boundaries through the type of work that they do: “As agents develop a continuity in their local practices, it allows them to act knowledgeably in a given material, historical, and social context. . . . At the same time, however, they are also distinguished from others who do not practice in a similar fashion” (Levina and Vaast 2005, p. 336). In other words, when people work together in a similar way (perhaps because management assigned them to do so), their practice becomes similar to each other’s practice and different from that of those that they do not work with, resulting in a boundary. Many boundaries emerge as the result of formal managerial decisions (like staffing people into different departments, hiring people into specific functions, or locating workers on the same team in offices around the globe) (Levina and Vaast 2008). For example, a managerial decision to offshore work globally would enact a geographic boundary between workers. Although the boundary would initially be created outside of practice (by a decision made by management), the fact that some people would be working in one country and other people would be working in another country would enact the boundary in practice and maintain it over time through the ways that people work (see, for example, Orlikowski 2002). Consequently, as Levina and Vaast (2013, p. 286) argued, “All practice, including boundary-spanning practice take place in fields, and therefore . . . even the boundary itself needs to be understood in relation to a particular field of practice. . . . Through their practices, agents are constantly engaged in shaping fields of practice as well as the boundaries that separate these fields.”

Because boundaries are enacted in practice and because practices shift to accommodate local exigencies (Pickering 2001), boundaries are always in flux. One way that boundaries change is that they may dissolve, which would happen if the difference among people disappeared. For example, a boundary prompted by differences in employment status in a group would dissolve if temporary workers gained permanent employment. Yet, some boundaries may never dissolve, because they are based on immutable differences. Absent changes in team membership, the geographic boundary in the study by Metiu and Rothbard (2013), for example, would remain unless continents collided, an unlikely event. Other studies of boundaries in offshoring show similar results (e.g., Kotlarsky et al. 2014). However, because not all differences among people are important in a given context, not all boundaries matter at all times. Rather, only certain boundaries are salient in a given context. For example, Watson-Manheim et al. (2012) argued in the context of virtual teams that a boundary is salient only if a person perceives that it prompts a discontinuity in work, whereby routine behaviors do not lead to expected actions and flows of information. A second way that boundaries change then is in their salience. Boundaries that never dissolve could nonetheless lose or gain salience, perhaps because some new work practice or event weakened or strengthened their importance to the group. For example, in the study by Barrett et al. (2012), hospital workers’ use of a new pharmacy robot seemingly changed the salience of an occupational boundary, weakening the boundary’s salience between pharmacists and technicians, because both groups’ roles expanded at no cost to the other; however, it increased the salience between these two groups and a third group of assistants who were increasingly neglected by pharmacists and controlled by technicians.

In much the same way that boundaries are enacted in practice and thus, always changing, the meaning of objects that people use in an attempt to communicate across boundaries changes as well. Practice theory suggests that the meaning and use of objects are not given by the objects themselves but that they are instead enacted in practice (e.g., Orlikowski 2000). In other words, an object takes on the meaning that it does as people engage with it and draw on their stock of social, cultural, and institutional knowledge to use it in their work. In doing so, people enact a particular meaning that may be not shared by people with a different stock of knowledge from which to draw when interacting with the object. The examination by Star and Griesemer (1989) of the establishment of the University of California, Berkeley’s Museum of Vertebrate Zoology; the examination by Leonardi (2011a) of a software development project involving re search and development (R&D) scientists, software vendors, and automobile engineers; the exploration by Majchrzak et al. (2012) of coordination in crossfunctional teams from a large knowledge-intensive firm, an industrial design consultancy, and a manufacturing company; the study by Bechky (2003) of technicians, assemblers, and engineers at a semiconductor equipment manufacturer; the examination by Carlile (2004) of engineers from vehicle styling, engine and powertrain engineering, and climate control and safety within an automobile company; and the study by Kellogg et al. (2006) of project management, client services, creative design, and information technology at a web-based marketing company all used objects to transform their knowledge so that they could understand each other’s concerns and all show that objects work as bridges across boundaries, because their meaning is enacted in practice.

Because people enact the meaning and use of an object in practice, that meaning and use can differ across a boundary, or they can change such that people across a boundary agree on a common meaning and use. Although this literature suggested from the outset that the meaning and use of the object can change across boundaries, the boundaries themselves were typically assumed to be fixed and stable in early work. For example, in the study by Bechky (2003), although the meaning and use of a new pump changed, the occupational boundary among assemblers, technicians, and engineers did not. Likewise, in the study by Carlile (2002), the meaning and use of three-dimensional models in automotive engineering changed as engineers from different groups worked together, but the knowledge boundary among those groups and what they were interested in (e.g., safety, styling, performance) did not.

Levina and Vaast (2013) suggested that the enactment of an object’s meaning and the enactment of the boundary in which that object is interpreted and used are closely linked. Following their work, we can view both objects and boundaries as phenomena that are enacted in practice. Given that boundaries produce the practices in which people enact the meanings and uses of objects (Levina and Vaast 2005), and the enactment of an object’s meaning and use allows people to work across a boundary (Levina and Vaast 2005), the most plausible relationship between objects and boundaries would seemingly be one of coevolution. For example, in a case study of offshoring IT work, Levina and Vaast (2008) illustrated how offshore managers with lower social capital and status traversed multiple, overlapping boundaries in their work by leveraging boundary objects to increase their legitimacy to collaborate with their onshore collaborators. For Levina and Vaast (2008), boundary spanners who bring objects into crossboundary collaborations are the primary agents of change. As Levina and Vaast (2005) explained in the summary of their findings of an insurance company’s intranet implementation, “boundary spanners-in-practice” redefine boundaries by creating new boundary objects that are not only useful within a local context but could also have utility within a new joint field.

Other researchers have drawn on the work of Levina and Vaast (2005, 2008, 2013) in the coevolution of boundaries and object use—particularly the insight that objects (often IT artifacts) used in transactive practices can expand access to knowledge, which can then change the contours of the boundary that separates multiple groups (Levina and Vaast 2005, 2008)—to show the importance of object use for the maintenance and change of boundaries. For example, the study by Barrett and Oborn (2010) of offshoring projects showed how certain uses of objects led people to entrench their work in practice and consequently, strengthen the boundary between an onshore site and an offshore site in a way that reinforced existing power structures. Nicolini et al. (2012) argued that people can use objects to create new community where none existed by providing knowledge that a heterogeneous group can share. As the authors suggested, the use of objects to share knowledge in practice can lead to the creation of new boundaries that did not exist before and that demarcate the community. Additionally, the study by Barrett et al. (2012) showed that a special kind of object—a robot—became entangled with the everyday practice of hospital pharmacy work in ways that reconfigured boundary relations among occupational groups. Although it seems from these examples that the objects and boundaries coevolve as objects create the conditions for new practice to emerge, it is not yet clear what mechanism drives this coevolution. Levina and Vaast (2013) hint that there might be something about the object itself and the way that it is used in practice that prompt such coevolution. Therefore, we turn our attention to theories of materiality and affordances.

## Materiality and Affordances

All objects—whether physical or digital—have materiality. Materiality refers to “the arrangement of a technological artifact’s physical and/or digital materials into particular forms that endure across differences in place and time and are important to users” (Leonardi 2012, p. 144). Objects have materiality, because they are constructed out of material properties that afford certain kinds of action and make other actions more difficult. For example, the materiality of the computer simulations that engineers used in the study of Carlile (2002) made it possible to render certain kinds of data into three-dimensional images. Likewise, the materiality of the pump in the study of Bechky (2003) made it possible for actors to see what kind of spacing existed between components by allowing them to put their hands inside the casing.

Our use of the concept of materiality follows a critical realist approach (Mutch 2013). In a critical realist approach, the materiality of an object can stand separate from and often predates use by human actors. This stance does not deny that an object’s materiality is formed through social practices and is enacted in the context of social practice (as argued in an agential realism that denies any ontological sep aration between the materiality of objects and the people who use those objects) (e.g., Orlikowski and Scott 2008, Orlikowski 2010). Instead, it simply acknowledges that objects often enter into a person’s or a community’s practice and present their materiality in ways that require users to cope with the things that they perceive it can and cannot allow them to do (Pickering 2001). As Leonardi (2012a) suggests, a critical realist approach is quite compatible with an agential realist approach, but it begins its inquiry by taking the materiality of an object as a starting point and examining the ways in which that materiality becomes implicated in social practice. From this perspective, after an object has left the developers hands and is implemented in particular organizational contexts, users experience a set of features that do certain things and do not do other things. Those things that it can and cannot do gain importance and are even perceivable, because the people who use them have goals that they would like to use the object to accomplish. Thus, in between the materiality of the object and the socially shaped goals of users is a perception of utility or impediment—of affordance or constraint. As Leonardi (2012b, p. 69) suggests, “it is a testament to the fixed nature of an artifact’s materiality that the variability in perceptions of affordance or constraint that scholars capture is so small. People’s perceptions of the technology are tied to its materiality, which pre-exists their use of the technology.” Put more simply, users are introduced to an object with materiality that has already been preconfigured for them. Although they may be able to change its materiality, even their perceptions of what changes could be made are constricted, to a large degree, by the initial materiality that they encounter when first using the technology (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014).

These perceptions represent affordances for action. Affordances are relational in that they exist between an object’s materiality and the goals of its users (Leonardi 2011b, Faraj and Azad 2012, Volkoff and Strong 2013). Because practice is typically characterized as “recurrent, materially bounded and situated action engaged in by members of a community” (Orlikowski 2002, p. 256), it makes sense to treat practices as the space in which the materiality of an object and the goals of users interact (Leonardi 2012). If affordances are the perceptual mechanism that relates materiality and goals through practice, it is likely that affordances mediate between objects and boundaries.

Objects and boundaries may coevolve as people choose how to use an object’s materiality—enacting specific affordances—in ways that change them or their environment. Because these changes, such as a change in knowledge, can lead to a change in practice (Brown and Duguid 2001), agents produce, reproduce, and transform boundaries through practice (Nandhakumar et al. 2013), and a change in practice can change boundaries (Zeiss and Groenewegen 2009). Such coevolution suggests that any mapping between materiality and boundaries would change as people’s goals and practices (hence, the affordances that they need in the objects) change over time.

If affordances are the mechanism that drives the coevolution of objects and boundaries, capturing them in process empirically would require that at least three important conditions are met. First, it would be important to have a longitudinal study that could show how boundaries and objects change either in response to each other or in parallel. Second, users would need to have the occasion to work across the same boundary at multiple time points with the same object to see whether the meaning of the object changed and whether the enactment or salience of the boundary changed over time. Third, it would be helpful for users to deploy new artifacts over time so as to make clear how the materiality of those objects is seen to afford the ability to communicate effectively across a new boundary or to a differently configured old boundary. The launch of a new captive offshore center at International Automobile Corporation (IAC), a major automobile manufacturer, and the use of that center during the subsequent six years met these three requirements well.

## Methods

## Research Site

Although most of IAC’s engineering workforce resided in its U.S. headquarters, IAC had engineering centers in eight countries, including Mexico. At the end of 2003, IAC opened an offshore center in Bangalore, India for the sole purpose of providing digital engineering services to IAC’s other global centers. The engineers in the India center were novices, in part because Bangalore was not the capital of the Indian automotive industry. IAC management had favored Bangalore nonetheless for two reasons. First, Bangalore was the hub for India’s burgeoning IT offshoring industry. The city had the IT infrastructure to support the sharing of digital engineering objects, and its residents were accustomed to working with global clients. Second, Bangalore was home to the Indian aeronautical industry, which had developed the computational analysis software that IAC’s engineers used for automotive design. IAC’s management thus reasoned that not only was Bangalore home to workers who were accustomed to distributed work but that the city’s engineers were familiar with IAC’s engineering software. As a senior director of engineering at the U.S. center noted, these factors were more important in the selection of Bangalore than automotive engineering task and domain knowledge:

Bangalore made a lot of sense for us because it’s a technical community. Good tech people there. And there’s a lot of offshore work there, so they know how to work already across time zones and distance and the like. Sure, they don’t really know much about vehicle design, but we can teach them that. That’s our expertise. It’s harder to teach them about the technologies or how to be good offshore employees.

Because of hiring freezes over several years, the U.S. and Mexico centers had no junior engineers; IAC management allowed new engineering hires only in India. Thus, engineers at the three centers initially worked across geographic and knowledge boundaries in which novices (and no experts) were in one site (India) and experts (and no novices) were in two others (the United States and Mexico).

## Data Collection

In 2006, we began a synchronic ethnographic study (studying multiple settings at the same time) (Barley 1990) to explore the use of objects at boundaries, with a specific focus on the way that the materiality of the various objects used to communicate across boundaries was perceived and the kinds of affordances that it provided for action. In 2006, the first author spent two and a half months observing the work of six randomly selected engineers at the Mexico center, all of whom regularly offshored work to India. Each engineer was observed for three separate 3- to 5-hour sessions, totaling 94 hours of observation. The first author also spent four months observing 12 engineers (each for 3–5 hours on three separate occasions) at the U.S. center for a total of 126 hours of observation. The first author and the second author, with the help of one other researcher, conducted four person-months of observation of 11 engineers at the India center (each for 3–5 hours on three separate occasions) for a total of 107 hours of observation.

To explore the mechanisms that drove the coevolution of objects and boundaries over time, we paid attention to practice, with a keen eye on the materiality of objects that the engineers used. During our observations, we wrote detailed field notes in which we recorded the engineer’s every action, including typed commands, conversations, software use, and object use. We asked for screenshots when engineers worked on the computer and made photocopies of physical documents engineers that used. We audiorecorded most conversations. After each session, we expanded our running field notes, incorporating into them transcriptions of recorded conversations and full descriptions of the objects that we had documented. The final record of action—dialogue interwoven with descriptions and images of objects keyed to the moment of their use—provided us with rich documentation of objects’ affordances, how engineers used objects in their engineering tasks, and how engineers communicated across boundaries with objects.

At the conclusion of the observations in the United States, Mexico, and India, we conducted semistructured interviews (Kvale 1996) with the informants who we observed and additional informants involved with sending tasks to India or receiving tasks from the United States and Mexico. We used these interviews to clarify and more deeply understand practices documented in our observations. We also asked informants to describe their actions around incidents that we observed in another country (e.g., we asked U.S. center engineers about practices that they undertook around object use that we observed in India). We developed protocols for informants based on location (the United States, Mexico, and India). Interviews ranged in length from 30 to 60 minutes, and they were audiorecorded and transcribed. In total, we conducted interviews with 25 U.S. engineers, 15 Mexican engineers, and 18 Indian engineers.

Our data showed that engineers in the United States and Mexico attempted to communicate across geographic and knowledge boundaries with engineers in India by sending a type of object called a simulation model—a computational analysis of a vehicle—meant to serve as an example in guiding the India center engineers’ creation of new simulation models. However, the simulation models did not share necessary task and domain knowledge; thus, the work of engineers in India was subpar, and offshoring work to India largely faltered. In the United States, offshoring succeeded to a minor degree, because American-trained Indian engineers called “coordinators” worked in the United States to fix poor-quality work from India before the U.S. center engineers received it. Shortly after our publication of these findings (Leonardi and Bailey 2008), the budget crisis of 2008 forced IAC to lay off their coordinators. After that, engineers had to rely more heavily on objects to communicate across geographic and knowledge boundaries.

Given this significant change, we designed a diachronic or longitudinal study (Barley 1990) at the India center to understand how communicating with objects across geographic and knowledge boundaries evolved. To build on the data from our earlier study, we returned to the India center three more times after our initial visit in 2006. In 2008, we interviewed 45 engineers; in 2010, we interviewed 35 engineers, and in 2012, we interviewed 27 engineers. For these follow-up interviews, we used a semistructured interview protocol that varied only slightly from the initial 2006 protocol so that we could chart changes (or lack thereof) in responses to the same questions over time. Over these four time periods, we interviewed many of the original informants multiple times as well as newer engineers who were hired since our previous visit. All interviews were audiorecorded and transcribed.

## Data Analysis

To explore what mechanisms led to the coevolution of objects and boundaries over time, we began by completely reanalyzing the data collected in 2006 and partially reported in Leonardi and Bailey (2008), and we added to it all new data collected in 2008, 2010 and 2012. By analyzing data collected at the India center every two years over a six-year period, we could focus specifically on the changes that occurred to affordances, object meaning and use, and boundaries over time, something that we were not able to examine in our synchronic 2006 data alone. In general, we followed a grounded theory approach to analyzing our data in which we compared and contrasted discussions of objects used for communicating across geographic and knowledge boundaries between engineers in the United States or Mexico and India (Glaser and Strauss 1967, Strauss and Corbin 1994). This approach entailed an iterative process of theoretical sampling: examining examples from the data to build theoretical categories, which we compared and interrelated to form the basis for this paper.

More specifically, in following this grounded approach, our new analysis of the observation and interview data collected in 2006 proceeded through several steps of coding. Table 1 outlines these coding steps. In Step 1, we began by identifying episodes in our field notes in which engineers sent an object from the United States or Mexico to India. Examining these episodes helped us to understand the kinds of problems that engineers faced when attempting to communicate with objects and provided us with many of the examples that we use in our findings. In Step 2, based on what we learned from these episodes in the observation data, we turned to our interviews and identified in them each mention of communicating with objects to the India center. We constructed codes describing (1) who sent the object and (2) what kind of object was sent (e.g., simulation model, PowerPoint slide deck). We then applied these codes to each mention of an object. This analysis helped us to understand the flow and types of objects. In Step 3, we restricted our attention to interviews of the Indi center engineers. We created codes for these interviews that summarized for all objects that we identified above if, on examination of the object, the Indian engineer suggested that he or she was readily able to use the task and domain knowledge communicated via the object to complete work without additional explanation or if the engineer suggested that an object was insufficient for this purpose.

With a list of objects used within this period, we then turned to Step 4: mapping the materiality and affordances of each object. By focusing on discussions of the objects, we constructed codes to capture (1) the noted or revealed material properties of the artifact (e.g., parameter specifications, arrows), (2) the noted or revealed affordances of the object (e.g., choice delineation, indexicality), (3) the meaning of the object and how that meaning emerged, and (4) whether or how an object’s material properties led to the transfer/receipt of task and domain knowledge. In Step 5, we created a final list of codes that linked the type of knowledge that engineers used in their practice in a given period to the evolution of geographic and knowledge boundaries in that period. We repeated Steps 2–5 for all interviews conducted in 2008, 2010, and 2012.

At the conclusion of this process (after we coded the data from each of the four interview rounds according to these steps), we used the recommendations of Lofland and Lofland (1995) for emergent longitudinal data comparison. This use involved three additional steps. In Step 6, we coded to capture the similarities and differences that existed in the types of objects that were sent over time, the way that those objects were used, and who was using them. Step 7 involved coding to capture which boundaries (geographic or expertise) were present in a given period, why those boundaries were present (i.e., how they were enacted in practice), and whether and why those boundaries changed from one period to the next. Step 8 required using the constant comparative method (Glaser and Strauss 1967) to evaluate similarities and differences in the way that codes for objects (Step 6) and boundaries (Step 7) emerged over time and explain why changes in object use and boundary enactment occurred as they did and in relation to each other. We confirmed our qualitative analysis with counts from our coding of mentions in the India center interviews reflecting how often objects of each kind were sent to the India center and to what avail.

We present the results of these analyses below, showing that how engineers used objects to communicate between the U.S. and Mexico centers and the India center shifted as the India center grew and matured. We show how the objects that were used (or the way that those objects were used) coevolved with the enactment of geographic and knowledge boundaries over time. To reflect these changes, we present our findings according to two phases in the India center’s development: fledgling operations (2003–2008) and maturing operations (2009–2012).

## Phase 1: Fledgling Operations at the India Center (2003–2008)

When the India center opened, engineers at the U.S. and Mexico centers were told that they could send simulation work to the India center to reduce their workloads and meet deadlines. According to the managers, the handoff would be straightforward: the engineers would send work request forms specifying the simulation models that they wanted built and the location of the digital vehicle files needed for mode creation. Engineers soon realized, however, that work request forms failed as communication objects. U.S. and Mexico center engineers then sent to India prior simulation models as examples, thinking that the models’ materiality would support the necessary affordances. These models also failed. The engineers next sent PowerPoint slide decks, the different ma teriality of which they perceived to provide different affordances for communication. The slide decks succeeded where work request forms and simulation models did not, because the engineers correctly perceived that decks’ different materiality would enable affordances that proved critical for communicating work requests across geographic and knowledge boundaries.

## Failed Communication with Work Request Forms

Work request forms were electronic document templates with specific fields that were to be filled by the requester. These fields were locked and could not be changed by users. They included information such as the type of analysis that needed to be done, the parameters of the model that were to be tested, and other data points that the creator of the form thought would help to communicate an engineer’s intentions for what he or she needed the India Center to do. As the first row of Table 2 indicates, information entered on work request forms could be typed or if printed, written by hand. This materiality afforded instruction from one engineer to another. The forms had an additional material property that was important—a digitized format as noted on the second row of the table—that afforded asynchronicity and electronic transfer: engineers in the United States and Mexico could send work at hours that were convenient for them, and engineers in India could receive those instructions at hours that were convenient for them. Despite perceiving the forms’ convenient affordances, engineers in the United States and Mexico quickly realized that, for two main reasons, sending work to India was not going to be as problem-free as their managers envisioned.

First, a geographic boundary created specific communication problems. In the highly visual culture in which U.S. and Mexico center engineers were accustomed to working side by side with their colleagues when making work requests, pointing to vehicle simulation models on computer screens while talking, distance made it difficult to ensure that an engineer in India comprehended a request as made in a form. In short, sending engineers perceived too late that the materiality of the work request forms failed to afford the kinds of side-by-side working (“I go to his desk”) and synchronous discourse (“we have a discussion”) that were central to making work requests locally Side-by-side working and synchronous discourse enabled synchronous visualization (“we look at the model together on the screen”), instruction (“trying to tell someone what needs to be done”), indexicality (“I point to parts,” “point to the model”), and viewed affirmation (“he nods,” “watch their eyes”). They also allowed engineers to call up prior models as examples for new work and delineate choices that they had made when creating those models. Supporting only instruction among these affordances, the materiality of the work request form was insufficient in affording the kind of communication necessary for crossing the geographic boundary.

Second, another reason derived from a knowledge boundary. Being new to automotive engineering, India center engineers lacked the task and domain knowledge that they needed to do the work. At the most basic level, because most India center engineers in these early years did not own their own vehicles and because they had not worked in the automotive industry before, they were unfamiliar with the names and locations of many vehicle parts. Because of this enacted knowledge boundary, when a U.S. or Mexico center engineer instructed an India center engineer to model one vehicle part differently than other parts via the work request form, the India center engineer would have no idea which part his colleague meant: the instructions merely noted the part; they did not identify it. Even if the engineer in India correctly identified and located the part, he was unlikely to understand exactly what he was supposed to do with it and why without thorough explanation. In short, the materiality of the work request form also failed to afford the identification, location, and explanation that novice engineers required to find parts within a vehicle design and understand how they were to model them. As one Mexico center engineer observed,

I thought when we started working with India that they were about at the same level as us. But they’re not. They have a lot of learning to do. So we’ve got to get them up to speed on how to do vehicle design so they can be useful resources for us here in Mexico.

On discovering the material limitations of the work request form, engineers in the United States and Mexico hoped to resolve communication problems at both boundaries by sending prior simulation models with their work request forms as examples to help the India center engineers understand how to build models for the current vehicle.

## Failed Communication with Simulation Models

Simulation models of prior vehicles had specific materiality of several types. To begin, each model included a graphic image of the vehicle for which it was designed. Engineers in the United States and Mexico thought that the image afforded the kind of visualization that would enable engineers in India to see how the vehicle’s parts fit together and thus, understand and replicate the work. Prior simulation models also contained specifications for a range of design parameters, such as the type of test to be run and the size and shape of elements to represent the surface of parts. Other parameters governed the choice of vehicle materials and their properties as well as the constraints and loads on the vehicle. Viewing these specifications through menu selections would presumably afford the delineation of U.S. and Mexico center engineers’ choices: engineers in India could look up recorded choices in the prior model and use this information to specify parameters in the current one. Overall, the finished model, in the minds of the sending engineers, afforded choice delineation via a complete example that India center engineers could follow.

From the perspective of the expert engineers in the United States and Mexico, these prior simulation models had meaning as knowledge repositories. The U.S. and Mexico center engineers thought that the engineers in India simply needed to examine the knowledge held in these repositories to discern how to do the work; in their minds, the materiality of the simulation models afforded visualization, choice delineation, and exemplification, which they believed were all required to communicate across a knowledge boundary. The engineers further perceived that the models’ digitized format would afford asynchronous work and electronic transfer, substituting for the sideby-side work that the geographic boundary made impossible. The models’ use would obviate their alternative, which would be for the sending engineers to write out the detailed knowledge that the engineers in the United States and Mexico shared easily via discussion when working in person. As one U.S. center engineer observed,

We almost always send [simulation models] with the jobs we package for India because they help show them [India center engineers] how to build a model a certain way or do a certain kind of analysis. They can just look at the [simulation model] and examine it to see what we did on past products. We have to send them because the Indian engineers are new and they don’t know how to do this kind of work yet. We can’t really teach them because they’re so far away and on a different time zone and they’re at work when we’re sleeping. So the [simula tion model] gives them something to copy. If they copy them [the simulation models] on their tasks we send them now, they’ll get the knowledge they need do the work and we don’t need to write some lengthy doc ument about how to do it.

Although the engineers in India could look up parameter specifications in the simulation models, they could not discern why expert engineers chose those specifications. The models lacked the materiality that would afford such understanding; for example, models lacked the functionality to place a note explaining why the model was built as it was. Understanding the logic implicit in choices was necessary, because the vehicles that the engineers in India worked on were not identical to those in the prior simulation models. Thus, a model was only useful for explaining what needed to be done if the novice could understand why a choice was made; then, she could apply that logic to the current vehicle. In short, although engineers in the United States and Mexico thought the materiality of prior simulation models afforded visualization, choice delineation, and exemplification, these affordances were not sufficient to communicate with engineers in India who did not have the expertise to understand the logic behind choices.

Because India center engineers could not fully enact these affordances, the simulation models held no meaning for them as knowledge repositories. The models did have meaning for them, however, as mechanisms for revealing what knowledge they did not have. The response of an India center engineer on receiving the simulation model in Figure 1 illustrates this meaning. This engineer was tasked with modeling the seventh generation of one of IAC’s midsize luxury vehicles. The vehicle’s structure had changed significantly between the sixth and seventh generations. When the engineer reviewed the model in Figure 1 from the sixth generation, he knew that he could not copy it exactly because of the altered structure. He explained to our observer his confusion:

Now I finally understand what he was talking about when he said to use differential mesh sizes. I just didn’t understand it when he was explaining it via email. But looking at the model I see it now. I see that they used a finer mesh for the lower trim pieces than for the upper trim pieces. As this example makes clear, simulation models sent to India from prior vehicles had meaning for novice engineers at the India center by helping them realize what they did not know. Overall, however, the models were insufficient objects for communication, because when India center engineers interacted with the material properties of the simulation models, they did not have sufficient expertise to fully enact the affordances that expert U.S. and Mexico engineers perceived. This finding is confirmed in the first data row of Table 3, which shows that, although simulations models were mentioned as objects used to communicate across geographic and knowledge boundaries in 83% of our interviews in the years 2003–2008, they were readily used without the need for additional explanation from the sending engineer in only 27% of those instances.

Although phone or email discussions as complements to simulation models helped U.S. and Mexico center engineers communicate with engineers in India, communication remained difficult, in part because the engineers had difficulty co-orienting: the simulation models did not afford the indexicality that would allow an engineer to graphically indicate in real time to which section of the model he or she was referring. To overcome this problem and others with the models, the expert engineers created new objects in the form of PowerPoint slide decks.

## Successful Communication with PowerPoint Slide Decks

As the final row in Table 2 illustrates, PowerPoint slide decks had several material properties that simulation models did not, thus providing, in the eyes of U.S. and Mexico center engineers, new affordances for communicating across geographic and knowledge boundaries. To begin, PowerPoint slide decks, like simulation models, afforded visualization via a graphic image of the vehicle. More helpful than simulation models, the slide decks also afforded part identification and location through screenshot images of the model that sending engineers could zoom in on or rotate to precisely indicate a part. The arrow and circle functions in the PowerPoint software afforded indexicality: by placing arrows and circles in desired locations, sending engineers could index speech (in the form of text boxes) and focus attention. The text boxes themselves afforded explanation. If there was an issue that a U.S. or Mexico center engineer wanted to explain, he could do so by adding a text box on top of the image or next to an arrow or circle. Finally, the PowerPoint slide decks’ digitized format afforded asynchronicity and electronic transfer. Expert engineers used the affordances that they perceived in the decks to digitally capture for distant novices the gestures, mutual gaze, and indexical gestures that would otherwise arise in side-by-side working.

The screen shot showed a dashboard with a hole for the steering wheel on the left. In the middle, where radio and stereo components resided, the expert engineer had placed a red circle and a red arrow. The text box below the arrow told the novice engineer that he needed to add components here and how to find them. ## Phase 2: Maturing Operations at the India Center (2009–2012)

The second phase, which spanned 2009–2012, was marked by two distinct changes from the earlier phase of fledgling operations. First, India center engineers had gained significant task and domain knowledge by 2009 such that the knowledge difference between them and engineers in the United States and Mexico had disappeared, a change that effectively dissolved the knowledge boundary. Previously, each time a U.S. or Mexico center engineer had requested work, the knowledge boundary had been enacted. Over time, because simulation models helped engineers in India to recognize what knowledge they lacked and because PowerPoint slide decks afforded knowledge sharing, India center engineers had to ask fewer questions of their distant colleagues, and U.S. and Mexico center engineers had to share less task and domain knowledge. Although work request forms were still insufficient (because even in side-by-side working, engineers used simulation models to convey instructions), simulation models no longer required augmentation by slide decks. Ultimately, simulation models took on new meaning and use across the geographic boundary that remained after the knowledge boundary dissolved. Meanwhile, a new knowledge boundary arose between senior engineers and recent hires at the India center, providing new meaning and use for PowerPoint slide decks.

Diminished Use of PowerPoint Slide Decks and Shifting Meaning of Simulation Models in India By 2009, engineers who had been with the India center since its launch thought that their expertise was now nearly equal to that of their distant colleagues. As the India center’s managing director noted,

Our senior engineers now are just as good as the engineers in other countries. And I can say this because I worked as an engineer in the U.S. for 20 years. They have really learned how to do automotive design.

U.S. and Mexico center engineers corroborated this claim. As one U.S. center engineer noted,

When we first sent work to India it came back pretty bad. . . . Now, the India center seems to be doing a good job. I’d say they’re really good engineers after working at it for all these years.

No longer needing to share task and domain knowledge with their Indian counterparts, U.S. and Mexico center engineers no longer sent slide decks, which en gineers from all three centers confirmed in 2010:

The [number] of things we send back and forth now is really small compared to what it was a few years ago. I still send them [simulation models] because they can look at those and get the knowledge they need from them with out me having to explain everything. . . . I don’t really send PowerPoints that were marked-up any more like I used to. I just don’t need to. If I say, “It’s right near the plenum” they just know what that is now. I don’t have to show them. —Mexico center engineer

These days we just [say] what we want done [in India] and where the [simulation models] are. Those guys [Indian engineers] look at those models and they can see what we want. They don’t have to come back with questions hardly at all anymore. —U.S. center engineer

We do not need to talk with the Mexican engineers so often and go over various learnings with them and send back and forth all of those PowerPoints. . . . We just need the [simulation models] and we can see what they want and start the work. —India center engineer

These quotes indicate that, although India center engineers’ knowledge had matured, thus rendering unnecessary the affordances of slide decks, the distance separating these engineers from their colleagues in the United States and Mexico still made it difficult to un derstand work requests without the visualization, choice delineation, and exemplification that simulation models afforded. For this reason, U.S. and Mexico center engineers still sent the models with the work request forms. As one U.S. center engineer observed,

Even though the engineers in India have really gotten way better at knowing how to build models, the forms we use to specify a job still don’t work. That’s not the Indian engineer’s fault. Our work is very visual, you know. We have to show things in order to explain and we always work in pictures and images. . . . So we still need to send the [simulation models] to India or they wouldn’t get what to do. That’s the only way we can deal with the distance.

The second data row of Table 3 confirms the change in object use for communication between the United States or Mexico and India. The reported use of simulation models did decrease from 83% of interviews in the first phase (2003–2008) to 77% in the second phase (2009–2012), but that decrease is minor compared with the drop in the use of slide decks, which fell from 70% to just 12%. Slide decks had fallen out of favor, because conveying task and domain knowledge was no longer needed.

## New Use for PowerPoint Slide Decks Within the India Center

Not every India center engineer was an expert in Phase 2. Recently hired novice engineers at the India center lacked task and domain knowledge, creating a new knowledge boundary within the India center. A senior engineer in

India confirmed his new role of teaching novices (or “freshers” as they were called at the India center):

Our freshers are good workers, but they remind me of what I was like when I started here in 2004. They are still learning the names of different vehicle parts and what is normal for certain kinds of loadcases and different standards and practices for meshing. It is our job to teach them all these things. We had our clients from other centers to teach us. Now that we are knowing it, we have to teach the freshers.

To ensure that U.S. and Mexico center engineers were not drawn into spending time interacting with novice engineers at the India center, the managing director of the India center forbid junior engineers in India to communicate with engineers abroad. By having freshers interact only with their seniors in India, project managers ensured the decoupling of the knowledge boundary from the geographic boundary. Now, in Phase 2, the knowledge boundary was only enacted within the engineering center, whereas the geographic boundary was only enacted between the India center and the other centers. Perhaps surprisingly, PowerPoint slide decks that once enabled knowledge sharing across distance now gained meaning and use strictly within the India center. Senior engineers in India used the slide decks to share task and domain knowledge with novice engineers, such as this senior engineer noted in 2010:

I still make many comments on PowerPoint slides for the freshers even though we are not really using these very often with engineers in other countries any more. It is just the easy way to convey the knowledge to them. . . . But I am not sending simulation models to the freshers. Because if they need to understand what I am meaning when I ask them to do something it is easier just to go over to their desks and look at it with them on their screen.

This use of PowerPoint slide decks was novel in the firm; it did not exist in the U.S. or Mexico centers, because engineers in those centers, back when they had novice engineers, could point to physical parts or crashed vehicles for instruction. However, because the India center did not have physical parts or access to crashed vehicles, engineers there used PowerPoint slide decks as a substitute. As one senior India center engineer commented,

If we had physical parts I would be using those when I talked with freshers instead of PowerPoint slides. But we do not have those, so the PowerPoint slides are the only good alternative for showing things when we are side by side.

The engineers at the India center did not use simulation models to teach novices. Rather, they used the models to communicate work requests in the same manner that U.S. and Mexico engineers did when giving instructions to colocated counterparts. Specifically, senior engineers did not send simulation models to freshers, because they did not need to: colocation meant that they simply pulled the models up on a screen, thereby leveraging affordances of side-by-side working, synchronous discourse and visualization, indexicality, and viewed affirmation, with the added affordances of choice delineation and exemplification that the models supported.

As the final row of Table 4 confirms, PowerPoint slide decks were still used within the India center during Phase 2 but for communication internally among senior engineers and freshers. The major change in the meaning of the decks between Phases 1 and 2 was that, in Phase 1, the U.S. and Mexico center engineers intended India center engineers to use the objects to develop task and domain knowledge, whereas in Phase 2, expert engineers in the India center intended for freshers to use the objects to do similar kinds of learning. Thus, although the materiality and the enacted affordances of the slide decks did not change across phases, their meaning and use did.

The last two data rows of Table 3 reflect this new use of slide decks, but not simulation models, for knowledge sharing within the India center. As the third row of the table shows, neither simulation models (6%) nor PowerPoint slide decks (3%) were shared among the India center’s engineers in the first phase (2003–2008): work came to novice engineers from expert engineers abroad, not within the India center, and moreover, novices had little task or domain knowledge to share with other novices. By the later phase (2009–2012), however, as the fourth row of the table shows, senior Indian engineers were now using PowerPoint slide decks (61%), but not simulation models (11%), to teach freshers. These decks were as useful in the colocated context as they had been in the distant context, with 92% reporting ready use in the later phase compared with 91% in the early phase.

## Discussion

A growing number of studies have begun to suggest that boundaries and the meanings of the objects that people use to communicate across them coevolve. Our goal in this study was to move beyond arguments for coevolution to begin theorizing the mechanisms by which such coevolution occurs. As the longitudinal study of communication across geographic and knowledge boundaries at IAC revealed, objects have meaning in the context of the boundaries at which they are used. Objects are used in ways that correspond to the meaning that people ascribe to them.

Because objects have certain materiality that affords particular actions while constraining others, use of an object changes behaviors in ways that shift the practices that enact the boundary. Consequently, certain boundaries dissolve, and other boundaries become more salient. When the boundary changes, the meaning of the object changes too, and in many cases, the object is no longer useful for crossing the new boundary. In such situations, people find a new object with materiality that they view can afford them the ability to communicate successfully across the boundary, and the coevolutionary cycle continues. Affordances enacted in practice in response to communication goals and objects’ materiality are the mechanisms by which the meaning of objects and boundaries coevolves.

Our specific study at IAC shows that this coevolution can be quite messy rather than orderly. Boundaries become more or less salient for individuals as the affordances of an object bring into relief particular uses of its materiality. Take, for example, the case of simulation models that were sent by engineers at the U.S. and Mexico centers to communicate knowledge in India. For U.S. and Mexican engineers, simulation models were objects that had meaning as knowledge repositories. For them, the materiality of the simulation models afforded visualization, choice delineation, and exemplification, which they believed were all required to communicate across a knowledge boundary. Additionally, they also perceived that the models’ digitized format would afford asynchronous work and electronic transfer, substituting for the side-by-side work that the geographic boundary made impossible. However, engineers in the fledgling India center were too novice to be able to even know why the models sent to them by U.S. and Mexico center engineers were built the way that they were. For them, the models did not afford choice delineation, exemplification, or electronic transfer. Instead, the simulation models had meaning for them as tools for revealing what knowledge they did not have. The models afforded them the ability to see what other engineers had done and when reflecting on those choice, to know what they did not know. With this meaning in hand, the India center engineers began requesting PowerPoint slides, the materiality of which would afford the learning of logics and principles of vehicle design. Because the PowerPoint slides enabled the novices to learn, the knowledge boundary between them and engineers in other countries dissolved. When this happened, the meaning of the simulation models changed for Indian engineers to become knowledge repositories, thus aligning the meanings of engineers on both sides of the geographic boundary. With the knowledge boundary dissolved, the geographic boundary became more salient, and IAC reinstated the use of work request forms that now also had new meaning to both parties and with materiality that now afforded speedy communication of project guidelines where previously it did not.

This rather messy process of coevolution showcases the role of affordances as a primary mechanism. It also shows how, through such affordances, the materiality of objects brings new boundaries into salience that must be confronted and dealt with by parties on either side of it in ways that may undermine the original intended use of objects. Time and change over time are critical ingredients to the recipe of coevolution of objects and boundaries. Our specification of affordances as mechanisms for the coevolutionary process offers several important implications for our existing theories of objects and boundaries and the way that communication flows or is impeded as objects are used to cross boundaries.

## Materiality’s Role in the Coevolution of Objects and Boundaries

One important contribution of our findings is the empirical specification of how objects and boundaries coevolve. In our study, the mechanism through which objects and boundaries coevolved was knowledge change, which was enabled by objects that afforded clear communication. When scholars talk about groups of people who use objects for communication across boundaries, they invariably start from the premise that each group has its own distinct knowledge base, perspective, or incentive (Bechky 2003, Carlile 2004, Kellogg et al. 2006, Ewenstein and Whyte 2009, Metiu and Rothbard 2013). In the case of our automotive engineers, no such occupational or epistemic differences were supposed or expected to exist. Although the India center engineers originally lacked the automotive task and domain knowledge that the U.S and Mexico center engineers had, they were expected to acquire that knowledge. Moreover, India center engineers held the same kinds of job titles within IAC as did the engineers in the United States and Mexico Compared to the evolutionary biologists, trappers, conservationists, amateur bird collectors, and conservationists in the study of Star and Griesemer (1989) who had multiple legitimate ways of thinking about the map of California, automotive engineers in our study had only one legitimate way of thinking about vehicle dynamics analysis in product development India center engineers, with their background in aerospace engineering, did not know in the early days what the one legitimate way was. Although India center engineers were familiar with the software used in automotive engineering analysis (recall that it was developed originally in aerospace engineering), they were not knowledgeable about how to use the soft ware to analyze automotive vehicle dynamics. Thus, knowledge was asymmetrically distributed: engineers in the United States and Mexico were experts in automotive engineering, whereas those in India were novices. The coevolution of objects and boundaries would occur first as U.S. and Mexico center engineers came to understand this knowledge asymmetry (moving from work request forms to simulation models to PowerPoint slide decks) and later, as India center engineers, with the help of their distant colleagues, corrected it (altering the use of PowerPoint decks).

In this context, simulation models and PowerPoint slide decks gained the meaning and took on the use that they did, because geographic and expertise boundaries were initially conflated. Both boundaries were enacted in practice between India and the United States and Mexico. However, because engineers in India used simulation models to recognize what they did not know and because engineers in the United States and Mexico shared needed task and domain knowledge with them through PowerPoint slide decks, Indian engineers gained more product development knowledge. After five years of conveying knowledge via these objects, the knowledge boundary between the India center and the U.S. and Mexico centers was no longer enacted. Consequently, it became decoupled from the geographic boundary. It was this decoupling that led U.S. and Mexican engineers to abandon the use of PowerPoint slide decks, because they no longer needed to share task and domain knowledge. Knowledgeable Indian engineers could now fully enact the affordances of simulation models. Thus, a change in the level of knowledge (and not in the type of knowledge) was the major catalyst for the coevolution of objects and boundaries in our case.

This finding is likely to generalize to other settings, because expertise boundaries are quite common in organizations (Vlaar et al. 2008, Hawk et al. 2009), more common than the occupational boundaries that have been the dominant focus of practice-based studies of object use (Wilczynski and Jennings 2003). When experts within an occupational community convey knowledge to task novices, the knowledge is unlikely to be local and contextual; rather, it is likely to be universal, true for all who labor in the occupation. Nor is the knowledge that experts convey to novices likely to be simply who knows what, such as is often the case in studies of knowledge sharing in which occupational, functional, or geographic boundaries are crossed (Cramton et al. 2007); such as in our study, all parties are likely to be clear which workers know a lot and which workers know very little when expertise boundaries are crossed. The knowledge that objects help to convey in the case of experts informing task novices then is knowledge of how to do the work. In this case, we expect that task novices gradually become experts, which means that expertise boundaries are fluid, and hence, objects for communicating are likely to change over time. In short, in the context of expertise boundaries, coevolution of objects and boundaries may be the norm

More research is needed to determine if coevolution similar to what we found in the context of a knowl edge boundary is likely in the context of other boundaries. For example, whereas the knowledge boundary in our study dissolved with the acquisition of the requisite knowledge, the geographic boundary remained. The former was the site of coevolution; the latter was not. Geographic boundaries would dissolve if colocation occurred; they would remain but lose salience if new technology rendered the full sense of side-by-side work for distant colleagues or if continued crossings over time inured people to the boundary itself, such as Watson-Manheim et al. (2012) suggest. Absent these changes, the geographic boundary would remain salient, no change in practice or objects would be suspected, and coevolution would seemingly not occur. Each boundary and its associated objects need some mechanism to prompt change.

Our study also heeds the calls of organizational and information system scholars to examine how materiality shapes object use (Osterlund 2008, Leonardi and Barley 2010, Faraj and Azad 2012, Robey et al. 2012, Mutch 2013). Throughout both phases of the India center’s development, the materiality of the objects used to communicate across geographic and expertise boundaries never changed. As Leonardi (2012a) noted, materiality was independent of use or users. However, the objects that engineers used did change over time, and materiality of the objects as reflected in their anticipated use (goal) and actual use (practice) was a strong reason why.

Each time that engineers found an object’s materiality to be insufficient to communicate effectively across a boundary, they switched to a new object with materiality that they hoped would provide the affordances that they needed. Thus, in Phase 1, when work request forms proved insufficient for conveying instructions over distance, engineers in the United States and Mexico initially presumed that the problem was related to the geographic boundary. Unable to point, gesture, or note facial expressions that might register understanding, the engineers realized that they could not rely merely on the typed or handwritten text of work request forms to replace communication in a highly visual culture. Instead, they turned to simulation models, with visualization, exemplification, and choice delineation affordances that far exceeded what work request forms offered. When simulation models also faltered in communication with the India center engineers, U.S. and Mexico engineers recognized that these objects were not capable of crossing the knowledge boundary between expert and novice engineers. Thus, they turned at last to PowerPoint slide decks, with materiality that yielded the affordances of identification/location, indexicality, and explanation that simulation models and work request forms lacked. Over time, use of the slide decks increased knowledge levels at the India center to the point that the affordances of simulation models coupled with work request forms were finally sufficient for crossing the only boundary that remained between the U.S. and Mexico centers and the India center: the geographic boundary. At that point, the affordances of the PowerPoint slide decks proved valuable in crossing the knowledge boundary, which now resided solely in the India center, separating senior engineers from freshers.

In this manner, materiality shaped choices of which objects to use to cross which boundaries. Solutions were not immediate, because people in the U.S. and Mexico centers were unfamiliar with the geographic boundary and unaware of the expertise one. Thus, not realizing the extent to which their own engineers visual practices mattered when tasking local engineers with work, senior IAC managers began by choosing a simple object (the work request form), with materiality that lent few affordances. Only in recognizing the implications of geographic and expertise boundaries did the U.S. and Mexico center engineers come to understand that they needed objects with different materiality and more extensive affordances than the work request forms.

One object with different materiality and extensive affordances that they could not choose was the vehicle itself. In our prior work (Barley et al. 2012), we noted that simulation models were iconic representations of the physical vehicles to which they referred. In the U.S. and Mexico centers, engineers could see, touch, hear, and smell these physical vehicle referents at experimental bays, teardown rooms, and providing grounds, acts that provided engineers with knowledge that they needed to build good simulation models. The India center did not have physical vehicles on site, an absence that placed a strong burden on digital objects to communicate knowledge. Managers’ failure to recognize the role that physical vehicles’ materiality played in engineers’ learning in the United States and Mexico, which we documented in our earlier paper, no doubt shaped managers’ early choice to use a simple work request form to communicate with India in the same way that it shaped their decision to offshore engineering work to the India center in the first place. Our earlier paper relied on data collected through 2006, the point at which the current paper begins its inquiry: the ways that we describe here of how objects and boundaries coevolved reflect a period during which automotive engineers around the world used digital objects, but not all of them used physical vehicles in their work practices. In other words, this paper reflects how engineers used digital objects to communicate knowledge when distance between the India center engineers and physical vehicles compounded the difficulties posed by geographic and expertise boundaries among the engineers. In the future, when physical vehicles become commonplace in the India center and accordingly, the communicative weight placed on digital objects is lessened, we expect that the ways in which geographic and expertise boundaries will coevolve might change. For example, with physical vehicles at the ready, senior engineers in India may not rely as heavily on PowerPoint decks to communicate knowledge to freshers, potentially leading to a retirement of these objects.

Materiality also prompted changes in practice that eventually dissolved a boundary (Jonsson et al. 2009). The dissolution of the knowledge boundary rendered objects once favored for their affordances unneeded, because the goal had changed: no longer did U.S. and Mexico engineers need to convey task and domain knowledge; they simply needed to provide instructions for requested work. Materiality was evident in the coevolution of objects and boundaries: a change in practice (Indian engineers no longer needed to gain task and domain knowledge) prompted a change in the choice of object (from slide deck to simulation model with request form) based on differences in materiality (e.g., text boxes versus a finished model) that yielded different affordances (e.g., explanation versus exemplification) to cross a changed set of boundaries (from geographic and expertise to geographic alone).

U.S. and Mexico engineers were initially oblivious to the circumstances of practice that shaped the needs of India center engineers as reflected at first in the insufficiency of the work request form and later, in the sending of simulation models, but they did eventually come to recognize what set of affordances was required in an object to communicate effectively across geographic and expertise boundaries. Although simulation models were objects of engineers everyday work before offshoring began, PowerPoint slide decks of the sort that they sent to the India center were not. Thus, our study suggests that, through trial and error, people may come to learn not only what affordances are needed but how to create objects with materiality that yields those affordances. In short, coevolution of objects and boundaries is made possible, because people are attuned to objects’ materiality and affordances.

## Communicating Knowledge Across Boundaries

A great deal has been written on the topic of communicating knowledge across boundaries in the last several years by information systems and organizational scholars who draw on practice theory. These scholars have found that objects help people to transcend knowledge differences (allowing multiple simultaneous meanings of objects), transform knowledge to build common ground (translate meanings), or create new knowledge (and with it, new object meanings) (Sapsed and Salter 2004, Ewenstein and Whyte 2009, Zeiss and Groenewegen 2009). One common assumption across these studies, even those that specifically call out the salient boundaries in an organization (e.g., Carlile 2004), is that knowledge transcendence, transformation, or creation is the outcome that object use generates regardless of boundary type. Our study questions this assumption by showing how different objects served distinct purposes at different kinds of boundaries. This finding was corroborated in our data by the coevolutionary process described above: when a boundary changed, the objects used to communicate knowledge across it no longer worked well.

For example, our findings show that objects can be useful for aiding comprehension across a geographical boundary. Comprehension is not transcendence, transformation, or creation; it is more banal than any of them but perhaps more important in daily work. Comprehension refers simply to understanding what a communication partner is trying to say. Engineers in our study used simulation models to aid comprehension across geographic boundaries, because they could not communicate job requests in the ways they were accustomed—by sitting down with the other person in front of a computer screen, talking that person through the task that they wanted done, pointing at the screen, and monitoring gaze and gestures for signs of understanding.

The use of simulation models to aid comprehension of work requests across a geographic boundary was effective in our study, because the knowledge that needed to be communicated was what Collins (2010) referred to as “ostensive knowledge” or “knowledge that can be learned only by pointing to some object . . . because the description in words, though everything is there to see and be described would be too complex to be spoken and apprehended” (Collins 2010, p. 93). Complex engineering tasks—such as setting up simulation models of vehicle dynamics—can be explained to others, but the explanation is much more difficult to accomplish without the aid of some object that encapsulates ostensive knowledge. Collins (2010) argued that objects are particularly useful for communicating ostensive knowledge, because when viewing them, “the learner has to ask questions, touch, work around the object to get different views at different times, request that the object or apparatus be subjected to one kind of manipulation or another, or perhaps taken apart so that the hidden parts can be seen” (Collins 2010, p. 93).

As our findings showed, simulation models were the preferred solution for transferring ostensive knowledge across geographic boundaries, because they enabled Indian engineers to decode the information embedded in them on the engineers’ own time absent lengthy discussions with the U.S. or Mexican engineers. Of course, engineers in the United States and Mexico preferred to use simulation models in conjunction with a discussion at their own computers; when working locally, they would simply call another engineer over to the model and show that engineer in person what task they wanted done. However, the geographic boundary made this type of in-person communication impossible; hence, U.S. and Mexican engineers sent simulation models to India.

Although simulation models worked well across the geographic boundary to convey ostensive task knowledge, they did not work well across the knowledge boundary, because that boundary involved a different type of knowledge, namely domain knowledge. Indian engineers had to open the “black box” of the simulation model to figure out why it worked the way that it did, understand the logic of the practices reflected in the model, and replicate that logic in their own models (Latour 1987). To facilitate this gaining of task and domain knowledge, U.S. and Mexican engineers used a second type of object—PowerPoint slide decks marked up with arrows, circles, and text boxes—that could explain why practices were done as they were in simulation models. The PowerPoint objects enabled Indian engineers to reconstruct for themselves the practices reflected in the simulation models in a similar way to that described by Boland and Tenkas (1995) and Boland and Yoo (2003).

By using one set of objects across geographic boundaries and another set across expertise boundaries, engineers at IAC’s U.S. and Mexico centers were able to convey ostensive task knowledge and help engineers at the company’s India center gain the domain knowledge necessary to do the work. This finding adds to the growing awareness that people can use multiple objects with varying levels of intensity within the same crossboundary interaction (Barley et al. 2012, Nicolini et al. 2012). Furthermore, our finding expands this notion by showing how the paired use of two kinds of objects in our study was initially coincident with boundaries that were conflated; when the boundaries were disjoined, one set of objects was associated with crossing one boundary, and the other set was associated with crossing the other boundary. In short, the objects served distinct communication goals. Without use of objects toward these communication goals, IAC might not have been able to reuse important and useful organizational knowledge as organizational scholars advocate (Katila 2004, Majchrzak et al. 2004).

## Conclusion

Our longitudinal study of global product development work shows that objects and boundaries coevolve as objects afford the communication of knowledge in ways that change the practices that constitute boundaries. As those boundaries change, so do the meanings of the objects used to cross them. Helping employees work successfully across boundaries of various types requires a nuanced understanding of how objects work in relation to the boundaries that separate realms of practice. The coevolutionary findings shown here take us one important step in the direction of learning when to use objects and what kind of objects to use (what materiality and affordances) to aid communication among employees as they cross various types of boundaries.
