# Integration design of material flow management in an e-business manufacturing environment

Robin G. Qiu <sup>a,b,\*</sup>, Ying Tang <sup>c,d</sup>, Qi Xu <sup>e</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Economics and Management, Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics, Nanjing 210016, PR China Department of Information Sciences, Pennsylvania State University, Malvern, PA 19355, USA

<sup>c</sup> Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Rowan University, Glassboro, NJ 08028, USA <sup>d</sup> Key Laboratory for Complex Systems and Intelligence Science, Institute of Automation, Chinese Academy of Sciences, P. O. Box 2728, Beijing 100080, PR China

Received 5 May 2004; received in revised form 28 September 2005; accepted 2 October 2005 Available online 28 November 2005

## Abstract

A material flow management (MFM) system that controls and manages material flows on the shop floor is a key component of enterprise information systems. It typically runs as a centralized software application, which becomes difficult in providing desirable system performance, robustness, flexibility, and interoperability in an e-business manufacturing environment. This paper proposes a solution to the design and development of a scalable, reconfigurable, and distributed MFM system. Using the concept of Virtual Production Lines, production lines can be logically configured, and operated, respectively, in processing different families of products; MFM modules/instances are accordingly created to control and manage material flows in corresponding production lines. By reducing the complexity of MFM through the use of decomposed MFM instances and taking advantage of family-type process configurations, a manufacturing system can cut down machine setup time, reduce production cycle time, and improve system responsiveness. Moreover, multi-agents are utilized in the decomposed MFM instances that directly interact with enterprise workflow systems, so information on material consumption and movement can be delivered to office-level planning systems in a timely manner.

## 1. Introduction

In an e-business manufacturing environment, it is essential for the completeness of both <sup>b</sup>e<sup>Q</sup> and <sup>b</sup>business<sup>Q</sup>. For instance, customer orders are taken over the Internet and the products are made and delivered as promised. Correspondingly, information systems in a manufacturing enterprise can be classified as a business- or office-level system and plant-level system. The office-level systems normally include office-level planning, scheduling, sales, supply chain, and logistics management applications, while the plantlevel systems would include applications directly tied with value-adding manufacturing operations and activities, such as engineering, manufacturing execution, and shop floor controls. As manufacturing business environments are confounded with rapid changes in business operations and customer demands, integrated information systems across different business unit domains become vital for manufacturing enterprise to ensure informed decisions made operationally, tactically, and strategically.

However, due to the lack of sufficient investigation and investment in enterprise integration, office-level systems usually do not have effective connectivity to shop floor equipment. As a result, implementation of enterprise integration is usually costly and painstaking; the majority of enterprises cannot afford to conduct enterprise-wide system integration [8]. How could the executives of a manufacturing enterprise make informed business decisions when the office-planning systems have no real-time and accurate data on the status of inventory availability, equipment utilization, and production schedules [2,7,18]? Manufacturing execution systems (MES) have been researched and developed to bridge the shop floor and office-level planning systems [3,20,25]. On the one hand, MES take in pertinent data from office-level planning systems and ensure that their information is acted on intelligently on the shop floor; on the other hand, MES consolidate shop floor data and transform them into business information, focusing on the delivery of accurate production status to office-level planning systems in a timely manner [21].

Material flow management (MFM) is an essential component and plays a critical role in MES [18]. This paper proposes a solution to the design and development of a scalable, reconfigurable, and distributed MFM system. Using autonomous software agents enables the direct communication between office-planning systems (e.g., supply chain management) and shop floor MFM systems. Material consumption data can therefore be directly delivered to users including both internal employees and supplier’s correspondents. In a great detail, this paper discusses how an MFM system governs material movement from equipment to equipment on the shop floor. By incorporating the concept of virtual production lines in production control, an MFM system can be developed as a distributed system through decomposition and coordination, resulting in improved performance, robustness, flexibility, and interoperability for the overall shop floor information systems.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly reviews a typical design of MFM and proposes a distributed MFM architecture based on virtual production lines. Section 3 discusses how a collection of distributed computing MFM instances can be decomposed and synchronized. Section 4 presents the method of synchronizing MFM activities between suppliers and the shop floor using multi-agent technologies. Section 5 describes an implementation of the proposed distributed MFM solution. Finally, Section 6 provides conclusions for this research and highlights the future research venue.

## 2. Distributed MFM architecture

Currently a manufacturing fabrication facility usually uses a process layout configuration [10,12]. An MFM system is typically preconfigured to execute best practices or a few predefined <sup>b</sup>super production lines<sup>Q</sup>, which is best in line with its current facility resource model and organization structure [17,16]. Materials are usually released in batches. For each batch, when one process is done, operators check the MFM and retrieve a list of available equipment for next process. If there are many choices, the operator or production manager uses his/her discretion to pick equipment to perform the required process. An MFM system could be integrated with real-time dispatching and scheduling applications to further improve shop floor manufacturing through reducing the work-in-process (WIP) level and shortening production cycle time [21].

With the rapid advance of manufacturing technologies, manufacturing has become more and more complicated and the resultant manufacturing systems become more competent, while giving rise to more intensive and complex setup and recalibration operations on the shop floor [2]. A current and widely adopted centralized MFM system, running as a mammoth and monolithic software application, is losing its designated performance due to the growing needs of data acquisition and analytical requirements and the increased number of more complicated manufacturing processes. In addition, inflexibility and poor reconfigurability and scalability are also the drawbacks of a centralized MFM system [22].

As the manufacturing environment gets more and more dynamic and fluctuating, a reconfigurable manufacturing system (RMS) is recognized as one of the solutions to addressing the future challenges [11]. An RMS is theoretically characterized by its reconfigurable production lines, reconfigurable machines, controllers, and interfaces, as well as methodologies for systematic design, development, deployment, and diagnosis, aimed at making the system throughout its life cycle capable of being rapidly adjusted to different needs, such as reusability, adaptation to new and/or rugged environmental constraints, and fluctuating market demands. To meet a variety of industrial needs, the

Engineering Research Center (ERC) at University of Michigan has been investigating on design and configuration methodologies as well as economic evaluation of RMS for a decade. According to ERC [5], an RMS <sup>b</sup>is one designed at the outset for rapid change in its structure, as well as its hardware and software components, in order to quickly adjust its production capacity and functionality within a part family in response to sudden market changes or intrinsic system change.<sup>Q</sup> By focusing on material flow management, this paper addresses how production capacity capable of being rapidly adjusted to fluctuations in product demand, how a variety of manufacturing functionality can be realized, and how well-informed decisions can be made through the operations of reconfigurable production lines and agent-based workflow management systems.

As discussed earlier, the configuration of an operational production line is highly dependent upon the physical facility layout (e.g., process type or family type) on the shop floor. It is well known that different types of physical facility layouts have different advantages and disadvantages [24]. For example, a familytype physical layout usually provides better responsiveness and generates low-level work-in-process, but product design changes could substantially negatively impact on its productivity; contrarily, a process-type physical layout provides high flexibility in allocating personnel and equipment since such a layout is not bound to any given product type, but it increases material handling requirements, requires more complicated production control, and most likely results in high-level work-in-process.

The cost is extremely high if reinstallation and production interruption occurs due to the change of physical configuration of a manufacturing system. Manufacturers typically do not want to make a change for their physical configurations of manufacturing systems after equipment is installed [24]. Empirically, however, different products usually require different configurations of production lines (i.e., best practices) in order to realize optimal system productivity. The contradiction between the need of production control and the configuration of production lines becomes a severe issue when the market demand frequently fluctuates. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new technologies to address the issue by providing manufacturing systems the essential production line reconfigurability.

In response to the needs, there has been much literature discussing virtual enterprise operations as more and more products are made coordinately by different enterprises that are typically separated geographically. For instance, by complying with the operational philosophy of virtual production lines (VPL) [16], no matter what an actual physical configuration a shop floor indeed uses, the shop floor can be logically configured as a product family-type layout to improve system responsiveness and minimize work-in-process level. In other words, by taking advantage of the tremendous advancement of information technology made during the last decade, production control can be implemented with sufficient flexibility when shop floor systems are considered as a collection of isolated <sup>b</sup>computational<sup>Q</sup> machines rather than rigid physically fixed production lines. As an example, Fig. 1 shows how a fixed process-type physical layout gets logically reconfigured as a family-type configuration. Apparently, the logical configuration of production lines is always reconfigurable whenever needed as the market demand changes.

Fig. 1. An illustration of reconfigurable production lines [19].

Fig. 1 illustrates, once a product is ordered and scheduled for production, a corresponding logical production line can be strategically and operationally formed based on the state of shop floor manufacturing systems [23]. To maximize the productivity of a manufacturing system, the corresponding logical line should be dismissed and all the equipment allocated in the logical line would be gradually and optimally reallocated as soon as the product gets finished. For instance, a production line can be assigned for processing a similar product, so the machine reconfiguration time is minimized, which leads to better production transition and system responsiveness [13].

A typical advanced manufacturing control and management system is generally of a hybrid nature (Fig. 2) [14]. An MFM system is a shop floor information application, which provides production control, WIP control and management, and shop floor accounting functions, and acts as one of the key interfaces between enterprise planning (i.e., office-level information systems) and shop floor execution (e.g., cell controllers). A distributed architecture for MFM is proposed for better scalability, performance, and reliability, where decomposed MFM instances are defined as distributed computing programs as shown in Fig. 2.

From the system engineering perspective, when a variety of products are manufactured at the same time, the line logically formed for a typical group of products will usually consist of a small subset of all the equipment on the shop floor. An MFM instance managing only the formed logical line can thus be easily spawned from the computing server. Overwhelming data collected from the equipment can be then filtered and transformed before they are transmitted to the server. Note that more computing processes (i.e., MFM instances) can be created as needed. Therefore, the computing load for a distributed MFM will not be over-loaded as the manufacturing system size and the number of product mix are increased. However, some high productivity and very expensive machines could be shared across lines, which result in overlapping in the decomposition of a complex manufacturing system. In other words, there is no free lunch for the proposed distributed MFM architecture; operations, activities, control logic, and data across different lines might be overlapped and conflicted. Coordination protocols and synchronization mechanisms are necessary to successfully implement the proposed distributed MFM.

## 3. Synchronization mechanisms for MFM instances

From the production control perspective, only when a product order is scheduled for production, a VPL dedicated to making that type of product is formed. Correspondingly, an MFM instance is spawned. When a VPL requires reconfiguration, a machine can be simply added or removed without substantially affecting other MFM instances. Apparently, the VPL concept would promote the scalability and flexibility of MFM implementation, i.e., a VPL-based MFM system is able to continue to operate well in terms of performance and response time as the system size grows and customers need changes.

As discussed, a VPL usually consists of a small subset of the shop floors, so the shop floor process flow diagram can be divided into multiple pieces; each diagram covers the constituent equipment assigned into the VPL. Fig. 3 shows how each decomposed MFM instance can be instantiated with a piece of the corresponding process flow diagram. An MFM instance governing a VPL can be running as a distributed computing process. When many logical production lines are running in parallel to process different customers’ orders, the complexity of an MFM instance is substantially reduced when compared to a monolithic MFM implementation as one MFM instance control and monitor only a fraction of the whole system. Note that critical and under-utilized machines in a VPL can be shared by another VPL [16,23], which creates a situation of overlap decomposition [1]. Distributed computing technologies have advanced significantly and rapidly, which make the proposed <sup>b</sup>spawning process<sup>Q</sup> doable while provide desirable system performance and robustness [4]; however, the effective interactions/coordination among the decomposed MFM instances and other system components is essential for the successful implementation of the proposed approach.

If there are m VPLs defined in a manufacturing system, the set of {Instance MFM 1, Instance MFM 2, . . ., Instance MFM m} can be instantiated with the defined VPLs. It should be noted that an instance corresponds to a computing process, which is in turn a transformation from a deterministic finite capacity transducer [15]. The MFM system cannot provide guaranteed real-time responses and make all the VPLs running in a coordinating manner unless it is seamlessly integrated with the shop floor control system (SFCS). Fig. 4 shows the layered structure of a VPL-based MFM and SFCS for an integrated shop floor information system [18].

In Fig. 4, four layers (i.e., MFM Server, MFM Instance, SFCS, and Equipment Executor) are proposed to be structured and integrated in a VPL-based MFM system. An MFM system server provides the capability of defining both a virtual production line dynamically and accomplishing normal MFM functionality. When a line is dynamically formed, a corresponding MFM instance is <sup>b</sup>spawned<sup>Q</sup>. Formally the integration system as a whole behaves as numerous composite automata, where a coupler $\psi _ { \mathrm { m f m } } { = } T _ { \mathrm { m f m } } , \mathrm { U _ { m f m } }$ could synchronize the computations of the MFM server/instance and the SFCS on the shop floor [15]. The coupler function essentially coordinates activities/tasks executed by two coupled layers in a stepwise manner. So when a task is created in the server, it will be refined and delivered to the spawned and coupled instance and also monitors the progress of execution; if the task fails to complete in the instance, a feedback will be generated and delivered back to the server; then the server will immediately act on the failure, and adjust the task accordingly.

## 4. Real-time business decisions and shop floor events delivery using agents

It is well known that workflow management technology promotes the automation of business processes [9,26]. Current workflow management systems have been successfully applied inside many enterprises. However, because of the lack of flexible mechanisms that deal with cross-enterprise business processes, workflow management technology has had little success in achieving dynamic coordination and interaction in applications such as supply chain management. Software agents can be designed to have the following attributes: autonomy, goal orientation, persistence, reactivity, mobility, intelligence, and interactivity [6]. Because of this, they have been used as a popular mechanism for integrating disparate processes, coordinating the effective use of information, and more efficiently utilizing networking resources in task execution and decision-making. In other words, software agent technology would technically provide users with flexible, distributed, and intelligent solutions to business processes in a cross-enterprise environment.

The manufacturing business is characterized by intense competition, entwined supply chain relationships, and a demand for shortened product lifecycles. A manufacturer and its entire network of business partners have to work collaboratively in order to stay worldclass and competitive. So when a request on an order, for instance a new order of a product or a change on an existing order, is processed on the supply chain, the information should be timely populated across the network and reflected in manufacturing planning. For a make-to-order e-business model, the pertinent information should be promptly delivered to the MFM server on the shop floor so an appropriate VPL can be due diligently either assigned or created; on the other hand, the real-time material movement and consumption should also be timely delivered to corresponding office-planning software applications, internally and/or externally. Fig. 6 shows how the interactivity between shop floor MFM instances and the supply chain management system can be enabled using agent technology.

Goal-oriented agents, such as the order agent, production agent, shipment agent, and logistic agent can be created. The more specific task execution agents in manufacturing planning, such as the VPL agent, equipment agent, and part agent, can also be deployed. Some agents are mobile. So they can be reconfigured in terms of attributes, algorithms, or goals <sup>b</sup>on the fly<sup>Q</sup> as needed. More detailed discussion on the design and development of these different agents can be found in our previous work [26]. In this MFM implementation, an MFM instance is created for each VPL. The relevant agents are downloaded as needed into the distributed computing process as soon as the instance is formed and dispatched. Therefore, collaboration can be initiated in a timely way from a partner on the supply chain to the value-adding manufacturing system.

![](/api/attachments/6NCFFHRM/fulltext/images/c2ebb8320a16b0242098b175aab0e112e54bc563d37291667436f8655d004833.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Synchronization using agents.

The creation of multiple agents takes place in the MFM server; and the execution of those agents occurs in the designated MFM instance. When a virtual production line is first formed or adjusted as an order is placed or changed, a corresponding VPL agent is created. When parts are introduced into the VPL, corresponding part agents are created and equipment agents are reconfigured based on inherited attributes and given part and process information. Those agents are then downloaded into the corresponding decomposed MFM instance. Based on all the production control requirements assigned for the VPL, the VPL agent controls the production process and sends task orders to equipment agents and part agents within the governed production line. The latter two agents execute tasks, monitor equipment and part statuses, and react to events occurring during production. Data are exchanged dynamically and in real-time between those agents, accordingly all the correspondents in the supply chain. Fig. 7 shows such an interaction flow among enterprises and also how cross-enterprise real-time manufacturing planning and operations can be realized.

VPL agents and part agents are dynamic because they allow for equipment to be added or removed when a virtual production line requires reconfiguration, for example when a product change or order request occurs. Once the production is finished, the VPL agent and part agents will be removed from the MFM instance and saved in an agent database for future use.

![](/api/attachments/6NCFFHRM/fulltext/images/abd6ef417358d43cc2edc27807cd3f91d2ae5182ac747db85bc33b7db572ab10.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Agents used to synchronize MFM activities and office planning.

The agent database acts as a local decision-making repository. When similar agents are required they can be retrieved from the database instead of being recreated. Because of the use of mobile computing agents, the shop floor control and management system can effectively realize the benefits of operating in the VPL environment. As a result, a series of decision activities concerned with planning, scheduling, dispatching, monitoring, controlling, and coordination between production processes as well as materials, parts and resources are capable of execution locally with much finer granularity (i.e. manufacturing micro planning and scheduling), which in turn assists rational system decision-making (i.e. manufacturing macro planning and scheduling) while improving the flexibility and responsiveness of manufacturing and services from all the aspects across the supply chain internally and/or externally. Thus manufacturers, distributors, and retailers are capable of reaching higher productivity with better quality at lower cost and adapt to frequent changes in a competitive e-business environment.

## 5. LotManager implementation

Based on the proposed methodology a distributed MFM system prototype, called LotManager [17], was developed for an e-business manufacturing environment. Fig. 8 shows how LotManager is in use across the network and participates in the enterprise-wide information system. The LotManager (MFM) server functions as the repository for functionality computations, algorithms, coordination, and system data. A LotManager (MFM) instance is a computing module spawned from the LotManager server and runs as a distributed computing process. A LotManager instance governs only one or a few VPLs defined in the LotManager server.

![](/api/attachments/6NCFFHRM/fulltext/images/09a3bd660e71c342b625e2007d9c29cc5ac37c387f271d409e7c8226c156a8fb.jpg)  
Fig. 9. A snapshot of a virtual production line in LotManager.

A testbed back-end semiconductor manufacturing system making ball grid array (BGA) products [18] is used to verify the design and development of LotManager. The test plan includes 4 different types of tests: functionality, communication, system, and stress and reliability. The testbed system as shown in Fig. 8 has 19 pieces of automated packaging and test equipment. Processes include entry mounting, die attach, cure, wire bonding, plasma cleaning, inspection, exception, and exit marking. Some operations are repeatable for a typical semiconductor product, for example cure, cleaning, and inspection. Different semiconductor products have different designs in terms of size, shape, and process requirements. Therefore process routing logic could be different from product to product. Fig. 9 partially gives an example of a configured VPL, which will be archived in the server repository. When the VPL’s MFM instance is spawned from the LotManager server, it communicates not only with the LotManager server but also interacts with office-planning systems (i.e., a supply chain management workflow system in the test).

Due to the restrictions on commercial confidentiality, details on design, development, and test of the prototype have to be hidden from the publication. However, as the planned test got successfully completed, the proposed integration design scheme of a distributed MFM for an e-business manufacturing environment is confirmed as a useful approach.

## 6. Conclusions

This article discussed a methodology for the integration design of a distributed MFM application on the shop floor. The concept of VPL was applied for the development of distributed, modularized, and decomposed MFM instances. First, a complex shop floor can be divided into different virtual production lines to manufacture different kinds of family products, so MFM can be simplified as material movement can be also logically separated from line to line. Data, activities, and control logics are coordinated and synchronized between lines using well-defined VPL formal models. The modularization and distributed computing is well known for promoting system scalability, flexibility, reusability, and reliability. For instance, when changes occur within a VPL, the impact onto the rest of the system is substantially restricted. An agent-based workflow management is incorporated in the proposed integration model to facilitate the timely delivery of data and information between the shop floor and a supply chain system. As a result, the manufacturing system governed by the proposed distributed MFM system is flexible, reconfigurable, and scaleable, ensuring the delivery of accurate and pertinent information to the proper users at the right time.

From the technical perspectives, although the research shows some positive results as expected, many distributed computing issues should further be investigated before the developed system can be running as a mission-critical application in a real production environment, such as fault tolerance, interoperability, information security and assurance, and error recovery. Some preliminary investigations on resource sharing, WIP control and VPL design and operations algorithms have been conducted [13,19,23]. Many other factors affecting system productivity should also be considered in the design and development of commercial MFM applications. To ensure such a system is practically applicable and sustainable, approaches to stimulating appropriate usages of information sharing along the supply chain should be investigated. In addition, a comprehensive framework for populating agents across other office-planning systems would be necessary as manufacturing enterprises are accelerating their e-business investment to meet the needs of future e-business.
