# A contingency model for the allocation of media content in publishing companies

Alexander Benlian, Thomas Hess

University of Munich, Institute for Information Systems and New Media, Ludwigstr. 28, 80539 Munich, Germany Received 15 February 2006; received in revised form 16 March 2007; accepted 9 May 2007 Available online 29 June 2007

## Abstract

Today, articles and papers can be published in many different media and the allocation of content by publishing firms is vital for their operational efficiency, time-to-market, and customer retention. Past studies in MIS research have focused on the allocation of other objects than content, e.g., data or hardware, with different distribution and integration requirements. Such studies have ignored the allocation of media content. We developed and tested a structural model of media content allocation behavior of publishing companies. Drawing on contingency theory, we hypothesized that organizational structure (centralization, formalization, and specialization), size, distribution of IT decision-making, and information processing requirements influenced the degree of conten distribution and integration. The model was tested using data from a survey of 115 companies in the book, newspaper, and magazine publishing industries. While content distribution was primarily contingent upon organizational formalization, organizationa specialization, IT decision-making, and information processing requirements, only organizational standardization and information processing requirements were found to be significant in affecting the way that publishing companies carried out content integration. <sup>#</sup> 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Content allocation; Distribution; Integration; Contingency theory; Infrastructure management; Publishing industry; Path analysis

## 1. Introduction

The production of media content can generally be characterized by a high fixed cost base incurred during the production of the original copy and relatively low reproduction costs. As initial investments in the first copy of a content product are very high, media companies usually try to recoup the costs by maximizing the number of copies sold [16,17]. Another specificity of media products is non-rivalry in consumption: the number sold does not decrease the value to other customers. Thus, there is no obstacle to the multiple usage of content on the customer side and a media company should reuse content in production and the marketplace. While production-side content reutilization involves incorporating a content module in at least two different packages (e.g., an article or catalogue), market-side content reutilization generates at least two different products from one package (e.g., offline or online article) [22]. Production-side content reutilization relies on economies of scale, as revenues from various media channels can be spread over fixed costs incurred in the production of original content modules. On the market-side, economies of scope can be exploited by offering customized media products at joint costs that are lower than costs that would arise from operating different output channels. Moreover, mass customization allows to both reduce production costs and also to raise customer loyalty and thus generate additional customer rents.

With the advancement of digitizing content and channels in the media industry, content reutilization has increased, as potential benefits appear to be higher than before. On the production side, digital and modular production technologies promise to bring expenses for content duplication down to nearly zero marginal costs leading to higher contribution margins to pay fixed costs. In addition, more efficient access mechanisms, such as ontologies in the Semantic Web) should increase the visibility, transferability, and use of content across different media channels. From a marketing perspective, new digital distribution channels demand media-specific product offerings (e.g., video-on-demand). These market-pull dynamics, in turn, increase the opportunities for new forms of content reutilization that may address additional demand in a cost-efficient way.

Although they are already using digital networks and professional application systems, the majority of media companies still have not determined how best to (store, integrate, and retrieve) digital content in production processes. Bertelsmann corporate network (BCN) for example, a cross-functional staff department at the board level of Bertelsmann AG, was founded in 2002 to promote standardization and harmonization between the product offerings of its subsidiaries. At the outset, the department’s strategy was mainly to foster multiple use of content in related business units, between the RTL Group (TV), Random House (book publishing), Gruner + Jahr (magazine publishing), and SonyBMG (music publishing). However, due to a lack of decision rules showing when and where to place content, the multiple use of content was merely in reusing formats, rights, brands, and customer data. Our hypothesis is that if they had developed a more profound understanding of the decision logic governing content allocation, it could have organized media content across business units around centralized content hubs or shared content service centers. This approach would also have substantiated BCN’s IT investment decisions.

In the IS field, a body of knowledge has grown around the problem of linking IT and organizational variables. Numerous studies have explored the logic underlying the allocation of IS decision making. From the majority of the work, however, organizations have learnt that allocation decisions depended upon context factors and are complex [18].

Two main research gaps become apparent when one attempts to transfer the existing findings to the allocation of content in media companies. First, as content is both a key input factor and an output product, its characteristics differ tremendously from plain data. However, no work has focused on the allocation of media content in publishing companies.

Second, although IS research on the allocation of IT resources is mature, it has not yet provided a comprehensive framework that recognizes the varied body of literature.

Thus, the scientific objective of our work was to examine multiple contingencies that explain the content allocation behavior of publishing companies in practice. We sought to develop and specify a comprehensive framework that would help in understanding the relationships between factors and the allocation of media content.

## 2. Theoretical background

The major area that provided a theoretical foundation for our study was contingency theory (CT). In IS research, several contingency antecedents have been included in the analysis of fit relationships. In particular, the effect of strategy, structure, size, environment, technology, task, and individual characteristics have been studied [26]. The dependent variables have ranged from micro-analytical variables, such as IS adoption and satisfaction, to macro-analytical factors, such as IS structure, performance, and effectiveness [14]. Corresponding to organizational CT research IS research has tended to apply an integrated CT view of the investigation of structural IT problems (e.g. [21]). The main objective of the study at hand was to trace variations in the allocation of content to organizational and technological explanatory factors, focusing on four contingency variables: organization structure, organization size, IT decision-making, and information processing requirements. These factors were chosen because they have received the most attention in this context.

## 2.1. Organization structure and organization size

Organizational form or structure is believed to affect the structure of IT systems directly [9]. In a functional organization, the structure is aligned with basic business functions such as marketing, finance, and accounting. In a divisional organization, the activities are grouped around product lines, customer groups, or geographies. In general, a centralized organizational structure capitalizes on efficiency, improved coordination, economies of scale, clear responsibility, standardization, and reduced managerial overhead. On the other hand, a decentralized organizational structure has effectiveness, autonomy, flexibility, responsiveness, specialization, and attention to local needs. In this regard, a large number of empirical studies have determined that an organization with a functional structure tends to rely on a centralized IT structure, because it fits the philosophy of structuring activities around functional departments. On the other hand, organizations with a divisional organization form tend to have a decentralized IT function, because it fits the philosophy of distributing the activities around productmarket divisions [2,11]. Publishing organizations with a rather centralized organization will tend to control and gather resources at a single location to keep hardware and personnel costs low. Centralizing tendencies will usually be expressed by a high degree of formalization and standardization of rules and procedures about the access and use of content. In order to take advantage of economies of scale, such companies seek to stimulate and foster multiple usage of content modules and packages. However, if a publishing firm is decentralized or specialized in its organization and task, content will generally be disseminated across editorial units. As the bureaucracy decreases, editorial units tend to have more autonomy and shape their own content workflows with separate repositories. Thus, it may be argued that organizational decentralization exerts effects on the allocation of content. From this, the following hypotheses were made:

Hypothesis 1.1. The higher the degree of organizational centralization, the more media content will be allocated centrally.

Hypothesis 1.2. The higher the degree of organizational formalization, the more media content will be allocated centrally.

Hypothesis 1.3. The lower the degree of organizational specialization, the more media content will be allocated centrally.

As a second organizational variable, the impact of organization size was examined. Size is often a contingency variable in empirical studies. In his theoretical work, Blau investigated the impact of the size of organizations on structural differentiation [3]. One of his propositions was that increasing organizational size generates structural differentiation at decelerating rates, which in turn enlarged the administrative component. The underlying reason for central power is to counteract differentiation. One integrative mechanism in larger publishing firms is the creation of central content repositories. Accordingly, due to the potential cost savings, the following hypothesis was formulated:

Hypothesis 2. The larger the organizational size of a publishing firm, the more content will be allocated centrally.

## 2.2. IT decision-making

A major contender to structure and size as a cause of structural differentiation is technology. A variety of different types of technology and their effect on organizational variables have been analyzed ranging from operations technology to specialized works including the relationship between the locus of IT decision-making and the placement of IT resources (e.g. [23]). The major objective underlying all these endeavors was to find out how technology and structural variables interacted.

Special attention has been paid to the relationship between decision making and organizational variables, the locus of IT decision-making authority. The success of recent systems, such as peer-to-peer and mobile technologies, suggested that IT management was, once more, leading IT-based business innovation towards decentralization.

Traditionally, three configurations have been considered. In these, the stakeholder takes different lead roles and responsibilities. In a centralized configuration, corporate IT management retains its IT decisionmaking authority over infrastructure, applications, and development. In the decentralized configuration, divisional IT management and business unit management have authority. In the federal configuration, IT decision-making authority is divided between corporate and divisional IT management [7,8].

It may thus be hypothesized that the content allocation structure in publishing companies is affected by the relative scope and extent of a corporate IT department’s hold over IT-related resources. This proposition is, for example, be supported by the empirical study of Ahituv et al., who found that the distribution of IT decisionmaking processes was significantly reflected in the degree of hardware distribution in the organization [1]. Thus, we formulated the hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. The more the influence of a central IT department on content allocation, the more content will be allocated centrally.

## 2.3. Information processing requirements (IPRs)

The influence of technology on structural variables has a long tradition. It has been seen as significant in developing comparative analyses, in specifying how organizations interact with their environment, and in measuring the amount of explained variance in organizational performance.

In IS research, three major streams of research about the fundamental relationship between IT and organizational structure have occured [10]. One asserts that IT causes organizational decentralization, with network technologies and digitization supporting teleworking arrangements. A second holds that computerization requires centralization, as growing information transparency allows better control and supervisory activities. The third, a technological imperative view, regards ‘‘technology as an exogenous force which determines or strongly constrains the behavior of individuals and organizations’’ [15]. Thus, there is no way to tell whether an organizational structure should be decentralized or centralized. Thus, technical capabilities, or IPRs ‘‘can be interpreted in the terminology of contingency theory as a determinant factor that influences structural outcome variables.’’; many different requirements have been given for them to meet expected service levels and support seamless processes. In the context of content allocation, five IPRs have emerged as important: system reliability and availability, performance, security, scalability, and extensibility.

The reliability of editorial systems or repositories has a crucial impact on the effectiveness of day-to-day operations of publishing companies. If the availability of these systems is impeded, disruptions and delays in production occur. For that reason, distribution and duplication of resources is normal practice.

Deficits in system performance can harm organizational effectiveness. To enhance the performance of systems, companies tend to centralize IT resources and reduce remote access to a minimum.

With the advent of peer-to-peer systems and increasing intrusion incidents by hackers, security measures have become a strategic factor. In the media sector, where property rights infringements have caused industry disruption, a variety of protection solutions (e.g. digital rights management) have been developed to guard against improper use of media content. This also applies to content allocation endeavors within publishing companies. When media content is disseminated across several different units, the probability rises that one or more repositories will fall victim to denial of service attacks, content corruption, or theft. On the other hand, if content is spread over several locations, it is then often easier to implement system-integrated fail–safe and resilience measures. In either case, decisions on the optimal security level of a company’s systems are highly dependent on content allocation.

System scalability and extensibility are also related. While system scalability affects a system’s ability to adjust to increasing levels of technical requirements without reconfiguration, system extensibility describes the costs and efforts incurred when new IT-related resources have to be integrated into an existing system; e.g., due to addition of another organizational unit). Farsighted investment into a system’s scalability and extensibility can be crucial. If both factors are not considered in the planning stages of IS design, a lack of scalability and extensibility can affect the firm financially and harm day-to-day production and bundling processes.

Non-fulfillment of a service level agreement will negatively affect a company’s operational performance. Accordingly, the following hypothesis was formulated: Hypothesis 4. The more optimally IPRs are met in a centralized as opposed to a decentralized content allocation arrangement, the more content will be allocated centrally.

## 3.2. Measures

Measures for the research variables and the corresponding sources for their adoption are shown in the Appendix A. Respondents provided information about the extent to which they agreed or disagreed with each statement. Five-point Likert-type scales were used for all items except those for content distribution, content integration, and organization size where respondents were asked to provide percentages or numbers.

The degree of content allocation was the central dependent variable. To provide a more differentiated picture of content allocation, measurement items had to reflect the extent to which content was physically dispersed among editorial units, while also determining the logical integration of content as indicated by the scope of access to content by editors in and between editorial units. Basically, low levels of content allocation went with low levels of content distribution and high levels of content. Hence, content allocation behavior related positively with content distribution and negatively with content integration. As no prior measurement items on content allocation could be found, they were developed and iteratively pilot-tested by the authors.

Measurement items of all other variables were drawn from those of previous authors. Organizational structure was based on centralization, formalization, or specialization as developed and validated by Dewar et al. [6]. The degree of organizational centralization in publishing companies captured the distribution of decisionmaking rights and responsibilities between the management and editorial units of publishers. The level of editorial autonomy had therefore to be assessed. The degree of organizational formalization refered to the degree of systematization and structuring in the companies. More specifically, it captured the extent to which rules, procedures, and/or operational guidelines were predetermined in the production and bundling of content. Finally, in order to capture the extent of the division of labor, the level of specialization in publishing companies had to reflect the scope of tasks that editors covered in day-to-day production and bundling operations. Organization size was computed as the natural logarithm of the number of editorial units and the number of employees (the number of full-time employees plus half the number of part-time employees). The log transformation followed several studies which suggested a curvilinear relationship between size and other organizational variables. Additionally, the circulation size of a publisher’s best-selling product was included as an organizational size criterion in order to compare publishers within their sub-industry. Three items for the measurement of IT decision-making were adopted from an empirical study by Ragu-Nathan et al. [19], which captured the extent to which the management of IT-related tasks (ITGov1), database control (ITGov2), and workflow activities (ITGov3) were supported by a central IT unit or department. Indicators for information processing requirements were adopted from several studies [13,20,25], with the wording modified to fit the context.

## 4. Results

The research model was tested using partial least squares (PLS), a modeling technique well suited to assessing complex predictive models. PLS concurrently tested the psychometric properties of the scales used to measure the variables and analyzed the strength and direction of the relationships among the variables. The PLS method was suitable for our study because one variable construct was a latent variable whose measurement items predicted or caused them (formative) while others were latent variables whose measurement items were caused or predicted by them (reflective). SmartPLS (Version 1.01) of the University of Hamburg, Germany was used for the path analysis and also as a bootstrap resampling tool to determine the significance of the paths within the model.

Content validity was established through the adoption of constructs that had been used in former studies and through pilot tests with practitioners in the print industry. The reflective measurement models were validated using the standard procedures of current literature (e.g. [5,24]). Items of scales in a related domain were pooled and factor-analyzed to assess their convergent and discriminant validity.

While convergent validity was determined both at the individual indicator level and at the specified construct level, discriminant validity was assessed by analyzing the average variance extracted and the interconstruct correlations (see Tables 3 and 4).

Items with factor loading values of less than 0.5 were abandoned for further analysis. One item of organizational centralization (OrgCent3) and one item of organizational specialization (OrgSpec1) were therefore dropped (Appendix A). The factor loadings of the final items indicated their unidimensionality.

## 4.2. Test of structural model

Fig. 3 shows the results of PLS analyses. The paths from organizational specialization and information processing requirements to both content distribution and content integration indicate a significant impact: the higher the level of organizational specialization and the more IPRs require decentralized content management, the higher the degree of content distribution and the lower the degree of content integration. Thus, Hypotheses 1.3 and 4 were supported for both content allocation sub-constructs.

To a lesser degree, content distribution was also explained by organizational formalization and IT decision-making, while no additional sources of explanation could be found for content integration. Hence, Hypotheses 1.2 and 3 can only be supported for the relationship to content distribution and must be rejected for the relationship to content integration.

Finally, significant effects of organizational centralization and organization size on content distribution or content integration could not be ascertained. Thus, Hypotheses 1.1 and 2 could not be supported.

As shown in Fig. 3, approximately 32% of the variance in content distribution and about 22% of the variance in content integration were accounted for by the independent constructs in the model, which could be interpreted as reasonably high.

## 5. Implications

## 5.1. Assessment of the results

Overall, the results indicated that both sub-constructs of content allocation (content distribution and content integration) were explained substantially by the factors we examined.

Information processing requirements exhibited a strong impact on content allocation, indicating that content availability, content access speed, the scalability of content processing, and security issues were of major concern to IT managers when deciding upon allocation of content.

IT managers should be aware of the conjunction between organizational specialization and content integration. Publishing firms that operate with greater division of labor were found to keep media content more disintegrated. Uncovered contingencies between organizational specialization and content integration may deliver more revealing and instructive information for horizontal design mechanisms in publishing companies: well-informed decisions on the allocation of content may enable publishing organizations to circumvent crises by flexibly adjusting the organizational design to unpredictable environmental change.

Despite their strong interdependency, content distribution and integration also exhibited different patterns. In particular, content distribution is influenced by the distribution of IT decision-making and the degree of organizational formalization.

IT decision-making was found to be attuned to content distribution in publishing companies. The more the central IT department set guidelines and controlled IT-related tasks, the more the content was allocated from a central unit. This added to the findings of a large body of MIS literature that agreed on the importance of sophisticated IT governance practices for the efficient allocation of IT-related resources. In particular, the allocation of data resources should be managed according to its context.

The degree of organizational formalization in a publishing company was also a factor with a significant influence on content distribution. The extent to which explicitly formulated rules, procedures, and workflows were used seemed to be a good indicator of organizing media content in systematic manner. Before adjusting their content allocation structure, IT managers should therefore check for consistency with existing guidelines or the documentation of established content workflows. Insights into this relationship could help to align organizational and technological variables.

While some research variables showed a significant impact on content allocation, it was interesting to note that a significant relationship between organization size and content allocation could not be detected.

Contradicting the findings presented in prior studies, a significant association between organizational centralization and content allocation could not be found. Consequently, it cannot be concluded that different content allocation structures in publishing organizations fit different organization designs.

## 5.2. Study contributions

Our findings emphasize the need to design publishing organizations that follow patterns of congruency and consistency in order to realize greater effectiveness.

In the case of Bertelsmann corporate network (BCN), for example, content allocation decisions could have been improved with knowledge from our findings. BCN, as a conglomerate of different business units, should have gone beyond the mere reutilization of brands, formats, and customer data by establishing a shared content service center. A centralized content hub would have been better aligned to its information processing requirements to reutilize content across business units. First, centralized hubs can more efficiently administer and structure large volumes of content ensuring scalability and extensibility. Second, shared service centers can more efficiently comply with required service levels by taking care of service reliability and security. And third, in a centralized and integrated environment, content is not only easier to find when generating new content bundles, but also more easily shared among business units.

Abstracting from this case, aligned content infrastructures can dramatically simplify the content workflows and enhance production and bundling.

Our results can also help practitioners develop new functionalities for the design of content logistics support systems as part of upcoming content management systems. Since the orchestration of content distribution and integration represent an important pillar of content architecture management, routing content to the place where it is needed in a timely manner is likely to improve production and bundling processes.

Finally, our study can apply to other organizations that have to manage large volumes of heterogeneous content on websites or in multi-media databases. IT managers may take advantage of the finding that ITrelated requirements (content availability, content access speed, scalability and extensibility of content processing, and security issues) are by far the most important factor in implementing a centralized or decentralized CMS architecture. In contrast, the way in which content is allocated between business users and a central IT department can be considered as independent of organizational size, providing degrees of freedom in the way small or large companies configure their underlying content infrastructure.

## 5.3. Limitations

Our study showed that the allocation of content is a complex, multi-faceted issue. However, the explanatory factors identified for content allocation are not the sole determinants. Other factors such as the type of business strategy or path dependencies could have been integrated into the framework. Concerning the dependent variable, we considered content allocation to be a continuum between centralization and decentralization. Another way of classifying content allocation might be to include hybrid forms allowing both centralized and decentralized allocation configurations at the same time.

There were other limitations. First, we relied exclusively on self-reported measures, which can introduce a common response bias across constructs. Second, generalization of the results may be limited due to the small sample size. Third, although all hypotheses were stated in associational terms, the logic behind them implies that contingency variables are not just associated with, but cause higher or lower levels of content allocation. Because the data in this study is cross-sectional and snapshot-like, its ability to support causal inferences is limited.

As this study focused instead on describing how publishing companies of different sizes and structures as well as with different IT decision-making models and IT processing requirements allocate and organize their content, it cannot predict which configuration for content allocation will be more efficient than others in terms of business performance.

## 6. Conclusions

Our analysis of survey data showed that the decision making of publishing companies on content allocation mainly rests on the fulfillment of their information processing requirements and the degree of organizational specialization. The contingency variables had a higher explanatory significance for content distribution, defined as the physical dissemination of content in one or multiple storage areas, than for content integration, defined as the extent to which content media are accessible in the publishing company.

All in all, we believe that we have added credence to a pluralistic research methodology that supports the integration of multiple contingencies into a comprehensive framework.
