# A Study on the Impact of Design Attributes on E-payment Service Utility

## Abstract

Prior research has shown how consumer perception, when risk is an important factor, may affect the adoption and the usage of E-payment service. Given this general knowledge, this research looks into the more specific issues of the mix of design attributes to drive consumer choice in using E-payment services. Using six design attributes defined by a group of practitioners and E-payment service users using the Delphi Method, an online conjoint experiment is conducted. We find out how these design attributes may impact consumer choice under risk, and the rank order of the magnitudes of their effects.

## Keywords:

E-payment service; Website design; B2C E-Commerce; Perceived risk; Conjoint analysis.

#

## 1. Introduction

The development of E-business technologies in the past two decades has significantly changed the way of conducting retail businesses. With the help of the latest E-business technologies, Business-to-Consumer (B2C) E-commerce operators can develop their retail businesses in the virtual world with minimal cost. Some companies, such as Amazon and those E-vendors operating on eBay, do not have any physical stores. This virtual business model provides B2C E-commerce operators with the opportunities to get connected with their online customers from all over the world on a 24×7 basis. However, some online customers only surf these websites but do not complete their transactions online because they perceive making payment online using E-payment service is risky (Hoffman, Novak, & Peralta, 1999; Hu, Lin, Whinston, & Zhang, 2004). Therefore, many electronic marketplaces, like eBay, have developed specific design attributes, such as a peer-evaluation system, to reduce the risk of making online payments (Ba & Pavlou, 2002; Dellarocas, 2003). Most of these design attributes increase the probability of closing online transactions by reducing the perceived risk of participating in online transactions.

Prior research has shown that both hedonic and functional qualities of E-service have positive impacts on its perceived values (Bernardo, Marimon, & Alonso-Almedia, 2012). However, the perceived risk of using E-services, including E-payment services, reduces perceived usefulness and the intent to use E-services by users (Featherman & Wells, 2010). In addition, Im, Kim and Han (2008) also reported that the perceived ease of use of a technology has a more significant impact on a user’s adoption of a risky technology compared with a less risky technology; and perceived usefulness of a technology has a more significant impact on a user’s adoption of a less risky technology than a risky technology. In a review conducted by Darley, Blankson, and Luethge (2010), it is reported that perceived risk is an important factor affecting online consumer behavior. Pavlou (2003) and Pavlou and Gefen (2004) showed that

#

perceived risk has a negative impact on consumers’ intention to undertake a transaction with an E-retailer. Gupta and Kim (2010) also reported that perceived risk has a negative impact to purchase intention in an online environment through the perceived value of the E-service concerned. On the other hand, marketing researchers have suggested that the attributes of a service have significant impacts on its perceived risk levels (Money, Tromp, & Wegner, 1988). Thus, by manipulating the attributes of a service, it is possible to vary the level of impact of perceived risk on the intent to use the service. Hence, if an E-payment service engineer develops some design attributes which can reduce the level of perceived risk for using such service, these attributes will reduce the overall negative impact of perceived risk on the intent to use E-service by users. This will convert more web surfers into online shoppers.

The core research issue in this study is to investigate issues related to the service design of an information system. Prior research has shown the importance of service attributes towards user satisfaction on E-services (See-To, Papagiannidis, & Cho, 2012). Venkatesh (2006) suggested IS researchers should conduct research studies on “the right mix of attributes to drive consumer choice” in service design research using statistical techniques like conjoint analysis. In this study, we investigate the impacts of six design attributes of an E-payment service selected by a group of practitioners and E-payment service users through the Delphi Method (Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004). Those attributes are believed to have impacts on the risk propensity of using E-payment services. The incorporation of the ideas of the group of practitioners and users into our research design echoes the suggestion by Lee (2010) who suggests that “the starting point of IS research need not be existing theory” but can be the wisdom or craftsmanship of IS professionals and practitioners, which eventually will enhance the practical implications of this research. Hence, our main task is to find the right mix of design attributes to drive consumer choice in using E-payment services (Venkatesh, 2006), which can help researchers and practitioners to gain a better understanding on how technology interacts with social and behavioral aspects of consumers in the E-payment service context. To achieve our research goal, conjoint analysis is selected as the statistical technique used in this study (Green & Srinivasan, 1990), which allows us to rank the impacts of different design attributes based on their relative importance, and to understand their relative impacts on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services.

The results of this study have several theoretical contributions and practical implications, and one methodological contribution. First, they extend knowledge on how design attributes of an E-payment system may affect the associated consumers’ utility through their influence on the perceived risk level. Second, this study shows the value of conjoint analysis, as a methodology, in analyzing the non-linear impact of constructs on information system adoption. For practical perspectives, our results help E-commerce operators to improve and better plan for the designs of their E-payment services by enhancing consumers’ utility of using these E-payment service websites. In addition, they provide insights to Ecommerce operators on the effectiveness of different design attributes on the consumers’ utility of using these E-payment service websites, which can be indirectly measured as a monetary value (Hann, Hui, Lee, & Png, 2008).

## 2. Research Model and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Research model

We investigate the impacts of different design attributes of E-payment services on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. The design attributes concerned are developed to reduce the negative effect (i.e., negative impact on utility) of different dimensions of perceived risk on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services, which directly affect and increase the intention to adopt the service (Featherman & Pavlou, 2003) due to the increase of risk propensity of consumers using the service. Prior research studies on perceived risk on consumer behavior can be dated back to the seminal article of Bauer

#

(1960), which defines that risk “will produce consequences which (consumer) cannot anticipate with anything approximating certainty, and some of which at least are likely to be unpleasant”. Other researchers, such as Peter and Ryan (1976), enriched the definitions of perceived risk as a single construct, other researchers, like Stone and Grønhaug (1993), suggested that perceived risk can be analyzed as a multidimensional construct (Featherman & Wells, 2010) or measured by different aspects (Verdegem & De Marez, 2011).

IS researchers have already studied the impact of perceived risk on information systems for over two decades. In particular, they are interested in the impact of perceived risk of the intention to adopt E-services, such as E-banking service (Meuter, Bitner, Ostrom, & Brown, 2005) and E-payment service (Featherman & Wells, 2010). Some of these IS studies treat perceived risk as a single construct in studying consumers’ intention to transact with Eretailers and observe the negative impact of perceived risk of transaction intention (Pavlou, 2003; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Nicolaou and McKnight (2006) also researched into the impact of perceived risk on performing online transactions in an exchange and showed that perceived information quality reduces the level of perceived risk of users. Kim, Ferrin, and Rao (2008) also used perceived risk as a single construct to study the impact of risk and trust on the intention of online consumers to purchase. More recently, Chang and Wu (2011) revisited the impact of perceived risk on purchase intention in an online environment and reported that the impact of perceived risk to purchase intention is moderated by the decisionmaking style.

Other IS studies investigated perceived risk based on its different risk dimensions. Ho and Ng (1994) investigated the level of perceived risk of different types of E-payment services using five risk dimensions. They showed that the level of perceived risk of different risk dimensions depends on the types of E-payment service concerned, and the amount of

#

money involved in the business transactions. Hann, Hui, Lee, and Png (2008) focused their investigation on the impact of privacy risk on consumers’ utility of E-commerce website design using a conjoint analysis. They suggested that it is possible to offset the negative effect of privacy risk by monetary reward and future convenience provided by the websites. Lou, Li, Zhang, and Shim (2010) studied the impact of perceived risk on E-banking services and showed that perceived risk has a negative impact on the intention to adopt E-banking services and the performance expectancy of E-banking services. Featherman and his colleagues also conducted several studies on E-payment services by operationalizing perceived risk by several risk dimensions (Featherman & Pavlou, 2003; Featherman & Wells, 2010; Featherman, Wright, Thatcher, Zimmer, & Pak, 2011). In this study, we define perceived risk using the perceived risk dimensions proposed by Featherman and Wells (2010), as reported in Table 1.

In this study, we focused our investigation on six design attributes of E-payment service, i.e., (i) monetary reward per transaction, (ii) consumers’ liability for losses per transaction, (iii) physical control feature through a physical token; (iv) anonymous payment record; (v) online information transfer; and (vi) acceptability of an E-payment services, on the consumer’s utility of using E-payment services. These six attributes were selected based on the recommendations from the group of practitioners and E-payment service users through the Delphi Method. This group consisted of 15 members, including ten practitioners and five users. Four of the practitioners were senior management of financial industries who had over ten years of experience and involvement in formulating banking policies in information security, compliance, credit and debit card services, and online enquiry services in relation to the E-banking services. The other three practitioners were senior management of the Ebusiness sectors who had over ten years of experience and involvement in the design of their

#

corporate website for retailing industries, which were able to handle online orders. The other three practitioners were senior management of E-service providers, whose companies were providing either E-payment service or web-portal services. These ten practitioners were involved to join the study based on their job relevance, as all of them were involved in the industries, which allow us to gain their insights of the E-payment service requirement across industries. The five users involved in the Delphi Study included two business school doctoral students, two MBA students who had more than five years of full-time working experience in major retailing companies, and one undergraduate student. The doctoral and MBA students were having more than ten years of experience in making purchases online and using Ebanking services, and the undergraduate student had around three years of similar experiences. We anticipate the doctoral students can provide us with opinions through the theoretical lens, the MBA students can provide us with opinions through the practical perspectives, and the undergraduate student can provide us with opinions of young people. Similar to other IS study (Peffers & Tuunanen, 2005; Okoli & Pawlowski, 2004; Worrell, Gangi, & Bush, 2013), we employed the Delphi Method to identify the important factors in the development of E-payment services from the practitioners’ side at the beginning of this research. This arrangement can help us to gain some insights in our research design from the experts and professional contexts (Landeta, Barrutia, & Lertxundi, 2011). Through three rounds of interview, we were able to boil down their suggestions and insights into the six design attributes that we mentioned. Afterwards, we started our in-depth literature review to find the theoretical support for including these six attributes into our research model which will be reported in the following sub-sections. Therefore, the selection was based on both the theoretical implications and practical values of these attributes. In the development of our research model, we were also concerned about the practical implications of our study of our theory development (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Desouza, El Sawy, Galliers, Lobbecke, & Watson, 2006; Lyytinen, 1999), which echoes Lee’s comment (2010) mentioned previously.

According to the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), the attitude toward a behavior (including attitude towards the use of an information system) is developed based on an expectancy-value model, which is a measurement of attitude based on its utility, as shown in Equation (1) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975):

$$
A = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} b _ {i} e _ {i}\tag{1}
$$

Therefore, when applying TRA into the adoption of E-services, the intention to adopt an E-payment service can be estimated by the sum of the product of the evaluation of its design attributes (i.e., e<sub>i</sub>) and its subjective probability related to the corresponding design attributes towards the use of the E-payment service (i.e., b<sub>i</sub>), and the intention to adopt the Epayment service is measured based on its consumers’ utility. Here, we define the consumers’ utility of using an E-payment service as the benefits gained from using the E-payment service by the consumer less the corresponding perceived usage risks (Featherman & Pavlou, 2003).

We manipulated the six design attributes of E-payment service in this study to facilitate investigation into whether they reduce the negative effect of different dimensions of perceived risk on the attitude of consumers on the intention to adopt an E-payment service. In other words, we would like to observe whether these design attributes can increase the risk propensity of the attitude of consumers on the intention to adopt these E-payment service. The six design attributes of E-payment services proposed by our group of practitioner and Epayment service users do not have any impacts on social risk and psychological risk as the impacts of these two risk dimensions are difficult to manipulate by varying design attributes. We also included three control variables in our research model, i.e., gender, age, and educational background, as well as a system feature as moderator. Figure 1 shows our research model.

## 2.2 Financial risk: monetary reward and consumers’ liability for losses per transaction

The first two design attributes in our research model are designed to reduce the negative impact of the financial risk on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. Financial risk, in the context of the usage of an E-payment service, is the assessment conducted by the consumers on “the potential financial losses because of potential Internet fraud” (Featherman & Wells, 2010). Ho and Ng (1994) suggested that the impact of financial risk can be reduced by mechanisms such as a money-back guarantee. In this study, we investigated into how the amount of monetary reward and consumers’ liability for losses affect the negative impact of financial risk on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. These two design attributes have financial impacts to their users (Smith & Walker, 1993), which can be explained using the Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992).

The provision of monetary reward has two impacts on consumers’ utility of using Epayment services. First, it offsets part of the financial loss of consumers if they face an Internet fraud. Plus, it helps to lock-in customers for using a particular E-payment service as the monetary reward is only usable on the particular E-payment service concerned. On the other hand, the provision of a low consumers’ liability for losses attracts customers as it provides financial protection as a money-back guarantee to and limited liability for the buyers in case they fall victim to an Internet fraud.

In the E-payment service perspective, the provision of a monetary reward of a consumer is actually a discount on the price of goods and service sold. Thus, it is an operating expense. Further, the consumers’ liability for losses imposed by E-payment service providers has a financial impact on the companies as it affects the money to be written-off for the losses related to Internet frauds. As both types are operating expenses for E-payment service

providers, it is important for E-payment service providers to understand which of these two mechanisms is more attractive to consumers, i.e., having a higher impact on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. Another possible alternative is to optimize the net effect on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services by developing a suitable mix of these two key attributes, which encourages consumers to participate in online transactions by reducing the negative attitude on the intention to use E-payment services.

Monetary reward per transaction, such as cash rebates or coupons are frequently provided for E-payment services to their consumers to encourage them to complete their transactions online. Apart from that, this arrangement also encourages customers to continue using their services in the future. Prior literature (Smith & Walker, 1993) shows that the provision of monetary reward has a significant but diminishing impact on consumers’ decision making. As suggested by the Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), there is a diminishing sensitivity to gain and loss. As shown in the first quadrant of Figure 2, people usually underestimate the expected value for high gain and overestimate the expected value for low gain (see the comparison between x<sub>1</sub> and $\mathbf { X } _ { 2 } ,$ and $\bf f ( x _ { 1 } )$ and $\tilde { \bf f } ( { \bf x } _ { 2 } ) )$ ). As a result, the slope or $\tilde { \mathbf { f } } ^ { \prime } ( \mathbf { x } _ { \mathrm { i } } )$ , which is the per unit effect of gain, is smaller for high gain when compared with the low gain, which is a diminishing effect (i.e., $\vec { \bf f } ^ { \prime } ( \mathbb { x } _ { \geq } ) <$ $\bar { \mathbf { f } } ^ { \prime } ( \mathbf { x _ { 1 } } ) )$ . Based on this idea, we conjecture that monetary reward reduces the negative impact of the financial risk on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services in a diminishing rate, i.e., the per-dollar effect of monetary reward on reducing the negative impact of financial risk. Thus, there is a positive, but a diminishing impact of monetary reward on consumers’ utility of using E-payment services.

H1.1. The monetary reward provided by an E-payment service has a positive, but a diminishing impact on the consumers’ utility of using the E-payment service.

While monetary reward stimulates consumers to use E-payment services through enhancing their utility on using the service, to cap the consumers’ liability for losses is an alternative approach for attracting consumers to use the E-payment services, which reduce the uncertainty (i.e., the potential level of financial lost) in using E-payment services (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Thaler, 1985; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992). Due to the risk of facing Internet fraud (Hu, Lin, Whinston, & Zhang, 2004), consumers need additional incentives to compensate for their potential losses caused by using E-payment services to purchase goods and services online. Thus, many E-payment service websites and third party payment systems have set an upper limit of financial liability for their consumers. Based on the premises of the Prospect Theory (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992), we suggest that the negative utility of the per unit liability for losses also has a diminishing return. As shown in the third quadrant of Figure 2, people usually underestimate the expected value for high loss and overestimate the expected value for low loss (see the comparison between ${ \bf X } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathbf { X } _ { 4 } ,$ and $\tilde { \bf f } ( { \bf x } _ { 3 } )$ and $\tilde { \mathbf { f } } ( \mathbf { x } _ { \perp } ) ) ,$ ). Based on this reason, we make the conjecture that there is a negative, but a diminishing impact on the amount of consumers’ liability for losses on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services.

H1.2. The consumers’ liability for losses set by an E-payment service has a negative, but a diminishing impact on the consumers’ utility of using the E-payment service.

2.3. Performance risk: physical control feature

The second group of design attributes in our research model is designed to reduce the negative impact of performance risk on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. Performance risk, in the context of the usage of an E-payment service, is the assessment

conducted by the consumer on “potential performance problems, malfunctioning, transaction processing errors, reliability and/or security problems” that prevent an E-payment service being able to perform as expected (Featherman & Wells, 2010). As suggested by our group of practitioners and E-payment service users, we concentrated on the security problem of the performance risk as this is the most important facet of performance risk, which most online customers care about.

One of the common methods to reduce the security problem in general is to implement an authentication process into the system. In this study, we investigated into the impact of using a physical security feature in the authentication process on consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. Prior literature shows that the provision of security features has a significant impact on consumers’ utility in using telephone shopping (Cox & Rich, 1964), which has a high similarity with today’s E-payment services. In addition, the use of physical security devices for authentication is a common practice in information security (Whitman & Mattord, 2009). Therefore, we anticipate that the use of a physical security feature for authentication reduces the negative impact of performance risk on the consumers’ utility of using the E-payment service. Hence, we have our H2 as follows:-

H2. Incorporating a physical control feature into an E-payment service has a positive effect on consumer utility, as compared to E-payment services without a physical control feature.

## 2.4. Privacy risk: anonymity payment method and offline transfer of information

The third group of design attributes in our research model is designed to reduce the negative impact of privacy risk on consumers’ utility of using E-payment services, which is receiving an increased concern by Internet users (Hong & Thong, 2013). Privacy risk, in the context of the usage of E-payment services, is the assessment conducted by the consumer on “potential losses to the privacy and confidentiality of personally identification information” which can lead to potential identity theft (Featherman & Wells, 2010). Privacy risk, and in

#

particular identity theft, is one of the main causes that makes consumers hesitate to complete their transactions online. When personal data, such as name, social security number, and credit or debit card information, are transferred online, it is always possible that these data are unlawfully intercepted by criminals. This issue has become a predominant problem as more and more users are using wireless-fidelity (Wi-Fi) networks to get online. Tsai, Egelman, Cranor, and Acquisti (2011) showed that some online consumers are more willing to purchase items from a website with privacy protection even if they need to pay a price premium. Hence, privacy protection provides consumers’ utility of the online purchase processes. Therefore, we conjecture that E-payment users prefer to have their payments handled anonymously as it reduces the risk of identity theft and provides privacy protection. In this case, even if criminals successfully intercept the information transmitted online or through the Wi-Fi, they cannot have full access to the information and cannot steal the identity of the users. For example, the E-payment service users can use their pre-assigned token for the processing of their payment and thus, they do not need to provide their personal data in the transaction processes. Therefore, the payment can be processed with anonymity. We also conjecture that E-payment service users prefer to have their information transferred offline compared to transferring their information online as it provides even further protection to their privacy. This arrangement can be done by allowing consumers to send payment requests from their computers to the E-payment service provider (which has the personal information and credit card information in the database when the consumer registered the service through a paperbased application) and the E-payment service provider will transfer such information to the Evendor directly. Such arrangement can help E-payment service users to enjoy the benefits of online payment (i.e., easier to search for products online and to complete the transactions in a fast and paperless way) as well as protecting their personal identity (i.e., their personal

information is transferred from the E-payment service provider to the E-vendor directly through an offline channel). Thus, we have the following two hypotheses:-

H3.1. Consumers’ utility of using an E-payment service with the anonymity payment method is higher than that with the named payment method.

H3.2. Consumers’ utility of using an E-payment service with the offline transfer of information is higher than that with the online transfer of information.

## 2.5. Time risk: acceptability

The last attribute that we studied is the acceptability of E-payment services by Evendors, which we conjecture reduces the negative impact of time risk on consumers’ utility of using E-payment services. Featherman and Wells (2010) defined time risk as the assessment by consumers on “the potential loss of convenience, time and effort caused by wasting time searching, purchasing, setting up, switching to, and learning how to use the Epayment service”. Ho and Ng (1994) also suggested that time risk is the risk of “taking up more time to complete a transaction” online. In this study, we define acceptability as the level of acceptance of the E-payment service by the online merchants. If more online merchants are willing to use a particular E-payment service, the users will take less time to complete an online transaction as they don’t need to waste their time in searching, setting up, and switching to E-payment service when they make their purchase with different online merchants, and thus, reduces the time risk.

We anticipate that if the level of the acceptability of E-payment services on the Internet is high, the consumers’ utility of using the E-payment service is also high. It is because the users of the E-payment service enjoy more convenience of online shopping if the E-payment services concerned are highly accepted by E-vendors (Bhatnagar, Misra, & Rao, 2000; Cox & Rich, 1964) and the time risk is reduced. This can help to log-in customers for future usage (Murray & Häubl, 2007; Zauberman, 2003). In this study, we tested the impact of the acceptability of E-payment services by E-vendors on consumers’ utility at two levels, i.e., the E-payment service is either accepted by all websites or accepted only by some pre-selected websites. We have our H4 as follows.

H4. The level of acceptability of E-payment service by E-vendors has a positive impact on the consumer’s utility of using the E-payment service.

## 2.6. The Impact of RFID-based stored-value smart-card type

In this study, we used an RFID-based stored-value smart card as the E-payment service platform for our investigation. This RFID-based stored-value smart card is used commonly in the city in which we conducted our investigation. This smart card is initially developed as a stored-value card for the subway system in the city, which has now evolved as the most predominant off-line payment smart card system in the metropolis which carries most of the features of a debit card. While it can be used for paying transportation fares for nearly all transportation systems in the city, it can also be used for settling transactions in supermarkets and convenience stores. The smart card users can top-up the stored value of the card by cash at train stations, supermarkets, and convenience stores, or by direct debit authorization via their bank or credit card accounts. There are two types of smart card, i.e., an anonymous card (with a unique card ID) which is without direct debit authorization function and its users need to use cash to top-up its value, and a personalized card which has the name of the user imprinted on the card and embedded with the direct debit authorization function. When a user is using these cards to conduct a transaction, the system will only capture the ID of the card and transmit it through the payment system. Therefore, the personal information of the user will not be transmitted through the system. It is obvious that the direct debit authorization function of the personalized card will inherit a higher level of risk to its users

when the card is being stolen, as there is a chance that the personal information of the users will be stolen and the culprits will be able to steal more money through the direct debit authorization function. Hence, we conjecture users of the personalized card would be the group of users who are more readily to take risks, whereas the users of the anonymous card would be more likely to be risk averse users. As prior research reports perceived risk level has an impact on a user’s intention to purchase online (Kim, Ferrin, & Rao, 2008), we anticipate that these two groups of users would have different attitudes towards the E-payment service we investigate in this study. Hence, we have our H5 as follows.

H5. The users of anonymous card and the users of personalized card will have different attitudes towards the different design features investigated in this study.

## 3. Research Methodology

## 3.1. Design of the Online Experiment

In our study, the experimental subjects were first presented with a proposed version of the E-payment service to be provided by the RFID-based stored-value smart card that included a combination of particular levels within the six design attributes that we wanted to investigate. As suggested by our group of practitioners and E-payment service users, we studied the impact of monetary reward on consumers’ utility using E-payment services with three different monetary levels, i.e., US\$0.64 (i.e., the price of a can of soft drink), US\$1.28 (approximately the price of a local magazine), and US\$6.41 (approximately the price of a set meal in a café), respectively. We also tested the impact of consumers’ liability to losses per transaction on consumers’ utility using E-payment services with three different monetary levels as proposed by the group, i.e., US\$64.1, US\$128.2, and US\$256.4. We studied the impact of physical control on consumers’ utility by incorporating the said smart card as a physical security feature for authentication during a transaction (Code = 1), compared with conducting a transaction without such authentication procedure (Code = 0). Concerning our

investigation on the privacy risk, we tested its impact by two methods, i.e., the anonymous payment method (Code = 0) versus a named payment method (Code = 1), and offline transfer of information (Code = 0) versus online transfer of information (Code = 1). Last but not least, we tested the level of acceptability of E-payment services by proposing the smart card system can be used for settling online payment for all websites (Code = 1) or for a limited number of websites (Code = 0).

To analyze the impact of the six design attributes of E-payment services on the consumers’ utility of E-payment service, we used a conjoint analysis. Conjoint analysis is one of the methods that researchers in economics (Debreu, 1960), psychology (Luce & Tukey, 1964), e-commerce product development (Helander & Jiao, 2002), and information systems use (Hann, Hui, Lee, & Png, 2008) for analyzing the relationship between the utility and the importance of different attributes of a product or a service in various marketing research studies (Mitchell, 1999). There are three major types of conjoint analysis, viz. full profile conjoint, adaptive and hybrid conjoint, and choice-based conjoint (Bakken & Frazier, 2006). In a full profile conjoint study, the experimenter presents the product or service as complete profiles consisting of all the attributes to the subjects and the subjects are asked to indicate their rank and utility score for the product or service (Green & Rao, 1971). In an adaptive and hybrid conjoint study, which is designed to handle scenarios which has an overwhelming number of attributes in the product/service, the subjects will be first asked to state their preferences and attribute importance before they review the profiles so that the researchers can reduce the number of profiles which the subjects need to review (Johnson, 1987). However, in a choice-based conjoint study, which applies the discrete choice model in the conjoint analysis, the subjects will be going through a sequence of experiments asking them asked to select their responses from a choice set made of different profiles (Louviere & Woodworth, 1983).

In this study, we used the full profile conjoint to analyze our data as there are not too many attributes included in our model. There are IS studies using conjoint analysis to analyze problems such as IT application services outsourcing (Schwarz, Jayatilaka, Hirschheim, & systems (Money, Tromp, & Wegner, 1988) and adoption of computer systems (Baker-Eveleth & Stone, 2008), etc. The data collected from a full conjoint analysis can be analyzed by multiple regression analysis.

Based on the premises of conjoint analysis, each of these proposed versions of attributes is called a conjoint stimulus. In total, there are a maximum of $3 \times 3 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2$ = 144 conjoint stimuli available in this study. To make our study manageable, we used the optimal orthogonal design proposed by Addelman (1962) to select 18 optimal designs for collecting our experimental data via an online experiment. The selection of these optimal designs can also be done by using an appropriate statistical software (such as SPSS) or performed manually based on the methods reported in the literature (Addelman, 1962; Green & Srinivasan, 1978; Steckel, DeSarbo, & Mahajan, 1991). The 18 optimal orthogonal designs used in this study are reported in Appendix A.

## 3.2. Control variables: gender, age, and educational background

Apart from studying the impacts of the six design attributes of E-payment services on consumers’ utility, we also included three control variables in our data analysis, i.e., gender, age, and educational background. Prior IS research revealed that gender factors have an impact on the intention to adopt information systems (Agarwal & Prasad, 1999; Bhatnagar, Misra, & Rao, 2000; Franek, 1990; Gefen & Straub, 1997; Venkatesh, Morris, & Ackerman, 2000; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). Other behavioral researchers reported that gender factors have significant impacts on decisions under risk (Fehr-Duda, Gennaro, & Schubert, 2006), in handling tangible and intangible claims (Holbrook, 1978), and in

considering objective and subjective claims when facing the risk (Darley & Smith, 1995). Gender was coded as a dichotomous variable, i.e., male = 0, female = 1, in our analysis.

Age has been shown as a mediator on the adoption of technology (Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003). Thus, it has been included in our model as a control variable, and was coded as a continuous variable (Dulebohn, 2002). Similarly, educational background was coded as a dummy variable, i.e., high school graduate or below = 1, college graduate (undergraduate and associate degree) = 2; and graduate degree holders = 3.

## 4. Data Collection

To study the impacts of the six design attributes on the consumers’ utility of Epayment services, we conducted an online experiment using the Government-to-Citizen (G2C) E-government portal of the municipal government concerned. We invited all the 160,000 subscribers of the G2C E-government portal to participate in this online experiment, which is one of the three different online experiments that we conducted simultaneously. To be eligible for participating in our online experiment, the participants were required to use their smart card to register on the online experiment website. Some small-value cash prizes as incentives were provided to our participants through a lucky draw. We collected over 2,500 responses from this online experiment during the four-week data collection period. After cleaning our data for missing or contradictory information, we got 1,795 subjects, providing a total of 10,879 pieces of usable responses. The demographic data are summarized in Table 3. When we compared the demographic profiles of our subjects with the local census about the Internet users, we noted that there is no significant difference between the age, gender, and educational background. The only difference is our subjects have more experience in conducting online purchase. This difference may due to the fact that our subjects are Egovernment portal users, who usually use the portal to obtain information and pay for government bills. Therefore, they are more likely to have prior experience in conduct online payment. As this research is investigating the design attributes of E-payment service, we are of the view that our subjects have more online purchase experience is good for this study as they can better understand the processes related to E-payment service.

When we conducted our experiment, the operator of the RFID-based smart card was considering launching a new service for their customers by extending its service to E-payment services. Therefore, the operator wanted to have a detailed market analysis on the impacts of different payment protection attributes on consumers’ utility using the card through conducting an online experiment. The content of the online video had been viewed by our group of practitioners and E-payment service users and other independent IS researchers. It was agreed that the content provided gave the plain facts of the functionality of the design attributes, and would not create any bias toward the design attributes.

Our online experiment consisted of two parts. First, our subjects were asked to provide their demographic data and their smart card information. Afterwards, we introduced the six design attributes of the E-payment services concerned in this study through the video previously mentioned. After they confirmed their selections, we asked them to rate their selected stimuli with a score ranging from 0 to 100. Based on the theory of

conjoint analysis, the scores reported were the proxy of their consumers’ utility. Thus, it was used as the dependent variable in this study.

As it is possible that the significance of the coefficients of the regression models are caused by the huge sample size when $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ are small, we follow the procedures suggested by Murphy, Myors, and Wolach (2009) and conducted a test for the minimum-effect hypotheses on these regression models using a 0.01 level of effect size, a 0.05 level of alpha, and 0.80 for power. As suggested by Harrison, Özayan, and Meyers (1998), the low adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values in conjoint analysis are usually caused by the differences in each subject’s subjective rating for the same service, which additional variation is introduced to the regression models due to the aggregating effect. As the dependent variable of our study. i.e., utility, is a psychometric measurement and is usually spread out in a relatively board range among different individual, we are of the view that the low $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ and adjusted $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values are not due to the weak relationship between our dependent variable with our independent variables, but due to the variance within the dependent variable as suggested by prior literature (Bass, Tigert, & Lonsdale, 1968).

## 6.1. The analysis of relative importance – overall sample

Table 6 and Figure 3 summarize the ranking of the relative importance of all design attributes of the regression models that we developed in this study, i.e., the results for the overall sample, anonymous card and personalized card, respectively. We observe that monetary reward per transaction is the first rank in the relative importance of the respective conjoint analyses. This result indicates that monetary reward is the most important attribute of E-payment services and it attracts more consumers to use E-payment services. It is probably because cash rebates and coupons provide immediate rewards to online consumers. Thus, it brings the most significant positive impact to consumers’ utility in using E-payment services. Plus, we note that its impact is diminishing, i.e., the positive per US\$ impact of monetary reward per transaction is reduced when the amount of reward is increased.

Our result for the overall sample shows that the information transfer method is the second most important feature for affecting their consumers’ utility of E-payment services. Basically, our subjects prefer to have their transaction information be transferred offline as predicted in our H3.2. Nowadays, many people who used to participate in online transactions are reluctant to do so due to the hazards of identity theft. It is noted that the physical control feature is the least important, but still a significant feature among these six features of E-payment services, which supports H2. We suggest that the impact of smart card types on the relative importance of the design attributes is related to the risk aversion level of the respective types of users, as prior IS research showed that risk aversion has an impact on the adoption of E-payment services (Kolodinsky, Hogarth, & Hilgert, 2004). Users of anonymous cards face a lower level of risk in using it that for an E-payment service as the anonymous version of the card which does not capture any personal information on the card. In addition, as it does not have the direct debit authorization feature, the financial risk of using it is smaller than for a personalized card. However, users of personalized cards face more privacy risk (as their cards have their personal information) and financial risk (as they may lose more money due to the direct debt authorization arrangement). This is also consistent with the result of the moderating regression analysis, which shows that personalized card users are more sensitive to the attributes related to financial risk (i.e., more positive and negative responses to monetary reward and consumers’ liability for losses, respectively) and privacy risk (i.e., more negative responses to online data transfer of information).

When we further investigated the relative importance of users of anonymous cards and users of personalized cards, we noticed that their rankings of relative importance of these design attributes are significantly different. While both put monetary reward per transaction and physical control feature as their first rank and sixth rank respectively, users of anonymous cards rank acceptability of E-payment services by E-vendors, information transfer method, payment method, and consumers’ liability for losses per transaction as their second to fifth ranks whereas those personalized card users rank information transfer method, consumers’ liability for losses, acceptability of E-payment services by E-vendors, and payment method as their second to fifth ranks respectively. These differences in the rankings of relative importance can also be explained by the differences in the design of these two types of smart card.

As the users of anonymous card have limited financial risk, consumers’ liability for losses brings limited consumers’ utility of using the E-payment service. As these users also face limited privacy risk, due to the fact that no personal information is stored on their cards, information is transferred online or not, does not bring much to the impact level of consumers’ utility of using the E-payment services. As suggested by Im, Kim, and Han (2008), perceived usefulness of a technology has a more significant impact on a user’s adoption of a less risky technology, we can expect that the users of anonymous card (which is a less risky technology compared with the personalized card) will rank those features which are more useful for them to complete the online payment. Therefore, for these users, acceptability of E-payment services by E-vendors, which is the feature related to the usefulness of the anonymous card, becomes the second most important factor affecting the consumers’ utility of using the E-payment services to settle online payments.

However, users of personalized cards face a higher level of privacy risk and financial risk when they use it to conduct online payments via the E-payment services. Therefore, consumers’ liability for losses per transaction and online transfer of information and become two important design attributes for protecting their wealth and privacy, respectively. ## 7. Theoretical and Methodological Contributions, and Practical Implications

This paper has several theoretical and methodological contributions. First, it responds to Venkatesh’s question (2006) on the need to conduct service design research, which probes into the impacts of different aspects of service attributes towards consumer choice. This study demonstrates how to study an IS service design question using a conjoint analysis as a methodology. Our result shows that IS researchers and practitioners can together investigate service design issues, and results in a research outcome with theoretical and practical implications (Benbasat & Zmud, 1999; Desouza, El Sawy, Galliers, Lobbecke, & Watson, 2006; Lee, 2010; Lyytinen, 1999). As suggested by prior research (Furlan & Corradetti, 2005), conjoint analysis is a suitable method to study the joint effect of attributes of a product or services, and the full conjoint analysis, i.e., the one we used in this study, provides “exacting” result compared with the other two types of conjoint analysis. Plus, even though other regression methods, such as ordinal regression, could be used to analyze the data collected, the results generated by other regression methods would be difficult for users to interpret the relative importance of the factors. Therefore, the result of this study shows the possibility of using conjoint analysis, and in particular, the full conjoint analysis, to study the design of an IS service.

#

We also show that conjoint analysis provides additional flexibility in analyzing the impact of the design attributes of an information system. Prior studies in TAM (Venkatesh, Morris, & Ackerman, 2000; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003) usually used structural equation modeling (SEM) to study the impacts of various factors on the intention of adopting an information system, of which those factors could be the design attributes of an IS service measured using a survey. For most of these studies, the impacts of the factors are assumed to be linear. If the researchers assume that a factor would have a non-linear effect, they would need to explore such effect using the squared term in their SEM developed.

However, it is easier for the researchers to use the conjoint analysis to study such effect. First, the conjoint analysis needs a different research design, which is more simple and direct as the respondents are providing their utility of a particular set of design, instead of responding to a multiple sets of multiple-item survey constructs. Second, the design of the conjoint analysis is flexible, and is easy for researchers to probe into nonlinear relationships, such as the diminishing impact of the monetary reward on the consumers’ utility of using E-payment services, in exploring this type of information system design problem. This makes the conjoint analysis has an edge for studies related to the IS service research with non-linear impacts.

The results of our conjoint analyses reveal consumers’ preferences in the choice of design features, which is the major theoretical contribution of the study. E-payment service users prefer to have a high level of monetary reward with minimal (if possible no) consumers’ liability for losses, i.e., reducing financial risk. In addition, they prefer the Epayment services to be able to help them to settle online transactions in all websites, i.e., reducing the time risk. Further, they enjoy a high degree of protection of their personal information as they prefer to have their transaction data transferred offline with anonymity, i.e., reducing the privacy risk. In brief, this research shows the possibility of using the

#

conjoint analysis to probe into the design of a website. We also show that it is possible to use different design attributes of a website to reduce the negative impact of risk on the consumers’ utility on E-payment service. In particular, we show that the provision of monetary reward, which targeted the negative impact of financial risk, and the method of information transfer (in anonymity) and the wide acceptance of the E-payment service, which target to reduce the privacy risk and time risk respectively, can significantly enhance consumers’ utility on E-payment service. Therefore, our results provide a framework for further research in developing theories in service design. Our results, in particular, have impacts on the literature related to studies relating to the business impact of IT service design.

Apart from the above theoretical and methodological implications, our results have two practical implications which are useful for practitioners. First, we observed different levels of diminishing returns on the monetary reward and consumers’ liability for losses with types of smart card users, which probably arising from the different levels of risk propensity of the two types of smart card users. These results provide E-payment service operators and designers with a yardstick to refine their designs of their E-payment services. In particular, these results provide them with an insight into how to allocate their resources for providing monetary reward and consumers’ liability for losses to their potential consumers. For example, if customers anticipate that the chance of facing Internet fraud is lower, the Epayment service provider should consider providing a low consumers’ liability for losses. It is because, from the consumers’ perspective, such liability brings a negative impact on their utility on using E-payment services, and thus, it will reduce their intention to adopt the Epayment service concerned. Also, consumers are very concerned about their privacy (as reflected by the concern of the information transfer method), the acceptance of the Epayment service by other online vendors. Therefore, practitioners should also incorporate

these features into the E-payment service, and to emphases the existence of these design attributes when they promote their service to the potential consumers.

Second, the moderating effect of the type of smart card has a significant implication to practitioners. Our results show that consumers with different types of smart card who faced different levels of risk have different preferences on ranking the relative importance of these design attributes of E-payment services, which means that practitioners may need to consider developing different types of E-payment services for different types of consumers. More importantly, we show that the choice of payment media, i.e., the choice of smart card, of this study, can be a proxy of the risk level faced by consumers. These findings can definitely help practitioners to enhance their design of E-payment services.

## 8. Limitations and Future Research

Similar to other research, this study also has limitations. First, we only considered one type of E-payment service, i.e., an RFID-based stored-value smart card, which is designed for micropayments. Therefore, our result may not be directly applicable to other types of Epayment services, such as a credit card payment system or a third party payment system. However, as the features investigated in this study are commonly used by other types of Epayment services, we anticipate that our results are still useful for other types of E-payment service. In addition, prior research results have shown that conjoint analysis can achieve a reasonable level of predictive accuracy (Vavra, Green, & Krieger, 1999), so we are of the view that our results can provide useful managerial implications.

Second, non-response bias is possible for our type of study. As mentioned in Section 4: Data Analysis, we compared the demographic profiles of our subjects with the city’s demographics reported in a local census and noted that they were very similar, except our subjects had more online purchase experience. This reflected that the samples collected were representative. Coupled with the high Internet penetration rate (100%) and computer literacy rate (94%) in the city, we suggest that the impact of non-response bias should be low.

For future research, IS researchers can consider investigating the impact of culture and other design features on other types of online payment services, such as a third party payment system. Another possible extension of this research is to study the impact of customization on the E-payment service design features by the users when they purchase different types of product, which prior research showed that customers’ valuation for the customization strategies of E-retailing has a correlation with the product types (Thirumalai & Sinha, 2009).

## 9. Conclusion

This is a pioneer study on E-payment service design research. We developed our research model based on recommendations from a group with practitioners and users of Epayment service through the Delphi Method. These design attributes were developed to counter the negative impacts of various facets of perceived risk. From the result of our conjoint analyses, we showed that we could enhance the consumers’ utility of using Epayment services by operationalizing the design attributes of E-payment services. We also showed that conjoint analysis is a promising method for conducting service design research. Our findings also have several significant practical implications. Practitioners may gain a better understanding of the consumers’ utility implications of E-payment service design attributes, and move forward to better E-payment service design.
