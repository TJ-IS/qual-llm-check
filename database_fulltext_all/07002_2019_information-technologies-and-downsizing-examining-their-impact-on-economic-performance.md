## 1. Introduction

Episodes of significant intentional reduction in workforce by firms by mass layoffs, that is, downsizing [1], have been commonplace since the 1980s. These job cuts, representing differ ent levels of slack reduction [2,3], are not only practices adopted by organizations with severe losses trying to restructure their operations to survive in the market but also specific strategies deployed by healthy companies looking to reduce costs and boost financial performance, productivity, and efficiency [4,5,6]. Regardless of the causes, downsizing has become a standard management practice in the US and Europe, so much so that it is considered an insti tutionalized protocol in western economies [7,8,6,9,10,11] and in countries with a deeply rooted tradition of job protection, as in the case of Japan [12].

One would therefore expect that the effects of downsizing on companies that use this policy would be beneficial, increasing their profitability and their probability of survival. However, eral consensus [13,14,15].

Several studies have found that downsizing may seriously damage the human capital stock and the learning capability of firms [16]. One of the possible consequences of downsizing is that employees with critical individual abilities and resources are being dispensed with, which at the same time damages the social networks that these employees participate in and the organizational memory, which are the basis of the organization’s learning capacity. If we consider that this capacity is related to the productivity and performance of the organization, this investigation puts forth the idea that downsizing will have a negative impact on the profitabil ity of the organization.

These contradictory results motivated the selection of a contingent approach to determine in which situations the implementation of downsizing can stimulate an improvement in both the performance and the productivity of an organization [17,18]. A number of studies have explored external contingencies [2] or factors that moderate the performance impact of workforce downsizing. Recently, studying responsible downsizing strategies has attracted the at tention of numerous scholars [13,19,20,21]. These authors conclude that the performance effects of downsizing depend on the manner in which downsizing is carried out.

Until now, a very small number of studies have analyzed these factors or internal contingencies, which examine the link between the workforce downsizing process and organizational performance.

Among these internal factors, information technology (IT) can be considered particularly rel evant. In particular, the implementation of IT is positively associated with the capability to acquire, disseminate, and interpret information, and along with organizational memory, IT could be considered critical in knowledge management, innovation management, and organizational learning [22]. In this manner, the possible negative effects of downsizing on organi zational learning could be offset by the positive effects of IT on organizational capacities. Human resource practices can also work to complement IT. Both contingencies are linked to the downsizing process and to organizational learning capacity and therefore might help to mitigate the generally negative effects of downsizing on organizations.

Studying the downsizing process in conjunction with the use of IT in companies is particular ly useful as it determines whether the individual consequences of dysfunctional downsizing, such as the lack of employee motivation, stress, and depression, along with organizational consequences such as the loss of organizational memory and human and social capital and a decline in the organization’s overall performance, can be attenuated by some specific organi zational practices [15, 23] associated with IT implementation.

Working with a sample of firms competing in the Spanish Chemical Industry, the main aim of this study is to justify and empirically analyze the moderating role of the implementation level of IT in the relationship between downsizing and organizational performance. IT can contribute significantly to improving the efficiency of the processes and to maintaining adequate levels of organizational learning and is crucial in compensating the losses that downsizing produces in these organizational dimensions.

In doing so, we intend to offer two main contributions. First, we provide arguments to show how IT resources may impact organizational economic performance by interacting with other organizational processes (e.g., downsizing). Second, we add empirical evidence to previous ditional evidence in distinct settings involving different contingencies is needed to clarify the controversial relationship between downsizing and organizational performance [14].

## 2. Literature review and hypothesis

## 2.1 Downsizing and performance

Even though there is a wide range of literature related to the economic and financial effects of organizational downsizing, this issue remains controversial even today. Downsizing is seen theoretically as a strategy adopted to increase organizational productivity and efficiency [25], but most studies have not been able to find evidence that supports the expected performance improvements associated with this workforce reduction strategy [20,26]. The organizational contexts in which these effects have been analyzed mainly include US companies [2,13,27, 28], but studies also exist in other contexts such as in Spain [29, 30], Portugal [31], China [32], and Germany [26].

Empirical literature reveals contradictory conclusions among studies on this topic. Some scholars argue that downsizing implies saving on personnel costs and general expenses, achieving a more competitive position, efficiency, and productivity, thus resulting in a posi tive effect on firm performance [14,33,34,35], even if downsizing benefits take several years to materialize [28]. Recent literature has offered evidence of positive downsizing effects un der certain circumstances using a contingent framework [21,27]. Thus, the type of downsizing strategy applied can influence the economic effects that the practice has, thereby improving

the results of those organizations that implement systematic or wide-reaching strategies [3]

which involve their employees and promote communication [36]. This can also be accom

plished when the downsizing measures are accompanied by asset restructuration [1] and are

carried out proactively [3]. In the same way, less negative repercussions have been seen in

stock markets when the cause of downsizing is restructuring and consolidation [37] or when

proactive layoffs are announced, characteristic of a downscoping or a restructuring strategy

[38]. It has also been proven that the stock market responds positively to downsizing

measures that do not imply the loss of critical knowledge of an organization, which consti

tutes the key to its competitive advantage [39].

Furthermore, there are other studies that have not found empirical evidence of such improve-

ments [29,40] or that have even cited the detrimental impact of downsizing on organizational

performance [26,30,41,42]. De Meuse et al. [14] identified a possible factor that contributes to

these negative results as the perception of the employee that there has been a breach of a psy-

chological contract established between the organization and its workers. As Datta et al. [15]

highlighted, downsizing has a significant potential to affect group and individual attitudes and

behavior, disrupt relationship networks, and destroy the trust and loyalty that bind employees

to their employers. Consequently, it can lead to many adverse effects such as reducing em

ployees’ organizational contributions, lowering creativity, or reducing job involvement, work

group support, and resource availability [43,44,45,46]. In addition, an increase in workplace

conflicts has been observed among downsized firms [47]. Therefore, this workforce reduction

strategy also contributes to a decline in the post-downsizing work environment [15].

The damage to human capital associated with workforce reductions might result in the loss of

employee knowledge, capabilities, and skills [48,49] or the destruction of social networks and

organizational learning capacity [16]. That is, the removal of individuals within an organiza tion not only damages its human capital but also alters the organization’s social structure and hence its social capital [21] and finally the organizational capital. Fisher and White [16] warned about the potential risk of downsizing to organizational learning and memory, consid ering a social network framework. When an organization is viewed as a collection of networks in which the interrelationships among individuals generate learning and accumulate a knowledge base (the firm’s memory), the potential damage of removing an individual in downsizing to organizational learning far exceeds that implied by linear head-count ratios. Removing an individual from a learning network will cause more damage to the individual loss of memory and personal skills and could seriously put the learning and memory capacity of organizations at risk.

On the other side, special attention has been paid to the impact of downsizing on survivors, who may experience negative feelings or attitudes (anger, job insecurity, low commitment, morale, or motivation) toward the organization [50,51,52,53,54], also known as the “survivor syndrome” [55]. These effects are expected to cause a deterioration in organizational learning and workforce productivity [11] and, therefore, the worsening of the economic performance of those companies that implement a downsizing strategy [28].

The loss of skills and individual knowledge and problems with employee motivations may reduce the overall level of organizational stock of knowledge and may hamper individual creativity [46] as well as the encouragement for experimentation and problem solving. The organizational capacity to acquire external and internal information may also be reduced. In addition, downsizing breaks up the networks of informal relationships [56] and social networks where ex-employees interacted. Job rotation opportunities are also lost because down sizing reduces the number of employees available to perform the work. All these generate communication problems, make disseminating and interpreting information difficult, and cause a decline in organizational problem-solving and the loss of the ability to create and share mental models and organizational memory [16]. In addition, downsizing may undermine an organization’s long-term competitive advantage if it leads to lower levels of motiva tion, commitment, and productivity among surviving employees [20,57]. To sum up, these arguments suggest that downsizing will in all likelihood negatively affect the organizations that carry out it, thus lessening their ability for organizational learning and, as a result, weak ening their economic performance.

H1: Downsizing will have a negative effect on firm economic performance.

## 2.2 Information technology and downsizing

## 2.2.1. IT and performance

Currently, IT plays a critical role in an information-intensive economy, and its effect on organizational strategy is indisputable. Moreover, IT is a key instrument for organizations when striving for a strategic competitive advantage [58]. IT is defined as the application of computers and telecommunications equipment to store, retrieve, transmit, and manipulate data, often in the context of a business or other enterprise [59]. In this context, IT is valuable as it offers an extensive menu of potential benefits ranging from flexibility and quality improvement to cost reduction and productivity enhancement [60]. Brynjolfsson and Hitt [61] argued that IT can change how organizations are designed and can transform business processes. Managing IT resources and applying these technologies to work processes have different effects on or ganizations and their performance. Most studies of IT have concentrated on efficiency and effectiveness, changes to organizational structure, and the effects on employees’ careers [62], but empirical evidence of any positive impact on firm performance due to IT investment remains limited [63, 64].

Studies analyzing the effects that IT played on organizational performance in the 1980s did

not find a general trend. This lack of significance regarding the impact of IT on organizational

outcomes was known as the “productivity paradox” [63]. Nevertheless, during the 1990s, the

development of more advanced IT and the complementary investment of organizational capi

tal (e.g., job training or business process redesign) resulted in productivity growth [65]. One

example of IT evolution during these decades could be found in enterprise systems. Manufac

turing resource planning (MRP) systems were introduced during the 1980s to integrate infor

mation across different manufacturing activities. During the 1990s, these MRP systems

evolved into enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, which integrate information across

more functional areas [65]. The benefits from these advanced integration systems allowed

better ways of organizing resources, thus allowing firms to be more productive.

Therefore, over the last decades, studies examining the association between IT and organiza

tional performance (also known as IT business value) are divergent. Although they agree in

the tenet that IT is valuable, the extent and dimensions are dependent upon internal and exter

nal factors [60]. Looking for some moderation variables in that relationship, recent research

[63,64,66] provides useful insights into the effects of CEO incentives, institutional pressure,

levels of IT investment, or human capital, among others. That is, investments in IT assets con-

tribute to business value through a complex chain of complementary relationships between IT

resources and business resources [67].

IT business value has been studied at different analysis levels, using different study designs and metrics. A meta-analysis, considering different measures considered by the literature, concluded that IT investment is positively related to firm financial performance across studies and years [68]. Another literature review [69] showed that the majority of studies obtained positive and mixed findings at the organizational level. Following these arguments and the evidence presented, we can posit that IT enhances organizational performance, and therefore, IT is valuable.

H2: There is a positive relationship between IT implementation and firm economic performance.

## 2.2.2. Information technologies, downsizing, and performance

Although IT frequently eases employee reductions, there remains only scarce literature on downsizing that analyzes IT effects on organizational outputs. Some studies found that the implementation of workforce reductions, combined with a better use of these technologies, will result in a more efficient and rational downsizing [70], thereby reducing the negative impact of this practice [71]. Many downsizing activities, especially top-down approaches, are mainly concerned with reducing the total number of individuals in a firm, typically through layoffs, redundancies, and natural attrition [36]. Following this cost-cutting action, it is likely that key employees leave the firm, thereby producing overall negative consequences on the firm’s knowledge base, organizational memory, and critical skills [72]. That is, ineffective downsizing damages a firm’s intellectual capital (IC): human capital, social capital, and organizational capital [21,36,16]. IC is generally related to the collective knowledge of organizational members that can be used to not only increase firm performance but also enhance organizational value [73,74,75]. In this sense, IT implementation might be able to avoid losing IC. That is, IT could help to increase employees’ knowledge and skills (i.e., human capital), facilitate group interaction and knowledge sharing (i.e., social capital), and enable organizations to store knowledge in systems, routines, processes, and cultures (i.e., organizational capital), which, in turn, drive organizational performance.

As said previously, downsizing not only results in the loss of human capital stock but also has As said previously, downsizing not only results in the loss of human capital stock but also has a negative impact on survivors’ morale, attitude, and behavior [15]. Nevertheless, IT could help to moderate this effect, as IT boots labor productivity and increases the demand for more skilled workers. That is, high-skilled labor is a precondition for the use of these new technologies, as the contribution of IT can increase by training workers in problem-solving, statistical cally substitute computer decision-making for human decision-making in routine work but they also produce large quantities of data, which can be adequately utilized only by highskilled workers, managers, and professionals [76]. Therefore, IT becomes a factor that complements human capital. IT contribution to social and organizational capital has also been described in the literature: IT enhances organizational learning by facilitating information availability or improving communication among employees [77,78], and learning capability positively impacts organizational performance [79,80]. The literature also found that IT could lead to improvements in organizational memory [81,82]. Thus, the combination of IT and downsizing could help to prevent and mitigate the loss of organizational memory [71]. At the organizational level, IT can change processes, thus encouraging flatter structures, leaner processes, and more empowered employees [83]. Such a change can improve the quality of organizational memory [71]. First, empowered employees can use their own memory in a more flexible and active way than less self-managed ones. Second, IT encourages team-based organizational structures. These are flatter structures than the traditional ones, thus allowing easy contact among team members. Therefore, over time, a database of solutions to special problems will be created at the team level. In this situation, the loss of members and high potential talent may not result in a severe loss of team-level memory. These social ties also build networks over time that facilitate knowledge acquisition and assimilation, thereby enhancing organizational learning [84].

The existing literature posits that organizational memory is a resource that firms can deploy to

improve their financial performance [85]. In this sense, several studies have shown that or-

ganizational memory contributes to organizational effectiveness [71]. As a mechanism of ac

quisition, storage, and retrieval of information [86], it can prevent organizational rigidity.

That is, it includes the combination of the acquisition of new information and the retrieval of

old, retained information [87,88]. A well-performed organizational memory process will

combine the progress of updating new information with the updating of retained information.

This system allows organizations to speed up problem-solving by recognizing problems in

recurrent situations and generating solutions from past experiences [71]. From this, we can

infer that organizational memory can improve firm performance.

In sum, we can conclude that IT implementation may improve firm performance, develop

human skills, and boost organizational memory and organizational learning, thereby mitigat

ing the damage that downsizing often produces on these organizational capabilities. Moreo

ver, Schenkel and Teigland [21] underlined the role that IT may have in achieving a higher

level of network responsiveness in times of downsizing through documenting knowledge,

improving knowledge connectivity, and using social media tools. Furthermore, advances in

computer technologies such as mapping technology could help organizations to identify the

potential impact of removing specific individuals in the process of downsizing or restructur-

ing [16], thereby minimizing the loss of organizational learning and memory.

The intensive use of IT seems to allow for more rational, less-bounded downsizing decisions,

thus leading to a more efficient and effective extraction and consumption of slack resources

[70]. Nevertheless, in a less IT-intensive organization, knowledge about IT, work, and struc

ture, and how they fit together might be limited. When downsizing was required, managers

were unable to efficiently use IT to do so, and they had to make cuts across the board. Moreo-

ver, the loss of skilled workers, social networks, and organizational learning and memory caused by downsizing will not be mitigated by the critical role of IT in developing information systems. Therefore, we suggest the following hypothesis:

H3: The negative association between downsizing and firm economic performance is moderated by IT, such that it is lower for higher levels of IT.

## 3. Empirical analysis

## 3.1 Data

Our framework is tested on a sample of firms competing in the Spanish Chemical Industry. The sample frame was elaborated from the SABI (Sistema de Análisis de Balances para Ibe ria) database, which reports the financial and accounting information of those firms that are registered in the Mercantile Account and provides consolidated scores. To provide a research context, we assume that Spain offers the possibility to contribute to the results of current literature on downsizing: a large proportion of theoretical conceptions and empirical findings about downsizing have been constructed on the basis of data collected from US organizations, and there is a call to include international samples [15].

The number of chemical firms listed in the SABI is approximately 1226 firms; however, because of several problems such as wrong figures or extinguished companies, we finally decided on a sample of 1075. From this sample, we extracted main financial and accounting information that allowed us to create a new database from a postal questionnaire sent to 1075 liability companies functioning in 2003. Information was also obtained from a unique selfreported questionnaire carried out specifically on CEOs and the Director of Human Resources with closed questions about strategies, innovation activities, organizational characteristics, human resource policies, and information systems. The percentage of response was approximately 17%,<sup>1</sup> thus the final sample had 177 companies with reliable information.

The sample was distributed throughout the Spanish territory, and although we did not restrict the sample to SMEs previously, firms included in this sample in fact corresponded more to this type of organization (86%). In practice, we had 25 large firms (more than 200 workers) and 152 small and medium-sized firms (less than 200 workers). In terms of sub-sectors, we were able to gather information using the three-digit information code from the Spanish National Classification of Economic, the most important activities among these firms being pharmaceutical products, paint products, and detergent products. Our final sample was re duced to 132 firms due to missing responses to some questions.

The study analyzed data for the years 2003 and 2004. From the SABI, we collected financial information for these 2 years to calculate employment reduction (downsizing) and 2004 data to measure economic performance (return on assets (ROA)), while from our primary data (the rest of the variables obtained from the questionnaire), we could use only the 2003 data. The Spanish Chemical Industry presents a certain relevance regarding the study of downsizing during these years and even before. The economic press showed that the most important world leaders in the chemical industry downsized their companies worldwide at this time. DuPont cut 4000 jobs in the USA and Europe over 2001 and 2002, Bayer conducted 15000 layoffs from 2002 to 2005, and Down Chemical, the leading chemical manufacturer in the USA, reduced 5500 jobs between 2003 and 2004. The year 2004 proved particularly critical for chemical groups such as BASF (Germany), the largest in the world, and Clariant in Switzerland, which announced 3600 and 4000 layoffs, respectively. BASF justified downsizing as a necessary strategy to face the strong competition coming from Asia and to deal with the strict European legislation for the chemical industry (economic press 5 días, November 23,

2004). In this context, it is clear that Spanish chemical firms had to face a highly competitive environment in which downsizing seemed to be an appropriate strategy to deal with the situa tion at the time.

## 3.2 Methodology

We examined the direct effects of downsizing and information systems on economic perforable “economic performance”. This approach is typically used in management studies; however, we assume that some limitations are involved, such as we had a limited time span to collect our primary data. To test our hypotheses, we used robust hierarchical regressions and examined the best fit for the data for our control and main effects, an approach similar to that used by Wiklund and Shepherd [90]. The hierarchical approach is appropriate when analyzing multiplicative terms in regression analysis or, more generally, when analyzing highly correlated independent variables [91,92]. The validity of this procedure was proven mathematically and in computer simulations. In each step of the hierarchical analysis, incremental $R ^ { 2 } \bar { \cdot }$ and $F _ { - }$ tests of statistical significance were evaluated. The magnitudes of higher order regression coefficients (as opposed to statistical significance) cannot be evaluated separately from lower order terms but instead have to be assessed jointly.

In addition, we also introduced the quantile estimation process to this procedure because our dependent variable suffers from heavy-tailed distribution, thus making it difficult to manage the usual linear regression model (LRM) [93]. In the LRM, we assume that the residuals are normally distributed and that the estimated parameters are unbiased and efficient. ## 3.3 Variables

## 3.3.1. Dependent variable

We measured our dependent variable economic performance as ROA according to the main literature [90,94,95]. As we explained earlier, we used the ROA in the 2004 data to capture the one lag that could occur in the determination of performance with respect to the main drivers of our study.

## 3.3.2. Independent variables

Downsizing is commonly defined in the literature as the yearly percentage of employee reduction by 5% or more [2,96]. We measured downsizing using a dichotomous variable and assigned it a value of 1 if the firm had laid off 5% or more of its employees between the beginning of years 2003 and 2004 and a value of 0 otherwise. We considered it most appropriate to measure downsizing with a dichotomous variable to facilitate the interpretation of the results, and we chose the reference figure of 5% as it is the one most commonly used.

Information Technologies: We split this variable using two different components, IT level of use (IT use) and ERP, and both of them were measured with the data from 2003. To analyze the relationship between IT and firm performance, several IT measures have been used in the literature [68,69,97]. For example, some empirical studies analyzed the relationship between a firm’s IT investments and its economic performance [98,99,100]. Other studies used different levels of use of distinct software packages or information systems [101,102]. Following these examples, we measured IT using the level of use of information systems in different areas of firm management. Because of the variety of activities of the sample firms (within each partic ular sector), specific software applications were not explored. Thus, IT use was measured with a 5-point Likert scale of seven items reflecting the information systems level of use (1 being very low and 5 very high) in different business areas: (1) inventory management, (2) accounting and finance applications, (3) human resource management, (4) revenue management, (5) orders and suppliers management, and (6) customers management. The seventh item measured the ease with which firms used their information systems. Cronbach’s -value for IT use scale was 0.80. ERP refers to the use of ERP systems. This variable is equal to 0 if a firm does not use ERP systems. Otherwise, this variable is equal to the mean of two 5-point Likert scale items (1 being not useful at all and 5 very useful): (1) Has ERP improved employee and sub sidiary supervision? (2) Have accountability systems been improved? We consider that the use of ERP involves the implementation of a more sophisticated system in any of the following modalities: SAP, BOONS, JD EDWARDS, or ORACLE.

## 3.3.3. Control variables

We control the firm strategy with the variable Proactive strategy. This variable is calculated using four scale items: whether the firm uses (1) external or/and (2) internal sources of knowledge to formulate the strategy and whether the firm revised (3) the formulation process and (4) the results of plans of the strategy. We also control the Human Resources policy variable using a 5-item Likert scale with a Cronbach’s -value of 0.78 in the data from the year 2003. This variable measures the existence of explicit firm policies regarding selection, recruitment, evaluation, and recognition<sup>2</sup> for the purpose of promoting innovation. These practices can minimize the negative effects of downsizing on economic performance [13, 20, 21]. In addition, we aimed to control for the implementation of a documentation human resource configuration (focused on knowledge documentation, employee work redesign, and employee suggestion systems). This variable was measured by using the Youndt & Snell [103] scale. This human resource setting should facilitate the development of organizational capital [103] firm’s economic performance.

We also controlled the effects of age, size, and environment on performance. Age is the number of years a company is in business, from its legal founding date. In addition, we constructed a dummy variable for SMEs to measure the size, which takes a value of 1 when the number of employees is less than 200.

To capture the differences in environmental effects, we defined the variable Industry. Rather than all the firms in our sample belonging to the same industry, we used this variable to isolate the environmental traits that these firms faced. We used the three-digit industrial classificomposed of 10 subsectors. This distribution showed that chemical firms are concentrated in the chemical products manufacturing sector (22%) and the paint and varnish manufacturing sector (20%). Finally, we included a variable that captures the level of innovativeness of firms; it is a dummy variable that has a value of 1 if the firm has introduced a product or a process innovation in the last 2 years.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

## 4.1. Discussion

Previous research showed that frequent downsizing implementation is unsuccessful. With the support found for Hypothesis 1, this study adds empirical evidence to previous literature that analyzed the negative effects of downsizing on firm performance [2,26]. This workforce reduction strategy results in a deterioration of employee productivity and a loss of organizational memory and human and social capital, which offset the cost-cutting benefits of downsizing.

We observed that economic performance worsens more so in firms with a higher profitability. This unfavorable result is consistent with previous evidence and has been explained by behav ioral motives [26]. In other words, layoffs are better understood and accepted by employees when they respond to a business downturn in a critical situation. On the contrary, when firms decide to downsize as a proactive strategy, the psychological contract between employees and stroy employee commitment and morale, thus undermining both their productivity and firm effectiveness [2,28].

The main contribution of this article is that it shows the moderating effect that some organizational characteristics might have on the economic results during a downsizing process. Regarding IT, this article adds new evidence that confirms previous literature, proving their positive effect on organizational performance. As well, IT business value can also play a moderating and complementary role in different firm factors such as using a downsizing strategy. That is, the expected negative effects of layoffs on the economic performance might be mitigated because of the implementation of IT. These empirical results support a recent re-IT and firm economic performance. Our study contributes to the IT literature by adding evidence of these mitigating effects when other factors threaten performance. Moreover, this work adds more empirical evidence to the downsizing literature that studies the moderating effects of different kinds of variables that affect organizational performance. Some scholars have analyzed the effect of different HR practices [13,30]; however, there is only limited literature related to how IT affects downsizing success.

We discovered that the convenience and availability of the information that IT produces for organizations with a lower profitability may affect their performance. On the one hand, IT improves organizational information systems and fosters communication among employees and teams, avoiding memory loss. On the other hand, these technologies have a proven posi tive effect on organizational performance. There was only evidence of this for firms with a lower profitability, which may suggest that organizations with a medium and higher profita bility show lesser benefits from the use of IT in their performance. These results may com plement studies that analyzed the direct impact of IT on an organization’s economic perfor mances [61,68,69], by showing that the relationship may be more intense in certain contexts (i.e., firms with performance problems or within certain timeframes like the beginning of the 21st century).

We sought to examine IT influence on economic performance when firms downsize. Our results confirm previous literature [20,55] that described a more efficient and rational downsizing with a less negative impact when organizations use IT, but only in the case of highperformance firms. Our results also showed that the negative impact of downsizing on performance is higher in this group of companies. This may explain why IT use moderates the relationship between downsizing and performance, only in this case.

In sum, this result coincides with the association of more rational and less-bounded downsiz ing decisions with the intensive use of IT [70], which will allow managers to downsize more efficiently, avoiding across-the-board cuts, thereby leading to a more rational and successful downsizing.

We can conclude that one of the most important contributions of this paper is the study of IT influence on the economic effects of layoffs. As Pinsonneault and Kraemer [70] highlighted, IT might play a more central role in organizational downsizing than that depicted in the downsizing literature. In fact, they concluded that IT effects depend on the context in which technology is used and on how it is applied. In this sense, managers in an IT-intensive envi ronment might be able to downsize their organizations more efficiently as they face a lessbounded downsizing decision-making process. On the contrary, in a less IT-intensive organi zation, managers’ limited knowledge of IT will lead them to make cuts across the board when downsizing is required. Recently, in a theoretical inquiry, Gong and Greenwood [71] suggested the combination of downsizing and IT to sustain organizational memory and to reduce labor costs. In our article, in the context of Spanish industrial firms, we offer empirical evidence of a positive effect of IT use on the economic effects of downsizing.

Our results have some contributions to information and management research. First, as we previously noted, our findings confirm a moderating effect that some organizational variables have on downsizing success, thereby adding more evidence to previous research that used a contingent framework for downsizing [2,13]. In particular, we can posit that a firm that down sizes and makes extensive use of IT systems could minimize the negative effects associated with downsizing. We also found that there is a positive relationship between extensive IT use in different business areas and firm performance. This is particularly true for this specific group of companies.

The third research contribution of this paper is that it offers empirical evidence for a setting other than the USA, where most other literature has based their analyses.

## Conclusions

This study has some limitations that should be noted. One limitation that can be identified is the period of time used for the study, which was only 3 years. However, as we explained before, a series of changes took place in the industry analyzed during that period; this led international chemical groups to face a highly competitive environment and, as a result, to implement layoffs. Therefore, the analysis of these years allows us to understand the operating capacity of downsizing in this particular sector. A second limitation is related to the measure of downsizing. As we did not have itemized information regarding the downsizing process, it was measured simply by considering “workforce reductions” [104], which is a much broader term, focused mainly on layoffs in general [105].

Another limitation is related to the possible difficulty of generalizing the results obtained with regard to other industries and other contexts, mainly because our study focused on the chemical industry in Spain in a particular temporal context.

These limitations may encourage further research. Future studies could broaden the analysis by studying a longer period of time, one which considers panel data that facilitate the study of causality relationships. The analysis could also be applied to different industries, with the objective of favoring the generalization of the results and implications obtained. Furthermore, it might be necessary to consider alternative measures of IT systems. It is intriguing that the use of ERP systems is not a significant moderator, unlike IT use. Future studies could also apply a more fine-grained measure of IT systems to analyze the specific value of investments in different IT areas when a firm undergoes a downsizing process.

However, our study provides useful insights for practice. First, our findings show that the implementation of IT has a positive effect on the performance of firms with a lower profita bility in the chemical industry. This supports the usefulness of investing in IT to improve firm profitability. Second, managers should regard downsizing strategies as management resources rather than as cost-cutting tools [20], thereby fostering dynamic organizational capabilities and developing organizational memory during downsizing. Therefore, firms that consider the option of downsizing as a result of either financial or productivity problems should also consider the implementation of IT, to reduce the negative impact of employee layoffs on firm profitability.
