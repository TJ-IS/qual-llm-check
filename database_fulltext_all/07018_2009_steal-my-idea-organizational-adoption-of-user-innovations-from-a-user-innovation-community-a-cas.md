# Steal my idea! Organizational adoption of user innovations from a user innovation community: A case study of Dell IdeaStorm

Paul M. Di Gangi ⁎, Molly Wasko <sup>1</sup>

Florida State University, College of Business, Department of Management Information Systems, Tallahassee, FL 32306, USA

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 13 October 2008 Received in revised form 16 March 2009 Accepted 6 April 2009 Available online 9 April 2009

Keywords: Open innovation User innovation communities Online community Absorptive capacity Sources of innovation Organizational adoption

## a b s t r a c t

Open innovation models suggest that an organization's end users can be a potentially bene<sup>fi</sup>cial source for new ideas and innovations. However, incorporating end users raises some interesting challenges regarding how to balance the decision making power between a <sup>fi</sup>rm and the user community. Using Diffusion of Innovation Theory, we propose hypotheses based on the perceived attributes of end user ideas and end user promotion efforts. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the decision to adopt a user innovation was based on the ability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to understand the technical requirements and respond to community concerns regarding the innovation.

## 1. Introduction

On February 16, 2007, Dell invited end users to share their ideas and collaborate with Dell to create or modify new products and services through an online community — Dell IdeaStorm (www. dellideastorm.com). With the launch of this website, Dell created a user innovation community where end users freely reveal innovative ideas with community members and Dell [28,27]. Through IdeaStorm, end users contribute their business ideas to be reviewed, discussed, and voted upon by the user community. While user innovation communities are not a new phenomenon [9,26], advances in information and communication technologies (ICTs) have enabled end users of an organization's products and services to organize and share innovations through the creation of online communities. These user innovation communities, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “distributed groups of individuals focused on solving a general problem and/or developing a new solution supported by computer mediated communication” [8, p. 1246], are becoming increasingly in<sup>fl</sup>uential in an organization's decisionmaking process regarding the adoption of new innovations.<sup>2</sup> We de<sup>fi</sup>ne adoption based on Rogers' de<sup>fi</sup>nition which states adoption is the “decision to make full use of an innovation as the best course of action available.” [18, p. 473]

Researchers and practitioners alike are strongly encouraging organizations to take more open approaches,<sup>3</sup> such as establishing user innovation communities, to complement internal innovation processes. Chesbrough [2] proposes that organizations in today's hyper-competitive environment must take an open innovation approach, combining internal and external components of an organization to successfully develop innovations and gain a competitive advantage. Laursen and Salter [13] argue that “the network of relationships between the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its external environment can play an important role in shaping performance” [13 p. 132]. As a result, organizations are beginning to view user innovation communities as strategic assets that provide external expertise, generate ideas on new innovations, and support innovation development [8]. Organizations that utilize user innovation communities can capture value by increasing an organization's capacity to continuously renew its competencies and better align itself with a changing business environment [22]. Von Hippel [25] argues that end users, acting in a proactive manner, are central to an organization's ability to leverage its own internal resources and innovation processes for acquiring user innovations.

Traditionally, organizational innovations are innovations that the developer expects to gain value by selling it. User innovations are innovations that the developer expects to gain value by using it. This makes opening the boundaries of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to include user innovation communities a risky proposition. First, the organization has little control over the content end users post. End users could easily post negative comments about the organization, creating a public forum for customer complaints that could generate very bad publicity for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Second, in order to support development of the user innovation community and capitalize on the innovations from end users, the host organization must engage in community building activities and acknowledge the innovations that are being generated. This requires that the host organization invest signi<sup>fi</sup>cant resources to participate in and support community activities. Third, as user innovation communities become more tightly integrated into the processes of the host organization, the possibility arises for con<sup>fl</sup>ict between the user community and the organization itself. Consequently, how an organization chooses which innovations to adopt and which to disregard requires balancing the interests of the organization with the interests of its end users.

For instance, the founder and chief architect of Digg.com, Kevin Rose, de<sup>fi</sup>ed a legal notice from an anti-piracy movie industry group by refusing to remove stories from the Digg.com website containing a single 32-digit code that unlocks the contents of HD-DVD and Blu-ray discs [5,10]. Rose posted the following response to community members: “But now, after seeing hundreds of stories and reading thousands of comments, you've made it clear. You'd rather see Digg go down <sup>fi</sup>ghting than bow down to a bigger company. We hear you, and effective immediately we won't delete stories or comments containing the code and will deal with whatever the consequences might be. If we lose, then what the hell, at least we died trying. . . .” [19]. In this example, end users deluged the site with stories and comments that included the key after Digg administrators began deleting usergenerated content in order to comply with the cease-and-desist letter. As a result, Digg.com faced strong external pressure from a key constituency, its end users, to change its decision. Organizations reliant on end user participation, such as Digg.com, must carefully balance organizational interests with end user pressures, or potentially be forced to choose between its end users and organizational survival. The interesting paradox arises in that the more successful the user innovation community, potentially the more power the community holds over the organization's decision-making process.

Research has yet to examine how user innovation communities, acting as a new form of organizational constituency, can both complement and/or disrupt an organization's innovation processes. Thus, the purpose of this study is to identify the factors that influence an organization's adoption decision when innovations come from outside the organization's formal boundaries. Using a mixed method case study approach utilizing data collected via survey, objective data from the website, and qualitative analysis of message postings, we examined Dell's decision-making process using components of Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations Theory [18]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examined whether the perceived attributes of the user innovations and the promotion efforts of end users in<sup>fl</sup>uenced Dell's adoption decision. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest Dell's adoption decisions were not in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the economic and compatibility attributes of the innovations nor the promotional efforts by end users; rather, Dell's adoption decisions were based on its absorptive capacity — the ability to understand the oftentimes complex user innovations to suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently address user concerns and meet user expectations. The main contribution of our study is that it expands our current theoretical and empirical understanding of the role of user innovation communities in support of organizational innovation processes, and how organizations leverage the creative innovations from end users to create new products and services.

## 2. Theoretical perspective and research model

Innovation is critical for today's organizations, and organizations that fail to innovate eventually die [1]. Proponents of an open model of organizational innovation recognize that because it is impossible for a single <sup>fi</sup>rm to hire all of the experts in a <sup>fi</sup>eld, and an innovation does not have to originate within the <sup>fi</sup>rm in order for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from it, organizations must make the best use of both internal and external sources of innovation [1,25,28]. The key assumption underlying open innovation models is that while <sup>fi</sup>rms should be open to both internal and external sources of innovation, <sup>fi</sup>rms should only adopt innovations that purposefully advance or support existing business models [1,20,28].

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations Theory identi<sup>fi</sup>es factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence adoption decisions [18]. In traditional adoption research, researchers have focused on the adoption of a single innovation by multiple potential adopters [24] (e.g., Iowa farmers adoption of the N-Track soil testing kit). However, the purpose of this study is to examine the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption decision of a single potential adopter across multiple innovations. While some of Rogers' adoption model is challenged by the focus on a single potential adopter, two factors — perceived attributes of the innovation and change agents' promotion efforts — create differences amongst the potential innovations to adopt. Prior research has suggested that the perceived attributes of the innovation signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption decision [17,32]. Within the perceived attributes of the innovation, <sup>fi</sup>ve key characteristics have been suggested — relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability.

Relative advantage concerns “the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes.” [18, p. 229]. Compatibility focuses on the perceived alignment between the existing beliefs of the organization as well as its ability to easily integrate the innovation into the organization's current product and service portfolio [18]. Complexity concerns the degree of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in understanding the innovation. Trialability and observability examine the potential adopters' ability to test an innovation before adopting and to view initial results of the innovation's success or failure. These attributes regarding the complexity of an innovation are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to convey in an electronic setting and often require a physical innovation to be present for the potential adopter to test and/or observe before deciding to adopt. For example, Rogers discusses a study concerning the adoption of the N-Track soil testing kit for Iowa farmers [12]. This physical innovation allowed the farmers to test the product and observe its results before fully adopting the innovation for widespread use. Based on Rogers' analysis, the N-Track innovation was not adopted due to its low relative advantage and observability for Iowa farmers. However, in a user innovation community, ideas about an innovation are exchanged in contrast to a physical innovation that is easily tested or observed. Because the innovation that is the focus of this study is a conceptual idea, it is not until the idea is adopted by the organization that the development of the physical innovation occurs. This makes the theoretical constructs of complexity, observability and trialability dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to translate to an online setting. Therefore, we examined the two most reliable and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attributes of innovations — perceived relative advantage and compatibility — and their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the organization's adoption decisions [17,24].

In addition to an innovation's attributes, the extent of change agents'promotion efforts has been shown to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of an innovation [32]. A change agent is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “an individual who in<sup>fl</sup>uences clients' innovation-decisions in a direction deemed desirable by a change agency” [18, p. 473]. In the context of a user innovation community, end users act as change agents pursuing the adoption of an innovation by convincing the client, in this case the hosting organization of the user innovation community, to adopt the innovation. This factor is particularly important in open innovation approaches where end users are integrated into an organization's innovation process. End users can impress upon the organization the perceived value of the innovation as well as the potential consequences for an organization that fails to adopt a popular user innovation. ## 3. Hypotheses

Organizations are more likely to adopt innovations that are perceived to provide the greatest relative advantage for the organization amongst alternative innovations [1–3]. Relative advantage is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the degree to which an innovation is considered superior to its alternatives [18]. Central to this de<sup>fi</sup>nition is the need for an organization to quantify the economic advantages gained from adopting the innovation [20]. Under the traditional closed innovation model, organizations are able to dictate what innovations are developed and how, relying on the <sup>fi</sup>rms internal assets to drive the innovation decision-making process. This ensures that the organization retains tight control over the innovation process, enabling <sup>fi</sup>rms to invest the greatest resources towards the innovations that generate the highest economic returns and require the lowest costs to produce. Even though a <sup>fi</sup>rm may intentionally blur <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries and seek external sources for new innovations through the open innovation approach, it is assumed that the <sup>fi</sup>rm will still implement only those innovations that promise the greatest relative advantage and economic returns.

In addition to traditional economic characteristics, organizations are able to gain a relative advantage when the adoption of an innovation results in the development of new <sup>fi</sup>rm capabilities [22]. Teece et al. suggest that by developing new capabilities, an organization can achieve strategic <sup>fl</sup>exibility to respond to a changing business environment and maximize its potential paths to pro<sup>fi</sup>t gains [20–22]. By possessing complementary assets and developing new capabilities, an organization can continuously create opportunities for added value and economic returns. Within this aspect of innovation, compatibility with existing products, services, and organizational capabilities can reduce the strain on an organization's innovation efforts [20–22]. When an organization possesses complementary assets (e.g., marketing, manufacturing, and customer support knowledge) that an innovation will require to be successful, the organization maximizes the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained. Therefore, when an organization perceives a user innovation to be compatible with its existing resources, it will be more likely to adopt the innovation.

In Democratizing Innovation, von Hippel [28] argues that user innovation communities may act as a central collection point, in which user innovations can become narrowly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and categorized based on innovation type. This suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms can leverage the user innovation community to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and develop ideas externally without expending <sup>fi</sup>rm resources, and then discriminate among the ideas proposed by the user innovation community that promise signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relative advantage in terms of increased economic returns, or compatibility with existing innovation resources. This leads to our <sup>fi</sup>rst set of hypotheses:

H1. Organizations will adopt user innovations that are perceived as providing the greatest relative advantage.

H2. Organizations will adopt user innovations that are perceived as being compatible with current product and service offerings.

Research on user innovation has shown that some of the most lucrative and novel innovations have been developed by users attempting to adapt existing products and processes to better suit their own needs [e.g., 14,23,26]. Many commercially attractive products that are at the leading edge of marketplace trends stem directly from user innovations [14,15]. This suggests that under open innovation models, organizations may want to consider novel end user ideas as early indicators of future market trends. Assuming that competitive advantage stems from understanding user needs and then developing products and services that meet or exceed these needs, organizations may pursue an innovation strategy that focuses more on customer demands than organizational assessments of relative advantage.

One way to identify customer needs is to let end users comment and vote on ideas, creating a direct channel for soliciting feedback from users. By allowing end users to vote on the ideas within the user innovation community, an organization creates the opportunity for end users to promote speci<sup>fi</sup>c innovations and differentiate ideas that should be adopted from those that may be considered unproductive or in less demand. Adopting the ideas that are the most popular in the user innovation community provides organizations with the opportunity to signal to end users that they are responsive to customer needs and helps build strong relational bonds and goodwill between the organization and its end users. Additionally, adopting the most popular ideas from the user innovation community ensures that there will be demand in the marketplace for the innovation.

By inviting user innovation communities to participate in innovation processes as an integral part of the organization's business model, an organization may feel more pressure to follow community interests and adopt popular ideas or face community repercussions. This leads to an alternative hypothesis regarding the organization's adoption decision:

H3. Organizations will adopt the user innovations that are the most popular in the community.

To participate, end users create usernames and post their innovative ideas about how Dell can improve existing products and services and/or create new products and services. End users can also post comments about an idea, promote or demote posted ideas (vote) and edit their own ideas. When a user submits an idea, he or she provides a title and a description. When users promote an idea, points are awarded and ideas with more points are given special status in the community by the label “most popular status”, which is shown on the front page of IdeaStorm. Demoted ideas or ideas that are no longer receiving votes are automatically pulled from the popular ideas page after a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed period of time, determined by Dell.

Each idea submitted to the IdeaStorm website is used as an indicator of an innovation with the potential for adoption. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the ideas adopted by Dell and the most popular ideas within the community but not adopted by Dell to test our hypotheses. IdeaStorm uses an Ideas in Action page which lists and describes all the ideas submitted by the community that have been, or are being implemented. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst four months of operation, Dell adopted 11 ideas from a wide variety of areas, ranging from pre-installed Linux operating system to the introduction of a new Tablet PC. The most popular ideas within the community were determined by identifying the 10 ideas that received the most community points, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting user votes, but not adopted by Dell. We chose to study both adopted and not adopted idea types to examine whether perceived attributes of the innovations or end user promotion efforts better predict an organization's decision to adopt user innovations from the community.

The idea is the focal unit of analysis. To assess the perceived attributes of the idea (innovation), we relied on subjective assessments of the adopted ideas and popular but not adopted ideas from subject matter experts. To assess the extent to which the idea possessed relative advantage attributes,12 expert raters (professionals in IT-related <sup>fi</sup>elds) evaluated the 21 ideas in terms of: 1) market value, 2) return on investment, 3) appropriability, and 4) the potential of the idea to generate new dynamic capabilities. Raters were asked to read the idea description and rate the idea using a 10-point Likert scale (e.g. 1 = low “market value” to 10 = high “market value”). These were all single-item scales. To assess the extent to which the idea possessed compatibility attributes, the same 12 expert raters evaluated the 21 ideas in terms of Dell's ability to leverage complementary assets.

Constructs, measures and their de<sup>fi</sup>nitions are summarized in Table 2. Objective data from the website was used to compare the user promotion efforts across ideas by assessing the vote count, the number of comments related to the idea and the number of unique users contributing comments about the idea. Table 3 provides a summary of the objective data about the 21 ideas included in this study.

Our expert raters had a mean age of 34.8 years and work experience of 12.7 years. Expert raters ranged in job titles from technical programmers to executive managers. Based on a web posting from an IdeaStorm representative, Dell's decision-making process incorporates employees from throughout its organization to provide insight into whether a user innovation should be adopted. “While every Dell employee obviously has access to IdeaStorm, we have varying degrees of engagement on the site across the different departments — Product Group, Online, Retail, Marketing, Linux, etc.” [11] Therefore, the use of expert raters with varying IT-related work experience complements Dell's public description of the innovation adoption decision process.

Table 4 displays the reliabilities for the survey measures. We calculated the reliability across all 12 raters using Cronbach's alpha, and raters were in large agreement on their assessments of the relative advantage of the ideas (Cronbach alphas were greater than .70) [16]. The assessments of relative advantage and compatibility were calculated by taking the average value across all raters (n=12).

## 5. Results

Table 5 presents the correlations between variables. The variables assessing change agents' promotion efforts were all highly correlated with one another. Also noteworthy, the only variable that had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant correlation with adoption/non-adoption is idea age (in days) — ideas that have not been adopted are older than adopted ideas. We tested hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 by comparing the mean values of the adopted ideas to the mean values of non-adopted ideas. Results of the ANOVA tests are provided in Table 6.

Hypothesis 1 predicted that organizations would adopt ideas based on the perceived attributes of the innovation. We hypothesized that ideas that have the greatest potential for relative advantage would be adopted. This hypothesis was not supported. None of the indicators of relative advantage were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differentiators between adopted and non-adopted ideas (market value: F=.001, p=.337; return on investment: F=.017, p=.432; appropriability: F=.349, p=.959; new dynamic capability: F=.045, p=.490). For Hypothesis 2, we predicted that organizations would adopt ideas that have compatibility with an organization's existing assets (e.g., marketing and technical knowledge). Contrary to expectations, this hypothesis was not supported (F=.136, p=.734). Adopted innovations were not statistically different from not adopted but popular ideas in terms of the organization's ability to capitalize on complementary assets already held by the organization.

## 6. Explaining adoption

The results from the hypotheses tests indicate that organizations are likely to base adoption decisions on factors beyond relative advantage, compatibility and the promotion efforts by members of the community. This suggests that the inter-related factors of complexity, observability and trialability are just as important (if not more so) in the decision to adopt ideas from a user innovation community [18]. The interesting twist to Rogers' original model is that now it is the adopting organization that has to grapple with the complexity, observability and trialability of innovations from the end user developers.

A major concern for an organization attempting to adopt user innovations is the need to understand the technical requirements of the innovation and end users' needs in order to extract value from the innovation [23,25,30]. The perceived complexity of an innovation relates to whether the organization easily understands the user innovation [18]. In order for a <sup>fi</sup>rm to be able to adopt user innovations, it must have the absorptive capacity that allows the transfer of knowledge from the user innovation community to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Absorptive capacity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to value, assimilate and apply new knowledge [6]. When an organization lacks absorptive capacity, it is costly to transfer information about user innovations from the user community to the organization due to the tacitness of the knowledge. In traditional, face to face settings, the tacit knowledge embedded in innovations is transferrable through observability and trialability [18]. Therefore, the perceived complexity of an innovation may be exacerbated in electronic settings due to dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of the medium to support articulation of the tacit knowledge needed for an idea to successfully transfer to the <sup>fi</sup>rm [23,29]. This implies that the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in adopting user innovations is intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the knowledge gap between user innovation communities and organizations attempting to exploit user-generated innovations [29].

Furthermore, ICT environments create the potential for an unknown number of end users to participate in an organization's innovation process. Through ICTs, end users can share their ideas with an organization regardless of geographic location, temporal considerations, and organizational af<sup>fi</sup>liation [7,8]. As a result, the organization has little control over the sheer quantity of end user ideas and/or innovations that are contributed, and is likely to have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty sifting through the ideas to identify those of particular relevance or quality.

Thus, incorporating end users through electronic user innovation communities requires management of a process where a large quantity of ideas with varying quality can overburden an organization's existing absorptive capacity. This leads to an important insight — in user innovation communities, the perceived complexity of the innovation varies both in terms of understanding its technical requirements (i.e., technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations required to develop the innovation) and the organization's ability to assimilate relevant information from the community without being bogged down by information overload. Therefore, given the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant results from the hypotheses testing, we further investigate an organization's adoption decision by examining the following research question:

RQ: How does the complexity of the user innovation affect the adoption decision?

To examine this question, we use a qualitative case study approach to compare the most popular idea adopted by Dell — pre-installed Linux, and the most popular but not adopted idea — pre-installed OpenOffice. These two ideas were chosen for in-depth analysis based on their similarities: both are immensely popular in the community and both ideas are based on the pre-installation of open source software, which begs the question — why did one idea win over the other? A comparison case-study design was used to ensure greater validity and generalizability [31]. Each case examines how the community articulated and re<sup>fi</sup>ned the idea through their comments so that Dell could better understand how to value, assimilate and apply the idea in the context of Dell's current operating practices. The data for the case study analyses included the objective data from the hypothesis tests as well as a comparison of the 1,865 comments posted to these two ideas.

## 6.1. Case one — adopted: pre-installed Linux | Ubuntu | Fedora | OpenSUSE | Multi-Boot

On the <sup>fi</sup>rst day of Dell IdeaStorm's launch (02/16/2007), the website experienced a deluge of innovative ideas from users. One of the <sup>fi</sup>rst ideas proposed was that Dell offer the option to have the three most popular Linux distributions pre-installed on all Dell PCs. The user's idea was based on the notion that consumers should be given choices in operating systems when purchasing a computer. Having an option to pre-install Linux gained immediate popularity within the user innovation community, and 491 comments were posted by community members in the <sup>fi</sup>rst week. In total, over 1500 comments by 981 unique community members were posted, and the idea received over 146,000 points through the community voting process, making it the most active idea on Dell IdeaStorm and demonstrating the tremendous passion of the user community's interest in Dell adopting the idea.

During the <sup>fi</sup>rst few weeks after the idea was posted, the community focused on re<sup>fi</sup>nement and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of the idea by providing additional descriptions of what user requirements would be needed for its successful adoption. Users posted comments that helped re<sup>fi</sup>ne the idea for Dell by providing details about user needs. Other re<sup>fi</sup>nements of the idea included technical speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, discussion of an option for no pre-installed operating system, a speci<sup>fi</sup>c graphical user interface (such as KDE or GNOME), and different Linux distributions other than the original distributions listed in the idea title. In addition to providing additional information to clarify the idea, users commented on their personal experiences. Personal experiences in the early stages of idea formulation focused on combating negative user comments that suggested users needed technical knowledge to successfully operate a Linux-based operating system.

Within the <sup>fi</sup>rst week (02/23/2007), Dell administrators acknowl edged the popularity of this idea and stated that it had begun discussions with Novell to certify corporate products as compatible with Linux, based partially on user comments referencing work environments. Furthermore, Dell stated “We don't want to pick one distribution and alienate users with a preference for another.” The acknowledgement of the idea's popularity set in motion a new set of comments in response to Dell's of<sup>fi</sup>cial announcement.

Out of curiosity, I just had Firefox count the number of instances a handful of popular Linux distributions have been mentioned in this comment thread. Here are the top three: 1. “ubuntu” — 280 mentions so far 2. “suse” — 84 mentions so far 3. “fedora” — 51 mentions so far. (User Comment, 02/28/2007)

As the popularity of the idea and the community around the idea grew, community unrest began to emerge because Dell failed to respond to the post-announcement comments in support of the idea. The announcement of Dell's intention to adopt the user idea sent celebratory comments throughout the idea's community. The <sup>fi</sup>nal key date was May 24, 2007 where Dell announced its selection of three systems, XPS 410n and Dimension

6.2. Case two — not adopted: pre-installed OpenOffice | alternative to MS Works & MS Office

One day after the website opened, and one day after the preinstalled Linux idea was posted, the idea for pre-installed OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce as an alternative to MS Of<sup>fi</sup>ce or MS Works was posted. Similar to preinstalled Linux, this idea also focused on the notion that consumers should be given choices for of<sup>fi</sup>ce software when purchasing a computer. In comparison to Case One, this idea was slower in gaining popularity among the user innovation community, although there was still substantial activity (119 comments posted by community members in the <sup>fi</sup>rst week). In total, 360 comments were posted to the user innovation community by 282 unique users. In addition, the idea received over 120,000 points through the community voting process.

Initial comments within the community, however, were less than positive. Demoters centered on two main arguments for not adopting the idea — the ability for users to freely download the OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce software package themselves, and concerns about the quality of the software compared to proprietary alternatives (MS Works and MS Of<sup>fi</sup>ce), such as the comment below.

that is one lame idea. Why would you want to buy a Dell with freeware? …Have you even seen Open Office? The spreadsheet in Open Office pales in comparison to Excel… (User Comment, 02/17/ 2007)

In addition, user <sup>fl</sup>aming appeared frequently as community members were split on whether or not the idea should be adopted by Dell. In response to the <sup>fl</sup>ame wars between supporters and demoters of the idea, individuals relied heavily on stories of personal experience to try and convince other users of the viability of the idea. Personal experiences included contextual references to how users used OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce or other open source software applications (such as GIMP, Firefox, and Pidgin) and the quality of the software compared to its proprietary counterpart.

Due to the initial mixed response in the community to the idea, comments for this idea continuously referenced pre-installed Linux rather than the OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce idea during this period. While both OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce and Linux are similar in that they are open source software applications, the comments within the community considered the viability of a pre-installed Linux option and/ or pre-installed Open-Of<sup>fi</sup>ce on a Linux computer.

So yeah, I'm all for machines pre-loaded with easy versions of Linux like Freespire and Linspire,with Firefox as the browser, Open Office and other goodies. (User Comment, 02/20/2007)

Even two months after the initial idea was posted, community members were unable to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the idea's focus and continued to comment on both pre-installed Linux and OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce or various other open source software programs as pre-install options. Comments such as the following one continued to appear, indicating the user community's preference for passionate pleas over clari<sup>fi</sup>cations of the idea and insights into how Dell should actually adopt the idea.

PLEASE Mr. Dell, 1) Put Open Office and Firefox on your computers. 2) Offer Linux — ANY version — pre-installed. Thank you for asking us. (User Comment, 04/20/2007)

The author's idea description was seeking to adopt a pre-install option for all computers, not referencing a preference for the operating system. The user innovation community appeared mostly focused on creating a debate about what Dell's approach should be towards open source software applications in general, indicating an inability of the user innovation community to more clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ne the idea that it wanted Dell to adopt.

On February 23, 2007, Dell formally acknowledged pre-installed Linux via its Idea's in Action page. Within this statement, Dell included a reference to OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce as an open source software solution that was widely discussed on the site, but no acknowledgement of this statement was presented within the community. Dell's lack of interest in adopting this idea is in stark contrast to Dell's open and active recognition of the pre-installed Linux idea. Given the inability of the community to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce idea, and the lack of interest from Dell in helping the community re<sup>fi</sup>ne the idea, comments continued as shown earlier, referring to pre-installed Linux and OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce software, indicating the diverging interests of community members. However, community statements concerning the delay in Dell's direct response surfaced. Community members made complaints that focused on Dell's inaction concerning the idea's adoption, failure by Dell to listen to the community, and threatening statements concerning Dell's survivability as a computer manufacturer.

Dear Dell, Why do you not give people a choice? Here's a choice for you: 1. Either listen to these ideas and give people an Open Source non Microsoft choice or 2. Go the way of the dinosaur. I urge you to choose pro-actively. (User Comment, 03/12/2007)

On May 3, 2007, a Dell site administrator informally acknowledged the idea as “Under Consideration”. However, this did not help the user innovation community focus its efforts towards re<sup>fi</sup>ning the idea — rather the community focused its efforts towards posting negative comments aimed at Dell. The <sup>fl</sup>ame wars continued, and this actually seemed to develop into a basic norm for the group — comments included statements with an expectation to be <sup>fl</sup>amed, but no expectation that the comment would result in a discussion of the idea based on its merits. User comments continued to focus pressure by demanding action from Dell towards adopting the idea. Comments continued to make reference to waiting for a response from Dell, highlighting the strong total point score of the idea on the site and the position of the idea as the second most popular on IdeaStorm. For example:

Just please IMPLEMENT THIS IDEA. I AM TIRED OF LOOKING AT IT ON THE FRONT PAGE. CAN YOU HEAR MEEEEEEEE? (User Comment, 05/ 08/2007)

After approximately three months since the idea's introduction, discussion of the idea did not advance beyond charging a small fee for bundling OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce and other open source software packages, and the possibility for Dell to create a new stream of revenue by offering open source support services. As of June 22, 2007, the pre-install OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce was not adopted by Dell or referenced within the Dell Idea's in Action page, indicating that the idea, while popular within the community, was not adopted by Dell. Furthermore, Dell seems to have had little interest in engaging with users around this idea.

## 7. Discussion

Under traditional innovation assumptions. Dell should choose to adopt an innovation based on the perceived attributes (e.g., relative advantage and compatibility) that increase pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and create additive value to the organization's business strategy. Alternatively, Dell should balance innovation attributes with the promotion efforts of end users, relinquishing some control over the adoption decisionmaking process to its end users. These assumptions have led us to the question of what drives an organization's adoption decision when user innovation communities are formalized within the organization's boundary. Our research suggests that neither change agents' promotional efforts nor the attributes of the innovations (relative advantage and compatibility attributes) in<sup>fl</sup>uences the adoption of user-driven innovations, rather the decision to adopt is based on the complexity of the innovation and Dell's ability to reduce the innovation complexity through its absorptive capacity.

## 7.1. Key insights from the pre-installed Linux Idea

The pre-installed Linux case described the process of idea re<sup>fi</sup>nement that emerged through the IdeaStorm site leading to its adoption. Over the course of the idea's lifespan, several critical patterns emerged that provide insights into how the user innovation community assisted Dell in valuing and assimilating the community's idea, and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced Dell to adopt the idea. First, end users articulated the requirements in terms of the technical complexity of the innovation to Dell. Second, Dell's lack of absorptive capacity resulted in its inability to understand the input from the user innovation community, causing Dell to react quickly, but not effectively. Dell's interpretation of user needs was initially based on user comments expressed concerning work experiences. Community members in their attempt to re<sup>fi</sup>ne and clarify the idea for Dell created a situation where Dell misinterpreted user comments to determine which product offerings would be affected by the idea's adoption (the focus on business/work related systems only). This could be due in part to the intensity of comments during the <sup>fi</sup>rst week (491 comments) forcing Dell to react quickly on this initial impression without fully grasping the user needs for both business and home computers with pre-installed Linux. The user innovation community responded by further re<sup>fi</sup>ning the idea for Dell, and Dell then followed up with a formal survey for the user innovation community to identify the end users needs which increased Dell's understanding of the innovation. This iterative process of communication between the user innovation community and Dell helped create the absorptive capacity needed to bridge the knowledge gap between the community and the organization.

Noticeably important was the user innovation community's ability to leverage both internal and external resources to pressure Dell by creating a powerful coalition quickly. Several comments included references to recruiting users from other electronic communities to build numbers in order to exert pressure on Dell to adopt the idea. External communities such as Digg.com, where members can post interesting story links, increased the Dell user community's public pro<sup>fi</sup>le on the internet. In addition, these external communities provided the user innovation community with the ability to quickly create a strong response to the user survey that provided Dell with formal user needs and expectations of a pre-installed Linux distribution.

The introduction of the user survey provided a third key insight — the ability to use surveys as a means to bridge the knowledge gap between the user innovation community and Dell, enabling Dell to quickly establish the absorptive capacity needed to implement the idea. The fourth key <sup>fi</sup>nding focuses on community responsibilities and power. In several comments, the community assumed the responsibility of determining which Linux distribution should be included as a pre-installed option. Through its discussion forum and the user survey, the community attempted to dictate to Dell which distribution and speci<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>guration should be adopted as a pre-installed option. This presents the question of whether Dell, through its leveraging of user innovation communities, must delegate authority to the community due to the original ownership of the idea being an external entity. Does incorporating external resources and users require a reduction in the level of control an organization can possess over its own innovation process? If so, organizations that utilize user innovation communities must acquiesce to demands for some decision-making authority in de<sup>fi</sup>ning several of the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs behind the innovation's adoption.

While reducing organizational decision-making power may reduce the level of ownership and control an organization possesses for selecting which innovations to adopt, there are several bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to delegating some authority to the user innovation community. In particular, Dell was able to capture further innovative ideas for the implementation phase of the idea; such as marketing plans and support mechanisms. Furthermore, because ownership of the idea originated within the community, several community members assumed responsibility for resolving potential issues that might arise from adopting such an innovation. Issues such as costs to support options and determining which distribution discussed earlier would have required Dell to appropriate internal resources for determining these solutions. Incorporating user innovation communities into its organizational resource portfolio allowed Dell to gain insights into solutions that mitigated these problems.

## 7.2. Key insights from the pre-install OpenOffice idea

Pre-install OpenOffice provides insights into why a very popular idea in terms of the end users promotion efforts was not adopted by Dell. First, the ambiguities and initial negative reaction towards the idea presented inherent problems in Dell's ability to gauge community interest in the idea. As a result, the complexity of the idea was high in terms of Dell understanding what end user needs for the innovation were. While Dell attempted to signal its interest in the OpenOf<sup>fi</sup>ce idea, the community failed to engage in discussions that would help re<sup>fi</sup>ne the idea in a way that Dell could accurately interpret. However, Dell did not give the community additional information that could have focused their attention, as Dell did in the pre-installed Linux idea. This reinforces the observation that organizations should carefully consider how they acknowledge and interact with user innovation communities. Although Dell formally acknowledged the idea, Dell made no attempt to articulate its concerns about the idea to the community, nor suggest concerns about the idea that could have been addressed by the community, such as narrowing the community's focus on speci<sup>fi</sup>c adoption issues.

In contrast to the pre-installed Linux idea, user <sup>fl</sup>aming was particularly noticeable for this idea, indicating that a second key factor in choosing which user-driven ideas to adopt is also dependent upon how the community presents itself in terms of its interactions and norms. In the pre-installed Linux idea, a member noted the growth and maturity of the community from an enthusiasts group to a community that was weighing the advantages and disadvantages in a fair and balanced manner. User <sup>fl</sup>aming appears to indicate a failure on the community's part to achieve this level of growth in order to be taken seriously. Flaming activities also do not help bridge the knowledge gap between the user innovation community and Dell in terms of how the idea can be valued and assimilated.

A <sup>fi</sup>nal insight gained from examining this community centers around the concept of applying pressure to Dell for adopting the idea. In the pre-installed Linux idea, the community was able to successfully exert pressure on Dell because of its clear de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the idea and user needs for its adoption. In contrast, end users participating in the preinstalled OpenOffice idea attempted to exert pressure by providing objective data references, such as total points and position in the popular ideas page, but failed to present clear reasoning for the idea's adoption. This indicates that a community's ability to apply pressure to an organization is based on both the clear description of the idea's requirements (i.e., reduced complexity of user concerns) and appropriately applied pressure based on the popularity of the idea within the site (i.e., change agent promotion efforts). Failure to apply both aspects of pressure may result in a downward negative spiral: the non-adoption decision by the organization and continued community unrest.

## 8. Conclusion

Researchers are accustomed to studying user innovation communities as external to an organization. However, research has yet to examine organizations that reposition user innovation communities as internal innovation resources, where the organization can capture user innovations directly, without the physical gap separating users and organizations. The ability to respond quickly to the needs of the community and withstand the intensity of the community's demands is necessary for organizations adopting user innovation communities. The interpretation, comprehension, and the ability to learn from prior experiences determine an organization's ability to fully grasp the complexity inherent in user innovations. Communities provide the necessary information on what the end result of an idea should look like, but until an organization can alter or combine its internal capabilities to successfully incorporate the innovation within its boundaries, it will fail to be adopted. As organizations gain more experience with user innovation communities, it may <sup>fi</sup>nd it far easier to value and assimilate new ideas based on its greater absorptive capacity.

In terms of user innovation communities, if users want an organization to “steal their idea,” the community must be able to articulate their needs precisely and come to a consensus quickly. The user innovation communities must help reduce the complexity of the innovation for the adopting organization. It appears that in order to compete for an organization's attention amongst potentially thousands of ideas, it is the community's responsibility to coalesce, come to a consensus and then clearly articulate the idea such that the adopting organization can value, assimilate and apply the idea within the context of its current business practices. After this is accomplished, the promotion efforts of the end users will provide additional in<sup>fl</sup>uence on an organization's adoption of a user innovation.
