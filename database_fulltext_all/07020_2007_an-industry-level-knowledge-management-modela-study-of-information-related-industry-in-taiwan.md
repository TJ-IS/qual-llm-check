# An industry-level knowledge management model—a study of information-related industry in Taiwan

Chinho Lin <sup>a,1</sup>, David C. Yen <sup>b,\*</sup>, David D.C. Tarn <sup>c,2</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Industrial and Information Management & Institute of Information Management, National Cheng Kung University, Tainan, Taiwan, ROC

<sup>b</sup> Department of Decision Sciences and Management Information Systems, Miami University, Oxford, OH 45056, USA

<sup>c</sup> Department of Business Administration, I-Shou University, Kaohsiung County, Taiwan, ROC

Received 21 June 2005; received in revised form 1 September 2006; accepted 24 September 2006 Available online 28 November 2006

## Abstract

Most knowledge management (KM) literature investigates related issues from the perspectives of organizational and national layer. While observing KM practices, especially in less developed countries (LDCs), many KM activities are happened in industrial layer. Hence, the primary purpose of this study is to explore the content of industry-layer knowledge management (i.e., ILKM) and how ILKM activities are practiced. This study, first, classifies ILKM as four modes, named knowledge clustering, knowledge enlarging, knowledge exchanging, and knowledge initiating. Following KM-related literature and practices, this study constructs the ILKM model, and defines and lists content and activities of the four ILKM modes, by which a four sub-construct, 20-item ILKM scale is constructed. A 123-respondents empirical survey, selecting Taiwan’s information equipment industry as the sample, confirms the reliability and validity of ILKM scale. This study also provides suggestions and directions toward ILKM practices and researches for the future.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge management (KM) has recently played a critical role in management and economics. Experts have tried to develop and promote KM ideology in order to maintain competitive advantage and improve the organizational use of IT. Several investigations have emphasized KM principles [43,62] and KM practices [19,84,105]. These efforts have encouraged many organizations to establish KM-related departments or high administrative positions (such as chief knowledge officer (CKO)) [79].

Recent studies, however, have tended to concentrate on the organizational level, e.g., work of Nonaka and Takeuchi and organizational learning [5,31,50,82,83,85]. Still, other work has emphasized a national level [22,57,59,95,98].

Under some conditions, the organizational and national perspectives might however, be insufficient. This is particularly true for certain industries and countries, e.g., the hi-tech industries in Asia is primarily made up of medium or small firms which do not have the same degree of independent knowledge resources as their larger competitors in the US and Europe [17]. As a result, they need to share knowledge, such as cooperative R&D, joint knowledge alliance, and even their patents, with other organizations in the same or similar industry [76,97,99]. Specifically, in less developed countries (LDCs), and even some developed countries, such as Japan, many industries conduct their knowledge activities (KA) by cooperating with other firms [1,26,102].

The PC manufacturing industries in Korea and Taiwan provide another example; since the capital of their manufacturing industries is smaller than their counterparts in the US and Europe, every independent manufacturer might have operate strategically to meet the challenges of providing a good knowledge capability [53]. In order to strengthen their technological capability, the Korean government teamed up with industry by providing R&D funds, employing foreign experts, and utilizing emerging technologies, thereby helping manufacturers to integrate related firms, and group manufacturers in joint R&D efforts [8,47,52]. On the other hand, the Industry Technology Research Institute (ITRI) in Taiwan, with support from the US, helps experts in PC-related fields produce new technologies and patents, and then transfer and diffuse them to other firms. These efforts make the PC manufacturing industry highly competitive. Thus, an industrial perspective of KM (industry-level knowledge management (ILKM)) can be considered a new and important perspective.

ILKM has significant impact on particular industries and countries because:

1. KM needs considerable support from R&D activities and the technological infrastructure. For example, large US-owned companies have devoted large amounts of R&D resources to KM and this would be difficult for smaller organizations [94]. Consequently, smaller firms need to group, provide financing in a team-oriented manner, and execute R&D activities in a joint effort. Indeed, this is the normal practice of firms in LDCs. These joint activities share the costs and also this achieves economy of scale [52,56].

2. For most LDCs, such as India, Singapore, Taiwan, Korea, and China, the connections (or ‘‘Guanxi’’) between firms are close, tight, inseparable, and sophisticated [20,102]. Even though the R&D infrastructure is weak, the firms can still utilize their knowledge or patents mutually via interaction. By doing so, they are able to create technological advantages. Finally, many LDCs develop excellent ‘‘knowledge teams’’ that share valuable knowledge activities. For instance, Fransman and Tanaka [24] analyzed how the Japanese government assisted their hi-tech industries by building a system that links universities, laboratories, business sectors, and individual researchers; this showed how the system transferred knowledge and was managed. Porter’s [71] knowledge clusters form ‘‘knowledge competitiveness’’ for nations, the industries, and their firms.

ILKM is not a new concept. Porter’s [71] industrial cluster, consisting of production and human resources factors, market demand conditions, strategy and structure of the firms, and the supporting and related industries considerations represents one previous example. Literature in the resource base view (RBV) revealed that ‘‘knowledge’’ was embedded in every element [35,93,100], signifying that an industrial cluster also has knowledge elements, especially in support and related industries. Moreover, many industries gain their international competitiveness by building industrial strategic alliances that focus on market information and customer knowledge (such as Japan’s large trading companies) and technological cooperation and subcontracting (such as that found in the watch makers of Switzerland). With this industry perspective, researchers have suggested that the German automotive industry divides its value-added activity through knowledge and technological cooperation [59], and that the German government transfers the knowledge generated in science parks to all related firms [95]. Thus, the industrial perspective matches the practice of KM.

## 2. The ILKM model

## 2.1. Classification dimensions for the ILKM model

Since ILKM is a new ideology, it is essential to classify the model before exploring it further. Ansoff’s [2] construction regarding corporate development strategy provides a theoretical base for building the model. He, after conducting, many case studies, classified corporate development strategy in two dimensions: (1) whether the firms stay in the same markets or enter into new ones based on their ontological view and (2) whether they concentrate on the existing products or develop new ones based on their epistemological perspective. In addition, Smith and Wolfe [89] provided an interfacial insight on the typology of knowledge activities by treating knowledge management as efforts to fit the knowledge body into the context that conducts knowledge activities. Hence, a two dimension, four-mode model was formed as presented in Fig. 1. This follows Ansoff and Smith and Wolfe [89], to classify ILKM.

The first dimension utilizes an ontological perspective [43], which classifies ILKM by determining whether or not KAs are executed in a way consistent with its industry or not. Consistent activities include diffusing, transferring [69], work assigning [27], and sharing existing knowledge with other firms, leading them into new knowledge domains, and expanding or extending the body of knowledge. On the other hand, KAs in an inconsistent industry scope lead the firms to cooperate, contract, and even deal and bargain with other organizations across the industry.

The second dimension takes the epistemological perspective, classifying ILKM by determining whether the body of knowledge is consistent with existing ones. We followed the classification of Bierly and Chakrabarti [7] and Lausen and Mahnke [41], which defined ECC knowledge activities under identical paradigm as those that are conducted in homogeneous knowledge and technological bases, such as modifying product and deepening the knowledge. Conversely, knowledge activities under a different paradigm refers to those with heterogeneous or diversified knowledge characteristics and technological bases, such as the development of new product lines and entering into state-of-the-art domain knowledge. Since ILKM might involve diversified firms and organizations, it was logical to infer that the knowledge body might also change. Therefore, this dimension was used to judge whether or not the knowledge body and paradigm had been altered.

Four ILKM modes were therefore defined and named: knowledge clustering, knowledge enlarging, knowledge exchanging, and knowledge initiating.

## 2.2. Knowledge clustering

Porter [71] argued that an industry improves and sustains its competitiveness via every well-organized activity and infrastructure in the value system. Every element in the knowledge cluster plays a particular role and creates specific value to it. Employing this concept in ILKM, an industry should improve the use of its knowledge by diffusing, circulating, and transferring it to related organizations [9], properly organizing knowledge [59], and adequately utilizing its effects [16]. Such efforts could reshape an industrial cluster. Table 1 summarizes a list of the related literature and contents about knowledge clustering.

Thus, knowledge clustering includes the following activities:

\- Knowledge flows, transfer, diffusion, learning, and assimilation within the industry and related arena [3,49,77,88,99,106].

\- Knowledge circulation within the industry and related fields [52,60,92].

\- Dividing knowledge works and duties to achieve the requirements of industrial synergy [59].

\- Making the best use, application, and employment to enlarge its value [10].

\- Establishing appropriate mechanisms and relationships to effectively transfer, diffuse, and intermediate knowledge in the industry and related fields [49,59,60,69,106].

The IC-related industries in Taiwan and Korea can be used to illustrate the value of knowledge clustering. The cluster consists of firms, governmental sectors, research institutes, and knowledge workers. Taiwan government officials, with a far-sighted vision in the 1970s, decided to initiate the IC industry after the oil crisis of 1974. Under the guidance of the US IC industry [54,94] and Taiwan’s National Science Council, the government started to build the infrastructure in its Science-based Industrial Park; this attracted more than 300 hi-tech firms and 100,000 knowledge workers. With help from US firms, the Park forms a solid network of vertical integration and strategic alliances that help the firms create a unique competitive edge. In addition, the ITRI in Taiwan endeavors to develop state-of-the-art IC technologies and carefully divides works within the industries. It assists the related industries in constructing a technological cluster comprising all operational activities, such as circuit design, manufacturing, testing, and packaging. Moreover, most R&D projects in Taiwan’s IC industry rely heavily on ITRI [104], two engineering-driven universities near ITRI, and many US and Japanese counterparts. These associations not only provide R&D resource and equipment for scientists and engineers, but also direct them to lead particular knowledge efforts and responsibilities. To this end, every activity is embedded in a cross-national IC value-added chain. These activities thus have a favorable geographical location for developing IC industrial cluster [52,86,104].

## 2.3. Knowledge enlarging

Knowledge enlarging occurs when KAs are executed within the industry while the knowledge body is being altered positively. According to Hedlund’s [33] concept of ‘‘T-shape’’ knowledge, knowledge enlarging could be divided in two, either extending or expanding [75,82,100]. Knowledge extending occurs when the existing knowledge is stretched in a vertical sense: the original knowledge body has been made deeper, more profound, and more perspicacious. Most cases of knowledge upgrading are in this category. On the other hand, knowledge expanding happens when the existing knowledge is broadened horizontally: the original knowledge has been altered to integrate and diversify it. One famous example of knowledge expanding occurred when Koa (the biggest cosmetics brand in Japan) utilized its knowledge of surfaces science to produce notebook computer monitors [48,72,75]. Knowledge created through the socialization, externalization, combination, and internalization (SECI) process can trigger a new spiral of knowledge creation, expanding it both horizontally and vertically. It is a dynamic process, starting from the individual level and expanding through communities of interaction that transcend sectional, departmental, divisional and even organizational boundaries [48,63,87]. The transfer of expertise takes place over an extended period and each phase represents an increase in the degree and scope of local control and responsibility [68]. Table 2 summarizes the findings of articles about knowledge enlarging.

According to the study of Sanchez and Heene [82], the knowledge life cycle gets shorter, and knowledge enlarging becomes an essential way to stretch the life cycle. The concepts of knowledge enlarging have been widely discussed with a focus mainly on the organizational layer. According to literature, knowledge enlarging may contain the following activities.

\- Leading members within the industry to enter new and correct knowledge domains [69,80,98,102].

\- Guiding firms/organizations within the industry to extend and/or expand knowledge using existing core knowledge of the industry [44,53].

\- Supporting members in knowledge extending and/or expanding via knowledge transfer, integration, modification, and purification [72,75,87,99].

Taiwan’s Textile Industry Development Association (TIDA) is an example of knowledge enlarging. In the 1960s, Taiwan’s textile industry manufactured only regular and typical fabrics and materials, receiving OEM orders from the US and Japan and utilizing outdated technology. To improve their international competitiveness, Taiwan’s government and textile manufacturers jointly established the TIDA. It has helped the small and medium textile firms in Taiwan in developing knowledge for effective designing, manufacturing, sourcing, material application, and international marketing, and has formed a world-class industrial knowledge team. At the start, with guidance from European and Japanese experts, TIDA instructed, modified, and localized textile fashion information and practices for Taiwan. Additionally, TIDA held international textile exhibitions to promote and expand new technology and ‘know-how’ to its members. Taiwan’s government also sponsored TIDA to improve its R&D capability. For instance, the Ministry of Education established textile-related programs and courses in colleges and universities. The National Science Council made a call for textile research projects from Taiwan and overseas academics. The Committee of Labor Affairs helped to set up courses and invited foreign experts to train new employees. Moreover, TIDA asked the Export and Trading Development Association (ETDA) of Taiwan to promote new products and ideas from Taiwan in European countries and provide assistance in constructing joint international brands, including prestige, middle class, and low-price merchandise. In recent years, with the development of manufacturing in China, TIDA has gradually helped to extend and expand the technologies to China and to form new ties between Taiwan and China.

## 2.4. Knowledge exchanging

The third model, knowledge exchanging, occurs when the activities of ILKM are implemented across industry borders under the existing knowledge paradigm [18]. The dynamic and changing environment makes knowledge cheap and easy to copy. KAs within one industry might be insufficient to sustain an advantage permanently and new knowledge and competition originates constantly and continuously across industrial and national borders. Thus, transferring, trading, and marketing competitive knowledge to other industries, might be a strategy to prolong knowledge life cycle and values [95]. Table 3 shows these knowledge exchanging activities.

The increasing cross-industrial KAs have made knowledge exchanging prevalent today. It consists of:

\- Knowledge licensing, franchising, strategic alliances and instructing across the industry [95].

\- Integrating and coordinating the existing knowledge between industries [72,74].

\- Cross-industry mercantile affairs, including knowledge trade, interchange, and related exchange behavior [32,80,83,87,89].

\- Knowledge cooperation among divergent interfaces, including industry, education sectors, research agencies, customers, and even competitors [43,58,61, 83,85].

\- Providing a proper culture and climate for knowledge cooperation [15,40,44,61,87,102].

\- Playing an intermediary role for knowledge cooperation [11,37,87].

\- Cross-national knowledge cooperation [67,79].

Government sectors in Asia and some European nations contribute substantially to knowledge exchange in their industries [28]. The Japanese biotechnology industry is controlled and supervised by its government providing technological interaction between industry, laboratories, and universities. The Ministry of International Trade and Industry regulates industrial and economic policy for biotechnology transfer and R&D interaction and dominates the cross-industrial and collective R&D projects. In addition, it asks the firms to designate researchers to develop joint cooperation in R&D projects with universities, etc. [21]. The Japanese government dominates all the agreements, contracts, transfer policies, international projects, and crossindustrial programs thus making knowledge exchanging successful.

## 2.5. Knowledge initiating

When there is a knowledge paradigm shift, KAs become more sophisticated and complicated. Most KAs related to knowledge initiating require major anticipation, coordination, connection, and innovation under conditions of unpredictability and risk. Accordingly, knowledge managers have to establish the policy, originate the concepts, coordinate the knowledge, determine rules, pioneer the product prototypes, set up the scales and standard of KAs, and manage outcomes (such as patents). Knowledge assets are constantly evolving [63]. The firm must allocate responsibilities for these activities and establish channels of communication and suitable incentives for participants. It may be worthwhile to broaden the goal of development activity by taking into account the potentials for more widespread use. Otherwise, it may be desirable to prioritize and restrict development to certain markets [68]. Table 4 lists the activities involved in knowledge initiating.

Practices associated with knowledge initiating include:

\- Setting up the scale, standards, and prototypes of knowledge and its outcomes [14,58,61,81,103].

\- Establishing proper mechanism for cross-industrial cooperation and organization [38,65,81].

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. The framework

In order to validate our model, we constructed a survey instrument to measure ILKM. CKO-class practitioners in the information-related industry in Taiwan were sampled. Criteria for testing the explanation ability (i.e., criterion validity) of ILKM were also developed. The research framework without the industrial knowledge performance (IKP) [99] of this study, portrayed in Fig. 2, was constructed by industrial knowledge clustering, enlarging, exchanging, and initiating.

Our study involved testing of the criterion validity of ILKM. To follow the ‘‘Environment-Strategy-Conduct-Performance’’ paradigm (ESCP), it was reasonable to select IKP as a criterion to study ILKM as shown in Table 5. To examine the effectiveness and validity of the scale, we used a three-type knowledge performance indicator: knowledge depth, knowledge broadness, and knowledge integration. The test on criterion validity was vital in examining the appropriateness of the data collected.

## 3.2. Scale construction and measures

Our study developed a preliminary scale consisting of 30 items. Four chief knowledge officers in the IRI who were familiar with ILKM were asked to assess the face validity of the original version of the questionnaire. After deleting 5 items, leaving a 25-item set, 36 pilot forms were sent to subordinates of the four CKOs. From this investigation, eight, ore items are dropped. Thus, a four sub-construct, 17-item ILKM scale remained. The operational definitions, sources, and descriptors of this scale are given in Table 6. The scale used a Likert’s 5-point scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree).

In addition, we employed three-item performance indices to measure IKP [42,106]. We also adopted Hedlund’s suggestion that the indices include the extent to which the industry made progress in knowledge depth, knowledge broadness, and knowledge integration during the past three years (using a Likert scale from 1 = made no progress to 5 = made significant progress). As to reliability of the construct, Cronbach’s a was found to be 0.657, and item-to-total correlation coefficients was from 0.567 to 0.658, indicating good internal consistency.

## 3.3. Sampling and the sample

We targeted the IRI in Taiwan for this empirical study. There were three reasons for this:

1. A competitive IRI needs a comprehensive industrial cluster. IRI in Taiwan contains over 3000 member firms to serve the industry. Thus, the sampling process could be reliable and meaningful.

2. The IRI is basically a ‘‘knowledge-driven’’ industry, and thus it matched the subject area of our study.

3. The IRI in Taiwan is well-organized: every firm has its own specific missions and division of works. Thus, the sampling framework was solid and the sampling process was feasible.

This study employed a sampling technique to select respondents [6]; 200 of about 600 members of the IRI Association, were mailed questionnaires in late 2003. One hundred and twenty-three effective responses (a 20.0% response rate) were collected. A Chi-square test signified homogeneity between the distributed samples and the respondents.

The data set was then, split into two groups consisting of 60 from the early return and 63 of the last ones [96]. Non-response biases were then computed by comparing the mean scores of the two data groups using t-tests. The results yielded no differences among the items; this further strengthened the validity of our study.

## 3.4. Statistical techniques for data analysis

In order to analyze the collected results, we employed multivariate statistical techniques. Cronbach’s a coefficients were used to test the internal consistency of the scale. Furthermore, following the evaluation criteria proposed by Joreskog and Sorbom’s [39] and Bagozzi and Yi’s [4], indices of LISREL’s Confirmatory Factor Analysis were used to examine convergent and discriminate validity of the model. Criterion validity of the scale was examined by testing the causality coefficients of LISREL (the gs in Fig. 4). Finally, some descriptive statistics were performed to further investigate the ILKM conditions of IRI.

The structural equation modeling approach is used in testing structural theory [96]. It incorporates both observed and latent variables, and is usually separated into measurement models and a structural model. Joreskog and Sorbom recommended a two-step modeling approach for assessing the fit of the structural model independently of the measurement models. Maruyama [55] proposed that, to ensure model identification, one should separate the measurement and structural models. Furthermore, if each measurement model is identified independently, then the structural model would be identified.

Therefore, a two-step modeling approach was conducted. The confirmatory factor models were tested prior to the structural model. The maximum likelihood (ML) estimation method was employed. According to Bagozzi and Yi, this is superior, since it has desirable asymptotic properties and is scale-free. For our study, seven measures of goodness of fit were used, including GFI, AGFI, RMSR, CFI, NFI, and the Chi-square value.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Internal consistency analysis

This study employed Cronbach’s a and item-to-total correlations as indices to test the internal consistency and convergence of the model, the results are listed in Table 6. The a coefficients for the four sub-constructs knowledge clustering, enlarging, exchanging, and initiating, are 0.841, 0.810, 0.833, and 0.879, respectively. The a coefficients all exceeded the threshold (0.70) suggested by Nunnally [64], indicating that the ILKM scale was internally consistent. Item-to-total correlation coefficients were also employed to appraise convergence of each sub-construct. We deleted eight items whose item-to-total correlation values were lower than 0.5 as suggested by Byrne. The coefficients from 17 items ranged from 0.50 (item 8) to 0.80 (item 17), all of which matched the significance level requirement $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ The item-to-total correlation evidence indicated good convergence and internal consistency on the four sub-constructs.

The internal consistency results of the IKP scale are given in Table 7. Cronbach’s a ranged from 0.758 to 0.818, and the item-to-total correlation coefficients were spread from 0.47 to 0.71, all of which were statistically significant. These results again verified the convergence of the IKP scale.

## 4.2. Structural equation model approach

## 4.2.1. Analysis of the measurement models

Although the Cronbach a and item-to-total correlations showed satisfactory reliability and validity of each construct, the independency among factors had not yet been proven. Confirmatory factor analysis was therefore performed, as suggested by Byrne [12], to assess the measurement model of knowledge clustering, knowledge enlarging, knowledge exchanging, knowledge initiating and organizational performance. To establish the scale for each latent variable in the model, the first regression path in each measurement model was fixed at 1 [55].

The partial results of the LISREL test were listed in the second right column of Table 7. All of the ls values are higher than 0.68 at a p < 0.01 significance. The empirical evidence indicated marginal construct validity (GFI = 0.898, AGFI = 0.888, CFI = 0.905, and cross-factor correlation coefficients range from 0.261 to 0.452). Thus, by integrating the testing results of convergent and discriminate validity, it was reasonable to conclude that the scales for measuring IKP contained good construct validity.

4.2.1.1. Measurement model of knowledge clustering. The knowledge clustering construct was the first measurement model tested. Tan’s study [96] indicated that a high modification index revealed high correlation between two factors. The modification indices suggested that KC1 and KC2 were mutually influenced. Since diffusing and circulating the existing knowledge to other members of our industry is likely to be part of universalizing the existing knowledge via knowledge intermediaries and agencies, an error covariance between the two factors was included and the knowledge clustering model was modified. Parameter estimates describe the ability of the observed variables to measure the corresponding latent variable. All parameter estimates were rather large and hence, statistically significant, with a t-value greater than 1.96. Results indicated that the model fit the sample data well, with GFI = 0.999. AGFI = 0.991, RMSR = 0.005, $\mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 9 7 $ , and NFI = 0.999. Standardized parameter estimates are shown in the first row of Table 8.

## 4.3. Some results from descriptive statistics

Table 6 gives mean values of the 17 ILKM variables that represent ILKM tendencies of IRI in Taiwan. The item means of knowledge clustering range from 2.24 to 2.80, indicating a medium level of knowledge clustering. Since knowledge clustering is the simplest and essential work of the four ILKM modes, this might imply that there is insufficient effort expended for knowledge diffusion and transfer for IRI in Taiwan. On the other hand, item means of knowledge enlarging are widely spread between 2.21 and 3.36, representing dispersion of extending and expanding behaviors in IRI. As for knowledge exchanging, the item means are located in a range from 2.19 to 3.14, showing that trading, interchanging, and licensing behaviors are not common in IRI. Finally, means of knowledge initiating range from 2.40 to 2.96; this is the lowest of the four ILKM modes.

## 5. Conclusions

## 5.1. Summary

As most studies tend to consider KM issues from organizational and national levels, they neglect the fact that many knowledge activities in developing countries occur at the industrial level. Our attempted to explore industrial-level knowledge management via model construction and an empirical survey. After reviewing prior work, we concluded that there were four distinct ILKM modes which we termed: knowledge clustering, enlarging, exchanging, and initiating. From responses to a questionnaire from 123 respondents in Taiwan we found that the ILKM scale matched the basic requirements of face validity, internal consistency reliability, and constructs validity. It also indicated that the tendencies of the four modes correlated positively with one another, implying good criterion validity for the ILKM model.

## 5.2. Limitations

This study, using literature and an empirical survey, provides primarily a new insight into KM issues from an ‘‘industrial’’ perspective. Firstly, even though it is very flourishing in practice, the industrial perspective of KM has been neglected during the past decades. It is the time to focus and work for ILKM now. Our study has provided a comprehensive checklist for ILKM. The items provided some guidance to start our ILKM efforts. However, the survey was conducted only in Taiwan. Secondly, this study constructs an ILKM scale, and the empirical evidence rudimentarily verifies its reliability and validity. Thirdly, this study samples IRI in Taiwan as respondents for the empirical survey. Since IRI is knowledge-based, the findings might solely reflect the effects of ILKM on hi-tech industries, and might not be generalized to ordinary and services industries. Finally, this study focuses on condition of ILKM but does not explore the determinants of ILKM. Solely understanding ILKM of a particular industry does not have much value, but exploring what, how, and when the ILKM conditions are produced may be meaningful.
