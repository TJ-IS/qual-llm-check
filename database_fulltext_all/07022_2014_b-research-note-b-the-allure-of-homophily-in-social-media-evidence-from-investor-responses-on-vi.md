## 1. Introduction

Homophily, which refers to people’s propensity to seek interactions with similar others, is one of the most prevalent phenomena in the social sciences (e.g., McPherson et al. 2001; see Mark 2003 for a review). Given the prevalence of homophily, a question of interest is the degree to which homophily is exhibited in online social media and what can potentially attenuate the homophily bias.

We study this question in the context of virtual investment communities (VICs). A VIC, which consists of a collection of message boards wherein each board focuses on one particular stock or investment instrument, allows investors to interact online with other investors to exchange information about individual stocks or investment instruments. VICs are becoming an increasingly important venue for online investors as the number of participants continues to grow. For example, Yahoo! Finance alone has 65,949 message boards for investment activities. Likewise, the number of do-it-yourself investors has grown to over 40 million since 1995 and many of them participate actively in VICs.

Assuming that the primary motivation of investors is to maximize their monetary net worth, one would expect investors to seek divergent, rather than homogenous, views on VICs. This is because exposure to divergent views is more likely to lead to objectively superior decisions (Bantel 1993, Corfman and Kahn 1995). On the other hand, it is possible that investors will exhibit homophily even in the context of VICs, since exposure to similar views and opinions leads to psychological benefits, such as confidence in the accuracy of opinions. The objectives of the present research are to assess the extent to which investors in financial markets exhibit homophily (or heterophily) on VICs, and to identify theoretically meaningful conditions under which the homophily exhibited by investors is attenuated.

To address our objectives, we analyzed a sample of 682,781 messages from investors on Yahoo! Finance message boards for 29<sup>1</sup> Dow Jones stocks during the months of June 1, 2009, to December 31, 2009. We assess how the investors make choices in their interactions and whether they exhibit homophily behavior. Like many online communities, investors interact in VICs through originating and responding to messages. Their responses to others’ messages form the basic fabric of social interactions in VICs (Faraj and Johnson 2011). Our analysis focuses on how investors make choices in such interactions. Our findings show that investors are not immune to the allure of homophily even in the context of VICs. Further, we find that the tendency to exhibit homophily is moderated by three factors that affect the trade-off between instrumental benefits and psychological benefits: investor experience in VICs, amount of information in information threads, and uncertainty.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we discuss the motivations that drive people toward homophily, and highlight conditions under which homophily is disadvantageous. In §3, we expand on our reasons for investigating the prevalence of homophily in the context of investor interactions in VICs. We also discuss the role of moderators expected to influence homophily in the context of VICs. In §4, we present data and methodology, followed by empirical analysis in §5. We discuss results and limitations in §6.

## 2. Theoretical Background: Psychological Drivers of Homophily and Heterophily

Although homophily can be based on demographic (e.g., age, gender, education, income) or psychographic variables (e.g., values, attitudes, beliefs), we expect homophily in VICs to be based more on psychographic variables. In particular, we expect people with similar sentiments on stock future directions to flock to each other.

Researchers have identified two broad reasons for why homophily occurs (e.g., Deutsch and Gerard 1955,

Raghunathan and Corfman 2006). First, people are motivated to build and maintain bonds with others. A long tradition of research in psychology has established that human beings are social animals (Schultz et al. 2007), and as such, are finely attuned to how others feel toward them. In particular, people crave the feeling of belongingness with others (Baumeister and Leary 1995), and act in ways that enhance the chances that others will like them. One means by which people get others to like them is by being similar to them, since similarity enhances likeability (Lakin and Chartrand 2003, Suedfeld et al. 1971). Suedfeld et al. (1971) found, for example, that marchers in an antiwar demonstration were more likely to sign the petition of a similarly (versus differently) dressed requester.

A second reason for homophily is rooted in the desire to feel confident about the accuracy of one’s opinions and attitudes. Findings show that the need for accuracy, generally manifested in the quest for a better understanding of one’s own abilities (e.g., Trope and Bassok 1983), is a fundamental driver of human behavior (e.g., Festinger 1954, Raghunathan and Trope 2002). One reason people have the need for accuracy is because they know that the validity of their judgments and decisions depend critically on the accuracy of their opinions and attitudes. One of the main ways in which people gauge the accuracy of their views is through social comparisons (Festinger 1954). When we encounter others with views similar to ours, we feel good because the confirmation boosts confidence in the accuracy of our views. In contrast, when we encounter others with views dissimilar to ours, we feel bad because the disconfirmation undermines confidence in the accuracy of our views (e.g., Swann et al. 1987).

In sum, homophily is driven by the twin desires of building relationships and feeling confident about the accuracy of one’s opinions. Both desires are deepseated and often activated automatically in driving human behavior (Bargh and Chartrand 1999, Fazio et al. 1986). It is therefore not surprising that homophily is prevalent. However, despite the psychological advantages that homophily confers on individuals, it can also lead to certain critical disadvantages. Specifically, in contexts in which it is important to hold objectively accurate opinions, homophily can be problematic. This difficulty occurs because the objective accuracy of opinions improves with exposure to a diverse (versus homogenous) set of views. Indeed, as Rogers and Bhownik (1970) argue, interacting solely with people who think alike will lead to exposure to redundant, and therefore useless, information. Consistent with this line of reasoning, findings in the group decision-making literature show that the quality of decisions improves significantly as a function of exposure to diverse information (e.g., Bantel 1993, Horwitz and Horwitz 2007).

To summarize, three forces may be said to act on individuals who have the opportunity to interact with others. The first two forces—the desire to build relationships, and the desire to feel confident about the accuracy of one’s opinions—steer people toward seeking confirmatory information (i.e., homophily). The third force—the desire to make good quality decisions—should steer people away from homophily and toward heterophily, assuming that people wish to improve the quality of their decisions. However, whether people in fact exhibit heterophily as predicted by the normative theory is an empirical question. It is possible that decision makers do not recognize the relative advantage of heterophily (versus homophily) for improving the quality of decisions—in which case, we would expect homophily even in contexts in which people would be better off exhibiting heterophily.

## 3. Investor Interactions in VICs: Theoretical and Substantive Considerations

Based on the discussion thus far, we conclude that the extent to which people exhibit homophily (versus heterophily) in a given context will depend on whether the instrumental benefits (improvement in the quality of decisions that results in higher monetary returns) associated with heterophily outweigh the psychological benefits associated with homophily relationship building. The trade-off between instrumental benefits and psychological benefits is often made subconsciously instead of deliberately by individuals. In most routine social situations (e.g., housewarming parties, casual conversations in online chat rooms), people are less likely to obtain instrumental benefits; as such, we expect people to exhibit homophily in these contexts because of the automatic activation of psychological bias. However, in contexts in which important outcomes (such as health-related or financial ones) are at stake, we may expect the otherwise prevalent tendency for homophily to be attenuated.

There are at least two reasons why we expect homophily to be attenuated in the context of VICs. First, the primary objective of investors is to make money—and not to build relationships or to feel good about the accuracy of one’s views and opinions. As already mentioned, findings in the group decisionmaking literature show that the quality of one’s decision improves as a function of exposure to diverse information (e.g., Bantel 1993, Horwitz and Horwitz 2007). The effect of exposure to diverse informational inputs on quality of decisions appears to be particularly pronounced in complex tasks (Stewart and

Barrick 2000), such as stock-investment decisions. In other words, the context of making stock-investment decisions appears particularly well suited for exhibiting heterophily (versus homophily).

Second, given that investment-relevant information is so easily available on the Internet, the transaction costs associated with sourcing disconfirming information is low. In contrast, before the advent of online forums, social structures imposed stringent structural (e.g., geographical) barriers, which made it much more difficult for people to interact with dissimilar (versus similar) others (Kandel 1978, Kossinets and Watts 2009). Thus, because it is easier to access disconfirming (versus confirming) information in online (versus offline) contexts, we expect that homophily will be attenuated in VICs.

In sum, for the reasons mentioned above, investors on VICs should be motivated to exhibit heterophily, and should also have ample opportunity to do so. However, whether they in fact exhibit heterophily is an empirical question. Studies on virtual communities find that participation behavior in online communities is often driven by the desire for social benefits and psychological bonding (Bateman et al. 2011, Wellman and Gulia 1999, Dholakia et al. 2004). These studies, however, do not focus on the investment community; rather, they deal with people who participate in forums of general interest, such as current events, sports, and fashion. A potentially important difference between people who participate in communities of general interest and investors who participate in VICs is that the latter are likely to be more focused on maximizing their monetary net-worth, and much less focused on psychological benefits. The investor group is likely to be interested in interacting with others who are trying to enhance the superiority of their judgments and decisions. Nevertheless, to the extent that the desire to build and maintain relationships and the desire to feel good about the accuracy and validity of one’s opinions are each deep-seated and powerful, it is conceivable that even investors may find it difficult to avoid the allure of psychological (versus instrumental) benefits associated with homophily.

There is yet another reason why even investors may find it difficult to overcome the tendency for homophily: The psychological motives underlying homophily may operate nonconsciously (Bargh and Ferguson 2000, Todorov and Uleman 2004, Petty et al. 1996). Hence, investors may be unaware they are exhibiting homophily and unable to guard against this tendency. Findings show that people are unaware of the influence that social motives (such as the desire to build and maintain relationships) have on their behaviors. Nisbett and Wilson (1977) review several findings showing that although people routinely revise their attitudes and opinions based on input from others—presumably to improve the quality of relationship with the others (e.g., Deutsch and Gerard 1955, Raghunathan and Corfman 2006)—they are not even aware that their attitudes and opinions have changed, let alone able to recognize the role social influence has played in effecting these changes. This situation suggests that the influence of social motives is likely to take place nonconsciously, a theme supported by several other findings (e.g., Nisbett and Borgida 1975; see Baumeister and Leary 1995 for a review). In contrast, given that the calculation of instrumental benefits is a much more conscious process (e.g., Ajzen and Fishbein 1972), the decision to exhibit heterophily (i.e., seek diverse and disconfirming information) is likely to be a much more conscious one. Extrapolated to the present research, these findings suggest that investors will be less likely to guard against homophily, and therefore will exhibit it to a greater extent than they should.

So, overall, either of the two competing hypotheses stated below may occur in the context of VICs, but for the reasons just discussed, we expect that the scales are tipped slightly in favor of H1A.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1A</sup> <sup>(H1A).</sup> Individuals are likely to demonstrate homophily behavior in their choice of discussion thread for response.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1B</sup> <sup>(H1B).</sup> Individuals are likely to demonstrate heterophily behavior in their choice of discussion thread for response.

In the next section, we turn our attention to factors we expect will moderate the tendency among investors to exhibit homophily in VICs.

## 3.1. Moderating Factors

As we mentioned earlier, interactions among investors are motivated by a trade-off between psychological and instrumental benefits. We examine three key factors—experience on VIC, amount of information, and uncertainty—that influence the magnitude of instrumental benefits or psychological benefits for an individual investor. As discussed below, these three factors are theoretically interesting and substantively relevant since they are triggers for conscious activation of information processing or nonconscious activation of psychological needs.

3.1.1. Experience on VICs. Although VICs have become increasingly popular, not all investors are equally experienced with them. We expect investor experience with VICs will moderate the extent of homophily in VICs. Investors having little (versus more) experience with VICs are more likely to exhibit homophily for three reasons. First, identifying useful information in VICs requires substantial learning.

although VICs may contain investment advice and opportunities to interact with experienced investors (including at times company insiders), they also contain substantial noise (Antweiler and Frank 2004). Navigating through tens of thousands of postings to separate information from noise and to identify trustworthy and knowledgeable posters requires time and experience. Second, and perhaps more importantly, investors new to VICs are unlikely to have learned the disadvantages of exhibiting homophily in this context—particularly because, as discussed earlier, the influence of social motives is likely to take place nonconsciously (Nisbett and Wilson 1977). In other words, investors are more likely to be steered by the powerful—and largely nonconscious— motivations toward homophily. In contrast, experienced investors are likely to have burned their fingers by prior exhibitions of homophily. Consequently, the experienced investors will have realized that to make good investment decisions, it is important to guard against homophily.

Third, an affect-laden factor is likely to steer less (versus more) experienced investors toward homophily. Online communities can incite intense emotions through trolling and flaming behaviors (Alonzo and Aiken 2004, Johnson 2009). An investor relatively new to VICs is likely to be influenced by these negative emotions into seeking homophily; findings show people are particularly likely to exhibit homophily when they feel negative (Gray et al. 2011). An experienced investor, by contrast, is less likely to be swayed by these emotional influences. For these reasons, we predict the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> An individual with more (less) experience with VICs is less (more) likely to exhibit homophily in VICs.

3.1.2. Amount of Information. Not all discussion threads within a VIC offer equal instrumental benefits. Therefore we expect investors’ tendency toward homophily will vary as a function of the instrumental benefits the thread provides. Because some threads contain postings with extensive analysis while others do not, the perceived instrumental value of threads will vary greatly. We argue that investors will use the amount of information in a particular thread as a proxy for its instrumental benefits. Specifically, investors interacting in threads with greater participation or greater content will perceive the thread to be important, and can reasonably expect they will receive valuable responses through the interactions. These instrumental benefits are likely to alert investors to be on guard against the nonconscious forces that steer people toward homophily, as well as recognize the value of exposing themselves to diverse opinions. Therefore, we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> The greater the amount of information in a discussion thread, the lower the tendency for investors to exhibit homophily in VICs.

3.1.3. Stock Volatility. Stock volatility is a key indicator of investor uncertainty on the future performance of a stock (Zhang 2006). Such uncertainty creates discomfort in the minds of individuals and, to ameliorate the discomfort, a common approach is to gain exposure to familiar information while avoiding conflicting information (Festinger 1957). As theories of social influence suggest, people’s desire to interact with similar others is particularly pronounced under conditions of high uncertainty (Festinger 1954, Gray et al. 2011). In the context of the present research, this state implies that high (versus low) stock volatility will lead to greater homophily. That is, volatility triggers a strong desire for investors to seek psychological comfort from others by interacting with those with similar views. Hence, we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> The greater the stock volatility, the more likely investors will exhibit homophily behavior.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data

Virtual investment-related communities provide an excellent research context to study individual interactions in virtual communities. Each stock message board covers one stock, and individual investors interact with one another by posting messages and replying to each other’s postings.

We collected stock message-board postings from Yahoo! Finance, one of the leading VICs. The data include a sample of 29 Dow Jones stocks during a sixmonth span from June 1, 2009 to December 31, 2009. The 29 stocks are large companies of significant interest to investors. When an individual first arrives at the message board, Yahoo! Finance presents the topic of the message board with a list of 20 discussion threads on each page. The discussion threads are offered in reverse chronological order based on the posting time of the last active message in each thread. After a site visitor clicks on a message, Yahoo! Finance presents the message content with a list of all postings in the discussion thread.

For each message, an investor can post a reply, and, in the reply, the investor has the option to select a sentiment label to indicate their opinion of the stock. On Yahoo! Finance stock message boards sentiment label identifies investor opinions in five categories: strong buy, buy, hold, sell, and strong sell. For our analysis, we collapsed “strong sell” and “sell” into one “sell” category, and “strong buy” and “buy” into one “buy” category. However, not all users provide sentiment labels in their messages. We collected a total of 682,781 messages<sup>2</sup> for the 29 Dow Jones stocks, and 116,473 (about one-sixth) had sentiment labels. Past researchers have used two approaches to address missing sentiments for these messages: text mining to estimate sentiments (Antweiler and Frank 2004, Das and Chen 2007), or ignoring messages without sentiments altogether (Gu et al. 2007). Given our focus on interactions in VICs, it is necessary to identify the sentiment of each message. We use the text-mining approach for identification of sentiments. A variety of text-mining methods can be used for sentiment extraction, and prior studies show they perform comparably (Das and Chen 2007). For consistency, we use the same text-mining method as in Antweiler and Frank (2004, see Appendix A). For our analysis, we assigned a numeric sentiment value to each opinion category. The postings in the “sell,” “hold,” and “buy” categories are given a sentiment value of −1, 0, and $^ { 1 , }$ respectively.

## 4.2. Thread Choice

Another key task of the data collection and processing is to reconstruct the environment under which an individual arrives at the stock message board and chooses a discussion thread for response and participation.<sup>3</sup> The design of the Yahoo! Finance stock message boards allows us to reconstruct the environment. Threads are presented in reverse chronological order on the message board. By default, Yahoo! Finance presents 20 of the most recent threads on the first screen. We thus focus our analysis on how individuals make posting decisions among these 20 threads.<sup>4</sup> Besides identifying the potential choice set for the replies, we also need to identify which of the 20 threads are actually chosen for response by an individual. The identification is straightforward, as Yahoo! Finance automatically identifies the thread that each message belongs to.

Identification of message sentiment, choice set presented to each individual, and actual thread choice for each reply provide the main variables needed for our analysis. In addition, we also collected the following attributes for each message: thread ID, message ID, author ID, posting date and time, message subject, and message content. Appendix B shows the descriptive statistics of the 29 stock message boards in the Yahoo! Finance community.

## 4.3. Empirical Model

We adopt a discrete choice model to analyze how individuals select a particular thread to respond. To apply this model, we first define the choice set for each individual at each decision point. As we mentioned earlier, Yahoo! Finance’s message boards present the 20 most recent threads in reverse chronological order on the first screen. We thus focus our analysis on the individual’s choice among these 20 threads. To capture the possibility that an individual chooses a message beyond the 20 most recent threads, we select the 21th thread or beyond as the reference category $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the outside option), while considering the first 20 threads as individual choices. Given the choice set, online investors evaluate each thread in the set and choose the one with the highest utility to post replies. We use a conditional logit (CL) framework to model the choice process. Specifically, the probability that the thread $k \ : \hat { ( k \in { 1 , \dots , 2 0 } }$ , outside option) is chosen by investor $i ,$ given the choice set $C _ { i }$ of the 20 most recent threads plus the outside option, is

$$
P (k \mid C _ {i}) = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i k} \boldsymbol {\beta})}{\sum_ {j \in C _ {i}} \exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\beta})}.\tag{1}
$$

In the above equation, $\mathbf { X } _ { i j }$ refers to a vector of independent variables related to investor i and discussion thread $j .$ Since utilities are a relative measure, the direct effect of individual and market characteristics on choice utility is cancelled out in the CL model (McFadden 1986, Feldman et al. 1989). To observe this situation, we note that if an individual characteristic or market characteristic $w _ { i }$ increases the choice utility of every alternative by w , the choice probability remains the same

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (k \mid C _ {i}) = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i k} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \delta w _ {i})}{\sum_ {j \in C _ {i}} \exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \delta w _ {i})} \\ \qquad = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i k} \boldsymbol {\beta}) \exp (\delta w _ {i})}{\sum_ {j \in C _ {i}} \exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\beta}) \exp (\delta w _ {i})} \\ \qquad = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i k} \boldsymbol {\beta}) \exp (\delta w _ {i})}{\exp (\delta w _ {i}) \sum_ {j \in C _ {i}} \exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\beta})} = \frac {\exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i k} \boldsymbol {\beta})}{\sum_ {j \in C _ {i}} \exp (\mathbf {X} _ {i j} \boldsymbol {\beta})}. \end{array}
$$

Thus, the influence of individual characteristics or market characteristics is through their interactions with choice characteristics. We explain the dependent and independent variables below and summarize them in Table 1.

4.3.1. Dependent Variable: Individual Choice of Discussion Threads. The dependent variable of the discrete choice model is a dummy variable indicating whether a thread in the choice set is chosen by the individual. We use a binary variable of 1 to indicate that the individual chooses to reply in a specific thread, or 0 otherwise.

4.3.2. Independent Variables. Opinion Distance. We use the term opinion distance to represent the distance between the individual opinion (i.e., opinion represented as a sentiment) and the aggregated opinion of a discussion thread. It is computed as the absolute difference of an investor’s sentiment value and average sentiment value of each thread in the choice set. A larger opinion distance indicates that the investor’s opinion is different from the specific thread in the choice set. A smaller opinion distance, on the other hand, suggests that they share similar opinions. Given our coding of sentiments, the minimum value of the opinion distance is 0 and the maximum value of the opinion distance is 2.

4.3.3. Moderating Effects. Author’s Experience. Hypothesis 2 suggests that an individual’s experience with VICs will influence that individual’s decision in responding to others’ postings. However, most authors hide their profiles on Yahoo! Finance, so it is difficult to obtain data on individuals’ characteristics. One variable that is visible from Yahoo! Finance is an individual’s level of cumulative posting activity. Someone with a substantial number of postings has more interactions with others in the virtual communities, and is thus more likely to be experienced. To identify the level of activity of an individual in the VIC, we use the total number of postings an individual has made in the community. We include the interaction term between the log value of author’s posting activity and opinion distance to assess the degree to which an author’s experience moderates that author’s homophily or heterophily behavior.<sup>5</sup> To reduce multicollinearity in the regression, we calculate the interaction terms using centered variables. One implication of this approach is that the coefficient on the main effect reflects the effect of an average investor in the VIC.

Thread Length. Hypothesis 3 suggests that the amount of information in a discussion thread moderates an individual’s homophily or heterophily behavior. We operationalize the amount of information in a discussion thread using three measures. The first measure is thread length, i.e., the total number of postings in a discussion thread at the moment that the individual posts a response. This measure is highlighted by Yahoo! Finance in its “topic view” of each stock message board, so it is clearly visible to each individual. We use the log value of the measure to control for possible nonlinearity. We include the interaction term between the centered log number of postings and the opinion distance to assess the impact of information richness on an individual’s homophily or heterophily behavior.

Posting Length. The second measure we use for the amount of information in a discussion thread is the posting length. It measures the average number of characters in each posting in a given thread at the moment an individual posts a response (Jones et al. 2004). To accommodate for the diminishing return of message information, we use the log value for our measure. We include the interaction term between the centered log posting length and the opinion distance to assess the impact of information richness on an individual’s homophily or heterophily behavior.

Posting Frequency. The third measure we use for the amount of information in a discussion thread is the posting frequency, which is the log value of the average number of postings per hour of the thread. To calculate that average, we take the total number of postings in the thread at the moment an individual observes the choice set. Then we divide that figure by the time difference between when the individual observes the choice set and when the first message in the thread was posted. We use the log value because the marginal influence of interaction frequency is likely to diminish with more frequent interactions.

Stock Volatility. We use daily stock-price range to identify the level of volatility for each stock (Beckers 1983). The daily price range refers to the price difference between the highest price (daily high) and the lowest price (daily low) in a given day for each stock. Studies suggest that range-based measures provide a highly efficient and robust proxy for stock volatility (Alizadeh et al. 2002). We obtain the daily stock high and low from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) and standardize the price range by the daily closing price. The price-range measure captures the effect of volatility on investor behavior as presented in Hypothesis 4.

4.3.4. Control Variables. Our CL model includes the following control variables that could also influence individual choice of threads for interaction.

Thread Position. Thread position refers to the position of each discussion thread relative to the most recent discussion thread. The position of a thread is positively associated with the cognitive costs of processing the thread. In particular, the cost of browsing increases as an individual scrolls down the discussion threads (Smith and Brynjolfsson 2001). The thread position is relative to the most recent threads observed by the investor on arriving at the message board: one means the most recent thread and 20 means the oldest thread in the choice set. We use the log value of thread position to capture the potential nonlinear relationship in the diminishing probability that a discussion thread is being chosen.

Thread Age. The probability of a thread being chosen varies not only with its position on the message board but also with its timeliness. Timeliness is particularly important in investment-related communities. A discussion thread with older postings is less relevant, given the fast changing market environment. The age is calculated as the log value of the time (in hours) elapsed from the posting of the first message in the discussion thread until the time an investor makes the choice decision.

Sentiment Variation. Sentiment variation in a discussion forum could influence an individual’s decision in choosing discussion threads and that person’s propensity to seek diversity. Individuals prefer avoiding disagreement with others in social settings (Torrance 1957). Experiments show that the discomfort of disagreeing with others triggers brain activities associated with pain, fear, and stress (Forsyth 2009). As a result, people tend not to post negative or disagreeing comments except in extreme cases (Dellarocas and Wood 2008). Such tendency to avoid disagreement is strongest when a group has a clear consensus view, whereas individuals are more likely to disagree or seek diversity in groups that have an ongoing debate. On the other hand, individuals who agree with the view of the majority of a discussion forum may have a strong incentive to join the debate and defend the majority view. To control for the influence of this norm of agreeing, we measure each forum’s sentiment variation using the standard deviation of average sentiments of the most recent 20 discussion threads at the moment an individual faces the choice set. We include both the direct effect of sentiment variation and interaction term between the centered log value of the sentiment variation and the opinion distance to control for its impact on individuals’ interaction behavior.

The descriptive statistics and correlations among the key variables in our model are given in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. The tables also show that, on average, a message contains 206 characters (with spaces) and a thread contains 5.6 postings. A typical author has posted an average of 16.82 postings.

## 6. Discussions and Limitations

With the growth of the Internet, virtual communities have increasingly become an important avenue for individuals to interact and share information with each other. The unprecedented access to information that virtual communities provide to investors presents us with a new and exciting opportunity for a better understanding of how individuals interact with one another in a context devoid of the types of barriers that prevented such interactions in the past.

Our findings show that investors exhibit homophilous behavior in virtual communities despite the instrumental benefits of interactions with others with different opinions. Investors are more likely to respond to those threads in VICs that echo their own opinions, exhibiting significant psychological bias. This is potentially due to the power of nonconscious cognitive process that drives individual behavior. Such homophily bias is further amplified under high stock volatility as investors seek comfort and assurance to their own beliefs (i.e., psychological benefits). This homophily bias is, however, attenuated when investors are more (versus less) experienced on VICs, and discussion threads have greater levels of information, suggesting that under certain conditions investors recognize instrumental benefits, although instrumental benefits rarely overweigh the strength of psychological bias.

These findings have important theoretical and substantive practical implications. From a theoretical standpoint, homophily leads to important psychological benefits. It makes a person feel better, because homophily allows for building and maintaining relationships with others. It also makes an individual feel more confident about that individual’s views and opinions. Yet in the long run, investors’ wellbeing would be determined more by instrumental benefits. As findings by Bantel (1993), Corfman and

Kahn (1995), and others (e.g., Horwitz and Horwitz 2007) show, the objective accuracy of one’s opinions improves with exposure to a diverse (versus homogenous) set of views (Rogers and Bhowmik 1970). Our finding suggests that, despite the fact that investors are objectively better off with exposure to a diverse set of opinions, there is a greater inclination to exhibit homophily. Thus, the tendency for homophily, which has been documented to be prevalent across a wide variety of contexts, is alive and well even in the supposedly cold and rational world of investing. This surprising result is potentially because of the fact that social motives appear to have nonconscious influences on people’s behaviors (Nisbett and Borgida 1975).

From a practical standpoint, our findings provide some insights into the design of virtual communities. Although preventing homophily behavior may not be feasible, the results reveal that the community operators can mitigate homophily behavior by encouraging more frequent and substantial interactions among their users. A number of virtual communities have taken actions in line with our findings. For example, some virtual communities allow users to vote on the helpfulness of each posting and display postings with more helpfulness votes in prominent positions. This incentive mechanism could motivate individuals to provide more substantive contents in their postings. Other virtual communities provide incentives to frequent contributors. Such inducements often take the form of granting special titles such as “platinum member” or “top 100 reviewers” to users who contribute frequently. These incentives increase interaction frequency and may reduce homophily behavior in the community. Our results also suggest that uncertainty could increase individuals’ propensity for homophily behavior. Such a finding suggests that operators of communities, especially those engaging in public debate, should be especially mindful of homophily behavior in uncertain environments.

This study has several limitations. First, it is conducted in VICs. Virtual investment-related communities have a number of unique characteristics that could influence individuals’ decisions to seek diversity in their choice of discussion threads. Readers need to be mindful of these unique characteristics, which may not generalize to other types of virtual communities. For instance, the primary goal of an investor is to increase financial gain, so that investor’s participation in the message board is presumably to make better investment choices. Second, we observe individuals’ choices of threads only through their posting behaviors, not their reading behaviors. It will be valuable in future research to assess homophily behavior explicitly in information acquisition and interpretation. Finally, unlike in survey studies, we do not have access to the motivations of online investors on VICs, since we rely on archived data. Although our analysis shows that individual behavior is largely consistent with the prediction of psychological theory, we cannot conclusively prove causality or eliminate all possible alternative explanations. Future research could combine both archival and survey data to provide a more in-depth understanding of user interactions in virtual communities.

Our results also raise new questions. In particular, homophilous behavior in virtual communities could have far-reaching influence on investors’ decision-making processes. Exploring how the behavioral biases in social media influence investors’ trading decisions would provide valuable insights. For instance, behavioral finance research has shown that individuals in the context of online investing demonstrate multiple psychological biases that distort decision-making and economic outcomes (Barber and Odean 2001, Kahneman and Tversky 1996). Our analysis suggests that investors self-select threads that align with their prior beliefs. That situation may lead to greater levels of confirmation bias and overconfidence. It could also create an illusion of control (Langer 1975). That is, people overestimate their ability to control events, when in fact some events are not controllable. The situation could also result in illusion of knowledge, in which people with access to information believe they are more knowledgeable than they really are (Deci and Ryan 1987). All of these possible consequences are well-known psychological biases. Illusion of control and illusion of knowledge may lead investors to become overconfident (Barber and Odean 2001, Konana and Balasubramanian 2005), especially when they are less experienced yet successful. They will thus hold stronger opinions about their ability to beat the market. It will be valuable to study how such psychological biases demonstrated in financial markets are related to investor homophily or heterophily behavior in virtual communities.

Finally, businesses increasingly use virtual communities as an important tool for knowledge sharing within and beyond organizational boundaries. Such knowledge-management practices could have significant implications for the organizational capability and competitive advantage of a business. Whereas our study focuses on interaction behavior in standalone VICs, homophilous behavior may also be present in firm-hosted virtual communities. An important question for future research is to develop knowledgesharing practices within firms to encourage interaction and information sharing among individuals with different viewpoints. Moreover, homophilous behavior is a pervasive phenomenon observed in a wide range of social environments. In the context of online social media, such bias could emerge in online consumer review sites, collaborative authorship sites (e.g.,

Wikipedia), and open-source development efforts. Further research on business strategies to mitigate homophilous behavior in social interactions would be instrumental in improving virtual collaboration and knowledge integration in online social media.

## Appendix A. Posting Sentiment Identification

A key measure in this study is the sentiment of postings in virtual investment communities. Although Yahoo! Finance allows a poster to indicate the sentiment for each posting, not all posters provide sentiment tags. We use a well-established text-mining algorithm—the naïve Bayes algorithm—to identify sentiment for postings without sentiment tags.

The naïve Bayes algorithm has been used in a number of prior studies to infer sentiments from postings in virtual investment communities. The algorithm used in this study is obtained from Antweiler and Frank (2004). The main assumption of the naïve Bayes algorithm is that each word carries sentiment and the overall sentiment of a posting is the collective sentiment of all words in the posting. The sentiment of each word is calculated from the frequency in which it appears in each posting type in the training data set. Specifically, assume that word $\bar { W _ { i } }$ appears $m _ { i }$ times in type T messages and $\bar { m } _ { i }$ in type $\bar { T }$ messages. Denote the total words in type $T$ messages and type $\breve { \bar { T } }$ messages as n and n¯, respectively. The conditional probability of observing W in message type $T$ and $\bar { T }$ are thus $P ( W _ { i } \mid T ) = m _ { i } / n$ and $P ( W _ { i } \mid \bar { T } ) = \bar { \bar { m } } _ { i } / \bar { \bar { n } } ,$ , respectively. Given a stream of words, naïve Bayes algorithm uses Bayes’ rule to update the probability that the words indicate sentiment T as follows:

$$
\frac {P (T \mid W _ {i})}{1 - P (T \mid W _ {i})} = \frac {P (T \mid W _ {i - 1})}{1 - P (T \mid W _ {i - 1})} \frac {P (W _ {i} \mid T)}{P (W _ {i} \mid \bar {T})}.
$$

Prior studies commonly use logs of odds ratios in place of odds ratios in the above equation to facilitate computation. The probability of the stream of words indicates sentiment T can be written as

$$
P (T \mid W _ {N}) = P (T) \exp \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \ln \frac {P \left(W _ {i} \mid T\right)}{P \left(W _ {i} \mid \bar {T}\right)}\right).
$$

We use a training data set of 5,000 messages with 1,000 messages for each sentiment type (strong buy, buy, hold, sell, and strong sell). The messages are randomly selected from all messages with tagged sentiment. We use SAS Text Miner to transform words in messages into stems. We then prune the vocabulary to 1,000 words based on their information value. The naïve Bayes algorithm is then applied to all messages without tagged sentiments and we obtain five probabilities for each posting for each of the five sentiment types. We choose the sentiment type with the highest probability as the sentiment of the posting. The accuracy of our classification algorithm for the five segment data is 33.46%, significantly higher than the accuracy of a random classification process (20%). To further improve the accuracy, we note that a key challenge for the text-mining algorithm is to distinguish strong sentiments from weak sentiments. We thus collapse strong buy and buy into one group and strong sell and sell into another. The accuracy of our classification algorithm for the three segment data is 59.95%, very close to the accuracy rate reported in Das and Chen (2007).
