# Detecting Fraudulent Behavior on Crowdfunding Platforms: The Role of Linguistic and Content-Based Cues in Static and Dynamic Contexts

MICHAEL SIERING, JASCHA-ALEXANDER KOCH, AND AMIT V. DEOKAR

ABSTRACT: Crowdfunding platforms offer founders the possibility to collect funding for project realization. With the advent of these platforms, the risk of fraud has risen. Fraudulent founders provide inaccurate information or pretend interest toward a project. Within this study, we propose deception detection support mechanisms to address this novel type of Internet fraud. We analyze a sample of fraudulent and nonfraudulent projects published at a leading crowdfunding platform. We examine whether the analysis of dynamic communication during the funding period is valuable for identifying fraudulent behavior—apart from analyzing only the static information related to the project. We investigate whether content-based cues and linguistic cues are valuable for fraud detection. The selection of cues and the subsequent feature engineering is based on theories in areas of communication, psychology, and computational linguistics. Our results should be helpful to the stakeholders of crowdfunding platforms and researchers of fraud detection.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: crowdfunding, deception detection, fraud detection, text mining, content-based cues, linguistic cues.

In recent years, crowdfunding platforms have emerged as online services offering project founders the possibility to collect funding for their project ideas [48]. These platforms have become increasingly important to collect funding for projects of various sizes. Until 2015, the leading platform, Kickstarter, has attracted more than 250,000 projects with a total amount of funding of US\$1.9 billion and a success rate of about 35 percent.

Nevertheless, the drawback of the increasing popularity of crowdfunding platforms is the rising risk of fraud related to the project campaigns prevalent on these open online services [42]. The nature of the Internet as well as the specific characteristics of crowdfunding platforms make it especially hard to detect deceivers. First, the environment of such Internet platforms is characterized by low entry barriers, spatial and temporal separation, and anonymity [59]. Also, given that project founders most often collect money for the realization of their project ideas, no actual product exists during the funding period (except prototypes) that can be evaluated by the funders and used to assess project legitimacy [32, 33].

A first analysis of projects published on kickstarter.com reveals that the majority of projects posted on the platform are nonfraudulent. Whereas more than 65 percent of the published projects fail, 30–35 percent of the projects are typically funded successfully, which means that the project founders actually receive the promised amount of funding. Nevertheless, there are also cases in which project founders fail to deliver the promised project results or the promised rewards for fund contributors are delivered late [48]. Consequently, crowdfunding platforms have introduced integrity teams that supervise the projects published on the platform and intervene in case of fraudulent behavior. Projects on Kickstarter are suspended if project founders violate the terms of use. An example of violation of the terms of use is a pretense of high interest toward the project through self-pledging or by creating multiple discussions related to a project with different user accounts [35]. Such fraudulent behavior is disadvantageous from the perspective of honest project founders as they are likely not to receive as much attention from the potential project funders active on the platform. Further, if such a fraudulent project is finally successfully funded, it is questionable whether the project goal will actually be reached and whether the rewards will finally be delivered to the project funders. Accordingly, fraudulent behavior detection on crowdfunding platforms is an important task. Extant research, however, has primarily focused on the question of which factors lead to successful funding of crowdfunding projects [36, 60] and neglects the question of how to identify suspicious projects. In this study, we close this research gap by providing and evaluating a fraud detection approach to distinguish fraudulent from nonfraudulent crowdfunding projects.

Previous research in the field of fraud detection has already extensively focused on the identification of static fraudulent website content [1, 2, 61] as well as fraudulent computer-mediated communication occurring dynamically between different agents [64]. The novel environment of crowdfunding platforms offers the opportunity to combine both research streams and to detect fraud based on static and dynamic communication: On crowdfunding platforms, project founders are able to publish information regarding their projects such as project descriptions, images, or videos. Furthermore, they are allowed to dynamically interact with their potential funders through different channels. First, users can post comments and questions regarding the project and the project founder can interact with them by providing answers. Second, project founders can provide additional information regarding the project by means of status updates. Finally, during the campaign’s funding period, the project founder can provide answers to frequently asked questions that have been received from funders.

To identify fraudulent behavior from unstructured data in the form of texts, previous research has relied on linguistic and content-based cues [65]. On one hand, linguistic cues are derived from texts and encompass abstract aspects such as the emotionality expressed or characteristics such as the diversity of the vocabulary used [63]. On the other hand, content-based cues are directly related to the text as they encompass the single words used, for instance, represented by a bag-of-words text-mining approach [27]. Especially compared to fraud detection approaches that rely only on single structured attributes such as project and user characteristics, the rich information represented in texts can arguably add much value in detecting suspicious behavior.

With this study, we extend the previous understanding by addressing the field of deception detection on crowdfunding platforms. Therefore, we analyze a real-world data set in the form of a sample of projects published on Kickstarter and we propose and evaluate different deception detection mechanisms. In addition to our contribution to the field of crowdfunding research, we also contribute to the field of deception detection in general by combining the analysis of static and dynamic communication to detect suspicious behavior. Besides the structured project and user information, we also take into account the rich information contained in texts and, guided by related theories of deception, extract linguistic and content-based cues related to the different types of communication. Finally, we also propose a novel economic evaluation methodology in order to outline the economic relevance of the proposed fraud detection classifiers.

## Background and Research Questions

## Crowdfunding Platforms

On crowdfunding platforms, project founders can collect money from potential funders (i.e., the users of the platform) in order to be able to realize their projects.

During this process, they typically communicate a rich information set related to their project idea to convince users on the platform to provide funding [47]. In previous research, it has been found that project characteristics such as the funding goal, the duration of the funding period, or a founder’s number of Facebook friends influence the successful funding of projects [48]. Furthermore, previous studies have investigated the impact of profit-orientation [50] or donor motivations in the context of crowdfunding platforms [10].

Most of the projects published are either funded successfully at the end of the funding period or their funding period ends without reaching the funding goal. Nevertheless, several projects are suspended after the start of the funding period due to fraudulent founder behavior. On the crowdfunding platform Kickstarter, the decision to suspend a project is made by the integrity team of the platform for multiple reasons connected to fraud, which are presented in Table 1 [35]. The existence of fraudulent projects is disadvantageous for the platform itself as well as for the stakeholders on the platform. If a fraudulent project goes undetected but is successfully funded, the founder might not deliver the promised project result, which can result in the loss of project funders investment. Furthermore, if a fraudulent project gains popularity in the media due to the fraudulent activities, other nonfraudulent projects can suffer because they will likely not receive the attention they would earn without the fraudulent project. Finally, if fraud occurs on the platform, this also reduces the credibility of the platform itself, which in turn might result in lower user activity, and is thus a critical risk for the platform operator. Consequently, deception detection techniques can support the identification of fraudulent projects using the information published.

Project founders have different possibilities to present their projects and to communicate with their (potential) funders. Users can select whether to review the project information (Campaign, including the frequently asked questions [FAQ]), to read the updates posted during the funding period (Updates), or to refer and contribute to users’ comments (Comments).

## Fraud Detection in Static and Dynamic Contexts

Fraud, a form of deception, is an intentional scheme aimed to create a false impression or conclusion, often for gaining an unjust advantage over another party [6]. Traditionally, detecting fraud and deception has been studied extensively in faceto-face communication, primarily by social scientists but also in the field of computer-mediated communication. Next to this dynamic type of communication, where interactive discourse between different agents occurs dynamically over time (i.e., through the interaction of different agents), fraud can also occur in static contexts. Here, a fraudulent agent publishes fraudulent information, for instance, by means of fraudulent news [19], fraudulent corporate communication [29], or by means of fake websites [61]. In contrast to dynamic communication, which can be assumed to occur more spontaneously due to an increased level of interactivity, fraudulent agents can be assumed to put more time in shaping their static communication, for instance, by means of impression management techniques [18].

In previous research, several studies have focused on detecting fraudulent static and dynamic communication on the Internet. Thereby, especially content-based and linguistic cues to deception have been distinguished [62]. Both represent verbal cues, which are (in contrast to nonverbal cues) detectable through automated analysis of texts. Content-based cues focus on “what is conveyed,” that is, on the actual content, whereas linguistic cues focus on “how deception is conveyed in a natural language” [65, p. 120]. Approaches relying on content-based cues mainly represent texts based on the bag-of-words representation. Related approaches analyze a message’s text and draw conclusions about its suspiciousness by means of analyzing the specific words contained. In contrast, linguistic cues are derived from a message’s text, for instance by determining the number of spelling errors [63].

Different studies have analyzed fraudulent static communication and proposed approaches for deception detection. A large stream of literature focuses on the detection of fraudulent websites, whereas an overview of different approaches is given by Zahedi et al. [61]. For instance, Abbasi et al. [3] propose a system to detect fraudulent websites taking into account content-based as well as linguistic cues. Next to fraudulent websites, corporate communication is also focused on, whereas another stream of literature analyzes corporate reports and aims at deception detection by means of linguistic cues (e.g., [29]) or by means of content-based cues (e.g., [23]). Finally, Fuller et al. [22] use linguistic cues for detecting fraudulent statements.

Previous research in the field of dynamic communication has focused mostly on the analysis of computer-mediated communication and the identification of fraudulent behavior. For instance, Zhou et al. [64] propose different fraud detection classifiers based on linguistic cues for the analysis of e-mails exchanged. Furthermore, Twitchell et al. [54] use speech act profiling as a technique for deception detection in synchronous computer-mediated communication, whereas the different patterns in the speech act profiles are shown to help in identifying deception.

In contrast to the different contexts previously analyzed, the unique environment in the field of crowdfunding platforms offers the possibility for both static and dynamic communication to occur. Nevertheless, the combination of the analysis of static and dynamic communication has not been the focus of previous studies, which instead have concentrated on analyzing single sources of communication. However, the combination of both seems to be promising since the rich information given in static communication as well as the more spontaneous and therefore possibly less fraudulent dynamic communication can be made use of simultaneously. With this study, we close this research gap and investigate whether the analysis of static and dynamic communication based on content-based and linguistic cues can add value in the specific field of identifying fraudulent crowdfunding projects and in the area of fraud detection in general.

In the four-factor theory (FFT), Zuckerman et al. [66] identify four underlying factors at play from a psychological standpoint when deception is occurring. These include (a) arousal, (b) attempted control, (c) affective response, and (d) cognitive factors. Deceivers feel anxiety and arousal (e.g., word repetition, hesitation) during lying as a natural human response from their nervous system. This is likely attributed to the fear of getting caught or a dissonance between values and behaviors. This is also associated with attempts to overcompensate by controlling their behavior. Further, deceivers have mixed emotional responses (e.g., guilt, duping delight) while lying. Finally, the cognitive burden encountered to conceal the lie is also reflected outwardly (e.g., using general statements, longer pauses for thinking). These factors essentially categorize the verbal and nonverbal cues that are inadvertently exhibited by the deceiver. In a similar vein, Ekman and Freisen [15] have proposed a psychiatric theoretical model “leakage theory” (LT), which focuses on deception cues leaked due to the thought processes and affective response during the act of deception. Factors related to neuroanatomy and cultural influences are posited to be jointly responsible for the deceiver expressing body movements and facial expressions that do not succeed in concealing the deception act and their emergence as “leakage” or deception cues. Given that information such as project description and user information in crowdfunding projects is thoughtfully provided by the project founders up front in the process, we argue that leakage mechanisms are not likely at play, but rather that information is being strategically conveyed through these channels. However, during the discussions and such asynchronous modes of communication, verbal cues are likely to be leaked by fraudulent project founders.

From a cognitive science perspective, Johnson et al. [33], present a competence model of fraud detection (CM) which is instantiated and tested in the context of detecting financial fraud cases in accounting audits. The model is premised on the use of deception tactics of masking, dazzling, decoying, repackaging, mimicking, and double play by the deceiver [32]. At the heart of this model is a hypothesis generation and testing process used by message receivers that tries to explain inconsistencies between expectations and information given in a set of content-specific cues. In case of crowdfunding, we argue that such inconsistencies may be reflected as specific content-based cues, in addition to the more generic linguistic cues.

McCornack [44] and McCornack et al. [45] analyze deception from a communication perspective in the information manipulation theory (IMT). It is posited that deceivers violate four key communication principles expected in a regular truthful conversation. Deceivers tend to use too little or too much information, quantitatively, trying to conceal or misrepresent information. The quality of such information is dubious at best, and a blatant lie at worst. Also, the information presented is not completely relevant to the topic at hand or comprehensible within the context, with a goal of concealing the truth, or misleading the receiver. Finally, the information being communicated is equivocal rather than being clear and succinct. In the crowdfunding context, we expect fraudulent project founders to employ these approaches while sharing project descriptions or communicating with the potential funders.

Veracity assessment techniques are used to assess the truthfulness in spoken accounts of events in criminal investigations. Criteria-based content analysis (CBCA) is a core component of the statement validity assessment (SVA) technique that is widely used across the world by practitioners to detect deception in verbal accounts in many different contexts [57]. The evaluators assess the presence or absence of nineteen different criteria (e.g., logical structure and superfluous details) specified in CBCA to assess an account’s veracity. The fewer criteria present, the greater the likelihood of deceptiveness of the account based on the premise that statements grounded in actual memories are likely to contain many more contextual details than the deceptive counterparts. Scientific content analysis (SCAN) [13] and reality monitoring (RM) [31] are other related techniques that also rely on linguistic cues for truth assessment from suspects’ written statements. For example, RM takes the view that truthful sentences can be distinguished from deceptive statements based on the difference in perceptual details embedded. The assumption is that deceivers rely on more cognitively oriented actions such as thinking and hoping that are then reflected in their communications in trying to falsify or make up an event. Some criteria such as number of details can be usefully analyzed through linguistic analysis of crowdfunding data as well, though not all such details (e.g., subjective mental state of the statement) given the different nature of data that are not reliant on memory-based accounts.

Channel expansion theory (CET) [8], an extension of media richness theory [11], brings attention to the varied communication media and their effect on deceivers in terms of how they strategically conceal or inadvertently leak deception cues as well as the ability of researchers to detect these cues. Perceived richness of communication media is suggested to be directly related to the participants’ experience using that medium channel. Participants perceiving a medium to be richer are posited to either use it to their advantage by strategically concealing possible deception cues successfully or use it very extensively and leaking cues in that process. Regardless of the goal of evading deception being the same in different channels, it is argued that the linguistic patterns found in face-to-face communication may also be found in text-based online communication. This informs our conception of what might happen in crowdfunding. There are multiple ways of communicating within the platform. The static information about the project is likely to be intentionally falsified by deceivers in a strategic manner. Nevertheless, we anticipate that the linguistic cues that abstract the general patterns of describing a potential project as well as content-specific cues will likely differ between genuine projects and fraudulent projects. The dynamic information about the project in the form of updates, frequently asked questions, and discussions may also show useful patterns across both categories of cues.

The interpersonal deception theory (IDT) [4, 5] builds on the communication perspective to analyze deception in interpersonal communication as an interchange of messages occurring between the sender and receiver. The eighteen propositions posited by Buller and Burgoon [4] describe the expected behavior of parties engaged in an interpersonal communication involving deception, and have been used as a theory base for deception detection research in computer-mediated communication. The theory is premised on the observation that deception is a goal-oriented endeavor undertaken by the deceiver. In achieving that goal, the deceiver partakes in purposeful actions such as manipulating information in terms of completeness, accuracy, and relevance. Another premise is that the deceiver is also aiming to minimize the attribution to herself if the deception is intercepted and fails. The interactive and dynamic nature of this theory lends itself well as a foundation for analyzing the interactive message exchanges between the project founder and the potential funders through the channels afforded by the crowdfunding platform, such as discussion forums and updates.

Like the IDT, interaction adaptation theory (IAT) [7] also views deception as a continuous process that spans a period of time, and specifically focuses on the reciprocity and dynamics in the interpersonal interaction. The key implication is that taking a summative view of all messages is likely to provide the overall context. While deceitful information may be embedded within truthful information periodically, IAT posits that aggregating all messages for analysis than analyzing individual messages is likely to better capture the underlying dynamics. Based on this foundation, we posit that distinguishing between static and dynamic information will be useful in the analysis, as each can shed light on different patterns occurring in the information.

## Research Questions

Together, the aforementioned theories provide the foundation for the formulation of research questions on the detection of fraudulent crowdfunding projects. Thereby, according to the model of deception detection by [33], deception can be detected if inconsistencies and anomalies can be sensed by individuals, or, in this context, by deception detection support mechanisms. In the case of crowdfunding campaigns, such inconsistencies can be sensed within static and dynamic communication and either be represented in the specific content-based cues or in the more general linguistic cues. In other words, inconsistencies and anomalies can be implicitly derived through different types of cues, which can aid data-mining techniques to differentiate between fraudulent and nonfraudulent projects.

Content-based cues encompass the text published in static or dynamic communication related to the specific project. As is known from the model of deception detection, domain knowledge plays an important role in deception detection because it eases the sensing of anomalies [33]. In the field of crowdfunding campaigns, such domain knowledge can be represented by domain-specific terminology contained within static and dynamic communication, such as “pledging goal,” “campaign,” or different “reward levels.” Basically, in contrast to the broader linguistic cues, it can already be expected that the general word selection (including the usage of crowdfunding-related terminology) differs in case of nonfraudulent and fraudulent projects. Therefore, we investigate the first research question:

Research question 1a: Are content-based cues extracted from static communication valuable for detecting fraudulent crowdfunding projects?

Research question 1b: Are content-based cues extracted from dynamic communication valuable for detecting fraudulent crowdfunding projects?

Against the theoretical background of deceptive communication, the linguistic structure of the communication by deceivers can be expected to differ from the linguistic structure of the communication of nonfraudulent project founders. Drawing on the theories discussed earlier, particularly FFT, LT, IMT, CBCA, SCAN, RM, and IDT, various linguistic constructs promise to present significant differences in segmenting fraudulent projects from nonfraudulent ones [21]. For instance, Fuller et al. [20] note over 150 potential verbal and nonverbal deception indicators, and report on a variety of verbal cues used across several deception detection studies. Drawing on the theories discussed and the successful studies in such contexts, we therefore investigate:

Research question 2a: Are linguistic cues extracted from static communication valuable for detecting fraudulent crowdfunding projects?

Research question 2b: Are linguistic cues extracted from dynamic communication valuable for detecting fraudulent crowdfunding projects?

The CET provides the understanding that different communication channels may be used differently by deceivers. The static and dynamic communication occurring in crowdfunding projects may be conceived as a higher level abstraction of channels, and thus we expect that cues from both areas will provide useful but complementary information to detect fraudulent projects. Furthermore, based on the arguments in IDT and IAT, it makes sense to analyze the entire communication spectrum for each project to allow capturing of a holistic view rather than a snapshot view. Just as experts in one area are able to provide better expertise in that area, we expect that computational learners who are trained to analyze static information may do this task very well, while those trained to analyze dynamic information may excel in that other task. To collectively assess the value of both kinds of information, it makes sense to combine them in a meaningful way that leverages each other’s strengths. Based on this rationale, we examine:

Research question 3: Is a combined analysis of static and dynamic communication more valuable for identifying fraudulent crowdfunding projects than either analyzing static or dynamic communication alone?

## Research Methodology

## Data Mining Process

We adapt the knowledge discovery in databases process model proposed by Fayyad et al. [17]. When compared to other process models, it is considered to be the most suitable for data mining projects that require substantial data preprocessing activities [39]. Furthermore, it has gained a large amount of attention within the literature on related data mining studies [39].

We create the target data set by means of data extraction from Kickstarter. Thereafter, we clean and preprocess the data, especially by determining linguistic and content-based cues. In a next step, we determine appropriate data mining and ensemble learning strategies and evaluate the proposed classifiers by means of a classic evaluation as well as by an economic evaluation. The following sections provide detailed descriptions of the specific steps performed.

## Creating a Target Data Set

The data analyzed are acquired from the online crowdfunding platform kickstarter.com. On Kickstarter, projects related to a variety of topics can be published by project founders and funded by the platform’s users. To be able to conduct our analysis, we collected all suspended projects available as of October 2014, which resulted in 326 projects (about 0.74 percent of all projects available at the time of data acquisition). As noted in Table 1, the suspended projects are indicative of fraudulent behavior and we refer to them as suspended or fraudulent projects interchangeably. To deal with the class imbalance issue, we followed an undersampling strategy [25] that allows the rarer (fraudulent) class to be represented equally to the majority (nonfraudulent) class. Accordingly, we randomly selected a same-sized sample of nonsuspended projects from the same source to obtain a balanced data set. We refer to these projects as nonsuspended or nonfraudulent projects. In the context of such projects, we ensured that the sample includes a representative amount of successfully and nonsuccessfully funded projects so that the trained classifier is built on a realistic sample of projects. All 652 projects in the data set have a single target variable “suspicious” based on whether the project is suspended (1) or nonsuspended (0). For each project, we acquired general information about the project itself (e.g., funding period and pledging goal) as well as about the project founder (e.g., number of previous projects funded and founded). Furthermore, we also acquired the static and dynamic communication, including the text of the project description as static communication and the text of the updates, comments, and frequently asked questions representing dynamic communication.

SVM aims at solving two-class recognition problems and was first introduced by Vapnik [56]. SVM aims at finding a decision surface that maximizes the margin between the different classes [38]. When the data points cannot be separated linearly, a transformation (kernel function) can be conducted so that a linear separation is possible. According to Hsu et al. [28], the radial basis function (RBF) kernel is a reasonable first choice within SVM classification. In the case of the RBF function, two parameters have to be set: C, representing a penalty parameter, and γ, a parameter of the RBF function. Both are chosen by means of a grid-search. Furthermore, for text mining, we also apply a linear kernel [30].

Artificial neural networks mimic the behavior of human neural networks and therefore consist of different interconnected artificial neurons. The connections of the different neurons have different weights. The input for each neuron is processed by means of an activation function that takes into account the inputs’ weights and then determines the artificial neuron’s output [25]. During the learning process, the weights of the different neurons are adjusted so that the output of the neural network comes close to actual classification [49]. We use a feed-forward neural network and a sigmoid activation function [22].

Naїve Bayes is a classifier based on the Bayes theorem. Thereby, instances are classified by assigning a class according to the joint probabilities of their features. Naїve Bayes classifiers have been shown to perform surprisingly well [40] with high computational efficiency.

K-nearest neighbors classifies an instance according to the majority of its k nearest neighbors [25, 58]. Here, the class of the majority of the k-nearest neighbors is assigned to the instance under investigation. Within this study, we evaluate different values of k ranging from 1 to 3 and use the Euclidean distance as distance metric [43].

Decision trees are often applied to provide more transparent classifications than do other classification techniques [25]. This advantage is closely related to the design of decision trees, which are composed of a root, branches, nodes, and leaf nodes, where the nodes represent the attributes, the branches the corresponding attribute values, and the leaf nodes the class. After inductively building the decision tree using the C4.5 algorithm [25], we apply pruning to prevent overfitting and to increase the computational efficiency of the classification [14].

Ensemble methods combine a set of classifiers in order to classify instances by taking a vote of the different classifiers’ predictions [12]. Especially in cases where individual classifiers disagree, ensembles can add value [26]. To explicitly investigate the joint value of taking into account different forms of communication, we adopt a majority voting mechanism, that is, instances are classified in a specific class if the majority of classifiers vote for this class [9, 21].

## Machine Learning Evaluation Methodology

In order to avoid overfitting, we split the whole data set into different parts, ensuring that data used for training are not used for testing the developed models. We make use of tenfold stratified cross-validation as this validation technique performs best for realworld data sets [37] and avoids overoptimistic results [46]. In the course of k-fold stratified cross-validation (k = 10), k subsets of the whole data set are generated, each with the same class distributions. In a next step, k – 1 subsets are used for training the classifier and 1 subset is used for testing the classifier. This procedure is repeated k times, where in total, each subset is used once for testing and k – 1 times for training. Following each step, a confusion matrix is built (see Table 6).

Table 6 contains the number of correctly and incorrectly classified examples. These are denoted respectively as true positives (TP), true negatives (TN); and false positives (FP), false negatives (FN). Finally, at the end of the tenfold crossvalidation, the different values are summed up to calculate a global table. Based on the global table, the performance metrics accuracy, precision, recall, and $F _ { 1 }$ [27, 38, 55] are calculated. They are defined as follows:

$$
A c c u r a c y = \frac {T P + T N}{T P + F P + T N + F N}\tag{1}
$$

$$
R e c a l l = \frac {T P}{T P + F N}\tag{2}
$$

$$
P r e c i s i o n = \frac {T P}{T P + F P}\tag{3}
$$

$$
F _ {1} = 2 \cdot \frac {\text { Precision } \cdot \text { Recall }}{\text { Precision } + \text { Recall }}\tag{4}
$$

## Economic Evaluation Methodology

To provide insights into the economic relevance of the proposed classifiers, we perform an additional economic evaluation. This is especially important because previous research has outlined that economic relevance can be shown best by an economic evaluation rather than by means of classical machine learning evaluation metrics [24]. Therefore, we construct a novel evaluation methodology that takes into account the specific characteristics of crowdfunding projects.

To assess the economic value of the project, we focus on the project’s pledging goal. In case of a nonfraudulent project that is successfully financed, an economic gain of the amount of funding can be assumed as the project founder can produce or provide the promised product or service. In case of a fraudulent project, the funders support the project but no actual product or service is delivered. Thus, an economic loss in the amount of the funding goal occurs. If a deception detection mechanism is in place, depending on the different classification outcomes of a project, several cases can be considered: (1) the project is classified correctly: if the project is fraudulent, an economic loss in the amount of the pledging goal is prevented (and can thus be considered an economic gain), whereas in the case of a nonfraudulent project, an economic gain occurs in the amount of the pledging goal as the promised product or service can actually be delivered; (2) the project is classified incorrectly: an economic loss occurs if the project is fraudulent but not detected or if the project is nonfraudulent but considered fraudulent (and consequently, the founder does not receive the funding). Based on these considerations, we calculate the economic value (v) for a project by the economic gain or economic loss resulting from the project classification (c), whereas c can either be correct (TP, TN) or incorrect (FP, FN). If there is an economic gain, we assume a positive economic value in the amount of the pledging goal (p), whereas in case of an economic loss, we assume a negative economic value in the amount of the pledging goal (–p). This is also outlined in Equation (5):

$$
v = \left\{ \begin{array}{l} p \text {   if   } c \in \{T P, T N \} \\ - p \text {   if   } c \in \{F P, F N \} \end{array} \right.\tag{5}
$$

To compare the performance of different classifiers, we compare their median economic value. In order to avoid overfitting, we again rely on the classifications of the test data sets during tenfold stratified cross-validation. By using the median, we ensure that projects with a particularly high or low pledging goal do not excessively influence the measured economic value.

According to the crowdfunding platform’s rules, the founder receives the money pledged if the pledging goal is reached at the end of the funding period. Otherwise, the founder does not receive the funding, which in turn does not lead to economic gains (in case of successful funding and nonfraudulent projects) or economic losses (in case of successful funding and fraudulent projects). Due to simplicity and because the exact number of projects funded successfully cannot be determined properly, we assume for fraudulent and nonfraudulent projects that every project will reach the funding goal. Nevertheless, the results from the economic evaluation remain stable if we only assume a certain number of projects to be successfully funded and thus to have an economic impact: if we make use of an appropriate discount factor (e.g., about 35 percent of projects funded successfully), each economic gain and economic loss is reduced accordingly and the results in comparison of the different projects remain robust.

Here, we take into account the economic value of the classifiers’ decisions as outlined in the research methodology section.

Table 13 shows the median economic value according to the different classifiers’ classifications. ## Discussion

Our results show that deception detection in the field of crowdfunding can be adequately addressed by the proposed classifiers. Already the baseline approaches taking into account only structured project or user data achieve a good classification performance and their application causes a considerable economic value for the different participants on crowdfunding platforms.

These results also provide evidence that static and dynamic communication should be analyzed for detecting fraud in the field of crowdfunding platforms. Therefore, content-based and linguistic cues extracted from static and dynamic contexts are valuable as they provide good classification results (RQ1, RQ2). Nevertheless, classifiers relying on single sources do not necessarily outperform the baseline approaches. As shown by both machine learning and economic evaluation, ensembles based on static and dynamic communication outperform the remaining approaches (RQ3), whereas a linguistic cues-based ensemble performs best.

To summarize, the results of our study clearly outline that a combined analysis of static and dynamic communication in the field of fraud detection adds value in contrast to basic fraud detection approaches. In comparison to the baseline classifiers, the economic value achieved by a classifier that encompasses both forms of communication is increased by a factor of almost than 2.5. Furthermore, the combined approach also outperforms the best classifier based on a single form of communication type by 50 percent. Consequently, future fraud detection approaches should especially consider the analysis of different communication sources.

The data set underlying this study has been balanced in order to deal with the problem of unequal class distribution of suspended and nonsuspended projects. The study results indicate that the developed classifiers clearly outperform the benchmark accuracy of 50 percent that would be achieved by a naive classifier classifying every project either as suspicious or nonsuspicious. To further investigate the classifier performance in case of the original unbalanced class distribution, we also computed the projected performance metrics considering the class distribution with 0.74 percent suspicious projects. Here, the number of nonsuspended projects is much larger than the number of suspended projects. As such, for the naive classifier classifying every project as belonging to the majority class, that is, nonsuspicious, accuracy is higher than 99 percent and recall is 0 percent. In the context of fraud detection, due to higher damaging costs that may be caused by fraudulent projects, it is important to ensure the identification of as many fraudulent projects as practically possible, even if it means incorrectly flagging and investigating some actually benign projects. This implies that recall is more critical than precision and accuracy when considering the actual class distribution. Furthermore, recall remains unchanged whether the proposed classifiers are evaluated with respect to the actual unbalanced data set or the balanced data set. Considering this perspective, recall is observed to be very high for the proposed classifiers as compared to the naive approach. It should also be noted that depending on the specific crowdfunding platform and considering that the number of false positives increases due to large number of nonsuspended projects, further adjustments of the trained models by means of cost-based learning might be appropriate (e.g., to take into account the specific costs of manually inspecting projects identified as suspicious). Regardless, taking into account linguistic and content-based cues from static and dynamic communication still adds value in this context.

We are also aware of several limitations of this study. For determining cases of fraudulent behavior, we rely on projects published on Kickstarter that have been manually discovered and suspended by their integrity team. As such, our sample does not cover projects that are fraudulent and not manually identified by the Kickstarter staff. Nevertheless, our current results show that fraudulent behavior on crowdfunding platforms can be well detected. In addition, if further fraudulent behavior that has not been detected by Kickstarter becomes public in the future due to legal prosecution (e.g., if a founder fails to deliver the promised rewards), the classifiers can be adapted by training on updated data taking such cases into account.

Related to the economic evaluation, we acknowledge that the current setup might not fully reflect the actual economic behavior on the crowdfunding platform since we assume each project to be successfully funded and calculate the economic impact on that basis. We also considered the possibility of taking into account a certain degree of success. In that case, the amount of the pledging goal to be realized as economic success or loss would have to be adjusted. Nevertheless, this would have equally reduced the economic impact and loss that occurred and thus would not affect the general results of this study. Furthermore, it cannot be said for sure ex ante which fraudulent projects will be funded successfully and which projects will not be funded (and would therefore not lead to an economic loss).

Finally, as also in cases related to other fraud detection mechanisms, fraudulent users might adapt their behavior to avoid being detected by the proposed deception detection mechanisms. Nevertheless, as the proposed approach covers a variety of cues and focuses on static and dynamic communication simultaneously, countermeasures are envisioned to be complicated.

## Conclusion

Crowdfunding platforms have become popular for publishing project ideas and for collecting funding so that the published projects can be realized. With the increasing popularity of such platforms, the risk of fraudulent behavior rises as well. This especially holds true because crowdfunding platforms are characterized by spatial as well as temporal separation of users, a high level of anonymity, and projects that deal with not yet finished products.

With this study, we contribute to the literature on crowdfunding by focusing on the detection of fraudulent behavior on crowdfunding platforms. In contrast to previous studies that consider mainly the question of which factors lead to a successful funding of projects, we address the so far unaddressed challenge of using automated deception detection techniques to identify fraudulent projects. Building on different theories, we derive different linguistic and content-based cues that are used as input for various fraud detection classifiers. The best-performing classifier is able to detect fraudulent projects with an accuracy of almost 80 percent.

This study also contributes to the literature on fraud detection in general by showing the value of a concurrent analysis of static and dynamic communication. Whereas previous research mainly incorporates either static or dynamic communication alone, building on CET, IDT, and IAT theories, we show that an approach building on both types of communication is superior. Finally, we provide an evaluation methodology for fraud detection classifiers to outline the economic value resulting from their classifications. This methodology can also be adapted to provide further evidence on the usefulness of such fraud detection approaches.

From a practical perspective, our results are highly relevant for the various stakeholders on crowdfunding platforms. We have shown that the application of the proposed deception detection mechanisms leads to a significant economic value for the platform participants. Especially for (potential) project funders, these deception detection mechanisms reduce the risk of being deceived and can thus be expected (besides reducing losses in the case of fraudulent projects) to also increase users’ level of trust and in turn the willingness to fund projects published on the platform. Further, founders of legitimate crowdfunding projects profit from the application of the proposed deception detection mechanisms as their projects receive more attention when fraudulent projects that otherwise pretend high interest are suspended and are thus not competing for attention on the platform. In addition, integrity teams on crowdfunding platforms are offered a decision aid in regard to which projects to concentrate on for manual investigation. This reduces information overload and can therefore allow for better measures against fraudsters on crowdfunding platforms. The proposed fraud detection mechanisms are also advantageous for platform operators insofar as increased trust toward the platform might also increase user activity and therefore the revenue generated in the case of successful project funding.

The analysis of static and dynamic communication can also be transferred to other fields of fraud detection. Especially in contexts where dynamic communication plays a major role next to static communication, the analysis of both types of communication might be promising. For instance, in social commerce, platforms like etsy. com enable product manufacturers to present their products and offer dynamic communication features to their users. Here, the proposed techniques could be applied to identify fraudulent behavior and to add value for the platform’s participants.
