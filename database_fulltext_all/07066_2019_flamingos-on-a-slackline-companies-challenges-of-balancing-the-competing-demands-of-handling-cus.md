# Flamingos on a slackline: Companies' challenges of balancing the competing demands of handling customer information and privacy

## Abstract

Providers of online services are under increasing pressure to leverage the value inherent in customer data to remain competitive. At the same time, however, Internet users' privacy has become more and more subject to public debate, and companies must protect this privacy if they want to attract and retain customers. It seems that companies struggle to satisfy these competing demands, and neglecting either could have detrimental effects on their success. Different streams of research underline the importance of either privacy protection or the collection and use of customer information. But they remain largely silent about the resulting challenges that companies face. In this study, we address this gap by exploring how companies perceive and handle the tensions between their organizational information needs that necessitate some degree of privacy intrusion and their need to attract and retain customers, which requires privacy protection. We develop a grounded theory that explains how companies balance these opposing demands from an organizational perspective. More specifically, we identify four challenging tensions that define the conditions under which companies must operate. We find that companies try to achieve and maintain a state of balance in the presence of these tensions, and we discover three categories of tactics that help companies in their balancing acts. Our research contributes to a provider‐centric perspective on information privacy and uncovers the management of customer information and privacy as a new but important context in which organizational ambidexterity is required. We offer a detailed perspective on the specific challenges that companies face with respect to customer information and privacy, and our results can guide managers who have to deal with these challenges.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

In recent years, companies who offer online services have recognized the value of customer data as a key resource for their businesses. Indeed, it is often noted that customer information can present a major competitive advantage (eg, Detwiler, 2015, March 2; Iyer & Subramaniam, 2015, April 13). For instance, these providers (ie, companies that provide online services) might leverage their customers' data to make personalized offerings and therefore enhance retention rates. At the same time, the collection and use of such data can entail negative consequences for companies, as customers become increasingly aware about the potential for privacy intrusion that is associated with the massive collection and use of their data (eg, Lowry, Dinev, & Willison, 2017). This double‐edged nature of collecting and using customer data poses significant challenges to businesses that do not want to lose their competitive edge by disregarding the value of their customers' data but must, at the same time, refrain from extensive collection and use in order not to lose customers due to privacy‐related reasons. Consider Facebook's privacy scandal in 2018 as an example for these challenges, which was enabled by rather permissive options for third‐party developers to acquire personal information over the network. As a result of this scandal, the company is reportedly fighting losses in reputation, user engagement, and stock value (eg, Duggan, 2018, March 26). Similarly, Apple is experiencing difficulty keeping up with Amazon and Google with respect to intelligent assistants like “Siri” because the company's information privacy priorities restrict enhancements based on customer data (Hardwick, 2017, June 8).

Little research on how companies experience and handle the challenges associated with customer information and privacy has been conducted. From the perspective of extant privacy research, the majority of studies has a consumer‐centric focus and thus highlights the importance of privacy protection (eg, Hui, Teo, & Lee, 2007; Krasnova Spiekermann Koroleva & Hildebrand 2010: Tsai Egelman, Cranor, & Acguisti 2011) But companies do not seem to strictly follow these recommendations (eg, Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Lee, Ahn, & Bang, 2011; Wall, Lowry, & Barlow, 2016). Indeed, organizational theory and marketing research as well as literature on business intelligence and analytics emphasizes organizations' needs to collect and use information (eg, Chen, Chiang, & Storey, 2012; Choo, 1996; Huber, 1990; Moorman, 1995). But these literatures do not address the privacy‐related challenges associated with the fulfilment of such organizational information needs. Overall, and as we will argue i more detail below, the current literature does not adeguately address how companies experience and handle the challenging and competing demands with respect to customer information and privacy.

To summarize, the inherent link between the handling of customer information and individuals' privacy implies significant challenges for companies. On the one hand, catering to companies' needs of collecting and utilizing custome information is associated with a certain extent of privacy intrusion, which, in turn, scares away (potential) customers. Protecting customers' privacy, on the other hand, by collecting and using less personal information might be beneficia in terms of attracting customers, but it neglects the companies' information needs. We lack an understanding of how companies experience and handle these tensions around the collection and use of personal information and privacy to achieve their organizational goals. Against this background, we investigate the following research question:

How do companies perceive and handle the tensions between their need to intrude customer privacy to address organizational information needs and their need to protect privacy to attract and retain customers?

To answer this question, we conduct a grounded theory study based on qualitative data obtained from providers of online services that collect and use customer information. We consult ambidexterity research as a meta‐perspective that is concerned with organizations' abilities to pursue two disparate things at the same time (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw 2004). This meta‐theoretical perspective helps us theorize the privacy‐related challenges of balancing an organization's information needs and the need to attract and retain customers. Based on our analysis, we discover four contextspecific tensions that define the conditions under which companies that handle customer information must operate We consider a tension to be a relationship between opposing forces that leads to conflicting implications (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Kreiner, Hollensbe, & Sheep, 2006; Larson & Pepper, 2003), and our discovery includes four suc tensions, which we describe in detail below: (1) trading off data against customers, (2) timing the problem, (3) image related costs of customer data, and (4) losing customers due to data utilization. Our results suggest that, given these tensions and additional contextual pressures that companies experience, they must try to achieve and maintain a balance between the competing demands. We identify three categories of tactics (ie, ways of dealing with the tensions and their associated challenges) that can help to maintain or restore this balance, in case of a disturbance: (1) surrogate tactics, (2) transparency tactics, and (3) segmentation tactics. Overall, our research contributes to our understanding of how companies experience and handle the competing demands related to customer information and privacy. It can serve as a foundation for much‐needed future studies dedicated to better understand how organizations can address the diverging needs of companies and customers with respect to individuals' personal information

In the next section, we provide the theoretical background for our research by discussing the importance of information for organizations and offering an overview of organization‐centric information privacy research Following that, we introduce grounded theory as our research methodology and explain how we followed the principles of this approach. We then present and theorize the results of our study. Finally, we conclude by integrating ou findings with existing research and discussing the implications of our findings as well as future research opportunities

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Organizational information needs

Like other companies, providers of online services can be understood as organizations that create value, interact with their environment, and offer products or services to their customers. The fundamental need of such organizations, to acquire and utilize information coming from outside the boundaries of the company, has been emphasized in many organizational studies (eg, Choo, 1996; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Feldman & March, 1981). At an abstract level, organizations can be viewed as information‐processing systems that need information to operate in uncertain environments and make them more predictable (eg, Choo, 1996). More specifically, organizations need to gathe and utilize information to improve the speed and quality of their decisions (eg, Huber, 1990; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012), make sense of their environment (eg, Choo, 1996; Weick, 1995), develop innovative products (eg, Choo, 1996; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), engage in marketing practices (eg, Moorman, 1995), and ideally create a competitive advantage (eg, Evans & Wurster, 1997; Porter & Millar, 1985). This perspective of organizations as informationprocessing systems is fundamental for business intelligence and analytics research, which strives to improve the information‐processing capabilities of organizational members to make better decisions (eg, Chen et al., 2012; Davenport, 2010; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012).

However, what is missing from these studies is an understanding of how companies experience and handle the privacy‐related challenges associated with the fulfilment of these information needs, given the inherent link between the collection and use of personal information and customers' privacy. This link is well documented in the information privacy literature. But, as we will argue below, this literature has paid little attention to companies' competing demands and tensions around customer information as well.

## 2.2 | Organization‐centric research on information privacy

The importance of information privacy as a research topic is reflected by the large amount of literature in the field (eg, Acquisti, John, & Loewenstein, 2013; Cavusoglu, Phan, Cavusoglu, & Airoldi, 2016; Keith, Babb, Lowry, Furner, & Abdullat, 2015; Lowry, Cao, & Everard, 2011; Xu, Dinev, Smith, & Hart, 2011). Studies in this field have often defined privacy as the “the ability of individuals to control the terms under which their personal information is acquired and used” (Culnan & Bies, 2003, p. 326)—a definition that we also adopt in our research. Given that information asymmetries between a company and its customers exist, the company's collection and use of customer information can be seen as an intrusion into individuals' privacy since the latter give up control over their data to a certain extent when these data are accessible and usable by somebody else, other than the individuals. Thus protecting privacy as a company not only involves the proper handling of data already collected but also includes refraining from the collection of customer information in the first place (eg, H. J. Smith, Milberg, & Burke, 1996).

As mentioned earlier, the great majority of the information privacy literature has taken a consumer‐centric perspective that is based on surveys or experiments involving customers of online services. As a result, these studies often recommend that companies protect privacy by reducing their data collection, engaging in fair information practices, and actively communicating their privacy‐friendly behaviour (eg, Hui et al., 2007; Krasnova et al., 2010; Pavlou, Huigang, & Yajiong, 2007; Tsai et al., 2011). Despite their valuable contributions, the results of consumer‐centric studies cannot simply be transferred to managerial practice, as they represent only one side o the coin, neglecting the centrality of organizational information needs such as those mentioned above

More than two decades ago, Jeff Smith (1993) noted the lack of research concerned with an organizationa perspective on companies' privacy‐related practices, and more than 10 years later, Mary Culnan made the same observation with more drastic wording: “Privacy is an organizational issue that is grossly under‐researched” (Chan et al., 2005, p. 272). It is surprising that since then, still only a handful of studies have addressed this research need (eg, Greenaway, Chan, & Crossler, 2015; Lee et al., 2011; Parks, Xu, Chu, & Lowry, 2017; Wall et al., 2016). The few studies that do consider the organizational perspective on information privacy are summarized in Table 1. A common denominator of these studies is that dealing with customer information and protecting privacy is associated with significant challenges for companies.

One important area that requires further investigation is presented by companies' challenge of collecting and using personal information to address organizational needs for information, on the one hand, and the need to protec individuals' privacy to attract and retain customers on the other. Indeed, Bélanger and Crossler (2011, p. 1029) emphasize that privacy researchers need to start taking companies' own demands and requirements with regard to customer information into account and. “should explore whether organizations have different stakes in information privacy than the consumers they serve.” To date, two studies have addressed this particular challenge (Greenaway et al., 2015; Greenaway & Chan, 2005). Greenaway and Chan (2005) suggest that the resource‐based view can explain why companies would emphasize one need over the other. In particular, they argue that companies that seek competitive advantage through superior knowledge about their customers will subordinate privacy protection in order to collect and use as much customer information as possible. In contrast, companies that seek a competitive advantage through superior trustworthiness will subordinate the use of customer information and protect customers privacy. Greenaway et al. (2015, p. 580) derive four categories of companies that differ in the level of privacy they offer to their customers in order to help understand how companies position themselves along the continuum of ignoring or protecting privacy.

## 3 | METHODOLOGY

As outlined earlier, the extant literature provides little insight with respect to an underresearched question: How do providers of online services perceive and handle the tensions between their need to intrude customer privacy to address organizational information needs and their need to protect privacy to attract and retain customers? In such contexts, where existing theoretical perspectives are largely missing, a grounded theory study can be an effective way of exploring an emerging research area. Grounded theory is an inductive research approach that is oriented towards theory building (as opposed to theory testing) and allows researchers to avoid taking an a priori theoretica stance that might predetermine the set of possible outcomes. It allows an open‐minded exploration of a phenomenon, avoids force‐fitting data into predefined theory, and lets meaningful concepts emerge from the data (eg, Birks, Fernández, Levina, & Nasirin, 2013; Charmaz, 2014; Urquhart & Fernández, 2013). In IS research, grounded theory is enjoying increasing popularity and has been applied in the contexts of IS outsourcing projects (Gregory, Beck, & Keil. 2013), enterprise systems (Strong & Volkoff, 2010), sustainable cities (Corbett & Mellouli. 2017), ano many others. In the information privacy domain, a recent study used a grounded theory approach to explore intended and unintended consequences of privacy safeguards (Parks et al., 2017).

At a highly abstract level, grounded theory research proceeds as follows (eg, Birks et al., 2013; Charmaz, 2014; Urquhart, Lehmann, & Myers, 2010). After selecting an area of enquiry and defining the topic, the researcher begin an iterative process that alternates between data collection and analysis. Thereby, all data collection is guided by theoretical sampling: When iterating between data collection and analysis, the researcher decides on theoretica grounds where to sample from next and what to sample. The aim is to obtain data that answer the theoretica questions that have arisen at a given point during the research process (eg, Birks et al., 2013; Urquhart & Fernández, 2013). During the constant comparison and coding of the data while proceeding with theoretical sampling, themes and categories for the data emerge and are gradually abstracted to a conceptual level. Throughout this entire process, the researcher engages in extensive memo writing to reflect on the emerging concepts and theory (Urquhart et al., 2010). At the point where saturation occurs (ie, no new concepts or relationships between them emerge), the researcher ends data collection and reduces the emergent concepts and relationships to a meaningful core—the grounded theory. This theory must then be related to existing theories, in order to contribute to theoretica integration in the discipline. The result is a grounded theory that is firmly rooted in the data, fits everyday reality, and is interesting because its ideas have “earned” their way into the theory (Glaser, 1978, p. 8). In the following subsections, we provide details on our data collection and analysis

Before proceeding, we briefly note that there are two strands of grounded theory research resulting from a disagreement between its two cofounders over the “right way” to code data during a study. We do not want to go into detail here, as excellent summaries of this debate exist elsewhere (eg, Seidel & Urquhart, 2013; Urquhart et al., 2010). Instead, we follow Birks et al. (2013), who advocate that researchers should not be overly concerned with the type of grounded theory adopted for a given piece of research, as this only distracts from elements that are common to both strands and that are “far more important” than the dissimilarities (p. 4). Still, to avoid any misunderstandings, we point out that our research is more oriented towards the Glaserian strand, as we did not use axial coding or the paradigm model,<sup>1</sup> which can be seen as important elements of the Straussian strand (eg, Seide & Urquhart, 2013; Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

## 3.1 | Data collection

We collected data from online service providers that acquired and used personal information from their customers; semistructured interviews served as our main source of data. Given that our research required firm representatives to talk about what can be viewed as a delicate topic, we chose not to approach random companies for participation Instead, we contacted companies through our personal networks, which gave us access to either board‐leve executives or product managers for the services. This approach ensured a certain level of trust existed between the company representatives and us, which increased our confidence in the honesty of the answers they provided

Although the difficulties associated with gathering data from organizational participants on the topic of informatio privacy have been noted (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011), we managed to obtain a diverse sample of 23 different companies that participated in our study.

Our data collection was guided by the principle of theoretical sampling (eg, Birks et al., 2013). We adhered to this principle in two different ways. First, we varied our sampling between different companies and services to explore whether different company or service characteristics would change the way we were thinking about our emerging theory. For instance, we obtained data from companies of different sizes, different domains of business, and services that were provided either through an Internet website or through a mobile app. Second, we used questions inspire by earlier data to guide our data collection in later stages of our research. For example, in the early stages, our dat collection was mainly directed at exploring the tensions reported by companies when managing customer information. Later, after we gained a deeper understanding of these tensions, our emphasis shifted towards how the providers managed these tensions and tried to maintain a balance in this context. Accordingly, our interview questions became more focused and structured (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Orlikowski, 1993). We ended our sampling at the point of saturation, when no new insights emerged from additional data collection and analysis.

Overall, we conducted 27 semistructured interviews with representatives of the 23 companies. Some of the interviews were follow‐ups that aimed to enrich data collected earlier with new perspectives from subsequent iterations of data analysis. Interviews are the primary source of data in many grounded theory studies in IS research (eg, Corbett & Mellouli, 2017; Gregory, Keil, Muntermann, & Mähring, 2015; Urquhart et al., 2010), and they afforded us strong control over the conversation. As mentioned above, the providers' informants were managers who were responsible for the firms' services (ie, product managers for larger companies and CEOs in the case of start‐ups or medium‐sized firms). Consequently, our interviewees were involved in major decisions about the services and thus were able to provide detailed answers about the collection and use of customer data and the privacy‐related challenges associated with it. All interviews started with a brief introduction (eg, an introduction to ourselves and to the interview process, as well as a confidentiality assurance), during which we also elaborated on our interest in the topic of information privacy from an organizational point of view and what we meant by that. The interviews followed an interview guide (see Appendix A) that evolved over the course of the project. On average, we interviewed each company's representative for about 48 minutes, and all interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. Overall, this process resulted in more than 18 hours of interview material and about 320 pages of single‐spaced text. To triangulate participants' statements and verify their contents, we always relied on secondary data sources by closely examining the services in question, such as looking at the customer information that was demanded or analysing the services' privacy policies.

Our final sample presents 23 diverse companies offering a wide range of services. For instance, the companies differed in size, with some being small start-ups and others multinational corporations. The services' user bases ranged from a few thousand to several million users per month. The services were conducted in different domain (eg, e‐commerce, health, and mobile games). The firms requested a broad range of information from their customers ranging from demographic to location data, shopping data, and ID card scans. An overview of the characteristics of our sample is provided in Appendix B.

## 3.2 | Data analysis, coding, and memo writing

Our data analysis was based on the coding procedures described by Glaser (1978), We used the software package Atlas.ti to support and organize these procedures. Although three coding stages are described separately here, we note that they were inseparable in practice as we moved back and forth between the stages (Glaser, 1978). By constantly comparing different slices of data, we identified similarities and differences in our interviewees' responses, refined previous codes, and began to create categories to describe observations that were related to each other. In this way, we gradually raised the level of abstraction. Throughout the entire coding process, we also engaged in extensive memo writing. Beginning with the early stages, we wrote memos describing our ideas for emerging concepts. Later, we wrote memos to stimulate our thinking about the relationships among emerging categories. For instance, we created multiple diagrams that illustrated the potential relationships among categories. We reread and analysed the memos many times during the data analysis to make sense of our observations

The first level of coding was an open coding of our data to identify common themes and make sense of our observations (Glaser, 1978). To this end, we analysed our data line by line, attaching conceptual labels to meaningful units of text at the phrase, sentence, half‐sentence, and word levels. To ensure that the coding results were based on multiple perspectives, the coding process was accompanied by intense discussions between the authors. These discussions stimulated different ways of thinking about the data. As a consequence, previous coding results were often revisited and coded afresh. Based on our open coding, we engaged in selective coding to group emergent codes, focus on a core phenomenon, and only code data that were sufficiently related to this phenomenon (Glaser, 1978). Given our research objectives, we selected the tensions that providers faced and the balance they tried to achieve as core phenomena for our study. We focused on and coded around these phenomena and identified several categories that were of interest for studies that investigate how providers handle customer information. For instance, we identified several tactics that providers used to maintain or return to a state of balance if it had been disturbed. As a last stage of coding, we used theoretical coding to identify relationships among the major categories that had emerged from our selective coding (Glaser, 1978). This resulted in a theory that explains how providers negotiate certain tensions that we discovered by trying to achieve a balance and how outside and inside pressures as well as companies' behaviours affect this balance

Overall, constant comparisons, iterations between data collection and analysis, and extensive memo‐writing led us to our final theory, which we present next. While building this theory, we strictly adhered to the principles of grounded theory during the entire research process. ## 4 | STUDY RESULTS

In this section, we present a theory that explains how companies experience and handle the competing needs to intrude privacy to address information needs on the one side and the need to protect customers' privacy to attract and retain customers on the other. Below, we first report our discovery of different areas of tension that resulted from this overarching challenge. These tensions defined challenging conditions that influenced companies in their handling of customer information. We further discuss that companies need to achieve a balance against the back ground of these tensions but that creating and sustaining such a balance presents a challenge in itself due to interna and external pressures. We then report our observations of various tactics the companies employed to restore or proactively maintain balance.

## 4.1 | Providers' tensions related to the collection and use of customer data

We discovered four different tensions that were related to the collection and use of customer data. As has been done in prior research that has studied overarching competing demands experienced by organizations (eg, Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; W. K. Smith & Tushman, 2005), we distinguished between these four nested tensions, as they concerned different operational practices or issues according to the participants. Figure 1 illustrates the four tension that defined the context in which the companies had to operate.

## 4.1.1 | Tension 1: trading off data against customers

A maior tension experienced by the providers resulted from the companies' need to collect customer data which would compete with the need to win and retain customers. On the one hand, acquiring customer data was a prerequisite for addressing many organizational demands. For instance, collecting more information about customers could lead to an increase in the overall service quality, greater effectiveness of marketing efforts, or guide strategic decisions. On the other hand, collecting more information could also scare customers away. ## 4.1.2 | Tension 2: timing the problem

A different tension was based on the question of whether data that would not be used at the present time but might be needed in the future should be collected now or at a later point in time. A provider of a fitness service who decided not to collect data for future purposes at the present time reported (S#05): “For us, the discussion was whether to request this information now (…) or decide against it. It's always better for your conversion if you request less information, especially if you don't make use of it [today].”

The collection of data at the present time could have the same negative consequences as described for the previou tension: scaring away customers who are concerned about the amount of data collected. But organizations need to pla ahead, and certain data that will not be used today might become necessary in the future, as the next quote illustrates:

S#15: You don't want to request all sorts of things right at the beginning to avoid losing them [customers] in the process of signing up. We want to have that hurdle as low as possible. (…) But you need these data at some point for certain things.

However, acquiring permission at a later time to collect these data items would also have negative effects because existing customers must be notified about changes in privacy policies or mobile app permissions. This could ver explicitly draw customers' attention to the data collection and cause them to abandon the service. A provider of mobile news apps explained the difficulty of requesting additional data at a later point in time (S#03): “Every time you request more [mobile app permissions], you need the users' confirmation, for example, for contact data or push notifications. And each step, you'll probably lose 15% of the users.” Thus, companies face the tension between asking for data today and postponing this until a later point in time. Collecting data now or in the future seemed to be a difficult choice to make.

## 4.1.3 | Tension 3: image‐related costs of customer data

Companies were also required to look beyond the immediate impacts their practices would have on the success of their services. Companies such as those who offered more than one service or who offered a mobile app or website to complement their core business had to be conscious of the spillover effects their dealings with customer dat could have on their company or brand image. Collecting more customer data or using data for additional purposes could come at the cost of damaging this image—for instance, this could happen if a brand or company name was asso ciated with trust and responsibility. In this regard, a provider of an e‐commerce service warned how collecting to much data could damage the company's image (S#21): “Yes, we at [large e‐commerce service] fear that our image wil get hurt [by collecting too much data]. That's why we're really careful in that regard.”

Likewise, a company that offered an online shop in addition to its brick‐and‐mortar stores was highly concerned about maintaining its positive brand image, given the brand's popularity in the offline channel

Our brand has existed for 30 years now (…) and it would be devastating if customers think (…) “[the company is] just like the big ones that collect and collect [data].” (…) That would be a huge problem for us, so we definitely have that in mind.

Interviewer: Is that a discussion? That the collection and use of [customer] data could be a risk to the company's image?

S#14: (…) That's a particular challenge for us, since we're a fully owned subsidiary of [name of the parent company].

But again, not collecting or using data would mean that companies would have to neglect their own needs for better decision making and marketing, among other things, leaving them with the challenge of trading off these needs against their company or brand image

## 4.1.4 | Tension 4: losing customers due to data utilization

The fourth tension was associated with how a provider can use previously collected customer data. This tension is conceptually different from the first tension (which is associated with the issue of information acquisition). as it con: cerns the utilization and processing of information that has already been acquired (eg, Culnan, 1993; Kettinger & Marchand, 2011; Moorman, 1995). The companies often felt restricted in the ways they could use data already available to them. Yet they were pressured to keep up with the competition and to learn more about their customers to increase efficiency, and therefore, they had to maximize the value of data they already possessed. After collecting data about their customers, providers' options seemed manifold. But increasing the variety of purposes for using the data could alienate customers who felt their privacy intruded upon. One provider of a news service had agreed with a publishing company that personal information of those customers who would consent to this practice would be transferred to this third party. But this additional way of using personal information would come at the cost of losing customers who would not agree to this practice:

S#03: We have started a new project with a publisher where we need to transfer user information at the end of the project. So that's part of the contract. In this case, we need to update the user agreement and the users have to agree. Unfortunately, we will probably lose those [customers] who don't agree.

Talking to companies helped us understand that some of the customers' privacy had to be sacrificed to allow providers to run their business. Therefore, complete privacy protection for every customer was usually not considered a realistic aim from the providers' perspective. A provider of a fitness service explained the need to work wit third‐party companies to generate revenue:

S#09: We would do it [personalized advertising], just without transferring any data. In our opinion, people need to understand that we need to earn money. People want to use it [the service] for free, but eventually the money has to come from somewhere.

Due to the negative consequences of customers' perceived privacy risks, this tension forced the companies to carefully think about whether different ways of using customers' data would actually benefit the providers in the long run. Changes to a service that were based on an additional use of customer information could entail customer losses (S#22): “If we do something [based on customer data] that's problematic within the app, then we often observe that the apps get uninstalled.”

Additional quotes for all four tensions presented in this section can be found in Table D1. Overall, these tension were perceived as highly challenging, and they defined the context in which companies had to operate and make decisions.

## 4.2 | Ambidexterity as an emergent, meta‐theoretical lens

The tensions described above presented contextual conditions under which providers had to navigate in an ever changing environment. In these circumstances, the providers were left with the challenging task of trying to juggle these competing demands. Acting in one way would go hand in hand with neglecting the other. Based on these insights, we adopted ambidexterity theory as a meta‐theoretical lens that helped us to support and enrich our findings. As is often the case with grounded theory studies, this perspective emerged as relevant throughout th research process (eg, Urquhart & Fernández, 2013)

Organizational ambidexterity refers broadly to “an organization's ability to pursue two disparate things at the same time” (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004, p. 210). Research on organizational ambidexterity recognizes that demands on organizations always conflict to some degree and that, as a result, trade‐offs need to be made (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). Examples of such conflicts include those between efficiency and flexibility (eg, Adler, Goldoftas, & Levine, 1999), exploration and exploitation (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; March, 1991), and differentiation and low‐cost strategies (Porter, 1996). In our study, the “two disparate things” were a company's need to intrude privacy to address its information needs and the need to protect privacy to attract an retain customers.

In order to reconcile competing demands such as these, organizations have to find a way to manage the opposing forces, and the ideal outcome has been described as balance (eg, March, 1991; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008; Ramesh, Mohan, & Cao, 2012). Although trade‐offs can never be fully eliminated, the achievement of balance has been linked to superior performance and long‐term competitiveness (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; March, 1991; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). This balance‐perspective aligned well with our empirical observations, underlining the appropriateness of ambidexterity as a meta‐theoretical lens. For instance, a manager of a news service told us (S#22): “We always try to navigate and sometimes you steer a little bit in one direction, sometimes in the other [direction], but in general, we try to find a balance there.” Others used similar expressions to refer to this need to balance competing demands, such as having a “healthy proportion” (S#14). “performing a split” (S#12) between the two opposing forces making sure “not to overdo things” (S#18), and finding a “happy medium” (S#21).

It is important to note that what constitutes an effectively balanced configuration of activities depends on context and situation (Auh & Menguc, 2005; Cao, Gedajlovic, & Zhang, 2009; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). For instance, the same exploratory or exploitative actions can vary in their effectiveness depending on the environment in which a company operates (Auh & Menguc, 2005). Likewise, the effects of balance or imbalance between competing demands on organizational outcomes can vary depending on contextual factors such as company size and environmental munificence (Cao et al., 2009). In a similar vein, two individuals can have different ideas about the conditions under which they perceive their work and home domain in balance (Kreiner et al., 2006). Thus “balance is dynamic and cannot only change over time, but can take different forms depending on the specifics of the situation” (Cardinal, Sitkin, & Long, 2004, p. 412). In our study, balance was perceived by each company individually as well. A state that one company perceived as balanced could be perceived as imbalanced by another

## 4.3 | Maintaining balance in a dynamic context

We will now report our observations as to how companies tried to balance the competing demands they faced and present tactics they used to maintain or regain balance if it had been disturbed. Figure 2 provides an overview of our findings in this regard. It shows how a company's balance could be disrupted by internal and external factors, which pressed it to change its practices regarding customer information and thus create imbalance. A decision to adopt a new practice of collecting or using information would entail some degree of privacy intrusion with the potential of custome or image loss. Rejecting a practice would protect customers' privacy but neglect the company's information needs. A middle course could serve as a compromise. Depending on the decisions made, companies employed three categories of tactics to restore or proactively maintain balance. Below, we will elaborate on each of these findings in detail

## 4.3.1 | Balance‐disturbing pressures and the need to maintain balance

In dynamic environments such as the companies', enduring states of balance are difficult to create and sustain because events and pressures can disrupt these states and lead to imbalance (eg, Cardinal et al., 2004; Gregory et al., 2013: Raisch. Birkinshaw. Probst, & Tushman, 2009), In our case, states of balance were often disrupted by decisions about the collection or use of customer data that were triggered by pressures external or internal to a com pany. We observed several contextual factors that exerted balance‐disturbing pressures and affected companies decisions regarding certain information practices. Although it is beyond the scope of our research to offer a complete account of the mechanisms through which these factors operated, we want to provide a brief overview of those fac tors we observed in our data. Overall, we coded these factors into two high‐level categories—one that included factors external to the company and one associated with internal factors that were related to the company and its activities itself. Note that not every factor might be relevant in every context, and we do not seek to claim complete ness. We report those factors that emerged as salient in our sample of online services; these offer rich potential for future investigations, and our research can serve as a starting point.

First, we identified factors internal to a company or its service that could require consideration of a certain information practice: the company's goals, its available resources, the business value of the information practice, the sensitivity of the information involved, and the user experience of the service. With respect to companies' current goals some providers had clearly prioritized gaining a strong user base and therefore reduced their data collection efforts to a minimum. One participant summarized the need to consider the company's current goals (S#05): “I think you have to consider the situation your company is in and what its current goals are.” Similarly, companies' available resources often constrained the feasibility of certain practices, so they had to decide against their implementation (S#01): “That's a matter of priorities. Our resources are very scarce and we can't do everything at once.” Moreover, the business value of collecting or using a specific data item would play a central role in companies' decisions abou their practices. A participant from a company that offered a mobile news app reflected on a decision against collecting customers' email addresses (S#01): “That's when we thought, ‘Maybe it's not that important to have their email address but it's more important to gain their attention and have them try out our system.” Companies also had to consider the sensitivity of the information in question when making decisions about specific practices. An e‐commerce company considered product information in certain product categories as being more sensitive than others and therefore refrained from sending personalized offers including such information to customers (S#10): “That's why we differentiate between product categories and don't send out [personalized offers] for every product.” Finally, collecting or using customer information could affect the user experience of a service, another factor that could play a role in companies' considerations. The following quote underlines that requesting information could get annoying for customers at some point, leading to higher dropout rates (S#17): “Usability is important, and the dropout rates at that point where you request data. So if users say, ‘Now, that's the fifth page where I need to fill out forms’ (…).”

Second. factors external to a company could pressure them and lead to balance-disturbing decisions: externa threats, competition, the company's legal environment, and its customers' preferences. Eor example, external threats such as a risk of cyberattacks could influence whether a data item should be collected. One company in the health business decided against the collection of nonanonymized customer information (S#07): “We had to consider the potential danger of cyberattacks. (…) So we had to weigh the benefits against what could happen to us. And then we decided to anonymize the data. We only collect what we really need to allow for these analyses.” Providers also felt the need to keep pace with competitors and therefore explore new ways of acquiring and using customer information. One participant told us (S#12): “The Internet is so competitive, and the competition always increases but never sleeps. So you have to watch for new opportunities [for collecting and using customer information] and have regular meetings to see what's possible and what can be done.”

In addition, a company's legal environment could also constrain or require certain practices. Just prior to our interview with a provider of a financial service, a new law on money laundering came into effect and, as a conse quence, a powerful banking partner of the service demanded that it should request additional information from its customers. The following quote illustrates how this could disturb the balance the company tried to maintain (S#17): “That got a little out of balance. We had to implement the changes, since we were forced by our partner bank But we're working closely together with the steering committee, trying to find a solution and re‐establish balance.”

Finally, the customers' preferences in terms of privacy and information use could be heterogeneous and thus affect companies in their decisions. As the following quote shows, a heterogeneous customer base required the company to consider differentiated approaches in terms of data collection and use (S#22): “Half of them [customers] are ver skeptical and they gave us rather aggressive feedback, but then there are those people, about 30 percent, who sa ‘I can really imagine using that [personalized service]’.”

In all, the contextual factors listed above exerted pressures because they required decisions with respect to cus tomer information and privacy that could disturb companies' balance and left them with the challenge of dealing with the downsides of deciding for or against the collection or use of customer information. In turbulent and ever‐changing contexts such as these, the act of balancing becomes “recurrently and intentionally tuning and directing actions toward key objectives” (Gregory et al., 2013, p. 1225). Actors thus attempt to regain and maintain balance by engaging in balancing acts, which results in balance‐imbalance‐rebalance sequences (eg, Cardinal et al., 2004; Kreiner et al. 2006). To regain or maintain balance, providers in our study engaged in different tactics to alleviate the detrimenta effects of their decisions and practices. Below, we report on three broad categories of tactics we observed: surrogate tactics, transparency tactics, and segmentation tactics. These corresponded to the three possible outcomes when decisions had to be made about the collection and/or use of customer information: adopt a practice, reject it, or tak a middle course. In the following, we describe each category of tactics in detail. Additional quotes for each tactic can be found in Table D2.

## 4.3.2 | Surrogate tactics to compensate for neglected information needs

To counter the negative consequences of rejecting a certain practice (ie, neglecting organizational information needs) providers tended to engage in two tactics that we subsumed under the label of surrogate tactics. When deciding against the collection or use of personal information, surrogate tactics were aimed at compensating for the potentia benefits the companies would give up. We found two subforms of surrogate tactics in our data: using available dat and seeking a less intrusive alternative.

If a rejected practice was aimed at gaining certain insights about customers, some providers tried to pursue these aims anyway by relying on data that was already available to them and thus did not require the collection of new data. The available data could include items the companies already had collected, were already allowed to collect, or data that could be gathered through technical means without having to explicitly request information from customers. For instance, information offered by customers' web browsers could help to personalize offerings despit a lack of specific data:

S#13: For instance, it's relevant to know whether the user is using Internet Explorer 7 or the newest

version of Chrome. You can correlate this information and estimate how old a certain user is.

Eventually, every piece of information that is statistically valid is also relevant.

The second subtactic to compensate for neglected information needs was to find an alternative that was less intrusive regarding the customers' privacy. In certain contexts, the providers could think of ways to achieve their goals without collecting or using additional customer data or doing so to a lesser extent. The representative of an e‐commerce service offered an example of how the lack of customer data to create personalized newsletters coul be compensated by brainwork to find an alternative that did not rely on personal information

S#12: If I had the information “what's your favorite color?” then I could build my newsletter based on that [information]. One group gets a blue one and the others get green. But why? You could just as well sit down for a while and ask yourself how to design one [newsletter] that everybody likes. That's how you need to do it.

Overall, providers leaned towards surrogate tactics if they would decide against a certain practice, protect customers' privacy, and had to be creative to accomplish their original goals and address their organizational information needs.

## 4.3.3 | Transparency tactics to prevent customer or image loss

In the case where providers would decide to adopt a certain practice that involved the collection or use of customer information, they had to somehow deal with the negative consequences of intruding their customers' privacy (ie, customer or image loss). Thus, we observed companies varying the degree of transparency with which they communi cated the activities they engaged in. Specifically, they varied the extent to which they emphasized the “what, how and why” of their activities that involved customer information. Two different subforms of transparency tactic emerged: avoiding overemphasis (less transparency) and offering an explanation (more transparency)

Sometimes. companies decided to adopt certain practices that involved customer data but did not put much emphasis on them. For instance, the providers assumed that Internet users were already accustomed to a certain way of using or collecting their information, when this practice was also widely adopted by other companies. Further providers often felt that certain activities would not be something their customers would naturally think about. In these cases, the companies chose to avoid overemphasizing their activities in order to avoid drawing unnecessary attention to them. The provider of a news service reported having corrected the mistake of overemphasizing its pri vacy‐related activities:

S#22: We had placed the privacy policy very, very prominently within our app. As a consequence, a lot of

people read it, got scared, and uninstalled the app. (…) So we simply removed it. Users who are looking fo

it can still find it, but we really don't want to put it right in front of them.

This way, the company tried not to trigger customers to think about the ways in which data were used by the company and the information that was collected.

In other cases, providers would purposefully choose to adopt a certain data practice that would draw the atten tion of their customers. These could be activities that were rather unusual, that involved very sensitive data, or that were inherently visible, such as form fields requesting information on a website. As a consequence, companies had t prevent customers from abandoning the service because they perceived a risk for their privacy if they proceeded. We observed that, to counteract this perception, companies would try to reduce information asymmetries in their favour by communicating the “what,” “why,” and “how” of their handling of customer information. A provider of a financia service offered an example where the company had to collect more data but justified to its customers that lega requirements were the reason for this change:

S#17: We let our users know that we now need to request these additional data, even our existing users.

We tell them that this is based on legal requirements and that we don't just want more data to make a

In doing so, the companies hoped that customers would not rely on their own sense making and arrive at false conclusions.

So far, we have discussed tactics that seem to be an appropriate fit for clear decisions for or against a certain practice. But as mentioned earlier, providers often refrained from making such binary decisions if preferences and concerns seemed to vary across the population of potential customers.

## 4.3.4 | The middle course: segmentation tactics

Due to individual differences among their customers, simply refraining from collecting or using information was ofte not an obvious choice because providers felt that they would forgo opportunities to keep up with their competitior or offer attractive services. Just as setting a single price for a product or service can leave consumer surplus uncaptured due to different levels of willingness to pay, a one‐size‐fits‐all approach to companies' information prac tices might also lead to suboptimal outcomes. As a result, we observed companies engaging in tactics that were intended to address this issue. In particular, providers of online services attempted to segment their customers with respect to individual preferences regarding privacy. Analysing our data, we found three different types of segmenta tion tactics: voluntary contributions, functional versioning, and discrimination without self-selection

“Voluntary contributions” seemed to be a straightforward tactic. Customers were given a choice to provide a certain type of data or to agree with a certain practice on a voluntary basis. This way, each customer could freely choose based on his or her own preferences. Regarding an attempt to obtain more detailed information for customer profiles a provider of a customer loyalty service explained:

S#13: In addition, we ask our users during the onboarding process to select certain [online] shops relevant to them. (…) Then we know which kinds of shops they like. That's not irrelevant. But that's also nothing that is required. It's an optional step

In a similar fashion, providers offered their customers the option to receive newsletters and to create an account for using a service.

The subtactic of functional versioning followed a similar logic, but it incentivized customers to provide additional information or agree with additional usage purposes by adding more functionality to a service. As is often the case with premium versions of software products, for instance, customers were offered the option to pay more (ie, pro vide more information or agree to additional usage purposes) to receive additional functionality in return. The provider of a news service explained:

S#01: The user doesn't have to provide personal data to use the app. Of course, doing so has benefits. We provide several features that tempt users to subscribe with their email addresses. It's good to have email addresses to communicate directly with people.

Finally, some providers segmented their customers (or at least planned to do so) based on their own analyses and followed a discrimination tactic without self‐selection. In particular, companies tried to address individual differences among their customers based on their own assessments. As a result, certain practices could target only a subset of customers, namely, those who were considered to be open to these activities. Based on our observations, this tactic reguired precise statistical analyses to arrive at meaningful customer segments. One provider explained this mechanism as follows:

S#14: We're trying to segment [our customers]. We're trying to find out which users perceive that [newsletters] as beneficial, to interact with us, opening these emails. Right now, we have different test groups and the options are zero and one [do not/do send newsletters]. But in the future, we want to measure this much better and see who benefits from this practice and who doesn't—and then, of course, only send it to those who see an advantage.

These three segmentation tactics could provide an advantage if a one‐size‐fits‐all approach would have left significant benefits for the companies untapped. Interestingly, we observed that companies sometimes complemented their segmentation tactics by the simultaneous use of surrogate or transparency tactics. For instance the tactic of “voluntary contributions” could be complemented by the transparency tactic of “offering explanations” to encourage customers to provide their data voluntarily.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

In this study, we have presented a theory that explains how companies handling customer information balance the competing demands that result from their information needs on the one hand and their need to attract and retain customers on the other hand. Our theory constitutes what Gregor (2006, p. 624) labels a “Type II” theory (ie, a theory for explaining), which places “an emphasis on showing others how the world may be viewed in a certai way” and presents a common outcome of grounded theory studies in IS research (Seidel & Urquhart, 2013). In what follows, we integrate our theory with existing research and discuss its implications and future researc opportunities.

## 5.1 | Integration with existing research and theoretical contributions

At the highest level, our research makes a contribution by explaining how companies experience and handle the com peting demands related to customer information and privacy and therefore provides an “understanding of how things are or why they are as they are” (Gregor, 2006, p. 624). Despite the abundance of information privacy research and the significance of the topic, this issue is still poorly understood (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). We have explored companies' challenges in this regard and have uncovered the management of customer information and privacy as a new but important context in which organizational ambidexterity is required, and therefore also extend previous ambidexterity research (eg, Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; Gregory et al., 2015). This high‐level finding emphasizes that a purely consumer‐centric perspective is an incomplete basis for making recommendations for managerial practice regarding information privacy (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011). Instead, privacy research needs to take into account that ambidexterity is required to deal with the challenges around customer infor mation and privacy.

Our study contextualizes the notion of ambidexterity in the area of managing customer information and privacy and therefore offers specific concepts that can inform future research. Like ambidexterity studies in other contexts that have started with an overarching challenge (eg, Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; W. K. Smith & Tushman, 2005), we contribute to our understanding of this topic by uncovering four nested tensions that define the conditions under which companies that are dealing with customer information must operate: (1) trading off data against customers, (2) timing the problem, (3) image‐related costs of customer data, and (4) losing customers due to data utilization. The specific tensions we have discovered can serve as a framework that can guide future organization‐centric research that seeks to explore the challenges associated with companies' handling of custome information and privacy. In particular, these tensions provide useful information about what must be balanced to achieve ambidexterity in the context of information privacy. The tensions relate to existing privacy theory, but also go beyond it. More specifically, privacy calculus theory, a prevalent consumer‐centric theory of information privacy, offers a rationale for why these tensions exist. This theory suggests that customers weigh the benefits and risks o disclosing information to a service and therefore explains why customers might be scared away due to increased perceptions of risk if they are reguired to consent to additional data collection or use (eg, Choi, Wu. Yu. & Land 2018: Kehr. Kowatsch. Wentzel. & Fleisch, 2015: Kordzadeh & Warren, 2017: Ozdemir, Smith, & Benamati 2017). However, because of its consumer‐centric perspective, this theory does not consider companies' own information needs and therefore lacks a more detailed perspective on the resulting tensions for companies that offer services to their customers.

Organizational privacy researchers have called for studies on the strategies that companies adopt to deal with the challenges of managing privacy (Greenaway & Chan, 2005). We address this need for research by offering a balancing perspective. Balancing has been found to present an important organizational activity in other contexts such as control, learning, and performance (eg, Auh & Menguc, 2005: Fisenhardt, Furr, & Bingham, 2010: Gregory et al., 2013). Extending these studies' findings to the context of managing user information and privacy, our research provides insights into the different tactics that the companies in our study employed as balancing activities. In particular, we have identified three categories of tactics and several subforms for each category that can serve as a foundation for research on organizational privacy practices. Our observations suggest that these tactics were used by the providers to deal with the negative effects of their decisions regarding the collection and use of customer data.

The particular surrogate tactic of using already available data indicates that companies engage in a form of bricolage—the skill of making use of whatever is at hand in the case of resource constraints (Baker & Nelson 2005). Further, we have discovered transparency tactics that can help deal with the detrimental outcomes o collecting and using customer information. Although the concept of transparency has been discussed in previous studies (eg, Awad & Krishnan, 2006; Venkatesh, Thong, Chan, & Hu, 2016), research on firms' strategies wit regard to transparency is limited and has therefore been called for (Granados & Gupta, 2013). Our findings with respect to transparency tactics address this call and explain why companies might not always follow previously offered advice to be as transparent as possible (Krasnova et al., 2010; Malhotra, Sung, & Agarwal, 2004). Indeed, we observed that providers try to reduce transparency in some cases by avoiding overemphasis of certain practices—a strategy of concealing information, as suggested by Granados and Gupta (2013). Segmentation tactics help companies address the heterogeneity of customers' preferences and concerns suggested by consumer‐centric privacy research (eg, Ozdemir et al., 2017; H. J. Smith, Dinev, & Xu, 2011). Interestingly, these segmentation tactics resemble what is known as traditional price discrimination for products and digital services (eg, Lehmann & Buxmann, 2009; Shapiro & Varian, 1999). Providers of online services often generate revenue by monetizing customer information and trading their services not for money but against privacy (Acquisti et al., 2013). Our observations of “privacy discrimination tactics” therefore integrate with the traditional price discrimination literature.

## 5.2 | Managerial implications

Our findings can aid providers of online services when they are making decisions about the handling of customer information. Ambidexterity theory suggests that in the presence of competing demands, organizations should strive for a state of balance between the opposing forces, as this is associated with superior performance and long‐term competitiveness (eg, Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004; March, 1991; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008). In our context, this means that companies should not neglect customers' demands for privacy protection—as establishe by extant privacy research—but they also should not disregard their own needs to intrude this privacy to a certain extent if they want to stay competitive. When decision outcomes disturb a company's state of balance, it should explore ways to re‐establish this balance. The particular tensions and tactics identified in this research can help in this regard.

Recognition of the tensions identified in this research can provide more structure for companies' discussions about the challenges they face with regard to their practices. For instance, when thinking about the introduction of a new practice such as the collection of additional customer information or a new way of using the data they already have, providers can discuss the downsides associated with these practices based on the tensions we have discovered. Likewise, start‐ups as well as established companies planning to offer new online services can benefit from a priori knowledge about these tensions when preparing and evaluating their business plans. In this way, they can identify potential challenges regarding the collection and use of customer information beforehand and plan ahead.

If a company's balance is endangered or already disturbed, the tactics we have reported can help the company to maintain or re-establish balance. Reiecting a practice can leave providers with an unfulfilled need for insights, which in turn creates a disadvantage for these companies in terms of improving their business, marketing effectiveness, customer service, and so forth. In this case, providers can look for possible ways to engage in surrogate tactics to counter the drawbacks of their decision. For instance, companies can revisit the often rich amount of data they already possess about their customers and try to derive insights based on such available data instead of simply collecting additional information. On the other hand, adopting a practice can leave companies with the risk of losing customers or harming their image due to their customers' privacy concerns. Transparency tactics can help deal with these challenges either by not overemphasizing practices that are less salient for their customers or by explaining their practices to customers who feel uncomfortable. A recent example for a tactic of increasing transparency is offered by Facebook's announcement to publish its “privacy principles” for the first time in 2018, to provide more details on how the company handles personal information (eg, Meredith, 2018, January 29). Finally, companies can make use of segmentation tactics if they need to deal with heterogeneous customer preferences. For instance, differences in attitudes towards personalized advertising might exist. While a group of customers might have a positive attitude regarding notifications about relevant products or services, another group might be concerned abou companies tracking and analysing their preferences

We conclude our practical implications with a word of caution regarding the use of tactics in general, transparency tactics in particular, and prolonged states of imbalance. Given the focus of our study of exploring “what is” (as opposed to “what is best”). we cannot make recommendations regarding the long-term effectiveness of the tactics presented here. In addition, one might interpret our results in a way suggesting that providers could hide privacy‐intrusive information practices from their customers. We want to clearly distance ourselves from such methods and emphasize that our results should be used to help providers recognize when they might benefit from explaining their information practices and when they might scare customers in an unnecessary manner because a practice is expected and tolerated by customers as an exchange for benefits. Harming customers, however, is no advisable, and we emphasize that other instances exist, which reduce information asymmetries and make information practices transparent. A recent example is given by the fitness app Runkeeper, which submitted customers movement data to a third‐party advertising network even when the app was inactive. This example illustrates how mass media can make detrimental practices transparent quickly (eg, De Looper, 2016, May 13; Meyer, 2016, May 18). Finally, our study does not inform about the potential consequences of prolonged states of imbalance. Ignoring a state of imbalance for a longer period of time could entail significant negative consequences for a company and it customers that do not materialize immediately.

## 5.3 | Limitations and future research

Our study has limitations, and further opportunities for future research exist. First, this exploratory study is based on self‐reports, which always carry the risk of socially desirable answers. Relatedly, our study is based on a convenience sample, as the companies were selected from the authors' personal and professional networks. The upside of this approach is that a certain level of trust between our interviewees and us existed as we discussed the sensitive topic of customer information and privacy (which might have also helped to prevent social desirability issues to a certain extent). On the downside, a more representative sample could have revealed additional aspects not covered in our research. Still, our observations are based on data from varying, heterogeneous sources. For instance, our sample includes several well‐established companies with many years of experience as well as smaller start‐ups at different stages of maturity. The services selected covered both utilitarian and hedonic types, and their user bases also differed in size (up to 19M users per month).

It must be noted that our theory does not attempt to explain potential differences in the relevance of the fou tensions for different companies. It seems probable that the weight of each tension for a company depends on th company's context. Likewise, we have observed that the perception of balance can vary between organizations What is considered as a balanced state by one organization might be perceived as an imbalance by another company. Future larger-scale quantitative studies will be better suited to address these issues, given the limited sample size of our qualitative study. Such studies can assess company and service characteristics in a more structured and quantitative manner, potentially identifying clusters of companies that are similar in how they weigh the different tensions and what they perceive as the optimal balance. Such clusters or company categories could serve future stud ies by explaining differences in companies' activities, strategies, and success

Our research presents only a small step in investigating the organizational challenge of handling customer information and privacy. Given the significance of the topic and the lack of research from an organizationa perspective, more studies are needed, and for these, our findings can present a foundation. For instance, our findings remain at a relatively high level. identifving the overall mechanism and concepts related to organizations' balancing acts around their information needs and customer privacy. In‐depth case studies could compare different companies to elaborate on the conditions for balance and success in more detail. As in prior research that has studied companies dealing with organizational tensions, ambidexterity theory can then serve as theoretical foundation for such endeavours (eg, Adler et al., 1999; Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009).

Moreover, our discovery of a tension between collecting and using customer information, on the one hand, and maintaining the company's image, on the other, can inform future research that studies antecedents of corporate or brand image. Our research suggests that a company's handling of customer information should be taken into consideration, as it might affect this image. Brand equity theory can be used to conceptualize the image and offer insights into its potential antecedents (eg, Dawar & Pillutla, 2000; Keller, 1993).

Future studies could also realize the rich potential for research that is offered by the contextual factors we have identified that exert pressure and influence companies' decisions regarding the collection and use of customer information. For example, these factors could be used in studies that seek a better understanding of why and how decisions for or against certain information practices are made, how these factors relate to balance perceptions and companies' levels of ambidexterity. Theories of organizational decision making could serve as one theoretica foundation in this regard (eg, Eisenhardt, 1989; Nutt, 1998).

## 5.4 | Conclusion

Providers of online services face increasing demands to both intrude and protect individuals' privacy to address their organizational information needs while winning and retaining customers. Our study illustrates that companies try to balance these opposing demands to remain competitive but they operate in turbulent environments, in which internal and external pressures can disturb them in their balance. As they are standing on “wobbling slacklines,” companies have to become flamingos and learn to maintain a balanced stance without falling over.

## INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

The following questions served as a coarse guideline for our interviews. Each interview could take a slightly different direction as interesting ideas were spontaneously pursued. Further, the interview guide evolved over the course of our research as we tried to obtain data that answered the theoretical questions that were salient at a given point dur ing the research process (eg, Glaser, 1978)

Exemplary interview questions:

• Please provide a short description of the service/product provided by your company.

• How important is user data for your company? What is its value based on?

• What role does user data play for your business model/your business processes?

• How do you decide about which user data is collected and which permissions are obtained?

• Which user data do you collect and which rights do you obtain? Why?

• In which particular processes are user data needed?

• How far and how do you inform users about your data collection and usage?

• In this regard, do you signal something to the users that you actually do not do?

• Do you worry about how users might perceive your data collection and usage practices?

• Do you experience tensions with regard to your data collection and usage? If so, how do you deal with these tensions?
