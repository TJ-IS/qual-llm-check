## A B S T R A C T

The unique environment of the information technology (IT) worker is prone to create work exhaustion, a conceptual component of job burnout. Prior research on the IT worker focuses primarily on the antecedent conditions to work exhaustion, uniquely identifying the IT work environment including emotional dissonance, perceived workload, role ambiguity and conflict, autonomy, and fairness of rewards. However, though work exhaustion is a critical product of conditions in the IT work environment, two ignored dimensions of job burnout theory, depersonalization and lessened feelings of personal accomplishment, create a more complete picture and extend current models of IT worker burnout. The extended model with established antecedents is empirically tested through survey techniques and found to hold. Management needs to be aware of these additional symptoms of burnout to circumvent undesirable consequences.

## 1. Introduction

How information technology (IT) workers respond to their work demands is of significant consequence to organizations. One of the most studied concepts in the management literature is that of job burnout, a syndrome where the stressors of a job alter responses to task assignments in negative ways [31]. Consequences have been established to be both individual in terms of destructive behaviors such as personal conflicts, actual illness and substance abuse as well as organizational in terms of turnover, absenteeism, and reduced job performance [18]. Several dimensions impact the reactions that are usually grouped into three categories: organizational, occupational, and individual [30]. Occupational considerations lead one to believe that certain occupations may suffer higher rates of burnout, including those in information technology [18]. Of particular interest to IT management and researchers must be occupational characteristics that describe the unique field of IT careers as identified in prior IT studies [21,27].

Job burnout theory considers burnout to manifest through three different response syndromes; emotional exhaustion as the depletion of emotional resources, depersonalization as the dehumanizing of relationships, and reduced feelings of personal accomplishment or heightened feelings of incompetence [18]. Prior studies of IT workers have focused on the syndrome of emotional exhaustion, through the extended variable of work exhaustion that includes physical and mental depletion [21]. As a variant, work exhaustion has long been recognized as a crucial consequence of IT job artifacts [2,20]. Furthermore, work exhaustion leads IT professionals to such outcomes as reduced job satisfaction and turnover [1,22]. However, the IT literature has not brought the remaining response syndromes into a more complete nomological net for IT workers.

The purpose of this study is, therefore, to expand the current IT professional job burnout framework by incorporating depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment. More specifically, based upon Moore’s [21] IT work exhaustion model and job burnout theory, depersonalization and diminished personal achievement are proposed as two further consequences. The more complete picture may be important as other disciplines have found that the three dimensions of job burnout are all important in explaining consequences beyond turnover and job satisfaction. As examples, job performance was negatively associated with diminished personal accomplishment [3] and absenteeism was positively related to depersonalization [28]. Thus, the question is whether IT job characteristics also lead to the exhibition of the remaining job burnout traits of depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment. If so, then researchers in IT can use these further syndromes to examine additional consequences in a more complete framework of study and IT managers have additional symptoms to consider in preventing adverse consequences of job burnout.

## 2. Background of IT worker job burnout

Work exhaustion in the IT environment was first examined in Moore’s seminal study. This syndrome was originally encompassed by the construct of tedium, defined as a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion caused by long-term involvement in demanding situations [24]. Job burnout theory conceptualized a more limited concept of emotional exhaustion [16]. Moore [21] modified Maslach and Jackson’s [16] emotional exhaustion scale to encompass emotional, mental, as well as physical exhaustion and to eliminate references to people as the source of exhaustion in order to study the IT professionals’ work exhaustion phenomenon. The antecedents and consequences of work exhaustion were proposed based upon an extensive literature review and empirical tests. The result indicated that work exhaustion among IT professionals was highly associated with low levels of job satisfaction and high levels of turnover intention; furthermore, work overload, role ambiguity and conflict, lack of autonomy and lack of rewards were antecedents of work exhaustion among IT professionals. Moore’s proposed model not only links two frequently observed phenomena among IT professionals (turnover and work exhaustion) but also provides a theoretical foundation for studying antecedents and consequences of IT work exhaustion.

To gain a deeper insight into the antecedents of the work exhaustion phenomenon of IT professionals, effort has been devoted to examine additional IT job characteristics (e.g., skill variety, job feedback, job significance) [4,23], IT work characteristics (e.g., role-stress, promotion, job security, developmental prospects) [20,15], and outside the work characteristics (e.g., work–family conflict, outside job opportunities) [1] as potential antecedents of IT professionals work exhaustion. Further, considerations of organizational support factors [2] and individual factors [26] have been considered in alleviating the magnitude of impact demonstrated by the antecedents of work exhaustion.

Moore’s base model also serves as a foundation for extensions. For example, Rutner et al. [27] noticed that Moore’s [21] study excluded ‘‘people’’ as a source of exhaustion and have observed that ‘‘emotional dissonance,’’ defined as the perceived conflict between the way one feels toward interaction partners and the emotion one feels compelled to display toward those individuals, is a critical source of stress among IT professionals. As a result, they theoretically and empirically extended Moore’s [21] model by adding the relationship where emotional dissonance affects IT professionals’ work exhaustion and job satisfaction. The results of all these empirical evidences and extensions have established the relationship between certain IT job characteristics (i.e., antecedents) and work exhaustion among IT professionals. Unfortunately, other dimensions of job burnout so dominant in other fields have not been incorporated into current models or empirically established for IT workers [21,27,22,13].

In addition to the component of emotional exhaustion, job burnout theory recognizes depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment. In particular, depersonalization (negative, callous, or excessively detached behavior toward others), and diminished personal accomplishment (a decline in one’s feeling of competence and successful achievement) have traditionally been observed to co-exist with emotional exhaustion. Shirom [29] argued that depersonalization is a coping method of exhaustion and not the same concept or construct as emotional exhaustion. In fact, as indicated by Moore [22], almost all the empirical evidences measuring job burnout in the management discipline treated these three components as consequents of overall burnout. In particular, the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) incorporates all these and is an accepted standard to measure the degree of job burnout [17].

Among these three dimensions, a conceptual question has been raised – the actual dimensionality of the three job burnout syndromes. Moderate to high correlations between emotional exhaustion and depersonalization suggest the possibility of alternative models [34]. In fact, some researchers have argued a two-factor model consisting of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization along with personal accomplishment [8]. On the other hand, Cordes et al. [7] argued that burnout consists of all three distinct, but related, components. Although early researchers typically adopt the three components as a unidimensional construct in their empirical studies using the MBI, several recent studies have adopted a three component model to examine job burnout in different professional occupations [31]. Among those studies, emotional exhaustion is the central quality of burnout and the most obvious manifestation of the syndrome with emotional exhaustion as the trigger and depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment developing sequentially thereafter.

Moore [22] argued that individual experiencing work exhaustion might or might not adopt the associated behaviors such as depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment. In other words, depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment must be separated conceptually from IT work exhaustion and should join the list of other variables identified by researchers as potential consequences of IT work exhaustion (e.g., along with reduced job satisfaction). Still, the empirical evidences of the theoretical relationship between IT work exhaustion and depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment has not yet been established. Thus, by separating IT work exhaustion from the traditional job burnout construct Moore [21] established a theoretical foundation for the study of IT workers. The current study, on the other hand, examines whether depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment serve as consequences of work exhaustion among IT professionals. Fig. 1 shows the proposed research model. The model considers the current understanding of IT worker burnout that incorporates the job characteristics of the IT worker identified by Moore [21] to create work exhaustion and the additional emotional dissonance considerations added by Rutner et al. [27]. The additions due to job burnout theory include depersonalization and personal accomplishment.

## 3. Hypotheses

One component of burnout, emotional exhaustion, is characterized by a lack of energy and a feeling that one’s emotional resources are depleted. Emotional exhaustion generally coexists with feelings of frustration and tension [30]. IT work exhaustion in technology professionals is operationalized by modifying items from Maslach and Jackson’s [16] emotional exhaustion scale to include mental, physical and emotional exhaustion, but to eliminate the people parts as the source of exhaustion. The second component of burnout as defined by job burnout theory is depersonalization, which is characterized by a tendency of employees to treat other people as objects. Employees may become cynical toward coworkers and clients, and display a detached or an emotional callousness. Since IT workers must frequently interact with users and other stakeholders, this becomes an important consideration [11]. The third observed syndrome of burnout is diminished personal accomplishment, which is marked by a tendency to evaluate oneself negatively.

Among these three dimensions, there still remains an argument about the order of development given moderate to high correlations between emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [34]. Typically, recent research argues that emotional exhaustion is the central quality of burnout and the most obvious manifestation of the syndrome where emotional exhaustion was the trigger for depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment to develop sequentially [7,14]. Employees faced with a steady stream of problems requiring some display of emotion as an occupational tool may experience fatigue when the stream never lets up [33]. As a coping response, individuals may distance themselves psychologically in order to stem the demands on their emotional reserves. They depersonalize their interactions, adopting a callous, cynical, or detached way of dealing with the situation and the individuals with whom they interact. This provides a type of emotional buffer. As they devote less time and energy to their interactions, they experience a feeling of diminished personal accomplishment in their jobs. They feel inadequate in their ability to relate effectively and successfully with the very people they had once ideally believed they could help [32].

A few recent empirical studies have attempted to provide longitudinal evidence for the conceptualization of burnout as a developmental process with no conclusive results [31,32]. Still, the existing empirical evidences suggest that emotional exhaustion leads to depersonalization which, in turn, leads to diminished personal accomplishment adding to further emotional exhaustion in a cycle. Based upon the above discussion and empirical evidences examining the exhaustion effect in other work contexts, we proposed the following two hypotheses.

H1: IT work exhaustion is positively associated with depersonalization for the IT worker.

H2: Depersonalization is negatively associated with personal accomplishment harbored by the IT worker.

## 4. Research methods

## 4.1. Data collection

This study assesses individual perceptions of IT workers. IT workers fall into the following jobs for the purpose of this study: Programmer, developer, systems analysts, system designers, IS engineer and software engineer [19]. IT workers were targeted in the Taiwanese manufacturing sector. Fifty prospective companies were randomly drawn from the directory of the CommonWealth 1000 largest manufacturing firms in Taiwan [6]. We contacted a member of IT management in each identified organization. Of the fifty managers contacted, 36 agreed to participate. Managers agreeing to participate were visited by a member of the research team to further explain the research objectives. Each IT manager identified 15–18 potential participants that fell into one of the job classifications listed above.

The instruments and associated cover letters made no reference to job burnout to minimize the occurrence of survey bias [21]. All respondents were assured that their responses would be kept confidential. To reduce the likelihood of common method effects, we asked the respondents to complete two instruments at distinct time points [25]. Instrument #1 assessed job characteristics and demographic information. Of the 566 potential respondents, 537 returned the first instrument. Instrument #2 assessed the outcome variables (job burnout dimensions and job satisfaction). Instrument #2 was sent to all respondents of instrument #1. ## 5. Measures

Work exhaustion describes feelings of being emotionally overextended and exhausted by one’s work. The five items are as applied by Moore [21]. Depersonalization and personal accomplishment were measured with Maslach burnout inventory [17]. Job satisfaction was measure with three items drawn from the job diagnostic survey (JDS) [12]. Perceived workload was measured with four items used by Moore [21]. Role ambiguity and role conflict were assessed with the scales used by Moore [21]. Autonomy was assessed by a four-item scale used by Rutner et al. [27]. Fairness of rewards was assessed using two items from the scale of distributive justice [21]. Negative emotional dissonance and positive emotional dissonance were measured with the scales used by Rutner et al. [27]. The items are all in Table 2.

All constructs used multiple-item scales. Each item was measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7) unless indicated otherwise in Table 2. Though all measures are from the literature, a pre-test was conducted to enhance validity of the instruments once the items had been compiled. Ten IT professionals who each had more than five years information system’s work experience examined the survey instrument for clarity. No items were removed at this point.

## 6. Data integrity

Structured equation modeling (SEM) with partial least squares (PLS) analysis allows empirical assessment of proposed measurement and structural models [5]. PLS-Graph was used to verify the constructs and test hypotheses. To start, individual item reliability was examined by observing the factor loading of each item. The loadings of all indicators are shown in Table 2. Personal accomplishment item 4 and role conflict item 1 loaded at less than 0.50, so they were removed from further analysis. To assess internal consistency, we examined both the Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Both reliability coefficients, shown in Table 3, are above the recommended level of 0.7 [9].

Convergent and discriminant validities consider whether the constructs are distinct and that indicators load on the appropriate constructs. Convergent validity should be assured through average variance extracted (AVE) by constructs, where AVE greater than 0.5 is preferred [5]. As Table 3 shows, each construct exceeded this requirement. Discriminant validity can be evaluated by two criteria [5]: (1) each indicator should load higher on the construct of interest than on any other latent construct, and (2) the square root of AVE of the construct should be larger than the correlations between constructs. As shown in Table 4, all item loadings were higher on their assigned construct than other constructs. Table 5 shows that the square root of the AVE was greater than all of the associated inter-construct correlations. The results suggest the measurement model has sufficient discriminant validity [10].

## 7. Results

PLS results for the structural model are summarized in Fig. 2. Overall, we found support for both the hypothesized relationships. Work exhaustion demonstrated a strong direct effect on depersonalization (supporting H1). In turn, depersonalization demonstrated a direct, negative relationship with personal accomplishment, supporting H2. The remaining links from existing models also showed results as expected, except for the link from role ambiguity to work exhaustion. This relationship has also been inconsistent in prior research, showing significance in the data of Rutner et al. [27] but not in the data of Moore [21]. Thus, the extended model holds to prior results and adds significant paths as expected from job burnout theory.

## 8. Conclusions

This study extended the existing models of job burnout for IT workers using further dimensions of job burnout theory. In addition to work exhaustion, depersonalization and a loss of personal accomplishment were found to be significant as subsequent variables in a model that considers the job characteristics of the IT professional. The model utilized the current understanding described in the literature extended to include the further dimensions. The added dimensions were found significant in tests of data collected from IT professionals. Therefore, work exhaustion of IT workers is a key factor not only leading to job satisfaction or turnover as the current literature suggests but affects depersonalization and sense of personal accomplishment. The extension is crucial because studies in other disciplines have found these different aspects of job burnout lead to different undesirable consequences, such as absenteeism, organizational citizenship behaviors and poor job performance. In addition, antecedents to work exhaustion established by prior literature continue to show importance in the extended model, adding validity to this and prior studies.

The results of this study have several implications including: (1) IT work exhaustion should be examined separately as a distinct concept for studying job burnout of IT personnel; and (2) IT work exhaustion is a trigger for the depersonalization and diminishing achievement, which could be associated with other IT employee behaviors such as reduced organizational commitment, decreased client/coworker interactions, absenteeism, and reduced job performance. Researchers would be able to examine potential strategies and antecedents to mitigate depersonalization and diminished personal accomplishment, instead of attempting to work on the antecedents of IT work exhaustion alone. Managers should be aware of these additional symptoms of job burnout and be prepared to respond with adjustments similar to those for lessened satisfaction due to common antecedents.

Limitations to the study include components of variables included as well as the target population. First, the consequences of depersonalizations and personal accomplishment were not included in the model, those require further study in the IS arena to determine if they hold to expectations established in other literatures. Secondly, in order to establish the remaining dimen sions of job burnout theory in the IT worker population, we followed the sequential order of burnout dimensions as suggested by Leiter and Maslach [14], neglecting other possible orderings of the burnout dimensions. Lastly, the data were collected from IT employees in manufacturing firms in an Asian-Pacific country which may limit the generalization to other contexts or countries.
