# Who influences whom? Analyzing workplace referents’ social influence on IT adoption and non-adoption

## Abstract

Technology adoption research has long struggled to incorporate normative beliefs from sources in the social environment of adopters into adoption models. We study the role of social influence from different workplace referent groups, like superiors and colleagues from the same or the IT department, on the intention to adopt. An empirical analysis, using data from 152 firms, based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology and related approaches reveals that social influence on adoption significantly differs with regard to both source (peer groups) and sink (adopters and non-adopters) of the influence. The results imply that a single cumulative subjective norm measure might be too naı¨ve and that future research might considerably improve our understanding of IT adoption and non-adoption by revealing the differential impact of various peer groups on adoption intention, and also on its antecedents.

Davis et al. stated that ‘More sophisticated methods for assessing the specific types of social influence processes at work in a computer acceptance context are clearly needed’ (Davis et al., 1989). On the grounds of these and other results regarding social influence (e.g. Mathieson, 1991), Ritu Agarwal identifies ‘a need for additional research that clarifies the precise role of social pressure in technology acceptance and isolates the contingencies under which such norms are likely to be more salient’ (Agarwal, 2000). Studying influence patterns from social norms and personal innovativeness in technology adoption brought June Lu et al. to the conclusion that ‘further research should study if these two factors generate any direct impacts on intention to adopt’ (Lu et al., 2005).

Given that several studies in the field of technology acceptance have typically taken subjective norm as a single construct that includes the effects of various salient referents, Mark Srite and Elena Karahanna ‘suggest that social norms need to be conceptualized in a more distinguishing manner to capture the nuances of the social environment’ (Srite and Karahanna, 2006). We aim to contribute to a more sophisticated understanding of normative beliefs by examining influence from different groups on an individual’s information technology (IT) adoption as well as non-adoption based on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al., 2003). We draw on the work of Lewis et al., who analyze the social influence of several referent groups on an individual’s intention to use a course Web site for teaching (Lewis et al., 2003). Furthermore, responding to their suggestion that ‘testing the robustness of these relationships across multiple workplace contexts and technologies would be a fruitful area for research’ (Lewis et al., 2003), we chose the working environment as the context for the examination of social influence. Unlike their measurement of social influence based on the regular measures of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991), we define social influence not only as an individual’s perception of the opinion of important others within his or her environment but also as their actual recommendations and behavior (Kim et al., 2007). In doing so, we follow the advice of several social psychology researchers (e.g. Ahtola, 1976; Warshaw, 1980; Miniard and Cohen, 1983; Liska, 1984) who criticize the basic measurement of subjective norm in the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) and the TPB (Ajzen, 1991), the perceived opinion of important others, as not distinctive enough to analyze social influence appropriately. Similar to the work of Karahanna et al., who observe the influence of attitudinal and normative beliefs on the behavioral intention to use Windows for users and potential adopters (Karahanna et al., 1999), we take a closer look at two different groups as well. We compare and analyze the different impact of workplace referents’ social influence on adopters and non-adopters of CV databases in staff recruitment against the backdrop of a global IT talent shortage (Thompson, 2007). We chose the human resource (HR) context as it represents a frequently demanded and under-researched case of non-adoption (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001; Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007). Current research emphasizes the need to carefully consider factors that are salient for non-adopters, ‘especially if the ultimate goal is to increase technology adoption’ (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001). Hence, the application domain of CV databases is relevant for firms as they are seen as a means of surviving in the ‘War for IT talent’ (Chambers et al., 1998; Cappelli, 2000). Our research questions are:

\- RQ1: Are there differences between the social influences of different workplace referents (colleagues from HR department, operations department, IT department, customers and superiors) on IT adoption/non-adoption?

\- RQ2: Is the impact of different workplace groups on IT adopters different from the impact on non-adopters?

The following section contains a description of the underlying theory for our research model, the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Based on this and related previous research on social influence in technology adoption, we derive hypotheses. The subsequent sections describe the research methodology, including data collection and analysis, and illustrate the results. Finally, limitations and implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## Theoretical background and hypotheses

In this section, the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) and other approaches to measuring social influence in technology adoption (Lewis et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2007) are introduced in order to derive the hypotheses for our research model.

## Adoption and non-adoption of IT

Firms invest substantial resources in their IT in the hope that this will help sustain their competitiveness on current and future markets. But if this technology is not sufficiently and adequately used by the employees, IT cannot contribute to firm goals, and return on IT is questionable. As a result, IS research has, among others, looked at factors influencing individuals’ adoption of IT and the impact of IT adoption and non-adoption on process design and process performance (Hirschheim, 2007). The majority of all models in technology adoption are based on theories coming from fields like behavioral science and social psychology. The most-cited and most frequently applied behavioral theories are the TRA (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) and the TPB (Ajzen, 1991). The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) is also based on this theoretical background (Davis et al., 1989). Further research has produced a number of popular TAM and TPB extensions such as the work of Taylor and Todd (1995) or TAM2 by Venkatesh and Davis (2000). Models especially designed for the analysis of individual adoption in specific contexts are the MATH model for the household context (Brown and Venkatesh, 2005) and the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) for individual adoption in the organizational context. Owing to the organizational context of our study we chose UTAUT as the underlying theory. The model with its related constructs and the derivation of our hypotheses is described in more detail in the following subsection.

Further discussions by leading IS researchers in subsequent years, such as Lee et al. (2006) or the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS) special issue ‘Quo vadis – TAM?’ (Hirschheim, 2007), ultimately suggest a distinct lack of research on factors that influence IT nonadopters. Despite its wide recognition, research in the field of IT non-adoption is still somewhat immature. The brief outcome so far primarily provides insights into factors which influence non-adopters in the household context (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001). Here, adopting individuals are primarily influenced by different outcomes (utilitarian, hedonic and social); in contrast, non-adopters are influenced strongly by the fear of obsolescence (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001). Observing the impact of attitudinal and normative beliefs on users and potential adopters shows that social norms can induce initial adoption while continuing usage is merely based on attitudinal beliefs (Karahanna et al., 1999). One of the first antecedents for influencing non-adoption intention was found by Ang and Pavri, who introduced the construct ‘resistance to change’ (Ang and Pavri, 1994). The impact of ‘resistance to change’ on IT non-adopters is supported for groups on the organizational level (Lapointe and Rivard, 2005; Lapointe and Rivard, 2007), as well as for medics in the healthcare sector (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet, 2007).

In the following, we analyze the impact of social influence on both groups, adopters and non-adopters, and identify potential differences. The following subsection introduces the theories underlying our research model.

Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology With the introduction of the TAM by Davis in 1989, one of the major research streams of the IS discipline was established. Many research studies based on TAM have been conducted over the last two decades. In 2007, a special issue of the JAIS discussed critically the use of TAM by IS researchers to explain individual usage and adoption behavior (Hirschheim, 2007). In 2008, Dwivedi et al. (2008) provide a profile of adoption and diffusion research, and highlight the fact that TAM has been the most popular model followed by the diffusion of innovation theory. Most of the published research models can be traced back to the TPB or TRA by Ajzen and Fishbein (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1985). The objective of all models is to explain the antecedents that predict an individual’s behavior and an individual’s intention to adopt a behavior. ‘Intentions are assumed to capture the motivational factors that influence a behavior: they are indications of how hard people are willing to try, of how much of an effort they are planning to exert, in order to perform the behavior’ (Ajzen, 1991). In TRA and TPB, antecedents of intention are an individual’s attitude toward the behavior, subjective norm and perceived behavioral control. Attitude conceptualizes ‘the degree to which a person has a favorable or unfavorable evaluation or appraisal of the behavior in question’ and subjective norm is referred to as ‘the perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform the behavior.’ As a general rule, Ajzen suggests that ‘the more favorable the attitude and subjective norm with respect to a behavior is, (y) the stronger should be an individual’s intention to perform the behavior under consideration’ (Ajzen, 1991). TAM suggests and evaluates perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as specific antecedents influencing the intention to use IT. In the years following the first publication of TAM, a lot of IT specific antecedents were discussed. Therefore, Venkatesh et al. (2003) intro duced the ‘UTAUT’ to summarize all relevant extensions of TAM. Consequently, we will use the UTAUT for discussing the social influence construct of behavioral intention research models. In the following sections, the four UTAUT constructs such as performance expectancy, effort expectance, facilitating conditions and social influ ence, which explain the intention to use a system, are discussed and their importance for the intention to use an IS is explained and hypothesized.

## Performance expectancy

Performance expectancy is one of the determinants known form the UTAUT. It is defined as ‘the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance’ (Venkatesh et al., 2003). In their analyses and re-conceptualization Venkatesh et al. extended Davis et al.’ (1989) construct perceived usefulness. Performance expectancy precedes the individual’s intention to use the system. Therefore, our first hypothesis is (a: adopters, b: non-adopters):

H1a and H1b: Performance expectancy (PE) has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters’ intention to use an IS.

## Effort expectancy

Perceived ease of use, as the second TAM antecedent of an individual’s intention to use a system, is the foundation for the effort expectancy construct of the UTAUT. PEOU is defined as ‘the degree to which the prospective user expects the target system to be free of effort’ (Davis et al., 1989). Venkatesh et al. (2003) combine PEOU with complexity known from Thompson et al. (1991) and ease of use (Moore and Benbasat, 1991) and define effort expectancy as ‘the degree of ease associated with the use of the system.’ It has been evaluated as an antecedent for behavioral intention by different research studies. Therefore, we assume that

H2a and H2b: Effort expectancy (EE) has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters’ intention to use an IS.

## Facilitating conditions

Another antecedent of behavioral intention is facilitating conditions. This construct was first introduced by the model of PC utilization (Thompson et al., 1991) and combined by Venkatesh et al. (2003) with the perceived behavioral control construct of the TPB (Ajzen, 1985). Facilitating conditions measure, for example, the provision of support for users and are defined as ‘the degree to which an individual believes that (y) an infrastructure exists to support the use of the system’ (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Therefore, following the assumptions of Venkatesh et al. (2003) and Ajzen (1985), we hypothesize that

H3a and H3b: Facilitating conditions (FC) have a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters’ intention to use an IS.

## Social influence in technology (non-) adoption

Social psychology research defines social influence as a change of mind in behaviors, thoughts or feelings from an individual’s perspective as revealed by interaction with another individual or a group. Another term for social influence is known as peer group pressure that is the pressure on an individual person to conform to a distinct group resulting in a specific behavior. Social influence in technology (non-) adoption is strongly related to the social comparison theory (Festinger, 1954) and the research about peer group pressure on individuals (Sherif, 1935; Asch, 1951). As a result, people tend to act in conformity with a distinct group as, on the one hand, they continuously compare their acting behavior with the behavior of important others; and on the other hand, they feel pressured to act in a way that will not make them stand out as lonely and disliked. In the following years, Burns and Stalker integrated social influence in the organizational context into their work on innovation. They stated that ‘in working organizations decisions are made either in the presence of others or with the knowledge that they will have to be understood or implemented or approved by others. (Burns and Stalker, 1961)

Another decade later behavioral research adopted these results and developed ‘subjective norm’ as a factor to model and explain the impact of normative beliefs on an individual’s intention to perform a behavior, or not, in terms of the TRA and the TPB (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991). ‘Subjective norm’ was defined as ‘a sum of the perceived expectations of specific referent individuals and/or groups weighted by the individual’s ‘motivation to comply’ with those expectations’ (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen, 1991). Especially in the context of the TPB, social influence was frequently measured as subjective norm in technology adoption research (Lee et al., 2006). Mostly it is used to measure and explain an individual’s intention to use a specific IS (e.g. Taylor and Todd, 1995; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Srite and Karahanna, 2006). Only a small number of researchers analyzed social influence on perceived usefulness (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000), perceived ease of use (Lu et al., 2005) or specific behavior (e.g. Igbaria et al., 1996; Van Raaij and Schepers, 2006).

Despite being used in adoption and diffusion research for over almost 20 years, social influence and peer group pressure are still among the most frequently discussed topics. Surprisingly, and despite other expectations, the social influence construct regularly proves difficult to measure and was found to be not significant in several studies about IT (non-) adoption, including the TAM in 1989 (Davis et al., 1989) and more recent work in top journals (e.g. Pavlou and Fygenson, 2006; Hsieh et al., 2008). It has mostly been social psychology researchers (Ahtola, 1976; Warshaw, 1980; Miniard and Cohen, 1983; Liska, 1984) who have ascribed this problem to the measurement and interdependency in the underlying behavioral theories: TRA (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) and TPB (Ajzen, 1991). The measurement was criticized for its definition of subjective norm as the person’s perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975). Other researchers raised the question of whether individuals really perceive any cumulative ‘important others’ in general circumstances (Ahtola, 1976). Researchers controversially suggested that the opinions of these referents or groups in a workplace environment differ or at least vary in size. In addition, it should be quite difficult to form these referents into a combined group of ‘important others.’ Examples of an individual’s workplace referents in the recruiting department are colleagues in the same department (Thompsor et al., 1991), superiors (Igbaria et al., 1996), the IT department (Karahanna et al., 1999), other departments within the organization (Lewis et al., 2003) or customers (Frambach and Schillewaert, 2002), who in our approach are applicants. To answer our research questions, we determine the social influence of these referent groups on adopters and non-adopters of a distinct technology in the recruiting department of firms. Similarly to the work of Brown and Venkatesh (2005) and Lewis et al. (2003), who examine social influence on individual adopters in a household and a university environment, we analyze the influence of workplace referents in an organizational context on technology adopters and additionally on non-adopters. Previous work almost exclusively measured social influence in terms of an individual’s perception; to enhance this approach we also integrate referents’ actual behavior and active recommendation in our measurement of social influence as introduced by Kim et al. (2007) Overall, we assume direct and positive relationships between the social influence of all workplace referents and an individual’s intention to adopt (hypotheses ‘a’)

or not to adopt (hypotheses ‘b’) an IS. In detail we hypothesize that

H4a and H4b: The social influence of colleagues in the same department has a direct, positive effect on adopters and non-adopters’ intention to use an IS.

H5a and H5b: The social influence of the operating department has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters’ intention to use an IS.

H6a and H6b: The social influence of the IT department has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and nonadopters’ intention to use an IS.

H7a and H7b: The social influence of customers has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters intention to use an IS.

H8a and H8b: The social influence of superiors has a direct, positive effect on adopters’ and non-adopters intention to use an IS.

## Research methodology

In this section, we describe our research object, the (non-) adoption of CV databases, and point out why we regard it as an appropriate example of non-adoption research. In the following sections, the data collection process is explained. The final subsection ‘Data analysis’ shows the evaluation of our research model with Partial Least Squares (PLS) and visualizes the results for the measurement and structural model.

## The (non-) adoption of CV databases

The observed case in our research approach is the (non-) adoption of CV databases by corporate recruiters. These CV databases, also known as candidate databases, are offered by job boards like CareerBuilder, SnagAJob.com, Careersinteractive.com, Bayt.com, hirefire.com and Monster.com, just to name a few. Users can typically deposit their CVs there and submit them to potential employers, while employers can search the databases for potential employees. Previous research discovered that candidate databases are valuable resources for advertised recruitment agencies and executive search firms (Fish and Macklin, 2004) and could support the corporate selection process effectively when combined with recommender systems (Malinowski et al., 2005). In a period of global talent shortage and a shrinking number of graduates for demographic reasons and high turnover rates, this might represent a meaningful alternative for the daily work of a recruiter (Thompson, 2007). However, the overall usage level of CV databases is quite low (Laumer et al., 2008). Therefore, CV databases seem to represent an interesting research subject to discuss IT adoption and non-adoption drivers.

In the next subsection, we describe how we collected and measured our empirical data about non-adopters of CV databases.

## Data collection

We used a paper-based questionnaire to evaluate our hypotheses. Our research model was operationalized and transferred into a Structural Equation Model (SEM). We analyzed the SEM with the PLS approach (Bagozzi and Yi, 1988; Chin, 2000) because PLS does not rely on normally distributed indicator data as the PLS estimation is distribution-free (Chin and Gopal, 1995; Chin, 1998). Tests using SPSS revealed that our data set contains a number of abnormally distributed variables. For the calculation of our result, we used SmartPLS (Ringle et al., 2005). The level of significance for support or rejection of hypotheses was chosen as P ¼ 0.05.

In 2008, our questionnaire was sent to managers responsible for the recruiting process in their organizations, who are able to decide whether to use CV databases as an IS or not. The companies included in the research are located in Germany ranging from 200,000 to 500 employees. In total, we received 152 usable responses, 54 of these firms stating that they use CV databases in their staff recruitment process and 98 of the participants saying they do not use CV databases in their recruiting. So we used 54 datasets to calculate our research model for adopters and 98 datasets for non-adopters.

Each construct in our research model is represented by a set of indicators. We measured intention, performance and effort expectancy as reflective indicators on a seven-point or five-point Likert scale using scales from ‘strongly agree’ to ‘strongly disagree.’ The indicators were designed following the proposition of Venkatesh et al. (2003). Social influence was measured as a formative construct following the guidelines by Petter et al. (2007) for formative constructs in general and by Hsieh et al. (2008) for social influence in particular. Table 1 presents the operationalization of our indicators in the field of CV databases. The original survey was written in German. Hence, items from the questionnaire described in the following sections have been translated using the back-translation method (Brislin, 1970).

## Data analysis

For data analysis, the PLS algorithms were conducted using SmartPLS 2.0 M3. For a structured analysis of our data and the testing of our hypothesis, we analyzed the data in three different steps. First, we evaluated the reflective and formative measurement models of our constructs; second, we evaluated the statistical power of the structural model; and third, we tested for significant difference using a t-test for mean equality conducted with SPSS 16.0.

## Measurement model

The quality of the reflective measurement model is determined by (1) content validity, (2) indicator reliability, (3) construct reliability and (4) discriminant validity (Bagozzi, 1979). For formative measures, (1) content validity (Straub et al., 2004), (2) weights (Chin, 1998) and (3) multicollinearity (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw, 2006) should be evaluated.

Content validity describes the degree to which the measured results stand for the content-semantic part of the construct (Nunnally and Bernstein, 1994). A condition for content validity evaluation is a very precise content definition for the constructs. We discussed our constructs in detail with several recruiting experts in organizations and job boards in order to ensure the content validity of our constructs (Churchill, 1979).

Indicator reliability and weights. In the models tested, all loadings for reflective measurement models are significant at P ¼ 0.001 level and above the recommended 0.6 parameter value (Table 2). Significance tests were conducted using the bootstrap routine with 200 samples (Chin, 2000). In addition, for the formative measurement model Table 2 lists the weights for the social influence construct.

Construct reliability demands that the indicators which are related to the same construct should have a strong correlation to each other (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). Construct reliability was tested using composite reliability (CR) and the average variance extracted (AVE). The estimated values were above the recommended thresholds of 0.7 for CR and 0.5 for AVE (Bagozzi and Yi, 1988) (Table 3).

Discriminant validity can be evaluated by looking at the cross-loadings. The loadings of our reflective indicators are higher for their respective constructs than for any other (Table 4). Additionally, the square root of the AVE for each construct is higher than correlations between constructs (Table 3). Therefore, the discriminant validity of the latent variables is high (Fornell and Larcker, 1981; Hulland, 1999).

Multicollinearity. The test for multicollinearity using SPSS 16 indicates that multicollinearity does not cause a problem. All VIF values for the items used to measure social influence are below the recommended level of 0.4 (Petter et al., 2007).

and for adopters and non-adopters. To test whether these differences are significant or not a multi-group comparison with PLS is conducted. Chin et al. (2003) argues that multigroup comparisons of PLS models with differences in path estimates for different sampled populations have been relatively naive. However, in the last 5 years more and more researchers have used multi-group comparison with PLS. For example, Hsieh et al. (2008) compares advantaged and disadvantaged users of Internet TV. Extending the early works of Edgington (1987), Thompson et al. (1994), Good (2000) and Keil et al. (2000), Dibbern and Chin (2005) evaluate an SEM to discuss cultural differences between Germany and the USA. Based on a procedure for a permutation test based on random assignments, they test their hypothesis that there are significant differences between German and the US firms. Following these approaches, a multi-group comparison using PLS and a t-test for the mean is conducted to test for differences in social influence on an adopter’s and non-adopter’s intention to use an IS.

The Levene-test indicates that the variance is equal between adopters and non-adopters for the research models of HR and OD. It can be supported for IT, CUST and SUP. From the results of the t-test, one can conclude that on a Po0.05 level there are significance differences for the OD, IT, CUST and SUP research model.

The results of the measurement and structural model validation as well as the t-tests results are discussed after the following section about the limitations of our study.

## Limitations

Even though we could corroborate all our hypotheses for social influence, except the social influence of customers which is insignificant for adopters and non-adopters, our research approach has limitations. As shown in the results within the previous subsection, the major limitation is the low adequacy of the UTAUT to explain the intention of non-adopters. Unlike the sample of adopters, the constructs effort expectancy and facilitating conditions are not valuable determinants for an individual’s intention not to adopt a specific IS. Furthermore, it should be noted that we have only discussed the (non-) adoption of CV databases. This might represent an isolated example deriving from these specific groups. Furthermore, given the group sizes, we cannot separate the subjects of our study into larger and smaller firms to test for the effect of firm size or industry. There might be differences for different organizational settings as well. Furthermore, our questionnaire was only answered by firms in Germany. So our results might not be directly transferable to any other country or culture. The use of CV databases might differ a lot from country to country as they are predominately offered by job boards in Western Europe, the USA and China. An EU survey revealed that 27.4% of the 1000 largest companies in the European Union search in CV databases frequently (Eckhardt et al., 2007).

In addition, our results may be affected by common method variance because we collected data from participants at the same time using the same survey (Podsakoff, 2003). However, the study was designed following guidelines to avoid CMV (King et al., 2007). The survey was designed with different scales and the order of item appearance in the survey varies. Another limitation of the study is that the weights for the formative social influence construct are not significant for some of the items. However, for formative constructs these items have to be retained in the measurement model (Bollen and Lennox, 1991).

## Results

This section highlights the important results of the structural model equation and the t-test for group comparison for adopters and non-adopters. To get a general idea of the results of our research model, we section the outcomes into two different parts. First, we show and summarize the results of the structural model to corroborate or reject our hypotheses. Then we have a close look at the overall effect size of every social influence in the working environment. Finally, we describe the results of the diversity t-tests if the effect of the specific social influence is significantly different for adopters and nonadopters.

## Structural model

Starting with the corroboration and rejection of our hypotheses, we determine the overall explanatory power of our research model for the different sorts of social influence observed. In general, we can conclude that the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) is an appropriate basic theory to measure the impact of social influence on IT adopters (Figure 2).

Table 5 summarizes the result of the structural model. As can be seen, there is statistical support for the influence of performance expectancy for every model tested for both adopters and non-adopters. Effort expectancy can be supported for adopters in the operations department, IT department, customer and superiors sub-model. For all non-adoption sub-models and for HR sub-model of adopters, the influence cannot be statistically supported and the hypothesis must be discarded. In addition, facilitating conditions can only be evaluated as a significant influencing factor for adopters. For non-adopters, the hypothesis that facilitating conditions have an impact on the intention not to use an IS must be discarded for all submodels tested. For social influence, the results are different. For the HR model for both adopters and non-adopters, this influence can be statistically supported. For the other four models, social influence by the operations department or the IT department is significant for non-adopters and not for adopters. Customers’ influence cannot be supported for either group. Superiors, however, are evaluated as significant for adopters and not significant for non-adopters.

In addition, Table 5 highlights the effect sizes f<sup>2</sup> for each sub-model and sample tested. In the HR sub-model for both adopters and non-adopters, the social influence construct has the largest effect size and therefore the largest influence on the intention of an individual. For the operations department as well as for the IT department sub-model, social influence can be evaluated as the second largest effect size for non-adopters and as the lowest one for adopters. In the customer sub-model, social influence is the number one effect size for adopters and number two for non-adopters. For superiors social influence, again, has the largest influence on both adopters and non-adopters. These results indicate that the importance of social influence varies when one compares different social influence groups. These results are also supported by the t-test, as the next section explains.

## Differences in social influence

The results of the t-test for mean equality as shown in Table 7 indicate that there are significant differences for the operations department, IT department, customer and superiors sub-model. For the HR sub-model, the t-test conducted does not provide evidence of a significant difference for adopters and non-adopters. The influence of peers from the HR department should be the same for both adopters and non-adopters. Furthermore, the Levenetest limits the results for the HR and operations department sub-models. Nonetheless, the results indicate differences for the models and sub-samples tested. For the operations department sub-model, the t-tests indicate that nonadopters are significantly stronger influenced by their peers from the operations departments than adopters. The same significantly stronger influence can be evaluated for the IT department and the customer sub-model. In contrast, for the superiors sub-model a significantly stronger influence for adopters can be statistically supported. The results of this research study are discussed in the following section.

## Discussion and implications

The results reveal the significant, and significantly different, social influence of various workplace referent groups on IT adoption. Overall, the strongest impact comes from superiors and the weakest from the IT department, with colleagues from HR in the middle. While the dominance of superiors is likely driven by hierarchical differences, it is somewhat saddening from an IT perspective to see that the IT group has the lowest to no social influence on IT adoption in the HR department. At first sight the focus on external CV databases might seem a likely explanation, and it would be interesting to see if the results are consistent when looking at purely internal projects. Yet internal and external integration necessities (Keim et al., 2005) make this a weak argument. Another possible reason could be the business units perceiving IT as a subordinate service provider, but we can only speculate about this and will try to control for these phenomena in future studies.

As the analysis shows, measuring the social influence of particular groups yields substantially better R<sup>2</sup> compared to the reduced model and also to traditionally measured subjective norms. As we find no indication of multicollinearity, we suggest that the concept of one cumulative social embeddedness might indeed be too naı¨ve (Ahtola, 1976). Instead, theoretical work should aim at revealing possible interaction effects and explicitly consider the adoption research object’s environment, derive relevant influential groups and treat their different impacts with due respect. As the groups scrutinized transcend the boundaries of one department, this calls for a contingent approach to IT diffusion as described by Gallivan (2001). Methodologically, a relevant measurement approach for subjective norm might thus be contingent on task, technology and group type (Eckhardt et al., 2008).

The overall finding that peer groups influence IT adoption and usage has some interesting practical implications as well. It has long been suggested, and practiced, that prospective key system users should be involved in IT development and rollout to overcome barriers to adoption. Our results indicate that the efficacy of such roles could vary with regard to where the person filling the role comes from. In particular, identifying relevant peer groups could be a strategy worth exploring to overcome assimilation gaps in individual and organization adoption (Gallivan, 2001).

Another finding is that social influence is different for adopters and non-adopters. Notably, in all cases facilitating conditions have no impact on behavioral intention among non-adopters. This supports the claims that adopters and non-adopters ‘are driven by different factors’ (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001; Dewan and Riggins, 2005; Lapointe and Rivard, 2007). In addition, the type of workplace referent group seems to make a difference as well as, for instance, social influence from the IT department is significant for non-adopters but not for adopters. Our results stem from a different context than most previous studies and cannot suggest underlying theoretical reasons yet. But they confirm that it might be instructive to develop somewhat different theoretical perspectives on adoption or non-adoption behavior. Similar to better understanding subjective norm, this will probably also mean being more precise as to the relation between established arguments surrounding the technology to be adopted (like perceived usefulness), and the adopter’s unary (from gender to live values) and relational (from peer groups, as analyzed for the workplace in this paper, to culture) particularities and their interplay (Venkatesh and Davis, 2000; Lu et al., 2005).

## Conclusion

In total, we can offer three contributions. First, based on the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003) and previous research by Lewis et al. (2003) and Kim et al. (2007), we developed a research model to measure workplace referents’ social influence on IT adopters and non-adopters. The empirical analysis shows significant impacts of social influence from workplace referents on IT adoption (precisely of colleagues in the same department on adopters and non-adopters, of the operating department on non-adopters, of the IT department on non-adopters and of superiors on adopters). Second, our analysis reveals interesting differences between adopters and non-adopters. In particular, social influence of the operating department, the IT department and superiors is significantly different for adopters and nonadopters, while the social influence of colleagues in the same department is significant for both adopters and nonadopters but not significantly different. Third, among others as an indication of a preferable direction for further research, an importance ranking could be derived using effect sizes for individual social influences on adopters and non-adopters. Excluding the non-significant relationships for social influence of customers, overall the strongest social influence on adopters and non-adopters comes from the group of superiors, followed by colleagues in the same department as well as the operating department and the IT department.

We conclude that disclosing how individuals make their technology adoption decisions remains an exciting and worthwhile research area. Our results call for an even stronger consideration of the social embeddedness of individuals. We expect that insights from social network analysis and also a social perspective on business IT alignment (Nelson and Cooprider, 1996; Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Tiwana et al., 2003) should turn out to be very useful. After all, only ‘Hermits have no peer pressure’ (Wright, 2008).
