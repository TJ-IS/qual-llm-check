# ALTAR: achieving learning through action research

## Abstract

This paper describes a collaborative action research (AR) study that took place between a U.K. university and a large U.K. manufacturing organisation, Britvic Soft Drinks Ltd. The ALTAR (Achieving Learning Through Action Research) project involved a number of AR cycles involving participants at different levels of the collaborating organisation. Britvic was already in the process of adopting knowledge management (KM) software but it was recognised that they could better exploit KM technologies by undertaking the AR project described in the paper. The project involved developing academic theory into practical concepts that influenced actions in the organisation and fed back into the academic research. The ALTAR approach and findings are described in this paper and conclusions are drawn about the AR study and its implications for AR, KM and for the future study of organisational learning through AR. The conclusions of the work are of practical use to managers in a variety of organisations and will inform academic research into cognate disciplines using the AR process.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) as a discipline embodies both theory and practice. Technologies, both hardware and software, are radically changing the way people use information and knowledge in a practical sense. The academic IS community must be involved in research and practice to facilitate these changes and to learn from them. IS researchers need to learn from practice with practitioners and this is in some ways the essence of IS. IS research is characterised by publication, an input of practice into teaching and a subsequent impact on practice, and a return to these IS ‘roots’ is called for if IS is not to disappear as a discipline. This paper reports work that is concerned with theory and practice, which utilises action research (AR) to facilitate the research, to initiate practical change and to foster organisational learning.

AR facilitates these multiple requirements as it combines in-depth theoretical development and improvement with the practical benefits for the participating company of managed change and organisational learning. AR has three main characteristics that make it suitable for research and practice. Firstly, the active involvement and collaboration of the researcher in the research can influence the research process (cf. case study research in which the researcher has the role of an observer) for a more direct ‘harvesting’ of learning from the research (Whyte, 1991). Secondly, the research is conducted with the express intention of bringing about change and improvement (cf. grounded theory research, which intends to develop an academic theory from observed practice). Thirdly, AR has an emphasis on learning and guiding change by using a cycle based on action, feedback and reflection on results to bring about improvement more quickly than would be possible through the more normal ‘academic’ publication of research (Avison et al., 2001). This paper describes an AR study that took place in a large U.K. manufacturing company, Britvic Soft Drinks Ltd. The AR study involved a number of AR cycles involving participants at different levels of the collaborating organisation. The organisation was already in the process of adopting knowledge management (KM) software but in discussion with the authors recognised that it could better exploit KM technologies by understanding what ‘knowledge’ was in its own context, what it required of the KM technologies and how this impacted on IS development strategy. The AR cycles at the different levels of the research are described. Conclusions are drawn about the complex AR study and implications for AR, KM and organisational learning through the application of AR. A narrative of the AR project is provided, an analysis is carried out and conclusions are drawn that may have significant implications for the theory and practice of organisational learning through AR. The ALTAR (Achieving Learning Through Action Research) approach was derived and evaluated during the KM project which is discussed in Shah et al. (2007). The reader is encouraged to read both papers to get an understanding of how ALTAR was used to facilitate organisational learning and influence KM practice.

## The development of AR in IS and organisation learning

The AR method has a relatively long history and has gone through a series of ‘incarnations’ as it has been applied to different domains. This was perhaps to be expected, as AR researchers identify two independent ‘roots’ for the method. One was the action-based social psychology of Kurt Lewin (Lewin, 1947), who developed a field-theory version of AR at the University of Michigan in the U.S. The other was work done by the Tavistock Clinic (later the Tavistock Institute) in the U.K., which independently developed a social psychology application of AR to study psychological and social disorders among war veterans and displaced persons (Trist, 1976). The two ‘schools’ of AR converged when Lewin joined the Tavistock team, although the natural resolution of social problems in the post-war era and a general reduction of research funding caused a reduction in the priority given to social psychology research. It also had the effect of emphasising ‘scientific’ research methods that satisfied the [UK] government’s vision of ‘science as progress’. This caused a decline in qualitative research in the U.K. and as a result AR methods ‘were seldom applied, and [were] often of marginal scientific quality’ (Baskerville, 1999).

The resurgence of AR as a research discipline coincided with another significant convergence, that of ‘social science’ research and the emerging field of IS development. The ETHICS method (Mumford & Weir, 1979), which Enid Mumford based on her work at the Tavistock Institute, used an AR approach to recognise the ‘participative’ or socio-technical nature of IS development projects. At ICI and Lancaster University in the U.K., Peter Checkland used AR to develop the influential Soft Systems Method (Checkland, 1981). AR was introduced to the IS community as a research method by Trevor Wood-Harper (1985) who explicitly incorporated AR concepts into the action-based IS development methodology called Multiview (Wood-Harper et al., 1985).

Galliers (2006) discusses the many developments that have taken place since the early work (of the Tavistock Institute) and points out that ‘emergence’ (i.e. incremental development) and ‘emancipation’ (of the end-user) are central themes of the socio-technical movement. He comments on the ‘work-in-progress’ and ‘design in use’ approaches that have come to typify the socio-technical approach to IS. This has clear relevance to the AR paradigm. There has also been recent enthusiasm and support for the use of AR in IS research journals, reflected in particular by special issues of MISQ (Baskerville & Myers, 2004) and IT&P (Lau & Kock, 2001) and a book edited by Ned Kock dedicated to this topic (Kock, 2006). In particular, there is a growing recognition in the U.S.A. of AR as an important research method for the IS discipline.

Lewin’s work was an inspiration for research into organisational learning by Chris Argyris, who had met Lewin at Clark University (Argyris & Scho¨n, 1978). Argyris believed that during the post-war period AR had become divorced from its original ‘theory building and testing’ role and had been compromised by attempts to introduce the ‘positivist rigour’ of scientific research (Argyris et al., 1985). This work to some extent rationalised the AR approach as a learning system, as Argyris & Scho¨n (1978) maintained that learning involves not just the detection but the correction of error. Using this view, a research project begins by defining a set of ‘governing variables’ associated with the research parameters. Researchers then pursue ‘action strategies’ that are intended to keep these governing values within the acceptable range. In ‘non-interventionist’ forms of research such as case study research or experimentation, the results or ‘consequences’ arising from the research actions are only used to modify or replace the action strategy, seeking another that will work within the governing variables.

This is what Argyris & Scho¨n (1974) call ‘single loop learning’. The AR approach is to question the governing variables themselves and to continually subject them to critical comparison with the consequences of the research actions. Argyris and Scho¨n describe this as ‘double loop learning’, which can use the feedback to question and possibly alter the governing variables and thereby alter the way in which the action strategies (and therefore the consequences) are framed (Anderson, 1997). Argyris and Scho¨n went on to apply this action approach to the learning process in organisations by defining ‘Type 1’ organisations (which inhibit double loop learning) and ‘Type 2’ (which promote it), thereby establishing an explicit link between AR and organisational learning. The importance of reflection in the personal learning process is stressed by Scho¨n (1983). This theme was taken up and extended to the organisational sphere by Peter Senge, who had been taught by Argyris at MIT, and who later produced a seminal and influential view of organisational learning (Senge, 1990) by combining some of the concepts of ‘action learning’ and ‘reflection’ with the same systems theory that had underpinned much IS research into a ‘Fifth Discipline’. Bell & Wood-Harper (2003) describe an approach combining Senge’s work and AR based on many years experience of IS in developing countries and this is now being used by one of the authors of that text who, interestingly, is now working for the Tavistock Institute. In the context of this research project, therefore, this correspondence between AR, the discipline of organisational learning and IS research suggests that the selection of AR as a research methodology for the KM project in Britvic is entirely appropriate.

## The research approach

It has been shown that the term ‘AR’ refers to a class of research approaches, rather than a single method, and that the various forms of AR share characteristics that distinguish AR from other ‘socio-technical’ research. A seminal survey of the AR literature presents a convincing genealogy of AR (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998, p. 94) and identifies four characteristics of AR approaches: process model, goals, structure and (possibly the most significant characteristic) the involvement of the researcher. These characteristics are summarised succinctly by Baskerville (1999, p. 4) and Lau developed a four-fold taxonomy of AR approaches: AR, participatory AR, action science and action learning. This taxonomy is then used to analyse three decades of IS AR articles (Lau, 1997).

This builds on seminal work by Peters & Robinson (1984), which identifies four common characteristics of the different AR approaches: orientation towards action and change in the short term, focus on an organisational problem, an ‘organic’ process involving a systematic series of iterative stages and a collaborative approach involving the researcher directly with participants. Baskerville & Wood-Harper (1996, 1998) emphasise the importance of relevance of the research method to the ‘real-world’ situation and stress the suitability of AR from this perspective. Susman & Evered (1978) recommend the following stages in an AR cycle: analysing the problem domain and defining the problem, planning and selection of activities to address the problem, executing the planned activities and applying them to the problem domain, evaluating the results and reflecting on what has been achieved and capturing the results of the learning from the research. It is important to differentiate between AR and ‘consulting’, as both can be traced back to the work of Lewin (Baskerville, 1999). In this paper it is taken that the main differentiator is publication (including teaching) for the good of the academic and business communities, not just for the organisation concerned.

## Background to Britvic Soft Drinks Ltd

Britvic is a leading U.K. manufacturer and distributor of fast-moving consumer goods. At the time of the project it had over 2700 employees and a turnover in excess of 600 million GB pounds. It makes and distributes a wide range of ‘household’ branded products, several of which are in the 20 top-selling brands, and is the holder of several multinational brand franchises. Before the AR project began, Britvic had been conducting a study into the use of KM technology and a decision had been made (with the advice of vendors and consultants) to acquire KM software. The authors were aware that little academic research had been done to investigate the application of KM in the manufacturing sector in the U.K. This created the potential for an AR project to ‘fill the knowledge gap’, and the fact that Britvic was already interested in adopting KM software made this a suitable choice of organisation for the AR study. In addition, they already had some experience of the KM domain and the technologies that are being marketed to support it. Managers at Britvic also felt that the organisation needed to understand the implications of the exploitation of KM and the adoption of KM technology for its existing well-developed IS planning and development strategies. In other words, there was an acceptance that it was more important for the organisation to learn as much as it could about the knowledge that it had and the potential that there may be for using this knowledge in commercial terms. This could be summarised by the phrase used by one of the managers, ‘The importance of knowing what Britvic knows’ and this ‘audit’ of organisational knowledge has been recognised as a major problem for some time (O’Dell & Grayson, 1998). What was lacking was an agreed definition of KM, a general understanding within the organisation of the potential of KM and what was required from KM technologies. It was also important to foster an understanding of KM in Britvic and to promote a KM culture.

## AR in practice – deriving ALTAR

AR has been used with apparent success in a number of projects that have similar properties to or are in similar domains to the Britvic research. Four AR projects are reported in Lo¨h et al. (2003), which describes research aimed at studying the incubation of new collaborative networks in the ICT, e-learning, and bio-technology industries in the U.K. The regional development agency contributed the conceptual framework, change management and research evaluation. The study offers some important lessons for AR planning in ‘real-world’ organisations. First, it follows a version of the long-established AR cycle described by Susman & Evered (1978) and shown in Figure 1. Secondly, it recognises the importance of a top-level AR cycle for developing and managing a collaborative project, ‘We used two levels of action research cycles in the process developmenty’ (Lo¨h et al., 2003, p. 1). Thirdly, the value of iteration as a way of ‘building learning’ is identified, ‘We then developed the detailed actions in smaller action research cyclesy’ (Lo¨h et al., 2003, p. 1) with apparent success in AR terms, ‘This approach allowed adjustments as we went alongywithout losing sight of the ‘‘big picture’’’ (Lo¨h et al., 2003, p. 1).

This iteration of cycles can be combined into a series of phases, each with a different purpose, which makes AR a powerful and flexible methodology for carrying out academic and industrial collaborative research that is proven in practice (see Figure 2). It was decided to follow the key strategies aimed at improving the rigour and contribution of AR (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996) as follows:

\- The ‘paradigm shift’ the University researchers planned to make sure that AR was appropriate for the research domain and that the outcomes and learning would be of interest.

\- A formal research agreement (the project initiation document) was to be drawn up at an early stage to define the ethical guidelines, to ensure that the subject participants gave ‘informed consent’ and that the Terms of Reference would authorise the research team to operate within the organisation.

\- A theoretical problem statement was defined as a premise to ensure that the intervention action would be valid as research and to provide a basis for any theories that may emerge from the research.

\- Data collection methods were planned as part of the operational level of the AR, including acquiring the technology to record workshops, make transcripts of meetings and to maintain structured diaries (Jepsen et al., 1989).

\- Collaboration and subject learning were promoted at the initiation of the AR project and steps were taken to foster and to maintain collaboration between the researchers and the participating subjects.

\- Iterations or cycles of AR were planned, which would continue until the immediate problem situation was explored in sufficient depth and were revealed (and possibly ‘solved’) to a satisfactory level.

\- Generalisation of results or theories was expected, but it was recognised that, ‘Generalities must be tempered with an interpretation of the extent of similar settings to which the theory can be expected to apply’ (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996).

The research approach was chosen with these key strategies in mind, taking into account the specific characteristics and requirements of Britvic as the subject organisation. It is noted that the first author spent a considerable amount of time building up knowledge of the organisation and creating what Baskerville calls ‘the client-system infrastructure’. This is based on mutual trust, ethical commitment and a joint recognition of shared interest in the research and its outcomes, ‘A key aspect of the infrastructure is the collaborative nature of the undertaking. The research scientists (sic) work closely with practitioners who are located within the clientsystem. These individuals provide the subject system knowledge and insight necessary to understand the anomalies being studied’ (Baskerville, 1999).

It was planned to conduct the management of the Britvic AR project at a number of levels and to break the project down into a number of cycles. The ‘levels of management’ of the research project (strategic, tactical and operational) were intended to match the longaccepted model of management as having three levels with different information characteristics attributed to each (Gorry & Scott-Morton, 1989). This approach was intended to ‘map onto’ the way in which the subject organisation was managed and was similar to that adopted in the Yorkshire Forward AR projects (Lo¨h et al., 2003), although the model was modified to recognise the fact that Britvic, in common with most organisations of its type, had strategic, tactical and operational management functions and possessed knowledge assets and resources at each of these levels (Shah et al., 2007).

## The levels of AR and organisational learning in ALTAR

The AR project was therefore organised, planned and managed on three levels:

\- The first level of the AR project was Strategic AR and was intended to involve strategic managers from Britvic and the first author in the first three AR cycles (see Figure 3). The three cycles; ‘Strategic Collaboration’, ‘AR Initiation’ and ‘organisational learning’, in which the strategic learning (i.e. learning derived from the whole research process), which is ‘fed up’ from the Tactical AR level. This strategic learning can be used to increase the knowledge of the organisation and its ability to increase its knowledge in the future. The results of this strategic learning are then ‘fed down’ to the Tactical AR level in the form of better strategic decisions, improved knowledge of strategic practices, etc., that provide an improved context for tactical decision-making and management.

\- The Tactical AR level also involved three cycles, ‘AR Planning’, ‘AR Co-ordination’ and ‘Harvesting AR Learning’, which in turn involved senior functional managers from Britvic and the authors of this paper as the University research management team. The planning cycle was intended to work out in advance the operational requirements for the AR, the coordination cycle served to maintain this aspect of the AR and to ‘link’ between the strategic and operational levels as the AR progressed. The ‘harvesting’ cycle was intended to evaluate the AR as a learning experience and to make the findings and experience of the research available in a form that could be ‘fed up’ to the strategic level as Tactical Learning (see Figure 3). The results of this learning process were ‘fed down’ to the operational level in the form of improved functional management (e.g. Brand Management).

\- The Operational AR level was designed to include four AR cycles: ‘AR Team Building’, ‘AR Supervising’, ‘AR Monitoring’ and ‘AR Reflection’. ‘Team Building’ would involve putting together a team of managers from Britvic and recruiting an operational researcher in the form of a Ph.D. student by the University. It was expected that the ‘Supervising’ and ‘Monitoring’ cycles would involve the usual research activities such as the design of the research instruments and data collection and analysis processes and the supervision of the student. It was also intended that this level would include a ‘reflection’ cycle in which the research team would collate the gathered data and ‘draw together’ operational learning (i.e. learning about the research process) which concluded with the results of the operational AR learning being ‘fed down’ to the AR Operations at the lowest level to inform and improve the AR operations at the lowest level of Figure 3 (Shah et al., 2007).

The AR operations (i.e. conducting interviews, collecting and analysing data and feeding back the results) were carried out under the control of this AR management structure, and the ‘learning’ at the Operational, Tactical and Strategic levels was ‘fed up’ through the AR project structure at all three levels through the regular workshops and reports. This direct contact between the research management structure and Britvic’s managers was instrumental in updating and improving its knowledge assets and resources through the learning gained, as was confirmed by the direct statements of the senior manager in charge of the project on behalf of Britvic.

Thus, the research operations informed and improved the University’s research management processes as well as the KM potential of Britvic. In this context, the whole KM research project can be viewed as a coordinated set of AR cycles at four levels (three managerial and one operational), each of which provided valuable learning and feedback for the organisation at its appropriate level. In Figure 3 the whole structure is displayed, the participant groups are identified and the type of learning is indicated. The model is informed by and relates to the ‘double-loop learning model’ of Argyris & Scho¨n (1978) in which Strategic AR level controls the governing variables of the organisational learning process (i.e. ‘sets the parameters’) through the AR, the Tactical AR level governs the ‘action strategy’ (i.e. the conduct and operations of the organisational learning process) through the AR, and the Operational AR level is directly involved with measuring and influencing the ‘consequences’ of the organisational learning process through the AR activities (see Figure 4). The double-loop learning links the consequences (at the Operational AR Level) and is therefore analogous to the strategic learning indicated in Figure 3, and the single loop learning indicates the link between the Operational and Tactical AR levels and relates to the operational and tactical learning that is gained at these respective levels.

## Undertaking the ALTAR project

A major challenge with AR is that it involves a very close participation (in fact a partnership) between the researchers and the research subjects and implies an ability on the part of the researchers to influence and to act upon the organisation being researched. This is particularly important when the AR project has the potential to impact not only the operational levels but also the strategic and tactical. This implies a considerable degree of trust on the part of the collaborating organisation and in the case of the University and Britvic this partnership was already in place. At the time the first author was already undertaking other projects with Britvic and was working with the strategic managers to develop a longer term strategic partnership. She took the opportunity to work with the organisation to understand the issues being faced by them and to identify ways in which the University could contribute. This was an innovative approach that entailed a certain amount of risk, but the strategic managers of Britvic were very open to it and could see the potential benefits. Throughout all levels of the AR project workshops and interviews were used regularly and frequently for discussion, planning, reflection, identifying learning and evaluation.

As a result the first author set up a strategic collaborative partnership event, inviting a number of Britvic’s senior IT managers to visit the University to learn more about the Faculty’s work and explore opportunities for collaboration. This was a significant commitment on their part as the cost of taking such a large number of the IT employees out of the day-to-day activities to participate was considerable both financially and logistically but underlying this was a commitment to innovation, research and partnership in order to ensure that they remained at the ‘leading edge’ of KM and also because they valued the contribution of the first author. As a result of this ‘Day of Collaboration’ and a number of meetings between the first author and strategic IT managers, Britvic identified a number of areas of possible collaboration between themselves and the University. One of these was the KM expertise of the authors and their ability to lead research and bring research-led thinking to practical KM projects.

The strategic AR involved the authors in a number of meetings at both Britvic and the University. As a result of these meetings it was agreed that KM had the potential for a collaborative project. Further meetings were held to develop the aims and objectives of the project and to determine how a potential collaboration might be the vehicle for achieving the project aims. At Britvic projects were typically initiated by development of an internal document project initiation document. These would be developed over a period of time and would be discussed with various stakeholders in draft form. Feedback from stakeholders would then be used to modify the document until it was agreed to be a suitable input to a meeting to consider the strategic viability of projects. The authors worked with a senior manager to develop a project initiation document for the proposed AR project which in this case went through a number of iterations. It was discussed and modified in discussion with an increasingly broad group until the main stakeholders were satisfied that it was ready to be considered as a project proposal. The project initiation document was submitted to a formal meeting of the ITSC (IT Steering Committee) where it was considered in competition with other proposed projects.

The strategic manager at Britvic who had collaborated with the authors to develop this project proposal went to the ITSC meeting to present the proposal. The project was seen to have merit and the ITSC approved its progress to the next stage. There was particular value placed on the continued strategic collaboration between Britvic and the University and the opportunity for Britvic to broaden the inputs to its project processes which prior to this largely relied on its own IT staff and External consultancies but could now benefit from academic research-led thinking. By going through the project initiation document development process, which had been very time consuming for the authors and the Britvic strategic manager, the project had established a presence among the potential stakeholders, a number of people important to the project had been ‘brought on board’ and the appropriate aims of the project had been defined. Another outcome was that the KM project had high visibility and the IT Director provided regular updates on its progress to Britvic Board meetings.

The tactical aspects began several months after starting discussions about the AR project when the authors were sent a letter of collaboration from Britvic, following its discussion and approval at the ITSC. A project sponsor was appointed from within Britvic who worked closely with the first author to detail the project more precisely and to appoint a project manager. The project was then further developed over a number of weeks leading to a brief letter specifying the financial and contractual arrangements between Britvic and the first author on behalf of the University. The first author had also to hold similar discussions with managers of the Research and Enterprise service of the University and the faculty Business Manager to discuss the contractual arrangements and the University staff to be involved. Further, the University showed their commitment to the AR project by providing the first author with a Ph.D. scholarship to be used for the student to be involved on the project and agreeing for this to be supplemented by a contribution from the monies the University would be paid by Britvic for the project. Setting up the AR project involved a significant financial investment by the collaborating organisation and the University and involved a considerable amount of work by the first author. It was seen, however, that the strategic management of the AR project was of crucial importance in securing the firm collaboration of both parties and ensuring a sound initiation of the project.

The operational aspects of the AR project were limited to well-defined interventions at Britvic which were carefully planned and scheduled. As part of the planning the first author was asked by Britvic to set up a research team of University participants as appropriate. The expectation of Britvic was that the AR operations would be managed at the strategic and tactical level and that the first author would be involved in all discussions regarding the interventions of the researchers. From the previous interactions between the first author and organisation, the strategic managers were satisfied that the first author understood their needs at all levels and had confidence in her ability to ensure that the operational interventions would involve attitudes, behaviours and language that would be suitable to their organisation. This aspect of the operational AR was managed by the first author, ensuring that prior to all interventions (e.g. workshops and participant interviews) involving the researchers in Britvic, the authors conducted detailed planning of how that interaction would be conducted, the tools and techniques to be employed and the particular terminology that would be used with the clients. The satisfactory conduct and conclusions of each cycle of the research was marked by a ‘debriefing’ workshop and the value of the organisational learning at each level was discussed at meetings between the authors and the relevant managers within Britvic. In meetings with the first author, feedback regarding any issues arising from the conduct of the AR operations being carried by the research student would also be given.

## Discussion

After the completion of the project, an evaluation session identified a number of important results, for Britvic, for the University and for the individuals involved in the project. These can be summarised as follows:

\- Although there were exceptions, a majority of the knowledge workers felt they had gained benefit from their involvement in the AR process. Many felt that the contact they had with the researchers and with their own managers in the AR had enhanced their own awareness of the knowledge resources that were available to them and how those resources could be used (Shah et al., 2007). They also reported that their involvement in AR had increased their perception of the value of ‘everyday’ knowledge in increasing their personal effectiveness in the workplace. Additionally, it was observed by some that the AR project had created an unusual opportunity for reflection on the nature of their work and of their use of knowledge. This indicates that the results of operational learning were being ‘fed down’ in an effective manner from the Operational AR Management Level (see Figure 3) and therefore the ‘single loop’ of organisational learning was complete (see Figure 4).

\- The managers and senior managers reported that the process of ‘team building’ which had taken place in an

AR context had produced benefits in terms of organisational effectiveness. Their staff members were sharing knowledge more effectively and with less evidence of personal ‘ownership’ of knowledge assets. They were also seen to be using corporate and functional knowledge more effectively and were sharing their personal learning experiences in a way that improved the effectiveness of their departments and teams. There were suggestions that the AR project had acted as a ‘pump’ primer for expanding personal learning into ‘departmental learning’. Some of the senior managers indicated that because of the benefits of organisational learning, they would be prepared to participate in AR projects in their departments or functions for purposes other than KM.

\- The strategic managers (and particularly the project sponsor) saw the whole AR project as a valuable exercise in both AR and KM. The overall effectiveness of the organisational learning gained from the project showed them the potential of AR as a way of mobilising the potential of staff and sharing organisational knowledge and learning not just within but between the various departments. This is confirmed by a letter from the project sponsor that was sent to the authors after the project.

\- For the researchers and the Universities, the project provided some valuable experience in the conduct of a substantial AR exercise and highlighted the potential for using AR in a large commercial organisation. There were also major outcomes in the area of KM, as the research student incorporated the research into a KM framework that formed the basis of a Doctoral qualification and later as a commercial product that is being marketed through consultancy (Sharp, 2004).

## Conclusions

A number of important conclusions were drawn from the ALTAR project. First, there was a ‘paradigm shift’ and change was implicit in the ALTAR process, most participants responded positively to the notion of change, as they felt more involved and ‘useful’ than if the change had been imposed from outside. Secondly, the ‘groundwork’ (the formal agreement and work that was done on project initiation was essential in defining in advance and maintaining a management focus for the project. Experience from our research indicates that academic– industrial research collaboration of this nature requires very careful negotiation of exactly what is involved in the project. The expectations of both sets of collaborators may be considerably different, and must be discussed in advance and a realistic set of expectations and objectives must be agreed. Thirdly, the academic theories that were informed by the AR process formed the basis of an outcome that was tested rigorously in academic terms (Sharp, 2004). It was clearly possible to differentiate between AR in this case and consultancy (Baskerville, 1999). Fourthly, the operational aspects of data collection and analysis proceeded smoothly due to careful work by the research student and were able to inform the theories that were developed from the AR by use of regular workshop sessions held by the researchers. Fifth, collaboration between the researchers and the managers and knowledge workers of Britvic was maintained throughout the project. The staff of Britvic ‘bought into’ the ALTAR project in a way that confirmed the feasibility of the approach in a ‘real’ commercial context. Sixth, the use of iterative cycles and levels showed that the AR approach embodied in ALTAR matched the organisational learning process ‘in the small’ as well as ‘in the large’.
