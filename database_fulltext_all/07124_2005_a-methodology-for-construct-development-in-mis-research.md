# A methodology for construct development in MIS research

Bruce R. Lewis<sup>1</sup>, Gary F. Templeton<sup>2</sup> and Terry Anthony Byrd<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

This paper presents a comprehensive methodology for developing constructs in MIS research. It is applicable to both individual and organizational levels of analysis, depending on the nature of the concept under study. The methodology is presented as a research guide progressing through three stages: (1) domain definition, (2) instrument construction, and (3) evaluation of measurement properties. The methodology addresses six key measurement properties (content validity, factorial validity, reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, nomological validity), which are discussed in detail. An assessment of the proposed methodology indicates that its adoption in MIS research would greatly improve the rigor of construct development projects. This is evidenced by the wide range of quality publications that have used its techniques and its performance when compared to a number of prominent standards for assessing construct development research. ## Introduction

Since its inception, the field of MIS has been criticized for its lack of formal and consistent development (Keen, 1980; Straub et al., 2004). The problems, although natural circumstances of young disciplines, have been addressed at the highest levels of MIS research (Straub, 1989; Boudreau et al., 2001; Benbasat & Zmud, 2003). For instance, the rigor vs relevance debate on the quality of research (Keen, 1991; Benbasat & Zmud, 1999) is one topic intended to address some of the methodological problem areas in the discipline. Sethi & King (1991) note that ‘greater attention to methodological issues is essential for the evolvement of the field’ (p. 455). Rigor has been problematic in MIS research, particularly with regard to measurement (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Chau (1999) reports that ‘scales developed more recently are no more reliable than those developed in the 1980s and that ‘for scales used in basic research, more than 40% of them had reliability lower than the minimal acceptable level’ (p. 217). It is apparent from these and other observations that there is room for improvement in the development of measurement instruments in MIS research.

According to the perspective expressed by Kuhn (1962), MIS would be categorized as experiencing a ‘revolutionary’ (as opposed to ‘normal’) science era, whereby its advancements and ongoing structure are consistently challenged due to the varying perspectives held on the subject. Advancing MIS from revolutionary to normal science requires the successful definition and measurement of its observed phenomena so that those structures can be tested for usefulness and acceptance. Straub et al.

(2004) noted that the scientific basis of the MIS field is at risk without solid validation of its measuring instruments. Further, research related to construct development plays a significant role in the developmental path of MIS research (Keen, 1980).

Perhaps the most influential methodological guide used in MIS construct development was articulated by Churchill (1979) in the field of marketing, and today methodologies derived from the Churchill approach have become the norm in marketing. On the other hand, only a small number of general guides have been used in the MIS field while the details have been addressed by using an extraordinarily varied and disparate set of techniques. Consequently, the resulting problems associated with construct development in MIS have become an increasing topic at conferences and in discussion groups. Until this point an adequately meticulous and detailed construct development methodology simply has not emerged in MIS.

With the goal of moving MIS toward a normal state, this paper presents a rigorous methodology for developing constructs, based on the Churchill (1979) framework. The methodology proposed is firmly grounded on sound psychometric practice, as summarized in the Discussion. The methods included in the proposed methodology have undergone extensive development and testing in a wide range of contexts and have been employed successfully in several MIS studies, as reported in the Assessment. Based on our experiences, researchers can effectively develop constructs and build valid measurement instruments through the application of this methodology. As it is novel (through its rigor and detail) and useful (as evidenced by the publication record of its users), we believe it contributes significantly to the general methodology literature by presenting a valuable and comprehensive set of guidelines for construct-based research. In particular, its successful track record indicates that it fills a distinct void in MIS research.

In the next section, we present a brief discussion of two basic issues relevant to the proposed methodology. This is followed by a description of the methodology and a discussion of how it addresses validity, the universal goal of construct development projects. Finally, we offer an evaluation of the proposed methodology.

## Foundation issues

Construct development endeavours ultimately intend to create more relevant and precise tools for measurement. In cases where constructs of interest are believed to be of great significance in practice, the unavailability of sound data collection instruments constrains further development of knowledge in research streams and compromises the ability to accurately assess phenomena in practice. As such, two issues must be addressed by any construct development methodology: level of analysis and philosophy of knowledge.

## Level of analysis

One of the most important ways to differentiate between instruments is by level of analysis, of which there are two primary categories: organization and individual. Organization-level instruments use human proxy respondents to assess aspects about organizational properties, such as its size, form, or performance. In MIS, organization-level measures traditionally focus on constructs related to the presence and/or use of technology in organizations. For instance, Byrd & Turner (2000) developed connectivity, functionality, compatibility, and data transparency measures to assess significant aspects of organizational IT infrastructure. Other examples include organizational learning (Templeton et al., 2002), information management strategy (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2001), CAPITA (Sethi & King, 1994), and interunit communication (Ghoshal et al., 1994). These measures emphasize the organizational situation and can help practitioners understand and formulate structures that better fit the organizational environment. Individual-level instruments in MIS studies assess human level perceptions through direct questioning and typically often focus on the interactions between humans and technology. Examples of such constructs are computer user satisfaction (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988), web-customer satisfaction (McKinney et al., 2002), and e-commerce trust (McKnight et al., 2002). Individual-level measures are used to help guide the design of IS for improvements in use and productivity. The methodology presented herein is applicable to creating measurement instruments for both organization and individual levels of analysis.

## Philosophy of knowledge

It is appropriate to articulate a baseline philosophy of knowledge before the proposed methodology is described. This section discusses (1) the philosophical elements that inform the proposed methodology and (2) usage considerations that are generally accepted across the social sciences.

## Philosophical elements

In instrumentation, knowledge exists at four abstraction levels that form the whole of the concept of interest. First, the domain definition is a brief yet holistic description that is a reasonable and supportable representation of the concept of interest. In MIS studies, domain definitions have derived from a variety of sources, including pre-existing philosophies (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Torkzadeh & Dhillon, 2002), personnel surveys (Segars & Grover, 1998), focus groups (Smith et al., 1996), and most commonly, literature reviews (Venkatraman, 1989; Sethi & King, 1991; Sethi & King, 1994; Byrd & Turner, 2000; Lewis & Byrd, 2003). The domain definition is a general guide to what the instrument is designed to assess. Based on our experience, a good domain definition includes four attributes. First, it expresses general components of meaning that should be easily accessible from the literature, practice, or logic. Second, it should include level of analysis to avoid confusion in the resulting question pool. Third, the purpose should be included to inform the researchers and research community as to what the construct generally leads. Finally, it is important to address the constraints (what it is not) of the definition in order to provide a limit to project scope.

Second, the dimensions abstraction level contains the theoretical parts of the domain definition. Just as any assertion by researchers, dimensions are justified by observations from practice, reviews of the literature and logic (Webster & Watson, 2002). In MIS studies, dimensions have been derived from sources ranging from researcher interpretations (Lewis et al., 1995) to multidimensional frameworks accepted in the field (Templeton et al., 2002). The complete set of dimensions forms a more precise depiction of the domain definition it intends to represent. We refer to these knowledge elements as ‘dimensions’ before empirical testing takes place and ‘construct components’ or ‘factors’ afterwards. The distinction is important because dimensional structures may not exactly be empirically supported. After empirical tests, dimensions are still accepted as the underlying sources of meaning for the final instrument (and are therefore sometimes referred to as ‘underlying dimensions’). However, the set of empirically derived construct components are accepted as a core knowledge contribution and essentially specify the practical operational definition of the construct.

Third, item stems are the theoretical sub-parts of dimensions and are often extracted from a combination of literature review and experience surveys in MIS studies (Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Venkatraman, 1989; Rainer & Harrison, 1993; Segars & Grover, 1998; Lewis & Byrd, 2003). This is the first abstraction level where the elements represent units of knowledge about the concept at hand and are therefore more tightly bound to its true meaning. The name of an item stem is a one- to threeword phrase that describes a part of a dimension (thus, several item stems combine to represent a single dimension). Since item stem names are chosen by researchers, they can be interpreted differently between observers and are therefore imprecise depictions of the construct. Discrepancies between researcher and respondent perceptions of the meanings of item stems can cause response bias for individual items across the sample. However, this bias is mitigated by an adequate sample size. As with the domain definition and the set of dimensions, the combined set intends to represent the whole of the concept of interest.

Fourth, statement items derive from the item stem set. MIS studies have used experience surveys as mechanisms for creating and validating the structure of statement items (Rainer & Harrison, 1993; Byrd & Turner, 2000). Each item stem is used to generate multiple alternative statements that will be deliberated and refined before a subjective choice is made of the single most fitting statement for that item stem. Generating and selecting among alternative statements for each item is called ‘domain sampling,’ a process that traditionally depends upon researcher rather than external judgment. Thus, statement items should represent a single stem, which represents a single dimension, which represents the overall domain definition. Researchers should take care that the meanings of statement items are adequately (1) unitary (have a single meaning) and (2) independent of other statement items. In classical testing theory, differences between true and observed scores are an accepted part of practice (Spector, 1992). Similarly, differences in fit (i.e. content validity) between item stems and associated statement candidates exist in instrumentation. While these deviations are an accepted part of statement generation, the selection process aims to reduce variation in content validity.

The philosophical elements described here represent a hierarchy that decomposes in succession beginning with the domain definition and ending with statement items. The deliberation and design of philosophical elements can have profound impacts on both project efficiency and the rate of scientific advancement in the field. During the initial creation of elements, a researcher may coordinate design efforts by eliciting the input of either (1) other members on the research team or (2) experts. The latter is preferred, although potentially more costly and less convenient. In cases where used, we suggest using two different expert pools from which to select question reviewers. First, practitioner and academic subject matter experts can be used to evaluate the fit between each item and associated construct. This can be an important early determinant of internal validity. Second, academic experts on measurement can assess instrumentation quality. In particular, the statement items should also reflect the intended sample frame. The quality of element and sample frame designs, in addition to how well the two fit, are considerations determined early in the project that will ultimately affect external validity (i.e. generalizability). Thus, the early and prudent use of experts in the design of philosophical elements can expedite scientific progress and make construct development projects more efficient.

## Use considerations

The principal use of this methodology, and the focus of this paper, is the initial exploration into the underlying meaning of a construct of interest and development of a corresponding measurement instrument. There are two clear contributions regarding the exploratory use of the methodology. First, this method guides researchers to settle on an adequate domain definition. Thus, the process of instrumentation helps advance science by requiring the articulation and justification of construct definitions. Second, the methodology challenges the researcher to initially construct a quality measure, based on its domain definition. Both products signify the contribution of new knowledge elements in the field. Researchers may augment this methodology a number of ways. For one, when presenting findings for organizational constructs, norms should be enumerated within each construct, industry, respondent group, or other meaningful criteria (see Rainer & Harrison, 1993; Lewis et al., 1995; Templeton et al., 2002). Researchers might also consider iterating back through the steps in order to enhance the quality of existing measures. For instance, Spector (1992) recommends that the items contained in a given instrument be modified (i.e. added, reduced, or edited) in order to achieve certain metrical goals.

It is generally accepted that construct development research requires extensive time and financial resources. For the sake of efficiency, and important research goal (Kerlinger, 1986), good practice includes adapting the methodology to fit the research context. As with IS development projects, prioritization is an important part of successful construct development work. An important consideration for construct developers is the external validation of each successive philosophical element (the domain definition, dimensions, item stems, and statement items). Once adequately validated (using historically accepted practice), philosophical elements become contributions to knowledge within themselves. Understanding this aspect of construct development can greatly enhance the efficiency of projects as well as progress in the topic area. In summary, researchers and reviewers should not view construct development projects as single studies resulting in single products. Such a mindset implies a great deal of risk. Assuming good practice, each phase of validation has a distinct deliverable that is both a contribution to the field as well as subject to subsequent research and deliberation. It has been our experience that each validation stage is a milestone researchers can publish and use in subsequent research.

## Description of the methodology

In this section, we describe a comprehensive methodology for developing sound measures for MIS constructs. This methodology is proposed for use by researchers who are creating new measurement instruments from scratch. The methodology is organized into three stages (see Figure 1). First, the domain of the construct is established. Next, based on that domain, a set of statement items is generated and a field survey instrument is designed, evaluated, and refined through multiple steps. In the final stage, data are collected through administrations of the instrument and its measurement properties are examined and iteratively optimized using multiple techniques.

This methodology evolved from the basic approach to construct development established by Churchill (1979). In the methodology described herein, four steps originating from Churchill are employed using techniques that have been added and refined over years of practice. Each phase in the Churchill approach focuses on satisfying one of four key measurement considerations: (1) construct domain specification, (2) construction of items, (3) data collection, and (4) measure purification. In the proposed methodology, particular attention is paid to managing data collection and iterative purification.

## Stage I: domain

The first stage in developing a construct is to establish the domain of the idea. This endeavour should produce three items of information: (1) the premise, which specifies the purpose and/or importance of the construct, (2) the conceptual definition, which describes the construct in general or theoretical terms, and (3) a list of dimensions, which represent the elements of the construct. These deliverables may be derived from numerous sources, such as a review of the literature, case studies, open-ended questionnaires, interviews, or some combination of these sources. However, in all situations, the material resulting from these efforts will need to be synthesized to produce the information necessary for the specification of the construct domain.

Content analysis, a common technique employed in the social sciences to draw inferences from text (Weber, 1985), is an appropriate method to address the domain specification task and can be used on any of the sources (i.e. literature, interview transcripts, and case studies). The material to be analyzed should, of course, come from relevant sources. For example, a review of the literature may target pieces that contain the name of the construct in the title, keyword list, or abstract. The rationale for this search standard would be based on the assumption that authors who, in some fashion, label or identify their publications with the construct name are contributing to the explanation of the concept. In a similar manner, reasonable standards must be specified for selection using other sources to insure that the perspectives assessed are appropriate to the construct under consideration.

A content analysis of selected material will typically involve multiple iterations through that material, each time targeted to different information. A useful approach is to make one or more iterations to determine the specific parts of the construct domain. The premise of the construct should be established first, since the conceptual definition should flow from it. Likewise, the dimensions of the construct should flow from the conceptual definition. In all likelihood, arriving at the construct dimensions will require the most effort. Toward this end, broad categories relating to the elements of the construct should be derived first. Next, these categories are iteratively refined until they represent relatively distinct groupings, which then embody the dimensions of the construct. The next task is to determine item stems, the sub-parts for each dimension. To the greatest extent possible, multiple item stems should be created for each of the dimensions of the construct. Final iterations should be made to assign specific statements from the authors to each item stem within each dimension. Lastly, the statement that ‘best’ represents the item stem is selected from the set of all statements assigned to each item stem. This approach is commonly referred to as ‘domain sampling’ within each item stem (Bollen & Lennox, 1991), which results in a collection of item statements that approximate a good description of the construct of interest.

The deliverables of Stage I, which comprise the domain of the construct under development, should be a formal statement of the premise of the construct, a conceptual definition of the construct, and a master list of item statements organized by construct dimensions. The item statements provide the basis for the instrument, which is the focus of Stage II.

## Stage II: instrument construction

In the second stage of the methodology, an instrument is produced and improved through multiple iterations. To begin, each item statement in the domain should be converted into an item on the instrument. The form of these statements will depend on the nature of the measurement scale (e.g. a Likert scale) and the unit of analysis that will be the target of the instrument (i.e. individual or organization). It is imperative that researchers uniformly apply the same unit of analysis to all item stems and item statements during their creation and modification.

## Pre-test

A pre-test of the questionnaire should be conducted next. Pre-testing is a first attempt to get empirical feedback from a highly controlled sample to assess the appropriateness of the original instrument. Pre-test subjects should be selected in accordance with the determined unit of analysis and should be knowledgeable about the construct under study. The pre-test respondents should be asked to complete the instrument first and then critique matters important for initial instrument design, such as format, content, understandability, terminology, and ease and speed of completion. Respondents should also be asked to identify specific items that should be added or deleted from the instrument, as well as to make suggestions for enhancements. Responses from the pretest should be reviewed and adjustments made to the instrument based on the feedback of the respondents. This step begins the cyclical process of data collection and instrument purification that continues throughout Stage II.

## Pilot test

Following revisions from the pre-test, a pilot test should be undertaken to further appraise and purify the instrument. The pilot test is a ‘dress-rehearsal’ of the instrument with a small sample. Respondents chosen for the pilot test should be selected based on the pre-defined unit of analysis, as well as being similar to the population that will be the target of the administrations of the instrument in Stage III. Pilot test respondents should be asked to fill out the instrument, and then comment on difficulties in completing the instrument and offer suggestions for improvement, including specifying any additional item statements they felt were missing or items that should be deleted. Again, results of the pilot test should be examined and appropriate adjustments made to the instrument based on the observations of the respondents.

## Item screening

The purpose of this step is to empirically screen the items on the instrument utilizing a quantitative procedure developed by Lawshe (1975) which determines whether each item on the instrument adequately represents the content domain of the construct. This procedure begins with the selection of a content evaluation panel composed of a group of experts knowledgeable about the concept. After the pilot test revisions, the panelists are sent a list of the items from the updated instrument and are asked to evaluate the relevance of each to the construct on a three-point scale: ‘1 ¼ Not Relevant,’ ‘2 ¼ Important (But Not Essential),’ ‘3 ¼ Essential.’ From these data provided by the expert panel, a content validity ratio (CVR) is computed for each item according to the following formula:

$$
\mathrm{CVR} = (\mathrm{n-N/2}) / (\mathrm{N/2})
$$

where N is the total number of respondents and n is the frequency count of the number of panelists rating the item as appropriate, either ‘3 ¼ Essential’ or ‘3 ¼ Essential and 2 ¼ Important (But Not Essential).’ Lawshe (1975) only utilized the ‘3 ¼ Essential’ response category in computing the CVR. However, a less stringent criterion (both 2 and 3) could also be justified since responses of both ‘Important (But Not Essential)’ and ‘Essential’ are positive indicators of an item’s relevance to the construct.

The CVR for each item should be evaluated for statistical significance, using the table published in Lawshe (1975). Statistical significance implies some level of content validity for the item, whereas statistical nonsignificance indicates an unacceptable level of content validity. At this point, items that are not statistically significant based on their CVR should be dropped from the instrument. All of the statistically significant items can be kept, or another procedure, such as a discrimination index, can be employed to determine which of the remaining items should be retained (Lawshe, 1975, P. 6).

The deliverable of Stage II is a final draft of the instrument. In Stage III, the working instrument will be iteratively refined and its measurement properties evaluated.

## Stage III: evaluation of measurement properties

In the third stage of the methodology, data are collected through multiple administrations of the instrument and the measurement properties of the instrument are assessed and optimized with each administration (i.e. in different samples from repeated collections). The samples should be randomly selected from populations of individuals or organizations that are relevant to the construct under investigation. Following a strategy of method triangulation, both exploratory and confirmatory techniques are applied sequentially to the different samples in order to achieve the best results.

## Exploratory assessment

For the first round, a random sample should be drawn from the sample frame based on the pre-established unit of analysis. The instrument, after revisions from the item screening, is then distributed to these target respondents. Once all responses have been received, three aspects of response quality should be evaluated. First, the response rate should be computed and should be at least 20% (Malhotra & Grover, 1998). Second, statistical power should be examined by calculating the subject-to-item ratio, which should be at least 5 to 1 (Hair et al., 1995). Third, non-response bias in the returned sample should be assessed to insure that the sample data adequately reflects the population. This can be accomplished by testing important strategic characteristics using either a one-sample $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test (Byrd & Turner, 2000) or where appropriate, a one-sample t-test (Rainer & Harrison, 1993).

Exploratory factor analysis should be used to empirically derive the initial set of factors for the construct. In order to verify that the data are amenable to factor analysis, two tests should first be executed: the Kaiser–Meyer– Olkin test and the Bartlett sphericity test. Assuming positive findings from these two tests, the factor analysis may proceed. The number of factors obtained from the factor analysis procedure should be based on the criteria that the eigenvalues of the chosen factors be one or greater (Nunnally, 1978). A scree plot could also be used to further verify the number of factors to be included in the solution. Several rotation techniques may be tried to reach the ‘best’ factor solution. The rotation solutions should be judged on simplicity (Sethi & King, 1991), interpretability (Lederer & Sethi, 1992), and the percent of variance explained by the set of designated factors (Straub, 1989).

Using the results of the selected rotation method, an appropriate factor-loading threshold must be established. The factor loading for each item is a measure of consistency between items in a factor. An item should be assigned to a factor if the item’s loading exceeds the established threshold. There are three considerations associated with threshold selection. First is to maximize the loading threshold. A high threshold ensures greater within-factor correlation, which improves reliability. Second, the threshold should be set in order to include as many items as possible, because the unassigned items explain a certain percentage of the systematic covariance among all items and the objective is to minimize this amount. Third, the threshold should minimize items loading on multiple factors. Thresholds for exploratory factor analysis used in other MIS construct development studies include: 0.50 (Straub, 1989), 0.45 (Lewis et al., 1995), and 0.35 (Lederer & Sethi, 1992). Items that have no loadings exceeding the threshold should be dropped from further analysis, and thus from the instrument in future administrations. In addition, items loading on multiple factors should be likewise dropped. However, subjective judgement should be applied, so that an item with strongly justified theoretical relevance is not lost at this point.

Once each item has been assigned to only one factor, labels for the factors should be created to represent the meaning of the factor based on the items that load on that factor. These factors, and their subordinate items, represent the first glimpse at an empirically derived operational definition for the construct of interest.

Reliability analysis is concerned with the internal consistency of a measurement instrument. A common statistic for evaluating reliability, the coefficient of internal consistency (alpha), is computed for each of the construct components (factors) determined from the factor analysis, using the same data (Cronbach, 1971). Cronbach’s alpha ranges from 0 (completely unreliable) to 1 (perfectly reliable). An alpha statistic of 0.5–0.6 is sufficient for exploratory research, but 0.8 or higher is inevitably more desirable (Nunnally, 1978). If a factor does not exhibit acceptable reliability, the approach for increasing the alpha statistic is to drop items from the factor based on the magnitude of the item loadings. At each iteration, items with the smallest loadings should be dropped and the affect on the alpha statistic of each factor observed. The process is stopped when an acceptable alpha is achieved.

During the exploratory analysis process the instrument is improved by removing items. This could be justified in the factor analysis step due to loadings below the established threshold or because an item loads on multiple factors. Likewise, items may be dropped to improve the reliability statistics on the factors.

## Confirmatory assessment

The final set of items from the exploratory analysis represents the initial a priori measurement model of the construct under investigation. These items should be cast into an updated edition of the measurement instrument and this new version should be administered to a different random sample. As with the first administration, the data from this second administration should be evaluated for response rate, statistical power, and nonresponse bias. The measurement properties of the instrument will again be evaluated and the instrument will be tuned using the second sample, this time via confirmatory factor analysis.

Confirmatory factor analysis ‘provides an appropriate means of assessing the efficacy of measurement among scale items and the consistency of a pre-specified structural equation model with its associated network of theoretical concepts’ (Segars & Grover, 1998, p. 148). In utilizing the confirmatory factor model, the step-bystep process suggested in Hair et al. (1995) should be applied. First, the assumptions underlying confirmatory factor analysis should be verified. The requirement of multivariate normality can be judged by testing each of the variables for univariate normality. To confirm the normality assumption relative to the data from the instrument items, the moments about the mean and the skewness and kurtosis statistics of these variables should be examined. Other statistical considerations include covariance vs correlation matrices, sample size, and identification of the structural model (Hair et al., 1995).

Confirmatory factor analysis was originally formulated for use with the variance/covariance matrix (Hair et al., 1995). However, now, the variance/covariance matrix, the correlation matrix, or even the data file itself can be used in most modern confirmatory factor analysis software packages. There are some small differences in using these different data forms when analyzing causal relationships in these software packages, but the results using either of them is very much the same in confirmatory factor analysis. Therefore, it is acceptable to use either the variance/covariance matrix, the correlation matrix, or the data file in the confirmatory factor analysis.

Sample size is also an important consideration in any statistical test because it provides a basis for the estimation of sampling error. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), used in the popular confirmatory factor analysis packages such as LISREL, typically requires a sample size of at least 100. However, as the sample size gets large, say 400 or more, MLE becomes too sensitive and almost any difference in the goodness-of-fit measures indicates a poor fit (Marsh et al., 1988). A good rule for the sample size is 5–10 observations for each estimated parameter (Hair et al., 1995). For a typical model, this rule generally means about 100–200 is a ‘good sample size’ if the more liberal number of observations is utilized (Hoelter, 1983).

Identification is the inability of the proposed model to generate unique or consistent estimates. Typical symptoms of an identification problem include getting obviously specious results from the computer program and the computer program not being able to complete its analysis thus giving incomplete results. A primary consideration in combating identification problems is that one must have more equations than unknowns in the analysis (Hair et al., 1995). Unfortunately, as the complexity of structural equation models has risen, there is no guaranteed approach for ensuring that these more complex models are identified and that they produce consistent results. The identification problem can be tested by re-estimating the model in multiple runs, each time with different starting values. Results that converge at approximately the same point each time provide evidence of a lack of identification problems in the data.

The factors from the exploratory factor analysis are the starting point for the confirmatory analysis. Using confirmatory factor analysis, the factor model should be re-evaluated with data from the second sample. The confirmatory evaluation of a construct should start by assessing the unidimensionality of the construct. Unidimensionality is defined as the existence of one latent trait or construct underlying a set of measures (Anderson et al., 1987). In order to thoroughly assess unidimensionality, the factors derived from confirmatory factor analysis should first be evaluated individually and then as a collective network. According to Segars & Grover (1998), this procedure ‘provides the fullest evidence of measurement efficacy and also reduces the likelihood of confounds in full structural equation modelling which may arise due to excessive error in measurement’ (p. 148). Unidimensionality in confirmatory factor analysis is usually evaluated with the values obtained in a set of measurement indicators from a series of runs of a factor program such as EQS or LISREL. To begin, the measurement properties of each factor should be examined separately with the objective of isolating and locating any errors in specifying the factor. Items should be retained in a factor if their standardized loading exceeds the established threshold; items with a loading below this cutoff should be dropped from further consideration. However, researchers must always be careful when dropping items. First, removing items from factors may cause identification problems (Segars & Grover, 1998). Furthermore, when items are dropped, some of the variation in the measure may be lost. Third, removing items may lessen the effectiveness or value of the construct since the items are dropped apart from theoretical considerations.

In determining the fit of the remaining items on their respective factors, indicators should be evaluated to determine the extent to which the set of items measuring each factor defines one latent trait of the construct under investigation. In confirmatory factor analysis, there are a number of ‘fit’ indicators that help researchers determine if a factor is indeed measuring an underlying trait. There are a group of measures in various packages that are collectively known as ‘goodness-of-fit’ indicators. In EQS, for example, the original goodness-of-fit indicators were the Bentler–Bonett Normed Fit Index, the Bentler–Bonett Nonnormed Fit Index, and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI). In LISREL, the measure is actually called the goodness-of-fit indicator or GIF. However, most modern confirmatory factor analysis packages have the option to output virtually all the goodness-of-fit indicators that were originally developed for rival packages as well as their own. Regardless of the goodness-of-fit indices that are considered, their value should ideally be above 0.9.

The adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 }$ is a measure of the $\chi ^ { \dot { 2 } }$ statistic that is determined in the analysis divided by its degrees of freedom. This measure helps to determine if a model is ‘overfitted’ and capitalizing on chance or if the model is not indicative of the underlying data and thus needs improvement. The value of the adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic for each factor should be between 1 and 5 (Bagozzi et al., 1991). The root mean square residual (RMSR) is the square root of the mean of the squared residuals – an average of the residuals between observed and estimated measures. Typically, a value for each factor of below 0.08 is desired. The average variance explained (AVE) should be above 0.5. If the package outputs Cronbach’s alpha, a measure of reliability, a value at or above 0.7 is expected for a good fit.

At this point the full model, including all factors with their respective items, should be evaluated via overall fit statistics for the complete model (the goodness-of-fit measures, adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ , and RMSR using the above-stated thresholds). The distribution of the standardized residuals should also be examined; a symmetrical distribution, centered around zero, is an indication of a wellfitting model.

Once the fit indicators are shown to be within the ranges as noted here, a baseline model has been created. There are now a number of validated factors for the construct being considered. The correlation coefficients among all possible pairs of factors should be appraised. Ideally, all of these coefficients should be statistically significant, indicating that the factors are all related to one another and are thus measuring aspects of the same construct (Smith et al., 1996). Further, these correlation coefficients should be tested to determine that they are statistically significantly different from one, which provides evidence that each factor is unique (Barki & Hartwick, 1994). This tests the discriminant validity between the factors. The evidence for this comparison can be obtained from the estimates of an unconstrained model that frees the correlation between the factor pair and a constrained model that sets this correlation to one. The difference between these chi-square values, with one degree of freedom, is also a $\chi ^ { 2 ^ { \scriptstyle * } }$ variate. Statistical significance of this $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic indicates that the unconstrained model is a better fit than the constrained model and confirms that there is discriminant validity between the factors.

A higher order model may be suggested by significant correlations among the factors in the full confirmatory model. This means that the construct of interest can be shown statistically to be composed of the factors that have been discovered, thus adding another level of validity. Although significant correlations may suggest the existence of a higher order model, checking the existence of correlations between the factors is not a rigorous test to determine the viability of a higher order construct. A more scrupulous examination would involve an inspection of results from a higher order confirmatory factor analysis with the construct under consideration as the higher order factor and the factors as underlying measures. Segars & Grover (1998) note that the efficacy of such a higher order model can be tested using a comparative methodology for such structures. The comparative methodology contrasts the full confirmatory model (baseline) with a model featuring the higher order construction. The baseline model is a model with correlations among all the relevant factors. The fit for the higher order model will never be as good as the fit of the baseline model. Accordingly, the baseline model can be thought of as the optimum model of fit. The higher order model can be compared to the baseline model to determine if its fit approaches that of the baseline model but using less degrees of freedom in the analysis.

The higher order model may be evaluated with the ratio of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistics of the baseline model to that of the higher order model (Marsh & Hocevar, 1985). The ratio should approach 1 if the higher order model sufficiently captures the relationships among the factors. If the ratio does approach 1, the higher order model is likely a truer representation of the model structure since it is more parsimonious (uses less degrees of freedom) and, thus, can be accepted over the baseline model (Segars & Grover, 1998). If the magnitude for all paths between the construct and the underlying factors in the higher order model are 0.5 or better with significant t-values, this will provide further supporting evidence of a viable higher order model.

Related variable analysis is conducted to investigate additional variables, which are anticipated to be theoretically related to the construct of interest. These additional variables could either represent antecedents of the construct or the construct could serve as an antecedent to them, or some combination of both. In order to conduct this analysis, measures for these other variables will need to be included in the administration of the instrument. The data from these measures should be included in the structural equation model along with the measure of the construct under investigation. If statistically significant paths are observed between the other variables and the construct of interest, as would be expected according to theory, this provides further evidence of the appropriateness of the measure.

## Discussion

In developing a quality measurement instrument for a construct, the ultimate goal is to achieve an adequate level of construct validity. Construct validity is the extent to which the measure, based on a suitable operational definition of the construct (Kerlinger & Lee, 1999), appropriately reflects the concept of interest (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). Assessing construct validity involves evaluating the following measurement properties: content validity, factorial validity, reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. Assessment of each of these properties is covered in the methodology described above (see Table 1).

Content validity refers to the appropriateness of the items on the instrument for measuring the construct (Nunnally, 1978). Each of the items should be representative of the construct and collectively they should comprehensively cover all aspects of the construct. Content validity in this methodology is established as a result of the cumulative procedures applied in Stages I and II. As a final test, content validity is statistically determined via the application of the Lawshe procedure in Stage II.

Factorial validity relates to the degree that a factor analysis solution reflects the theoretical dimensions of a construct (Barki & Hartwick, 1994). Straub (1989) observed that the appearance of logical factors in factor analysis was confirmation that the measure under investigation exhibited latent variables. As such, the degree of reasonableness of these factors in relation to the construct provides evidence to support the construct validity of the instrument. In this methodology, factorial validity is determined by comparing the factors from the factor analyses, in Stage III, to the dimensions established as part of the domain of the construct in Stage I.

Reliability refers to the lack of measurement error in the items on a scale (Kerlinger, 1986). Essentially, reliability is the consistency or stability of a measure across different samples. In this methodology, reliability is evaluated by computing the internal consistency, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, for each of the empirically derived components of the construct (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). In Stage III, the alpha statistics for each of the factors determined from the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses are assessed.

Convergent validity is the extent that multiple measures of a construct are in agreement with one another (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). Convergent validity is assessed at multiple points in Stage III of the methodology by investigating the relationships between the construct components, which each represent measures of different aspects of the construct. This is accomplished first via exploratory factor analysis by examining the loadings of each item on the derived factors. While there is ‘no generally accepted standard error of factor loadings’ (Kerlinger, 1986, p. 572), relatively high factor loadings indicate convergent validity (McKinney et al., 2002). Since items are assigned to factors only when their loadings exceed a specified threshold, the factors, by definition, exhibit a degree of convergent validity. Convergent validity is verified by examining correlations between the factors which are calculated in the examination of unidimensionality with confirmatory factor analysis. Correlations among the factors (construct components) provide evidence of convergent validity (Bagozzi et al., 1991).

Discriminant validity refers to the distinctness of the construct components (Campbell & Fiske, 1959). As with convergent validity, discriminant validity is evaluated in Stage III during both exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis. In the exploratory model, the lack of cross loadings of an item on the factors is evidence of discriminant validity (McKinney et al., 2002). In the confirmatory model, the correlations among the factors which are statistically o1 indicate discriminant validity (Smith et al., 1996).

Nomological validity involves tests of the relationships between the construct and its hypothesized antecedents and consequents. Nomological validity refers to the ability of a construct’s measure to predict measures of other constructs in a network of constructs, as would be theoretically anticipated (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). This aspect of validity is evaluated in the proposed methodology via related variable analysis in Stage III. As noted, in order to conduct this analysis, measures for these related variables will need to be included in the administration of the instrument. These measures are then used in the second-order confirmatory model along with the construct under development. Nomological validity is demonstrated if statistically significant paths are observed between the related variables and the construct of interest, as would be expected according to theory.

The proposed methodology is derived from sound theoretical sources and provides for the development of a conceptual and operational definition of a construct, as well as the construction of an acceptable measurement instrument. In addition, it addresses various aspects of construct validity to insure the dependability of the measurement. In the next section, a further assessment of the methodology is offered.

## Assessment of the proposed methodology

There are two additional ways to assess and justify the use of the proposed methodology. The first of these justifications flows from the fact that components of the methodology have been extensively utilized across a wide range of business research topics and vetted through the process of publication in refereed journals. The methodology presented herein is a substantially more refined variation of Churchill’s paradigm for instrument development (Churchill, 1979), which has been used as a guide in several MIS studies (i.e. Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Sethi & King, 1994; Smith et al., 1996; Templeton et al., 2002). In addition, many studies, addressing a broad range of constructs in MIS, have used individual components of the proposed methodology (see Table 2 for a sample). It should also be noted that studies in other disciplines have employed elements of the proposed methodology, for example Parasuraman et al. (2005). Finally, several studies that used an evolving version of the methodology have been published in leading MIS journals (see Table 3).

The second justification for this methodology is in its performance against established standards for evaluating instrument development research. The standardization of methods used in the field is crucial since replicating results (model factor structures) across different studies is the final test for validity. Malhotra & Grover (1998) refer to the many benefits of adhering to standards in POM research that we can apply to MIS: ‘careful adherence to standards can improve the chances for replication, enhance the cumulative tradition of theory development, increase respectability of the field, and enhance confidence in its prescriptions to the profession’ (p. 415). In addition, non-compliance to standards ‘ycan and will lead to erroneous conclusions and regression rather than progress in contribution to theory’ (p. 415).

## Conclusion

This article provides a comprehensive description of an espoused methodology and common issues users might encounter in its deployment. The methodology contains a compendium of practices and thought on construct development that derives from multiple sources. The end result of employing this methodology should be (1) a construct that is well defined and (2) an instrument that exhibits acceptable construct validity. In general, internal validity is addressed in Stages I and II of the methodology by examining the construct from a conceptual view, while external validity is the focus of Stage III in which data are empirically analyzed.
