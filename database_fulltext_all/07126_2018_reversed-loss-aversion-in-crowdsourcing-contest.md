# Reversed loss aversion in crowdsourcing contest

Haichao Zheng<sup>1</sup>, Bo Xu<sup>2</sup>, Linna Hao<sup>3</sup> and Zhangxi Lin<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

Crowdsourcing contest is an effective means for firms to outsource tasks online to a large group of solvers in order to obtain creative solutions. This study investigates loss aversion of solvers in crowdsourcing contests. An experiment was conducted, and reversed loss aversion was identified for solvers, suggesting that solvers experience more happiness from the gains when they win the contest than the pain from the equivalent losses when they fail. The results also suggested that solvers experience higher reversed loss aversion for ideation contests than for expertise-based contests. We then investigated the effects of reversed loss aversion from a game theory perspective. The solutions showed that solvers’ effort level is greater with reversed loss aversion, while the optimal reward for the contest remains the same. In light of our findings, sponsors should conduct contests to solve ideation problems in which the solvers are loss averse reversed and will input more effort. Diversified business models could be developed by crowdsourcing platforms to match solvers and different crowdsourcing tasks.

## Introduction

Information and communication technology, especially the Internet, enables firms to access external knowledge for innovation. Crowdsourcing is a typical Internet-based channel, through which firms outsource tasks, traditionally performed by their employee(s), to an undefined, generally large group or network of people in the form of an online open call (Howe, 2008). Crowdsourcing is to collect crowd wisdom in order to solve a problem. Thus, researchers often use term solver to denote crowdsourcee and sponsor to represent crowdsourcer (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). Crowdsourcing contest is a form of contest in which a firm (sponsor) outsources a business task to a crowd of competing solvers, and the solver(s) who submit the best solution(s) would obtain a reward specified by the firm (Howe, 2008; Mo & Zheng, 2010). Compared with the ideas generated by internal experts, solutions from crowdsourcing contests often score significantly higher in terms of novelty and customer benefit (Poetz & Schreier, 2012).

Crowdsourcing contest has been extensively adopted in the business context as a mechanism to solve business problems (Howe, 2008; Mortara et al, 2013; Zheng et al, 2011). For example, as of August 2013, InnoCentive (www.innocentive.com), a well-known crowdsourcing contest platform, has attracted more than 300,000 solvers from nearly 200 countries. Leading organizations, such as Air Force Research Labs, Booz Allen Hamilton,

Cleveland Clinic, Eli Lilly & Company, EMC Corporation, NASA, and the Nature Publishing Group, have outsourced more than 1600 business problems on InnoCentive.

It has been proved that solvers’ participation and effort is fundamentally important to the outcome of a crowdsourcing contest (Shao et al, 2012; Zheng et al, 2011). Currently, there are two main streams of research on crowdsourcing contest. One stream of research investigates this issue empirically from a behavioral perspective (Boudreau et al, 2011; Zhao & Zhu, 2014; Zheng et al, 2011), while the other one studies crowdsourcing contest from an economics perspective (Archak & Sundararajan, 2009; Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). From the behavioral perspective, researchers tried to discover the antecedents or affordances of solvers’ motivations. For example, Zheng et al (2011) found that contest task autonomy, variety, and analyzability are positively associated with the solvers’ intrinsic motivation, which is more important than extrinsic incentive in inducing solvers participation.

The economics research on crowdsourcing contest has been based on game theory, an important tool to study the dynamics of contest, in which sponsors and solvers may have different or even conflicting objectives. Research based on game theory has revealed solvers optimal effort level in the contest and optimal reward by sponsors (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). A contest is an event in which participants compete with each other by undertaking efforts in order to win prizes. In a crowdsourcing contest, solvers compete with each other to win the reward. Following the rule of crowdsourcing contest, only one or a small number of solvers are the lucky winner(s); however, others receive nothing although they submit solutions. Thus, similar to other types of contests, crowdsourcing contest incurs a potential loss for solvers. Prospect theory suggests that people are more sensitive to losses than to gains of the same magnitude and tend to avoid loss, which is known as loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). In this study, loss aversion is referred to as the phenomenon where solvers in a crowdsourcing contest perceive more pain from the loss when they fail than the happiness they may get from the equivalent gain when they win the contest. Thus they are more sensitive to the expected loss of effort when they fail than to the expected gain of same value when they win the contest. Loss aversion influences people’s decision making in a variety of domains, such as investing, negotiation, politics, and health (Kermer et al, 2006). Research indicates that in various kinds of contests (e.g., academic contest and consumer contest) loss aversion influences participants’ intention and effort spent. They may input more effort in the contests if they are of lower level of loss aversion (e.g., Barut & Noussair, 2002, Noussair & Silver, 2006). Thus, it is important to consider the effect of loss aversion in crowdsourcing contests, so as to promote effort from solvers.

Although loss aversion is a robust and common finding, it may be moderated, reduced, or even reversed in some contexts (Harinck et al, 2007). In order to provide realistic advice for the design of crowdsourcing contests and systems, it is important to understand how solvers experience loss aversion, and how it exerts effect on solvers behaviors in the contests. Currently, there has been no systematic research on loss aversion in the context of crowdsourcing contests. The economics research generally assumed that solvers are completely rational, ignoring the effect of loss aversion in crowdsourcing contests (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). Similarly, focusing on participants’ motivations, the behavioral studies did not count in the effect of potential loss, and it is unclear how loss aversion exists for solvers and how it is affected in a crowdsourcing contest. In this paper, in order to achieve a thorough and in-depth understanding of solvers’ loss aversion, we propose to answer the following two questions:

1. How do solvers experience loss aversion in different settings of a crowdsourcing contest?

2. How does loss aversion affect solvers’ behaviors and contest design in a crowdsourcing contest?

Answers to the above questions are intended to provide insights to design of the crowdsourcing contests and systems. We used different research methods for these two questions. Experiment has been the effective method and mostly used in previous research to investigate people’s loss aversion empirically (e.g., Harinck et al, 2007). For question one, an experiment was conducted to study the extent that loss aversion exists for solvers in contests of different types and with different task sizes (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). For the second question, the factor of loss aversion was integrated in game modeling in order to reveal how solvers’ reversed loss aversion identified from the preceding experiment has effect on their effort and the optimal reward level for the contest. A similar approach has been adopted in previous research, such as the research on loss aversion newsvendor problems (e.g., Wang &Webster, 2009). In addition, the results were compared with the level of solvers’ effort and contest reward without considering loss aversion in previous research (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008) for further understanding the effect of solvers’ reversed loss aversion. Thus, different from traditional research that assumed solvers in a crowdsourcing contest are completely rational, this study focused on loss aversion in order to achieve a more realistic and in-depth understanding of solvers’ feelings and behaviors. Particularly, we conducted an empirical study using an experiment to examine solvers’ loss aversion in crowdsourcing contests and then developed game theory models to study its effect on solvers’ effort level and optimal reward for crowdsourcing contests.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, the literature about crowdsourcing contest and loss aversion is reviewed. Second, the research hypotheses are developed, followed by an explanation of the experiment on loss aversion. We then study the effects of reversed loss aversion based on game theory modeling. Finally, we discuss the findings and implications for research and practice.

## Literature review

## Crowdsourcing contest

Crowdsourcing is to take ‘‘…a job traditionally performed by a designated agent (usually an employee) and outsourcing it to an undefined, generally large group of people in the form of an open call’’ (Howe, 2008). On a crowdsourcing platform such as innocentive.com, demand-side firms get solutions from an external crowd to solve their business problems by conducting contests. There are two typical kinds of crowdsourcing contest, ideation contest and expertise-based contest, which differ from each other in market uncertainty. An ideation contest (e.g., design contest for the logo for an event such as the FIFA world cup) does not have clear specifications and thus has high market uncertainty (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). An expertise-based contest (e.g., Android application develop contest) has clear goals set by the sponsor, which thereby implies low market uncertainty. There are two kinds of actors in the case of a crowdsourcing contest – crowdsourcers who sponsor the contest task, and crowdsourcees who participate in the contest to solve the business problem in order to win the reward set by the crowdsourcers. Research of crowdsourcing contest uses the term solvers to denote crowdsourcees and sponsors to denote crowdsourcers (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008).

The main purpose of sponsoring a crowdsourcing contest is to acquire creative ideas and solutions for business problems from solvers (Ebner et al, 2009). Thus, an important issue for crowdsourcing contest study is to solicit effort and creative solutions from solvers. One stream of research has focused on solvers’ motivations, participation and effort from the behavioral perspective. Leimeister et al (2009) identified four motivations for solvers to join ideation contests, which are learning, reward seeking, self-marketing, and social motives. Similarly, Brabham (2010) also found that motivations for solvers to participate in crowdsourcing contests include the opportunity to make money, to develop creative skills, to find additional work and job opportunities, and the positive attitudes toward the community involved. In addition to the extrinsic motivations, such as direct compensation and self-marketing, intrinsic motivations also play an important role in crowdsourcing contests (Lakhani et al, 2007; Zheng et al, 2011). The intrinsic motivations of solvers are critical even when the reward of a crowdsourcing contest is large (Lakhani et al. 2007). Based on Chinese crowdsourcing contests, Zheng et al (2011) found that both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations improved solvers’ participation intention, while intrinsic motivation was more important than extrinsic motivation in inducing participation. They also indicated that task autonomy, variety, and analyzability are positively associated with solvers’ intrinsic motivation, whereas contest tacitness is negatively associated with intrinsic motivation. Thus crowdsourcing contest tasks should be highly autonomous, explicitly specified, and less complex, as well as require a variety of skills (Zheng et al, 2011). In addition, task attributes like uncertainty and rivalry may also affect solvers’ motivation and thus are important factors to be considered. Boudreau et al (2011) found that greater rivalry reduces the motivations of all solvers to exert effort. However, task uncertainty can reduce the effort-reducing effect of rivalry.

Another stream of research follows the economics perspective in which the interaction between sponsors and solvers is considered as a game theory process (Archak & Sundararajan, 2009; DiPalantino & Vojnovic, 2009). In such a game, sponsors publish their contests to call for solutions. They set the reward for winner(s) to attract best solution from solvers, while solvers determine the effort level to maximize their expected utility based on the reward and winning probability (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). Research indicates that the reward plays a significant role in attracting ideal solutions. Contests with greater rewards are able to attract a larger group of solvers, including those who have the best expertise, and a higher number of solutions. In addition, the reward is also a significant determinant of the individual solvers’ effort and performance (Archak, 2010; DiPalantino & Vojnovic, 2009). It is also indicated that the number of solvers in a contest may have a negative effect on individual effort invested in the contest because it is negatively associated with the likelihood of winning the contest. Therefore, it is suggested that sponsors limit the number of solvers to two and reduce the number of solvers by setting an entry fee (Taylor, 1995), or conducting an auction to select solvers (Fullerton & McAfee, 1999). However, some research also proposed that sponsors may benefit from a large solver population because the diversity of solutions may outweigh the effect of solvers’ underinvestment in effort (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008).

Crowdsourcing contests incur potential loss for solvers since most of them will not win the reward although they make effort to create and submit solutions. Thus loss aversion may exist for solvers in a crowdsourcing contest, which has influence on solvers’ participation and effort level in the contest. Currently, the research on crowdsourcing contest assumed that solvers are completely rational. It is not known how loss aversion exists for solvers and has effect in crowdsourcing contests.

## Loss aversion

Prospect theory proposes that the value function is defined by deviations from the reference point (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). It is generally concave for gains and convex for losses, i.e., marginal value of both gains and losses decreases with their magnitude, while it is steeper for losses than for gains. This phenomenon is known as loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). It appears that for individuals, an intense anticipated reaction to losses leads to loss aversion (Zhang & Fishbach, 2005). Ariely et al (2005) used two constructs, emotional attachment and cognitive perspective, to characterize the contexts in which losses loom larger than gains. Emotional attachment moderates the degree to which parting with an item involves a loss, and shifts in cognitive perspective will lead to differential focus on the attributes of the transaction which will moderate the weight of the loss (Ariely et al, 2005).

Scholars use a number of approaches to measure loss aversion (Abdellaoui et al, 2007). For example, Kahneman and Tversky (1979) proposed that loss aversion should be defined as $\lambda = - U ( - x ) / U ( x )$ for all $x > 0 ,$ , where x is the interested objective. Loss aversion exists when $\lambda > 1$ . In Tversky and Kahneman (1992), loss aversion was measured with the ratio of the utility of negative one unit to the utility of positive one unit, i.e., $\lambda = - U ( - 1 ) / U ( 1 )$ Another definition of loss aversion is $\lambda = U ^ { \prime } ( - x ) / U ^ { \prime } ( x )$ which measures the ratio of the slope of the utility function between loss and gain (Wakker & Tversky, 1993). Ko¨bberling and Wakker (2005) defined loss aversion index as $\lambda = - U _ { \mathrm { l } } ^ { \prime } ( 0 ) / U _ { \uparrow } ^ { \prime } ( 0 )$ the ratio of the left derivative and the right derivative of the utility function at zero. Abdellaoui et al (2007) proposed a method to measure loss aversion in an experimental study without making any parametric assumptions.

Although loss aversion has been proven robust in diverse situations, some moderating factors have been identified (Rick, 2011). For example, an individual’s mood may moderate loss aversion, because a positive mood acts as a buffer that decreases concerns about negative feelings, whereas a negative mood increases concerns about these feelings (Rick, 2011). While emotional attachment to a good may be a cause of loss aversion, intention to exchange a good will moderate people’s reaction to a loss (Novemsky & Kahneman, 2005a). For example, an individual’s intention to exchange a good may break the emotional attachment to the good and reduce the anticipated discomfort of parting with that good (Novemsky & Kahneman, 2005b).

Kermer et al (2006) indicated that loss aversion is an affective forecasting error, because people believe that the hedonic impacts of a loss will be greater than the hedonic impact of an equal-sized gain. However, when people actually gamble, losses do not have as much of an emotional impact as they had originally predicted. Nicolau (2013) found that consumers buying highinvolvement non-frequently purchased hedonic products are less loss averse when using an intermediary than when dealing with each provider separately.

In addition, some studies have even found that losses may not be experienced more intensely than gains. Using self-reported emotional reactions to hypothetical gains and losses, with mixed results, Mellers et al (1997) did not find that the utility function was steeper for losses than for equivalent gains. Novemsky and Kahneman (2005a)

identified exceptions of loss aversion. For example, exchanging goods that are given up ‘‘as intended’’ does not exhibit loss aversion. Emotional attachment to the good and cognitive focus may explain how people’s intentions could moderate loss aversion (Novemsky & Kahneman, 2005b). Sokol-Hessner et al (2009) indicated that intentional cognitive regulation (e.g., thinking like a trader selectively) could reduce both behavioral loss aversion and arousal to losses. The scales of loss aversion measurement could affect the loss aversion level too. McGraw et al (2010) proposed that when gains are compared and judged on a common scale with losses, losses are likely to be rated as having greater impact than equivalent gains. However, if the participants evaluated only the gain or only the loss using a unipolar intensity scale (from no effect to very intensive effect), there was no evidence of loss aversion (McGraw et al, 2010). In addition, the amount of gains or losses also affects people’s loss aversion level (e.g., Harinck et al, 2007). For example, if outcomes are small, loss aversion is reversed, which means that gains loom larger than losses in this condition (Harinck et al, 2007). The reason for the reversed loss aversion is that people are more likely to discount a small negative outcome than a large negative outcome (Harinck et al, 2007).

Loss aversion influences people’s decision making and activities in a variety of domains, such as investing, negotiation, politics, and health (Kermer et al, 2006). Researchers in information systems have also found loss aversion to be useful to explain individual behaviors in IT adoption and information seeking (e.g., Davis & Ganeshan, 2009; Fleischmann et al, 2014; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Yin et al, 2012). For instance, Kim and Kankanhalli (2009) found that loss aversion could effectively explain users’ resistance to information project implementation. Yin et al (2012) stated that loss aversion is one of the mechanisms underlying the negativity bias in online review, because people are more concerned about negative consequences than positive consequences. Crowdsourcing contests incur potential loss for solvers since most of them will not win the reward although they make effort to create and submit solutions. Loss aversion provides a new theoretical lens and perspective to understand solvers’ participation and effort in crowdsourcing contests. In this study, crowdsourcing contest is studied from the perspective of loss aversion. We investigate solvers’ loss aversion using experiment and its effect in crowdsourcing contests through game modeling.

## Loss aversion for solvers: hypotheses development

To answer the first question: How do solvers experience loss aversion in different settings of a crowdsourcing contest, we design an experiment to investigate how solvers’ loss aversion changes in different contest settings. Although crowdsourcing contest is an attractive open innovation mechanism, it may not be suitable for all business problems (Mortara et al, 2013; Poetz & Schreier, 2012). It is concerned by the crowdsourcing platforms and sponsors that in what conditions contest can be applied as a good mechanism. For design of the crowdsourcing system by the platforms and design of contests by sponsors, it is essential to understand how solvers’ loss aversion is affected by the contest. In this section, hypotheses are first developed that the two important factors of contest: contest task size (small vs. large) and contest category (ideation vs. expertise-based) affect solver’s loss aversion, which refers to the solver’s feeling of loss compared with that of equivalent gain in a crowdsourcing contest (McGraw et al, 2010). Then the experiment is conducted to test the hypotheses empirically.

## Contest task size: small and large

Contest task size refers to the complexity of a crowdsourcing contest task. To simplify matters, in this study, the task size is classified as either small or large. For contests with a small task, the effort required to solve the crowdsourcing task is correspondingly small; for contests with a large task, the effort required to solve the crowdsourcing task is correspondingly large. The effort exerted on the task is termed as the reserved cost for the solvers. For solvers who fail in a contest, their losses are their reserved costs, which is the effort they exerted. Thus, the potential loss for a given solver is small in a contest with a small-sized task, and vice versa. The contest reward is the money set by the sponsor to pay the winning solver. The net gain for a solver who wins in the contest is the reward gained minus the effort made. For example, if the reward is 1000 and the effort value is 500, then the net gain is 500. Generally, the contest reward increases with the size of the contest task. For example, the recommended pricing list of www.680. com, the second largest crowdsourcing platform in China, suggests 50 Yuan for the processing of a photograph, and about 5000 Yuan for a Website design, which is much more complex than photo-processing.

Although loss aversion is universally found, some scholars have identified exceptions (Harinck et al, 2007). Reversed loss aversion, in which the feeling of gains is larger than that of equivalent losses, has been found to exist in some contexts. Harinck et al (2007) proposed that people would not care very much about the loss of one pound, but would be very happy to spy a pound coin and pick it up. This finding shows that when the outcome is small, people weigh gains more heavily than losses. Harinck et al (2007) used the hedonic principle to explain this phenomenon where people try to maximize pleasure and to minimize pain. This principle might also work in crowdsourcing contests. Thus, solvers would try to discount or minimize the negative feelings associated with the failure to obtain the reward, thus discounting or minimizing loss of the effort exerted in the crowdsourcing task. For example, they might think that failure in the contest of a small task is not that bad. It is easier for people to discount small negative outcomes than large negative outcomes (Harinck et al, 2007).

Crowdsourcing has been found to be a more useful tool for small tasks. For example, Howe (2008) proposed that ‘‘when it comes to crowdsourcing, any task worth doing is worth dividing up into its smallest possible components,’’ which means that small tasks are more suitable for crowdsourcing contest (Howe, 2008, p. 285). Task size is associated with the effort exerted in the contest. According to Cameron (2001), when the task size is large, solvers may have strong negative feelings associated with failure to win the reward. Conversely, if the task is small, solvers may not care so much about the loss. Thus, pleasure from the contest task can surpass the pain associated with loss (Brabham, 2010). From the loss aversion perspective, we propose that loss aversion in a crowdsourcing contest, which is solvers’ feeling of loss compared to that of equivalent gain, is moderated by the task size.

H1: Solvers’ loss aversion, which is the feeling of loss compared to that of the equivalent gain, is moderated by task size. Solvers are more loss averse for contests with large task than for contests with small task.

## Contest category: ideation and expertise-based

Terwiesch and Xu (2008) classified crowdsourcing contests using the dimensions of market uncertainty and technical uncertainty. Market uncertainty refers to when the sponsor has no clear goals, while technical uncertainty means the performance of the solutions does not depend on the solvers’ concrete professional knowledge (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). This leads to the definitions of ideation contest and expertise-based contest as introduced in Section 2. An ideation contest does not have clear specifications and thus has high market uncertainty. In an expertise-based contest, the sponsor has clear goals, meaning that the market uncertainty is low. Thus, the autonomy of an ideation contest is higher than that of an expertise-based contest.

Intention plays an important role in loss aversion reduction (Ariely et al, 2005). Novemsky and Kahneman (2005a) found that the exchange of goods that are given up ‘‘as intended’’ does not exhibit loss aversion. According to Novemsky and Kahneman (2005b), there are two ways through which intention to participate in a crowdsourcing contest will moderate solvers’ loss aversion. First, the intention can reduce or break solvers’ emotional attachment to the time and effort they spend pursuing the crowdsourcing reward (Novemsky & Kahneman, 2005b). If solvers are eager to participate, their emotional reaction to loss will be reduced. Second, intention can refocus solvers’ cognition on the crowdsourcing contest experience (Novemsky & Kahneman, 2005b). Thus, solvers who have a strong intention to participate will have less intensive feelings toward failures. Ideation contests have higher autonomy than expertise-based contests. They provide solvers with much freedom and control over how the contest task is solved (Zheng et al, 2011), which may generate more intrinsic incentives and participation intention for solvers. Thus, solvers in ideation contests may have lower level of negative feeling in case of failure.

In addition to the effects of intention, the hedonic principle also works to reduce the feeling of loss in ideation contests. The hedonic principle indicates that people are motivated to enjoy positive outcomes and to discount negative outcomes (Harinck et al, 2007). Ideation contests provide more hedonic value for solvers than expertise-based contests. This will motivate solvers to discount the feeling of loss. In addition, high hedonic value will generate a positive mood for the solvers (Rick, 2011), which will act as a buffer to decrease the intensity of feeling of loss in ideation contests. In expertise-based contests, the solvers have to conduct tedious tasks, which may lead to negative feelings. Rick (2011) proposed that negative feelings increase concerns about losses. Based on the above discussion, we believe that for ideation contests, solvers will experience lower feeling of loss than with expertisebased contests. Thus, we propose that loss aversion in a crowdsourcing contest, which is solvers’ feeling of loss compared to that of equivalent gain, is moderated by the contest category.

H2: Solvers’ loss aversion, which is the feeling of loss compared to that of the equivalent gain, is moderated by contest category. Solvers are more loss averse for expertise-based contests than for ideation contests.

## Loss aversion for solvers: research method

## Experiment design

We conducted a 2 (contest category: ideation vs. expertise-based) 9 2 (contest task size: small vs. large) 9 2 (gain/loss: gain vs. loss), between-subjects experiment using scenarios. Participants were randomly assigned to the 8 treatment groups shown in Table 1.

To generate the scenarios, slogan contests and translation contests were used as the ideation contests and expertise-based contests, respectively. The classification of ideation and expertise-based contests is based on the dimension of problem solving (Morgeson & Humphrey, 2006). Problem solving refers to the degree to which a contest task requires unique ideas or solutions. Based on Morgeson and Humphrey (2006), we developed three items to measure problem solving, i.e., ‘‘this contest requires me to be creative,’’ ‘‘this contest involves solving problems that have no obvious correct answer,’’ and ‘‘this contest requires unique ideas or solutions.’’ The participants were asked to answer the questions based on the contest description given in the experiment.

Two contest task size levels were used to represent the small- and large-sized contest tasks, while the value of the effort to complete a small task and a large task was 50 Yuan and 500 Yuan, respectively. For the small slogan contest, 100 Yuan was the reward for designing one slogan for the park, Floraland, located in Chengdu, China. For the large slogan contest, 1000 Yuan was the reward for designing 10 slogans for the same park. For the translation contests, 100 Yuan was the reward for translating one crowdsourcing project at Innocentive.com into Chinese, while 1000 Yuan was the reward for translating 10 crowdsourcing projects at InnoCentive.com into Chinese. The net gain for the solvers is equal to the reward minus the effort. Solvers who fail get nothing. The net loss is equal to the effort that the solvers invest in the contest. Thus the net gain and loss for the contests of small task were both 50 Yuan, while the net gain and loss for the contests of large task were both 500 Yuan. The experimental scenarios in this study are similar to the design in Harinck et al (2007), which does not specify the win/loss probability.

For the slogan contests, we presented the task with the reward of 100 Yuan as follows: ‘‘Floraland, located in Chengdu, is one of the National 4A Tourist Scenic Areas. Floraland provides tourists and residents with theme parks, 5-star hotels, a conference center, and other facilities, such as an international bilingual school, kindergarten, and hospitals. We are conducting a contest to develop a creative slogan for Floraland. We have two requirements for the slogan. First, it must be an original creative idea. There is no limitation on the number of words in the slogan. Second, the slogan should highlight the characteristics of Floraland. The best solution will win a reward of 100 Yuan.’’ Similarly, for the slogan contest with 1000 Yuan reward, solvers were required to submit ten slogans. After browsing the contest description, a success scenario or failure scenario was generated for the participants. Each participant was involved in only one scenario, i.e., there is one contest result for each participant: success or failure. Participants were not required to submit actual solutions. They imagined they were experiencing the contest, an approach similar to the experimental scenarios in Harinck et al (2007). For example, the success scenario for the slogan contest with the 100 Yuan reward is as follows: ‘‘Suppose you participate in the contest, and submit one slogan designed for Floraland. Based on the evaluation of the sponsor, Floraland, your solution is the best one. You then win the 100 Yuan reward. The effort you invested is worth 50 Yuan. Using the slider, please select the intensity of your feeling (high score means you are happy, and 0 means that you are neither sad nor happy).’’ The slider ranges from 0 to 100. Previous studies found that bipolar scales (i.e., from very negative feeling to very positive feeling) may sometimes disguise individual’s psychological phenomena (e.g., Klockars, 1979; McGraw et al, 2010; Schwarz et al, 1991), while unipolar scales do not elicit loss-averse judgment. Thus, the unipolar intensity scale was adopted in this study.

The best solution will win the reward of 100 Yuan.’’ For the translation contest with the 1000 Yuan reward, the requirement was to translate ten projects at Innocentive. Similar to the slogan contests, a success scenario or failure scenario was generated for the participants.

A pilot test was conducted before data collection. The scenarios were first drafted in English and then translated into Chinese independently by the authors who are proficient in both languages. Inconsistencies and ambiguities in the translation were located and resolved. Then the scenarios were pretested with 20 students. Their feedback indicated a general consensus that the scenarios were realistic and that participants had little difficulty placing themselves into the hypothetical position of the scenario characters. The feedback also resulted in minor wording changes to some of the scenarios to remove ambiguities.

## Subjects

The participants in the experiment were students from a major university in southwestern China. College students were used as participants for several reasons. First, students in universities frequently participate in many kinds of contests, such as programming contests and business planning contests, which are similar to crowdsourcing contests, although they are not the same as the contests in our experiment. Additionally, crowdsourcing is popular in China as an effective way to leverage the potential crowd intelligence to boost innovation. Currently, many of the solvers on crowdsourcing platforms are college students. Thus, college students are proper subjects since they may represent a major part of the real population of solvers. Second, it is easy to control other factors as the college students share many common attributes (e.g., age and income), and they do not get access to much confounding external information that may make the experiment design hard (Compeau et al, 2012; Friedman & Sunder, 1994). 204 students were randomly assigned to the 8 experiment scenarios. The experiment was conducted online. Each participant was presented with only one scenario and was asked to indicate a feeling of happiness or sadness using the slider ranging from 0 to 100 (i.e., from none to very intensive). At the end of the questionnaire, questions about the respondent’s demographics were asked, including age, gender, expenses per month, and risk attitude.

All of the questions were optional rather than mandatory for the participants. Finally, 177 students reported their feelings. We eliminated incomplete and inappropriate responses, as well as those from respondents who answered the survey more than once. A total of 168 usable responses were included in the final sample for data analysis. Sample statistics and the distribution of responses for the experiment scenarios are shown in Table 2.

## Hypotheses testing

An ANOVA test with the fixed factors of contest category, loss/gain, and contest task size was conducted. The dependent variable is the value of feeling. In addition to the main effects, two-way interactions of contest category, loss/gain, and contest task size were included in the ANOVA test. As the participants were randomly assigned to the experiment treatments, all of the random factors, such as gender, age, and monthly expenses, were not included in the model to extract more variance. Thus, the results shown in Table 5 are conservative, which means that if we included the random factors all of the F values would become slightly larger. We compared distribution of the random factors among the eight experiment groups, and no significant difference was found. The results in Table 5 show that the model is significant $( F \mathrm { \ v a l u e } = 2 4 . 0 1 5 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . In addition, the results reveal two main effects of contest category $( F \mathrm { v a l u e } = 3 . 6 3 8 , p = 0 . 0 5 8 < 0 . 1 )$ ) and loss/gain (F value = $1 3 6 . 1 7 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

The main effect of loss/gain is significant, which means that solvers’ feelings of loss and gain have significant difference. The results of independent sample t tests as shown in Table 6 and Figure 1 indicate that for all the four types of contests (small-task ideation, large-task ideation, small-task expertise-based, large-task expertisebased), solvers’ feeling of loss is significantly lower than that of gain, which means reversed loss aversion exists. The loss aversion ratio was calculated by dividing the mean of the feeling of loss by the mean of the feeling of the equivalent gain (Harinck et al, 2007). All the ratios were less than 1, indicating that loss aversion was reversed.

The interaction between task size and loss/gain is not significant $( F { \mathrm { ~ v a l u e } } = 0 . 0 0 4 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , which means that contest task size does not moderate the effect of loss/gain on solver’s feeling. Solvers were not more loss averse for contests with a large task than for contests with a small task. Thus, hypothesis 1 was not supported. The results of the independent sample t tests are shown in Table 7, indicating no significant difference for the feeling of loss and gain between small and large contest tasks.

We then compared the loss aversion ratio for small and large tasks. As shown in Table 6, for each kind of contest category the solvers’ loss aversion ratio for small and large tasks is approximately equal (ideation contest: 0.49 vs. 0.43; expertise-based contest: 0.57 vs. 0.64). Thus, it was confirmed that solver’s loss aversion does not change significantly for contests of small and large tasks. Hypothesis 1 was not supported.

The ANOVA test results in Table 5 indicate that the interaction of the contest category and loss/gain is marginally significant $( F \mathrm { v a l u e } = 3 . 4 1 4 , \dot { p } = 0 . 0 6 6 < 0 . 1 )$ The results in Table 6 and Figure 1 also show that the loss aversion ratios for expertise-based contests are larger than those for ideation contests, which means that solvers are more loss averse reversed for ideation contests than for expertise-based contests. Thus loss aversion was moderated by the contest category, and hypothesis 2 was supported. Figure 2 shows that the feeling of loss for ideation contests is lower than that for expertise-based contests. This implies why solvers’ loss aversion is different for the ideation and expertise-based contests.

## Effects of reversed loss aversion

## Game modeling

The results in Table 6 indicate that loss aversions are reversed for both the contests with a reward of 100 Yuan, and the contests with a reward of 1000 Yuan. To answer the second question: How does loss aversion affect solvers’ behaviors and contest design in a crowdsourcing contest, in this part we develop game theory models to further study how the solver’s level of reversed loss aversion affects his/her effort level and the optimal reward for the contest. We use a piecewise linear form of the loss aversion utility function for the solvers (Wang & Webster, 2007, 2009):

$$
U (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c} x - x _ {0}   x \geq x _ {0} \\ \lambda (x - x _ {0})   x <   x _ {0} \end{array} \right.\tag{1}
$$

where $x _ { 0 }$ denotes the solver’s reference point which refers to the value of effort exerted and the cost incurred in the contest, and x is the evaluation of what he/she receives from the contest. Following Tversky and Kahneman (1991) and Kivetz (2003), $0 < \lambda < 1$ is defined as the loss aversion reversed, $\lambda > 1$ is defined as the loss aversion, and $\lambda = 1$ is defined as the normal (no loss aversion and no loss aversion reversed). For simplicity, we assume that the solvers participating in the same kind of contest (ideation contest or expertise-based contest) have the same loss aversion coefficient (Hastie & Dawes, 2001).

For the ideation contest, the problem solving is stochastic. To capture this characteristic, we add a noise variable $\xi$ to the solution performance function (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). The random noise $\xi$ is assumed to be an independent and identically distributed Gumbel random variable with a mean of zero and a scale parameter l. In addition to the noise variable neffort e leads to a deterministic improvement $r ( e )$ of the solution performance, where $r ( e ) = \theta \ln ( e )$ is an increasing and concave function which measures the performance return of the solver’s effort (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). In the ideation contest, all solvers are assumed to be equally capable of solving the broad problem, i.e., $\beta _ { i } = \beta _ { j } = \beta ,$ , for all i and j. Thus, a given solver i has an expertise level $\beta _ { i }$ and exerts an effort of $e _ { i } .$ . The performance of the solution in the ideation contest is assumed to be of the following additive form: $\nu _ { i } ( e _ { i } ) = \beta + r ( e _ { i } ) + \zeta _ { i } .$

The solver’s decision is to determine the effort level to maximize his/her expected revenue, i.e., solving the following problem:

$$
\max _ {e \geq 0} E [ U (x (e)) ] = E [ p (A - c e - c _ {f}) - \lambda (1 - p) (c e + c _ {f}) ]\tag{2}
$$

where e represents the effort level, p indicates the probability of winning the first prize $A ,$ ce represents the cost of the solver, and $c _ { f }$ represents the fixed cost. $c e + c _ { f }$ denotes the solver’s reference level. Based on the property 1 in Terwiesch and Xu (2008), we formulated the expected revenue function of the ideation contest sponsor as $\beta + r ( e ( A ) ) + \mu \ln n - A$ . Thus, the sponsor has to solve the following problem to set the optimal reward A:

$$
\max _ {A > 0} E [ \Pi (A) ] = E [ \beta + r (e (A)) + \mu \ln n - A ]\tag{3}
$$

where n is the number of solvers who participate in the contest. The game model for the ideation contest is to solve functions (2) and (3).

For the expertise-based contest, the solution performance is completely determined by the solver’s expertise level and effort level. There is no stochastic influence from random noise. Thus, $\nu _ { i } ( \beta _ { i } , e _ { i } ) = \beta _ { i } + r ( e _ { i } )$ . Both the sponsor and the solvers have identical beliefs that $\beta _ { i }$ is distributed across solvers with cumulative density function $F ( . )$ and probability density function f(.) in the interval of $[ \underline { { \beta } } , \bar { \beta } ] .$ . The solver i determines the effort level e or solution performance v to maximize his/her expected revenue, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ , solving the following problem:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {0 <   v _ {i} \leq 1} U _ {s} (v _ {i} | \beta_ {i}, g (\beta_ {i})) \\ = F (g ^ {- 1} (v _ {i})) ^ {n - 1} (A - c e _ {i} (\beta_ {i}) - c _ {f}) - \lambda (1 - F (g ^ {- 1} (v _ {i})) ^ {n - 1}) \\ (c e _ {i} (\beta_ {i}) + c _ {f}) \\ = A F (g ^ {- 1} (v _ {i})) ^ {n - 1} + (\lambda - 1) (c e _ {i} (\beta_ {i}) + c _ {f}) F (g ^ {- 1} (v _ {i})) ^ {n - 1} \\ - \lambda (c e _ {i} (\beta_ {i}) + c _ {f}) \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Let $U ( \beta _ { f } ) = 0 .$ . We can obtain $\beta _ { f }$ which is the lowest expertise level of solvers who will join the expertise-based contest. In other words, the solvers whose expertise levels fall in the range $[ \beta _ { f } , \bar { \beta } ]$ will submit solutions. Thus, the revenue function is developed as follows:

$$
\max _ {A > 0} \Pi_ {s} (A) = \int_ {\beta_ {f}} ^ {\bar {\beta}} (\beta + r (e (\beta))) n F (\beta) ^ {n - 1} f (\beta) d \beta - A\tag{5}
$$

The game model for expertise-based contest is to solve functions (4) and (5).

We set the fixed cost $c _ { f } = 0$ for several reasons. First, ideation contests are open problems which need more innovative thinking rather than some fixed investment prior to participation. Second, this simplifies the comparison of the solutions with those in Terwiesch and Xu (2008) who only provided solutions for expertise-based contest when $c _ { f } = 0 \ :$ . Third, in this study, we do not study trial-and-error contests $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ a pill that reduces gray hair) which might require fixed costs for experimental equipment. The process for obtaining the solutions for the game models is similar to that in Terwiesch and Xu (2008). Therefore, we list the solutions in Table 8, rather than provide the complete proof details.

## Discussion

## Summary of results

To further investigate solvers’ behavior in crowdsourcing contests, loss aversion is introduced into crowdsourcing research in this study. We conducted an experiment to test the effects of task size and contest category on solvers’ loss aversion. The results indicate that some of the hypotheses were supported. In addition, based on the game between solvers and a sponsor, we revealed the effects of reversed loss aversion on the effort that solvers exert in the contest and the optimal contest reward.

Contest task size was not found to have a significant effect on solver’s loss aversion (H1). For the small tasks, the feeling of loss was less than that of the equivalent gain, which is consistent with Harinck et al (2007) who found that for small outcomes loss aversion is reversed. However, reversed loss aversion was also observed in this study for the large tasks, which contradicts the findings of Harinck et al (2007). The research design in this study is similar to that found in previous studies. For example, in Kermer et al (2006) participants watched the computer play either the win or loss version of a game and

The lower intensity of the feeling of loss in the ideation contests shows that such contests are more favorable for solvers, since ideation problems have high uncertainty. This is consistent with Boudreau et al (2011), who proposed that high uncertainty may reduce the negative effect of rivalry in crowdsourcing contests. First, the optimal reward does not increase in the reversed loss aversion condition. Second, if there are enough solvers joining the contest, the optimal effort is larger if the solvers are more loss averse reversed. ## Implications for research

This study makes contributions to crowdsourcing research. First, investigating solvers’ anticipated feeling toward failure or success prior to their actual joining in a contest provides a new perspective to understand solvers behavior. Uncovering solvers’ behavior is helpful for sponsors to attract more effort from solvers, which contribute significantly to the final solution quality (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). Prior studies tried to learn solvers’ participation intention from motivation perspectives (e.g., Leimeister et al, 2009; Zheng et al, 2011). However, few studies investigated how the solvers feeling of potential loss may affect their participation in crowdsourcing contests. This study provides new evidences – loss aversion – to explain the reasons behind solvers’ participation intention for different crowdsourcing contests. Besides the task size, this study introduced crowdsourcing contest categories to investigate their effect on solvers’ loss aversion.

Second, the findings shed light on research of design of crowdsourcing system and contest. The effect of loss aversion should be considered in design of the crowdsourcing system and contest since it may influence solvers’ effort exerted in the contest. Although contest is adopted in many famous crowdsourcing systems (e.g., www.innocentive.com and www.zhubajie.com), it does not work equally for every kind of innovation problem. From a loss aversion (reversed) perspective, this study demonstrates that crowdsourcing contest is a better option for ideation problems, which help the firms acquire early-stage ideas, than expertise-based tasks (Mortara et al, 2013; Poetz & Schreier, 2012). Thus diverse mechanisms should be considered by the platforms in design of the crowdsourcing system for different kinds of business problems and tasks. When sponsors design a contest, how to reduce solvers’ loss aversion is an important issue to be considered. Most research has investigated contest design based on task design theory (Zheng et al, 2011), game theory (Terwiesch & Xu, 2008), and innovation theory (Boudreau et al, 2011). In this study, we investigate this issue from the perspective of loss aversion, to gain new insight into the design of crowdsourcing system and contest.

Third, this study provides implications for research on loss aversion. Based on the prospect theory, loss aversion and its effects have been studied in various contexts in previous research. This study is one of the first to investigate loss aversion in the context of crowdsourcing contest. In this research setting, solvers were asked to predict their reaction to losses or gains. When making predictions, individuals often failed to anticipate how quickly they would recover from negative outcomes (Gilbert et al, 2004). In most cases, people overestimate the duration of their affective reaction toward negative outcomes (Gilbert et al, 1998). However, we found that solvers did not overestimate their feelings of losses in crowdsourcing contests, which is consistent with the findings in Harinck et al (2007). On the contrary, the findings suggested that reversed loss aversion exists for both ideation and expertise-based contests. The effect of loss on feelings is greater for expertise-based contests than for ideation contests. Solvers are more loss averse reversed for ideation contests than for expertise-based contests.

## Implications for practice

This research also provides some practical implications for sponsors, solvers, and crowdsourcing platforms. First, sponsors should keep in mind that reducing loss aversion is an important tactic in attracting enough solvers to participate in the contest and motivating them to exert effort to provide solutions. In addition, solvers’ affective reactions to loss in ideation contests are less intensive than in expertise-based contests. Adding some fun to the problem solving process with task design could increase solver’s intrinsic motivation, which will reduce their loss aversion. For example, increasing solvers’ autonomy, a distinction between ideation contests and expertisebased contests, could help the sponsor leverage solvers’ creativity to acquire unique ideas or solutions. The reversed loss aversion does not mean that the reward can be set lower than that in the setting without reversed loss aversion. The findings of game theory analysis suggested that the reward for the optimal solution should be equal to the optimal reward in the contest without loss aversion.

Second, solvers should choose to participate in ideation contests to reduce their negative feelings in case of failure, as we found that the effect of loss on feeling is lower for ideation contests than for expertise-based contests. One possible reason for the difference is that the solvers could effectively discount their negative feeling of losses in ideation contests. The solvers should also keep in mind that in order to maximize their utility they should exert more effort in the contest tasks than in the setting without loss aversion for both ideation and expertise-based contests.

Third, the research has implications for crowdsourcing platforms on system design. A crowdsourcing intermediary that connects solvers and sponsors should develop diverse models for different tasks. Contest is preferred to other models for ideation tasks. The industrial practice also shows that contests were widely adopted in many crowdsourcing platforms, such as on www.innocentive. com and www.witmart.com. However, to complete complex expertise-based tasks (e.g., information system development), a contest may not be a best mechanism. For this kind of business tasks, some crowdsourcing intermediary, such as www.witmart.com, uses contract to match sponsors with optimal solver(s). The contract model works in the following way – ‘‘choose a freelancer (solver) based on their qualifications: negotiate the payment (the ‘reward’), what the job entails and when it will be completed. You pay the reward only when the work is done!’’ (see http://www.witmart.com/about/ howworks/employer). Witmart advises sponsors to use contest for logo design, business card design, and slogan design which require solvers to submit creative solutions. On the contrary, this platform advises sponsors to conduct contract model for tasks such as Website development, programming, and translation. The advice on www.witmart.com to some extent supports our experiment scenarios of contest categories, i.e., slogan design and translation. ## Limitations and future research

There are several limitations in this study. First, the experiment did not ask the solvers to actually submit solutions to win the reward. Thus, they might not completely experience the real happiness of success or sadness of failure in the contest. To compare their anticipated and actually experienced affective reaction (e.g., Kermer et al, 2006), for future research, participants in the experiment could be required to actually finish and submit solutions to win the reward. Thus, we could see whether or not the difference between predicted and actual feelings affects their further participation in the future. Second, we manipulated the contest task size as the task workload rather than task difficulty levels. For example, in the ideation task we asked the solvers to submit one slogan for the small contest with the reward of 100 Yuan RMB, and for the large contest with the reward of 1000 Yuan RMB, the solvers were requested to submit 10 slogans. In future research, experiment design will be modified to test how the difficulty levels of tasks may have effect on solvers’ loss aversion. Third, Chinese students were used as subjects in the experiment for this study. In future research, subjects from other cultures will be used to see whether and how the cultural factors may affect the extent of loss aversion in crowdsourcing contests. Finally, to test the validity of the results from this study, we consider conducting field experiment on real crowdsourcing platforms such as Mechanical Turk, CrowdSpring, and Zhubajie, so that the solvers on these platforms could be used as participants in experiment.
