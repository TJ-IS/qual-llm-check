# DIGITAL PLATFORM ECOSYSTEM DYNAMICS: THE ROLES OF PRODUCT SCOPE, INNOVATION, AND COLLABORATIVE NETWORK CENTRALITY<sup>1</sup>

He Li Department of Management, Wilbur O. and Ann Powers College of Business Clemson University, Clemson, SC, U.S.A. {hl3@clemson.edu}

Chen Zhang Fogelman College of Business and Economics University of Memphis, Memphis, TN, U.S.A. {czhang12@memphis.edu}

William J. Kettinger Department of Management, Wilbur O. and Ann Powers College of Business Clemson University, Clemson, SC, U.S.A. {wkttngr@clemson.edu}

This research highlights the circulative nature of digital platform ecosystem dynamics. Investigating these dynamics, we examine the mutual influence between participants’ product scope and product innovation over time and probe the moderating role of co-created collaborative networks. We distinguish between two types of product innovation: new product development and existing product updates. Our longitudinal analysis of the Hadoop software ecosystem indicates that participants covering a broader scope of the platform’s technological layers are less likely to develop new products but more likely to update existing products. In turn, participants with more frequent new product development are more likely to expand their product scope, whereas those with more frequent existing product updates are less likely to pursue scope expansion. Participants’ centrality in the ecosystem’s collaborative network amplifies the bidirectional link between product scope and existing product updates but weakens the link between product scope and new product development. Our findings offer a theoretical and practical understanding of temporal dynamics between participants’ product scope choices and different forms of product innovations in the co-created collaborative network environment.

Keywords: Digital platform ecosystems, dynamics, product innovation, new product development, existing product update, product scope, ecosystem environment, collaborative network

## Introduction

Digital platform ecosystems require their participants to chart a strategic course in turbulent waters. A digital platform is a collection of technologies with dedicated functionalities upon which participants can develop complementary products (Tiwana et al., 2010). The digital platform and its owner(s), along with the many participants and diverse complementary products, collectively form a digital platform ecosystem.

Although prior studies have focused on platform participants’ product strategies such as modularization (Tiwana, 2015) and portfolio management (Lee & Raghu, 2014), firm strategies associated with product scope in these ecosystems have not received much attention. In addition, research findings on the strategic workings of product scope in the software industry remain largely inconsistent. This knowledge gap is noteworthy, as both strategy and marketing literature point to product scope as essential to firm strategy.

Unlike most industrial environments, digital platform ecosystems are centered around a platform with a layered modular architecture (Yoo et al., 2010), aiming at harnessing their participants’ complementary digital innovations to extend the focal platform (Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018). We contextualize the platform participant’s product scope as the extent to which the participant’s products cover the platform’s architectural layers, representing the breadth of its products’ functionalities or within-platform diversification. To examine its impact, we focus on participants’ innovation as reflected in their new product development (NPD) and existing product updates (EPU) (Foerderer, 2020). Thus, our first research question is: How does a digital platform participant’s product scope impact its product innovation?

Furthermore, in an environment with significant path dependencies and competitive dynamics, “seemingly minor differences in the timing and sequence of investments into complementary strategies can have profound impacts on a firm’s ability to capture and protect market share” (Tanriverdi & Lee, 2008, p. 385). Hence, it is imperative for participants to continually adjust their product strategies based on feedback about earlier strategies’ effects. Thus, our second research question is: How does a participant’s product innovation shape its subsequent product scope?

In addition to the feedback loops, participants’ strategies and outcomes coevolve with the ecosystem environment (Tiwana et al., 2010). Past research has focused on the impacts of the competitive ecosystem environment on participants innovation (e.g., Foerderer et al., 2018; Xue et al., 2019). Although participants do “actively collaborate and co-create through interfirm relationships” in platform ecosystems (van Angeren et al., 2016, p. 19), the impacts of such a co-created collaborative network environment in digital platform ecosystems have yet to be understood (Li & Kettinger, 2021). We focus on participants’ centrality in the collaborative network, given its well-established implications for firm innovation (e.g., Gilsing et al., 2008; Soh, 2010). Therefore, our last research question is: How does a participant’s centrality in a collaborative network impact the mutual influence between the participant’s product scope and product innovation?

Our longitudinal analysis of the Hadoop ecosystem demonstrates that platform participants’ product scope increases their existing product update rate but reduces their new product development rate. In turn, participants with faster NPD rates are more likely to expand their product scope, while those who have frequent EPU are less likely to do so. Participants’ collaborative network centrality amplifies the mutual influence between product scope and EPU but weakens the mutual influence between product scope and NPD.

This research delivers a multilevel model delineating the temporal dynamic among platform participants’ product scope, product innovation, and the co-created collaborative network environment. In this way, we address inconsistent findings on the impact of software product scope. We highlight the role of the emergent collaborative network environment in digital platform ecosystems. Our findings provide managers in charge of digital product decisions with a strategic framework entailing the complex dynamics within platform ecosystems that occur across time.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Background

## Impacts of Product Scope

Strategy research has extensively examined firm product scope (e.g., Cottrell & Nault, 2004) and related theoretical concepts such as product diversification (e.g., Hitt et al., 1997) and within-industry diversification (e.g., Tanriverdi & Lee, 2008). Theoretical tensions have risen regarding the impacts of product scope and within-industry diversification (Su & Tsang, 2015; Wernerfelt, 2005). Relatedly, Hashai (2015) recognizes the effects of within-industry diversification as contingent on industry and firm characteristics. Hence, theorizing economies of within-industry scope should carefully consider industry context and boundary conditions.

The industry most relevant to our study is the software industry, characterized by strong network externalities and significant path dependencies (Tanriverdi & Lee, 2008). This hypercompetitive industry features fast innovation and technology change rates as well as high turbulence (Lee et al., 2010). The development of innovations in a digital platform ecosystem is often facilitated by open platform resources, leading to lowered entry barriers; meanwhile, participants face a high level of uncertainty and experience highly skewed outcomes (Boudreau, 2012).

Software innovations encompass variations in cultural, intellectual, and aesthetic aspects (Potts et al., 2008) and have a large, multidimensional product space. They can expand into “a virtually infinite variety” (Boudreau, 2012, p. 1411). This product space is further broadened by extensive recombination and reuse of existing software components (Yoo et al., 2010). Innovations within the software industry tend to be “rapid sequential innovation, reuse, and recombination” (Cohen & Lemley, 2001, p. 6).

Recognizing these pronounced characteristics of the software industry, a few studies have examined economies of scope in this industry, but results vary. Boudreau (2012) finds that application software producers tend to keep the product scope narrow and specialized instead of expanding it. Cottrell and Nault (2004) provide evidence of diseconomies of scope in production. Lee and Raghu (2014) find the presence of scope economy in app markets—i.e., apps developed by developers with greater diversification across app categories are more likely to remain in the top charts and contribute to developers’ sales. In contrast, Tanriverdi and Lee (2008) suggest that implementing only market-related diversification leads to reduced sales growth. Overall, the impacts of software product scope have yet to be well-understood; even less is known in our research context—digital platform ecosystems.

## Product Innovation: New Product Development and Existing Product Updates

NPD and EPU compete for limited resources and managerial attention (Nuscheler et al., 2019) and have different impacts (Chaney & Devinney, 1992; Cottrell & Nault, 2004). Managers strive to “allocate scarce resources either to the maintenance of a range of products” or “to the efficient production of a small number of products” (Sorenson, 2000, p. 577).

A rich body of marketing and strategy research has identified NPD as a critical determinant of sustained firm performance (e.g., Olson et al., 1995, Schilling & Hill, 1998). NPD entails many activities, such as understanding the market and consumers, generating new product ideas, designing prototypes, strategizing market positioning, allocating human resources, and producing, testing, and launching the actual product (Tzokas et al., 2004). Established NPD routines (Cooper et al., 2003) and NPD process capabilities, combined with effective deployment of vital organizational resources, positively affect NPD outcomes. Adequate resource allocation, especially marketing and technical resources, is a critical success factor (see Ernst 2002 for a review). Although NPD can bring new opportunities and benefits to firms, it incurs substantial costs and risks, and failure rates tend to be high (Patel & Jayaram, 2014).

Research has also investigated existing product updates as a means of innovation. Software updates refer to “selfcontained modules of software” (Fleischmann et al., 2016, p. 84) provided to users for free to extend the functionality of the software already adopted. EPU are not stand-alone programs; they need to be installed and integrated into the existing software (e.g., Dunn, 2004). Software vendors have had a long history of using feature updates to enhance their products, especially in the software platform context (Mathias, 2012). Research on software management explores the lifecycle of software product management (Mens & Demeyer, 2008; Svahnberg et al., 2010), especially the fulfillment of important constraints such as technical limitations and the amount of effort, time, and resources spent on each product release (see

Svahnberg et al., 2010 for a review). After a software release, the EPU plan is updated based on customer feedback and market response (Ruhe & Momoh, 2005).

## Mutual  Between Product Scope and Product Innovation

Digital platform ecosystems are dynamic and evolving (Tiwana et al., 2010) because of such factors as frequent adjustment of platform owner’s strategies, updates to the platform architecture, low entry barriers in the ecosystem, and intense competition and innovation efforts by participants (Boudreau, 2012; Foerderer et al., 2018; Kapoor & Agarwal, 2017). Hence, beyond the “traditionally” studied unidirectional impacts of participants’ strategies, it is crucial for participants to constantly adjust their strategies based on the observed outcome of past strategic actions and the analysis of the fast-changing environment.

Importantly, due to generative innovation opportunities from the platform’s layered modular architecture (Yoo et al., 2010), participants’ product scope and innovation are intertwined in particularly complex ways. For example, NPD may remain within the firm’s current product scope and cover a layer of the platform already encompassed by the firm’s existing products. Alternatively, the firm’s new product may diversify into a new layer that existing products do not cover to leverage new opportunities. Similarly, an EPU may add a feature that expands into a new layer or improves functionality within its current scope. Given the potential two-way impact, it is vital to unpack the mutual influence between participants’ product scope and product innovation in digital platform ecosystems over time.

## Network Environment in Digital Platform Ecosystems

Firms must consider the context where they interact with other firms in strategy formulation and execution (Echols & Tsai, 2005). Platform literature acknowledges that participants’ endogenous choices coevolve with the ecosystem environment (Tiwana et al., 2010). In a highly competitive environment, participants emphasize ways to attract customers to their products and away from competitors’. In a collaborative environment, participants focus on accessing and developing resources from others in the network. Unlike prior platform literature that examines the impact of competitive environmental characteristics such as platform owner’s entry (Foerderer et al., 2018) and market concentration (Xue et al., 2019), we focus on the collaborative network environment co-created by participants’ interfirm relationships.

Extensive strategy research has identified the innovation impacts of interfirm collaborative networks and concluded that a firm’s position in the network affects its innovativeness (Ahuja, 2000). Centrality has been commonly used to measure the firm’s network position, which has been found to have both a direct (e.g., Gilsing et al., 2008; Soh, 2010) and a moderating impact (e.g., Paruchuri, 2010; Rothaermel & Hess, 2007) on firm innovation. Therefore, we examine how network centrality, as a characteristic of emergent collaborative networks co-created by platform participants, influences the mutual relationship between their product scope and product innovation.

## Effects of Product Scope on Product Innovation

Extrapolated from the product scope literature in other contexts (e.g., Cottrell & Nault, 2004; Tanriverdi & Lee, 2008), platform participants with products covering a wider range of the platform’s technological layers (e.g., data access, data storage, security) tend to serve more related markets and customer segments. Hence, they can better identify and understand the changing needs of these customers, which helps them identify unmet or emerging product needs to improve their performance. Furthermore, participants with a broader product scope can accumulate more technical knowledge about the platform through developers’ interactions across platform layers. As the platform architecture constantly evolves, firms can stay current in their technical know-how and identify market opportunities enabled by the advancements in the architecture. However, given the greater dependency of these participants on the underlying platform architecture, they may experience greater pressure to stay compatible with the evolving architecture (Tiwana, 2015). To leverage these opportunities and cope with the challenges related to having a broader product scope, platform participants can focus on more EPU or NPD.

Software vendors, especially in the digital platform context, have extensive experience using feature updates to enhance their existing products. To add new functionalities and improve existing products, participants often have already allocated tangible and intangible resources, especially platform architecture technical knowledge, to these products. They may need less time to recombine and experiment with various product modules to produce product updates. Also, there is little need to assign additional resources and commit extra managerial attention, a scarce resource identified by prior scope economies literature (Hashai, 2015), to oversee and coordinate product update efforts. By further enhancing their existing products, the participant signals to customers its commitment to improving customer satisfaction and meeting customer demands (Giarratana & Fosfuri, 2007). Another benefit to the participant is increasing switching costs for the existing customer and strengthening the lock-in effect. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1a: In a digital platform ecosystem, participants with broader product scope across the focal platform’s technological layers are more likely to update their existing products in the subsequent period.

To cope with the challenges and act on the opportunities introduced by the fast-progressing platform architecture and broad product scope, platform participants may choose to develop and launch new products separate from their existing products. However, while new products can push firms to tap into new opportunities, NPD often requires reallocating resources from other projects and may incur substantial costs and risks.

In the digital platform context, participants with a broader product scope across the platform’s layers face greater complexity; their existing resources, especially technical resources, are more likely locked into managing and maintaining the current product scope. Hence, it may be more challenging and time-consuming to redeploy these resources to NPD. Furthermore, the virtually unlimited product space, low entry barriers (Boudreau, 2010), and fast-paced evolution in platform ecosystems intensify the competition among participants, shortening the technology lifecycle and increasing uncertainty in the performance of new products. In this scenario, managers also need to pay close attention to the possible cannibalization of existing products by new ones (Hashai, 2015). Hence, we hypothesize that

H1b: In a digital platform ecosystem, participants with a broader product scope across the focal platform’s technological layers are less likely to develop new products in the subsequent period.

## Effects of Product Innovation on Product Scope

An EPU focus stresses the importance of satisfying existing customers’ needs (Bonner & Walker, 2004). Literature suggests that current customers favor a smoother customer engagement process, in which incremental but continuous improvements can facilitate customers’ transition to the updated version of the existing product.

Hence, participants with more frequent EPU are more likely to fine-tune existing products to improve user experiences without expanding into the platform’s new layers. Even if the firm can explore and identify which new layer to expand into, it may be challenging to integrate new functionalities with its existing product offerings without imposing a steep learning curve on existing customers (Chandy & Tellies, 2000), especially in the digital platform context featuring a high level of technical complexity and rapid technological advancements. In addition, frequent EPU enhances the participant’s familiarity with existing products, customers, and market segments (Tzokas et al., 2004), increasing organizational inertia of maintaining the current product scope and reducing the participant’s willingness to explore new platform layers. Even if they are willing to expand into new platform layers, most of their existing resources may be locked into the maintenance of existing products (Nuscheler et al., 2019). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2a: In a digital platform ecosystem, participants with more frequent updates to their existing products are less likely to expand their product scope across the platform’s technological layers in the subsequent period.

In the NPD process, firms’ investments to acquire new resources (Morgan & Berthon, 2008) and develop their R&D capabilities allow them to gather information from more diverse sources about market trends (Boeker & Wiltbank, 2005), receive more updates about the shifts in customer demands, and increase their awareness of the technological landscape (Nuscheler et al., 2019). During this process, firms improve their exploration-oriented mindset and the ability to sense new opportunities. Furthermore, firms with more frequent NPD are better positioned to learn from past NPD projects (Takeuchi & Nonaka, 1986), identify pervasive patterns, and fine-tune such practices in later NPD projects (Winter, 2003). They may engage in knowledge articulation and codification related to NPD activities more effectively over time, enhancing their abilities to seize market opportunities (Rothaermel & Hess, 2007).

The digital platform ecosystem represents a more volatile environment (e.g., more frequent platform architecture updates) than many other industries. In this context, filled with uncertainties and opportunities, participants often do not know ex ante the distribution of customers’ heterogeneous preferences. Expanding into new and often adjacent layers of the platform architecture allows these firms to “hedge their bets” (Sorenson, 2000, p. 579); each new layer they expand into provides a chance to find profitable opportunities by fulfilling the unmet demand of customers. As a result of their frequent NPD efforts and improved capabilities in sensing and seizing new opportunities, they are more likely to execute scope-expanding activities. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2b: In a digital platform ecosystem, participants with more frequent new product development are more likely to expand their product scope across the platform’s technological layers in the subsequent period.

## Moderating Effects of Collaborative Network Centrality

Firms central to a collaborative network can reach more partners in the network, thereby acquiring technical resources, market information, and knowledge more easily (Ahuja et al., 2009; Chellappa & Saraf, 2010). Participants with more central positions have more opportunities to access timely information about customer preferences, platform resources, and external technical resources from their partners, improving their capabilities to enhance existing products.

Such access to resources, information, and knowledge from a larger pool of connected firms complements the firm’s competencies acquired from its trial-and-error experiences within its existing product scope, facilitating further experimentation and recombination efforts (Gilsing et al., 2008) to develop product enhancements. Hence, a central position in the collaborative network makes it more likely for participants with a broader product scope to identify emerging opportunities and leverage them through EPU. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3a: In a digital platform ecosystem, the positive effect of product scope on existing product updates will be stronger for participants with higher centrality in the collaborative network.

Collaborative alliances help participants overcome resource constraints in NPD. In addition to acquiring resources and knowledge from their alliance partners, firms in a central position can build a stronger reputation (Ahuja et al., 2009) and leverage it to access scarce resources in the network, enhance network effects, and foster in-depth connections (Ravindran et al., 2015). Thus, the participant’s internal resource constraint for NPD may be alleviated to some extent by its access to resources in the collaborative network. The firm may also benefit from its partners’ knowledge and improve its competency in executing various activities such as idea generation and product launching, leading to improved NPD capabilities.

Furthermore, despite intense competition and dynamic evolution in a platform ecosystem, participants with greater network centrality are more likely to access up-to-date information to reduce the uncertainty in NPD activities. Central firms can leverage their position in the network to identify partners with access to new information promptly (Gnyawali & Madhavan, 2001). Prior literature points out that alliances not only serve as channels for distributing resources and knowledge but also offer more collaborative opportunities in NPD (Gilsing et al., 2008), mitigating the risks associated with NPD efforts. Hence, we hypothesize:

H3b: In a digital platform ecosystem, the negative effect of product scope on new product development will be weaker for participants with higher centrality in the collaborative network.

Participants with a more central position in the collaborative network have a greater need to achieve and maintain technical and social compatibility—i.e., they need to maintain system interoperability with alliance partners and invest in the integration of technological components (Chellappa & Saraf, 2010). Thus, participants with more frequent EPU may need to spend more effort on ensuring technical compatibility between their updated products and ecosystem partners products. Additional technical resources and managerial attention may need to be dedicated to integration efforts, reducing available resources for scope expansion initiatives.

Furthermore, central participants in the collaborative network have more access to information through their partners, helping them better identify the best features to add to their existing products. Given the significant uncertainty and resource requirement that expanding to new platform layers may incur, they may be less incentivized to explore opportunities beyond the current product scope. Central participants can also leverage the informational advantages to assess existing products’ performance and customer experience, leading to greater confidence in maintaining the existing product scope. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4a: In a digital platform ecosystem, the negative effect of existing product updates on product scope will be stronger for participants with higher centrality in the collaborative network.

Firms in collaborative networks may share their resources and information with partners (Gilsing et al., 2008). Thus, for firms with a more central position in the network, the resources invested in the NPD process and the knowledge gained from the NPD experience are likely to be diffused to other participants in the ecosystem. In the hypercompetitive environment, such diffusion of knowledge may further decrease their competitive advantage relative to other network members, thereby weakening the participants’ motivation and ability to exploit the opportunities in new layers. Also, prior social network research demonstrates that central firms in the network are often deeply immersed in cognitive schemes (March, 1991). Consequently, they may be less receptive to new information and knowledge, weakening their explorative mindset (Wang et al., 2014) and capabilities of sensing new opportunities.

Moreover, greater centrality in the collaborative network makes it easier for participants to leverage new opportunities by partnering with other participants. Given the high uncertainty associated with expanding into additional layers (Gans & Stern, 2003), to mitigate risks, participants in a more central position in the network may prefer partnering with others that provide the targeted new layers over investing their own resources. Importantly, participants with higher network centrality can better select “ideal” partners (Gnyawali & Madhavan, 2001). Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4b: In a digital platform ecosystem, the positive effect of new product development on product scope will be weaker for participants with higher centrality in the collaborative network.

## Methodology

## Research Context

We used the Hadoop ecosystem as the study’s empirical context for several reasons. First, the Hadoop ecosystem is practically relevant, as it is dominant in the big data and analytics market (Tambe, 2014). It presents tremendous business opportunities to both established firms (e.g., Cisco, IBM, Dell, and Microsoft) and startups (e.g., Cloudera and Hortonworks).

Second, as an architectural framework-based digital platform, Hadoop provides many open source technological components with different functionalities, ranging from data storage to data processing, data access, data management, security, etc. The recombinational use cases among layers of technological components are more explicit than the product “categories” listed on other platforms such as Android and iOS. Participants in the Hadoop ecosystem choose varying product scopes along these layers. Firms such as Splice Machine, Quest, and Jethro Data focus on limited Hadoop architecture layers; meanwhile, Hortonworks, Cloudera, and Talend work within multiple layers. Hence, the architectural framework and variations of participants’ products make the Hadoop platform well-suited to measuring participants’ product scope across the platform’s layered modular architecture.

Third, the Hadoop platform is owned by open source communities that encourage participants’ interactions and impose minimal restrictions on participation and the use of platform resources (Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018). As an open platform, it “is likely to continue as new kinds of technologies and new patterns of organizational and human behavior co-evolve” (de Reuver et al., 2018, p. 7). Participants actively engage in collaborative interactions, increasing the significance of the network environment cocreated by their strategic actions. Unlike in platforms governed by firms such as Android, SAP, and Firefox, which are commonly examined in prior platform literature, the twoway interaction between participants and co-created ecosystem environments is more salient in open platforms like Hadoop. Such open platforms’ hyperturbulence also triggers the criticality of participants’ dynamic adjustments of product scope and product innovations.

## Data

The Lexis-Nexis database was the starting point for our data collection. To find firms offering products within the Hadoop ecosystem, we searched for Hadoop-related keywords to retrieve relevant news articles. We performed a content analysis to identify firms that provide Hadoop-related products. Then, we collected each firm’s product releases and alliance announcements from the firm’s website, BusinessWire, PR Newswire, Lexis-Nexis database, and Google. Two authors jointly collected the data and decided on data entry/exclusion to ensure comprehensiveness and accuracy. The initial pool included 745 product releases and 954 interfirm alliances specific to the Hadoop ecosystem. Three coders (i.e., one domain expert and two authors of this paper) examined independently whether each release is related to Hadoop. This step of the data collection process yielded 523 unique product releases and 892 alliance events. Given that no existing secondary data sources maintain such information, to the best of our knowledge, this formalized approach produced a comprehensive list of product releases and interfirm alliances in the Hadoop ecosystem. We also collected additional data for control variables from the Crunchbase database and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). Table 1 presents the detailed process and criteria of our data collection and data filtering. The final dataset includes the quarterly data of 112 Hadoop ecosystem participants from the third quarter of 2009 to the first quarter of 2016.

## Measures

Product Innovation: For each product release announcement, we coded the name and version number of the products related to Hadoop (e.g., Cloudera Enterprise 5.2 and HDP 1.3). A firm may have multiple products associated with Hadoop. We code a product release as NPD if the product name is the first appearance in the firm’s Hadoop-related product release history, while its subsequent updates containing different version numbers are coded as EPU. We only count EPU that introduce new features, functionalities, or capabilities in the participant’s products, which are more consistent with the notion of innovation (Foerderer et al., 2018). Also, focusing on major updates can address the sample selection bias as firms often announce major EPU rather than minor maintenance updates such as bug fixes or performance enhancement. We created two variables to measure a firm’s product innovation— i.e., NPD<sub>it</sub> and EPU<sub>it</sub>, which measure the total number of NPD and major EPU that firm i released in quarter t, respectively.

Product Scope: We conducted a robust content analysis of the product releases to identify the technological layers covered in participants’ Hadoop-related products. We first performed a manual coding following a standard approach and spent the time necessary to understand and report the data (Elliott, 2018), which also helped us better understand the context and develop a “professional vision” of the data (Goodwin, 1994).

To mitigate concerns of subjectivity and bias and ensure data trustworthiness, we adopted a common practice to involve three coders (Church et al., 2019): two authors of the paper and one external independent coder with domain knowledge of Hadoop. The third external coder was not given any information about our research question and hypotheses (Luker, 2008). All three coders jointly and iteratively developed the codebook and used it to code product releases (Church et al., 2019; Erlingsson & Brysiewicz, 2017). In the iterative process, we relied on the intercoder reliability as a consistency indicator while maintaining the main objective of producing sense-making and consistent coding results (Spencer et al., 2014). We further validated our manual coding results through a text analysis approach. We transformed the codebook into dictionaries that use keywords and phrases to categorize product releases and contain exclusion rules in each category. We employed an algorithm that matches the dictionaries and applies exclusion rules to identify the covered layers in each product release. For clarity, we present the detailed content analysis process, coding samples, and codebook in Appendix A (Creswell, 2013; Noble & Smith, 2015). ## Empirical Approach and Model Identification

We utilized the panel vector autoregression (PVAR) approach for our empirical analysis. The PVAR model is particularly suitable for testing the bidirectional relationships between a system of endogenous variables without imposing restrictions (Chen et al., 2015; Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2014). The PVAR model uses the generalized method of moments (GMM) estimators and can control for firm-level unobserved heterogeneity (see Abrigo & Love, 2016 for more technical details of PVAR estimations). Although the PVAR model may have disadvantages in identifying causal impacts relative to other methods such as truly exogenous instruments (Chen et al., 2015), it is currently the most appropriate approach for testing mutual influences (Dewan & Ramaprasad, 2014). We specified our PVAR model as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} _ {i, t} = \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {p} \boldsymbol {\Phi} _ {s} \boldsymbol {y} _ {i, t - s} + \beta E x o g e n o u s _ {i, t - s} + \boldsymbol {f} _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\xi} _ {t} + \boldsymbol {v} _ {i, t}
$$

where ?? is a vector of all endogenous variables; Φ represents the matrices of endogenous variables’ coefficients; p indicates the number of lagged periods; Exogenous denotes the vector of all exogenous and control variables; ?? denotes the vector of firm fixed effects; ?? represents the vector of quarter fixed effects; and ?? is the matrix of error terms.

Since this study contains both direct and moderating effects, we estimated two models: Model 1 only includes three endogenous variables (i.e., NPD, EPU, and ProductScope) in vector ??; Model 2 is a six-equation system including three endogenous variables and their interactions with the moderator Centrality.<sup>2</sup>

We followed the standard procedure of the PVAR analysis. First, we transformed the variables using the forward orthogonal deviation (also known as the Helmert transformation) to eliminate firm-level fixed effects (Abrigo & Love, 2016). Second, we examined the stationarity assumption of endogenous variables using the unit-root test (Abrigo & Love, 2016). As shown in Figure D1 in Appendix D, the eigenvalues of all endogenous variables in both models lie inside the unit-circle, indicating that our estimations satisfy the stationarity condition. Third, we used Andrews and Lu’s (2001) selection criteria to identify the proper length of lags. The results (i.e., Table D1 in Appendix D) show that the first lag model has the smallest values on MBIC, MAIC, and MQIC. Hansen J statistics in the first-order model are also insignificant at the 5% level. We thus cannot reject the null hypothesis that the overidentification restrictions are valid. Therefore, we fit first-order PVAR models with all available lags of endogenous variables as instruments using GMM estimation. We report the estimation of PVAR coefficients of all estimated models (i.e., Tables D2- D5) in Appendix D.

The estimated PVAR coefficients may not be informative enough to establish causal relationships among variables due to the complicated dynamics in PVAR systems (Enders, 2008). Also, we may not directly interpret the PVAR coefficients because of multicollinearity concerns after adding lagged terms (Sims, 1980). Hence, following prior studies (e.g., Song et al., 2018), we relied on the postestimation impulse response functions (IRFs) to test our hypotheses. As Abrigo and Love (2016, p. 793) noted, IRFs “have known interpretation when the [PVAR] model is stable.” IRFs can describe how future values of a dependent variable change with one unit increase of a predictor variable and whether a shock to one predictor will have a permanent or transitory influence on the dependent variable. If the effect is transitory, IRFs can further demonstrate how long the effect will dissipate (Chen et al., 2015).

Given that our PVAR model satisfies the stationary condition, we can compute the simple IRFs by rewriting the model as an infinite vector moving-average (VMA). However, there is no causal interpretation in the simple IRFs because a shock to one variable may correlate with shocks to other variables. Also, the structural PVAR estimation results cannot be directly used to compute IRFs since it is impossible to recover all information about parameters, the variance of error terms, and covariance among error terms (Enders, 2008). To avoid this problem, we first transformed the VMA parameters into the orthogonalized IRFs. We also used the Cholesky decomposition (Sims, 1980) to impose a recursive structure on the PVAR. Given that the decomposition forces the PVAR system to be potentially asymmetric, the ordering of the variables may affect the accuracy of the forecast. We specified the ordering based on the Granger causality test results of each model (i.e., Table D6 in Appendix D). Using the orthogonalized IRFs derived from the recursive PVAR model, we simulated the fitted PVAR system using a Monte Carlo method with 200 runs. We tested our hypotheses using the estimated IRFs and confidence intervals based on robust standard errors clustered by firm.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted several robustness checks and found consistent results, as shown in Appendix E. First, selecting the number of lags as instruments in the PVAR model seems arbitrary. Abrigo and Love (2016, pp. 799-802) suggest a rule of thumb that the estimations using the specified number of lags as instruments should have insignificant Hansen J-statistics. Although our usage of all available lags as instruments satisfies this rule, it is necessary to demonstrate that our results are not sensitive to the specifications of instruments. Thus, we ran an analysis with twenty lags as instruments. Table E1 in Appendix E shows that the results are highly consistent with our hypothesis testing results.

Second, we used the three-year time window when constructing the collaborative network, which might challenge our results’ stability. We used the five-year moving window approach to construct the collaborative network and recalculate network-related measures to mitigate this concern. As shown in Table E2, almost all results remain stable, except that the impact of product scope on EPU becomes insignificant. This suggests that when longer-term alliance relationships are considered, participants with broader product scope may not always release EPU more frequently.

Third, to rule out potential bias caused by our raw count measurements of product scope and innovation, we conducted analyses using alternative measures. Table E3 shows the results when the natural log of product scope, EPU, and NPD measures are used. In addition, when measuring product scope, by counting the number of layers covered by a product, we implicitly assume all layers are similar. However, it is likely that different Hadoop layers have different levels of coding complexity. So, two products covering the same number of layers, albeit different layers, may be associated with varying amounts of technical knowledge and platform dependency, causing disparate impacts on product innovation.

## Discussion

We study the mutual influence between platform participants’ product scope and product innovation (i.e., EPU and NPD) as well as the moderating effect of the co-created collaborative network environments. Our longitudinal analysis of the Hadoop ecosystem indicates that participants’ product scope across the platform’s architectural layers contributes to more frequent EPU but decreases their NPD frequency. In turn, participants’ EPU frequency reduces their subsequent product scope, whereas their NPD frequency increases the subsequent product scope. Participants’ collaborative network centrality amplifies the mutual influence between product scope on EPU but weakens the mutual impact between product scope and NPD.

## Theoretical Implications

This research contributes a contextualized understanding of product scope and within-industry diversification in the digital platform ecosystem context. We consider the distinguishing features of the digital platform context in the following ways. First, we build on the digital platform’s unique layered modular architecture and contextualize the product scope’s definition as the extent to which the participant’s products cover the layers of the focal platform architecture. Second, given the digital platform’s emphasis on complementary innovation, we focus on participants’ innovation in the forms of NPD and EPU, which sets us apart from prior studies focusing on firm survival or financial performance in other contexts. Third, the fast-evolving and path-dependent nature of platform ecosystems motivated us to incorporate feedback loops—i.e., we look beyond the unidirectional impacts and focus on the mutual influence between the participant’s product scope and product innovation, adding a temporal distinction, “a useful starting point for bringing the time dimension into theory development” (Tiwana et al., 2010, p. 682). In doing so, we add insights addressing inconclusive understandings of software product scope’s impacts—e.g., our results highlight the differences in the mutual influence between product scope and NPD versus EPU over time.

This study also adds insights to the research stream on product scope and within-industry diversification in the software industry by investigating the link between product scope and software product portfolio management regarding existing products and new products. Although these two aspects of product-related strategies are highly intertwined (i.e., modifying product scope often needs adjustments to existing products or launching new products and vice versa), prior studies either examine them as independent and separate decisions (e.g., Cottrell & Nault, 2004) or inspect one or the other. Our research not only highlights them as mutually dependent yet different strategies but also empirically tests their relationship.

This research contributes to our understanding of the impacts of digital platform ecosystem environments on platform participants. Past platform research has focused on the impact of platform strategies and the competitive environment such as generational transitions, input control, market concentration, and platform owner’s entry on participants’ strategies and performance (e.g., Foerderer et al., 2018; Kapoor & Agarwal, 2017; Xue et al., 2019; Tiwana, 2015). We add insights into the role of the ecosystem’s emergent collaborative network that is co-created by participants’ strategic actions. In doing so, we move beyond the descriptive demonstration of the prevalence of collaborative networks in the platform ecosystem (e.g., Basole, 2009; van Angeren et al., 2016) by exploring how such a network impacts the relationship between product scope and innovation.

Taken together, our theory-building effort results in a multilevel theory explaining platform ecosystem dynamics. Specifically, our model delineates the mutual influences between participants’ product scope and innovation at a lower level (i.e., platform participant) and the moderating effect of the co-created higher-level collaborative network structure (i.e., top-down effects). Expressly, when all firms simultaneously undertake deliberate actions, they co-create and unintentionally shift the platform’s networked environment, creating boundary conditions for their success in the ecosystem. Firms’ innovation, therefore, is the joint effect of their own strategies and the co-created networked environment.

## Practical Implications

In dynamic and complex platform ecosystems, participants product strategies are not one-time static decisions—instead, managers need to dynamically adjust their strategies and follow the circular loop of strategies, outcomes, and environment. Participants make strategic scope decisions concerning the layers of the focal platform’s architecture. Positioning in a broader set of platform layers helps participants cover more market segments, facilitate recombinational innovation, and establish market power; however, it also requires allocating more resources to coordinate efforts associated with these layers. Thus, participants with a broader product scope often have more EPU but fewer NPD. Participants located in the center of the collaborative network tend to experience a stronger effect of product scope on EPU but a weaker effect on NPD. In turn, participants’ EPU vs. NPD frequency reflects their commitment to better serving current customers and improving existing products vs. exploring new opportunities and increasing product offerings. Participants with frequent EPU tend to stay within the existing layers of the architecture and develop associated competencies. In contrast, those with frequent NPD can better identify and pursue opportunities in new architectural layers. Participants’ network centrality amplifies their commitment to EPU because they need to maintain compatibility with collaborative partners, further decreasing their chance of expanding product scope. In contrast, participants’ collaborative network centrality makes new information and resources accumulated in NPD activities become obsolete or diffused to partners more quickly, weakening their motivation to pursue product scope expansion.

The above evolutionary paths of participants’ products provide Hadoop ecosystem managers with a useful decision tool. As suggested by the institutional theory, one strategy firms can adopt is to conform to the industry norm—e.g., on average, what other players are doing in the product scopeinnovation trajectories under different environmental attributes. In this sense, our results serve as a useful strategic benchmark of product evolutionary trajectories in the entire platform ecosystem. However, “deep-pocketed” ecosystem leaders with ample resources and superior capabilities may better understand how most participants evolve their product strategies and product portfolios and accordingly take innovative strategic actions to improve performance.

Our study also offers insights to platform owners on growing and sustaining their ecosystems. Platform owners may want to promote interconnectivity among participants proactively. For example, platform owners may facilitate collaborative networks for participants with a broader product scope, motivating them to update existing products, explore new products, and enhance their capabilities. Attracting and maintaining a strategic balance of participants with diverse product scope and innovation activities in NPD and EPU is also relevant for the platform ecosystem’s innovation outcomes and sustainability.

## Limitations and Future Research

Despite our theoretical and practical contributions, we recognize some limitations and discuss future research opportunities. We acknowledge the limitation in our measurement of the product scope and innovation constructs. Although we supplement the manual coding process with automated text analysis as much as the data allows, we recommend that future research study other digital platform ecosystems with more abundant data or develop more innovative methodological approaches to leverage the available data and automate the identification of product layers, NPD, and EPU. Our study focuses on product innovation as one aspect of firm performance. In other platform ecosystems such as mobile app ecosystems, where data on platform-related product sales, profits, or downloads are available, alternative dimensions of firm performance may be further explored.

Second, although the PVAR model best aligns with the structure of our theoretical model, we recognize its limitations in generating causal inferences (Chen et al., 2015). For instance, the GMM estimators using lagged variables as instruments may not completely mitigate endogeneity concerns. The number of parameters will rapidly increase as the number of endogenous variables and lags goes up, decreasing the efficiencies in the estimation. Future research can employ alternative identifications such as exogenous instruments to further address endogeneity issues.

In terms of theoretical perspectives, this research focuses on participants’ embeddedness in the collaborative ecosystem environment because it is prevalent but understudied. Future research can tackle dynamics in both competitive and collaborative ecosystem environments. For example, building on the social network perspective, future studies can examine how participants’ positions in competitive and collaborative networks influence their strategies and performance differently over time.

## Coding for Hadoop Product Layers

The first step is to make sense of the context—i.e., the Hadoop ecosystem and its major open source projects and functionalities. The authors first collected online articles about Hadoop ecosystem architecture and shared the compiled list of articles<sup>4</sup> among three coders. In the second stage, we independently developed the initial codebook and organized it as a table including columns of layers, definition of the layer, major functionalities, and included Hadoop open source projects and any special cases listed as bullet points. Following Church et al. (2019), we organized a group meeting to discuss and refine the codebook. Then, we had a coding procedure training session, which was a group discussion on how to code each product release announcement following the hands-on content analysis proposed by Erlingsson and Brysiewicz (2017) and using our initially agreed upon codebook.

Adopting Erlingsson and Brysiewicz’s (2017) approach, we identified the meaning units of each article, which essentially involves decomposing an article into groups of sentences based on two criteria: (1) the functionality of a firm’s products, and (2) the underlying Hadoop technical components used. Then, we converted these meaning units into condensed meaning units, which only included more generic keywords and phrases. Next, we transformed the condensed meaning units to code—i.e., the product’s major functions, which were utilized as the foundations of further abstracting into categories. The categories are what we labeled as Hadoop technological layers.

We independently coded the first 50 product releases and compared our coding of categories/layers. If there was any inconsistency, we compared our transformed code to make consistent coding decisions, and, if we failed to reach a consensus, we went back to compare our condensed meaning units. Based on our first-round coding, we revised our codebook and continued to code the next 50 announcements. We continued this coding process until it yielded consistent coding results. Table A1 presents our final codebook jointly developed and used in the coding process. Table A2 shows an illustrative example of our coding.

To further validate our coding results, we performed a text analysis. We processed the condensed meaning units into keywords and phrases categorizing each Hadoop layer. Given that product releases have richer, context-sensitive information, we defined a set of exclusion rule for certain categories. Table A3 summarizes the dictionaries for text analysis, including the categorization dictionaries and exclusion rules.

After comparing manual coding with the text analysis results, we only found four inconsistent coding results among the 523 product releases, yielding an accuracy rate of 99.24%. Two authors examined these four inconsistencies and jointly decided to favor the manual coding results (see Table A4). Overall, combining a formal manual coding approach and a text analysis helps minimize the measurement error and increase the reliability of the results.

## Description of Control Variables

FirmAgeit is the number of years between firm i’s founding and the end of quarter t. A firm’s age often impacts its resources, experience, and competitive capabilities, thereby influencing its market power, capabilities to dominate partners, and survival (Gans & Stern, 2003; Lin et al., 2007). TotalProductsit controls for firm i’s number of existing Hadoop-related products in quarter t.

Trademarks measure the firm’s marketing, advertising, and branding capabilities (Fosfuri & Giarratana, 2009) and communications of product differentiation (Gao & Hitt, 2012). Trademarkit is firm i’s number of Hadoop-related trademarks<sup>5</sup> denoted as “live” in the USPTO database in quarter t. We used patents to control a firm’s R&D capability (Jaffe, 1986), which is critical for its product development and innovation (Li et al., 2010). Given that 54.32% of observations have no patents during the study window, we used a binary variable Patentit, which took the value of 1 if firm i had an active patent in quarter t and 0 otherwise.

We controlled for other properties of the firm’s alliance portfolio that may impact its ability to access resources from partners (Hoffmann, 2007) and gain advantages related to the market, technology, and consumers (Chellappa & Saraf, 2010). TotalPartnersit measures firm i’s number of alliance partners during the three-year window up to quarter t, which impacts its access to resources from directly connected partners. AllianceDiversity1it is the entropy measure (Jacquemin & Berry, 1979) based on firm i’s partners’ technological layers in quarter t. AllianceDiversity2it is the standard deviation of firm i’s all partners’ Bonacich’s (1987) eigenvector centrality scores in quarter t. StructuralHoleit is the difference between 1 and firm i’s Burt’s (1992) constraint index in quarter t, which influences the firm’s alliance redundancy and access superiority due to the bridges of otherwise disconnected firms in the network.

We also included several platform ecosystem-level factors that may influence participants’ strategies and capabilities as controls. NetworkSizet represents the number of firms in the collaborative network in quarter t, which may be associated with the pool of network resources. NetworkDensityt is the number of actual ties divided by the total number of possible ties in the collaborative network (Rothaermel & Hess, 2007) in quarter t. It helps control for the overall speed of information delivery, trustworthiness between actors, and the depth of resource flow (Schilling & Phelps, 2007). In addition, participants’ product scope and innovation could be driven by competition in the ecosystem. We build a product competition network (Skilton & Bernardes, 2015) in each quarter—i.e., we treat each firm as a node and define the weighted edge as the Jaccard similarity between two firms’ sets of technological layers. Competitionit is firm i’s Bonacich’s eigenvector centrality score in this competition network in quarter t.

## Results of Robustness Checks

As shown in Table E7 and E8, while most theoretical hypotheses are supported in the two samples, we discuss some notable differences. First, startups are less likely to expand product scope after NPD because they often have limited resources to reconfigure NPD accumulated resources to explore new layers. This effect will be more salient when they have higher network centrality since they need to allocate more attention and efforts to ensure the new product’s compatibility with partners, discouraging them from expanding product scope after NPD. Furthermore, with high network centrality, startups with frequent EPU are more likely to expand product scope, which only manifests in the long term. Startups are often more open minded and flexible (Qian, 2002); thus, they can receive alternative perspectives and resources from their partners. Hence, startups’ network centralit helps them overcome possible EPU-related inertia, but they need time to build absorptive capacity and develop deeper interfirm relationships.

Second, established firms with a broader product scope have less frequent EPU, which will be amplified by network centrality. Since established firms usually make their existing products compatible with Hadoop, a broader product scope increases their EPU complexity. If established firms with broader product scope have higher network centrality, their innovation activities will likely increase the complexity in ensuring product compatibility with both their existing product family and partners’ products in the ecosystem. Hence, having acquired a central network position, established firms with broader product scope are more likely to launch NPD.
