## a b s t r a c t

In the past, many studies on business survival have been conducted but very few have explained the survival of online stores operating in the “long tail” market. To address this paucity, this study proposes a theoretical model, hypothesizing that online social networks, structural assurance, and online word-of-mouth (WOM) affect online store survival. An empirical study on 5772 online stores in two industries was conducted to validate the model. For social network factors, socialization efforts in SNS (social network site) personal space and online discussion forums were not found to be related to store survival. Structural assurance, as operationalized by the status of abiding by the “faithful description” consumer rights safeguarding program, has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on store life span. Furthermore, positive WOM, manifested as consistent DSR (Detailed Seller Rating) evaluations, was found to contribute to store survival. However, another WOM indicator, average DSR ratings, was found to be negatively related to store survival, In general, these results con<sup>fi</sup>rm some of our earlier hypotheses, while revealing some discrepancies from the theoretical predictions. Theoretical and practical implications on store survival in online environments are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The topic of <sup>fi</sup>rm survival has attracted scholars from economics [51], industrial organization [39] and strategy management [36, 57] for decades. Long time survival is an important goal of organizations, and a prerequisite for success in other terms such as pro<sup>fi</sup>tability and market share. In recent years, researchers have begun to attend to a new group of <sup>fi</sup>rms, Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms, and study their patterns of survival and exits from online marketplaces [31]. The turbulent online environment adds to the complexity of Internet <sup>fi</sup>rm survival.

This research focuses on the survival of a special group of Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms, stores operating on third party online marketplaces, such as eBay, Amazon and Taobao (the biggest e-Commerce platform in China, with a lion's share of 80% of the Chinese B2C and C2C markets). The number of stores has increased dramatically in recent years, accounting for a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the online marketplace sales. For example, in 2011, third party online sellers accounted for 30% of Amazon's total sales [58]. On Taobao, there were 8 million independent stores at the end of 2011 [35]. The reasons for the dramatic increase in the number of independent stores are two-fold. First, aiming at a higher market share, e-Commerce giants have a strong tendency to pursue the “long tail” retail strategy, wherein the platforms offer a large number of unique items in relatively small quantities (compared to the selling of few popular items in large quantities by traditional retail <sup>fi</sup>rms) by aggregating numerous independent sellers [3]. Second, the facilities and technical capabilities offered by these shopping platforms lower the entry barriers to Web-based store operation, and encourage the participation of small and medium sized (SME) merchants, many of which are Internet entrepreneurs that take a product niche on platforms.

The literature on entrepreneurship suggests that small <sup>fi</sup>rms may not choose to grow, but manage to survive [14, 15]. This is especially true for online stores working in niche markets, for which business success indicators not only include sales volume and growth rate, but also, more fundamentally, survival. Just as Brynjolfsson, Hu, and Smith [10] pointed out, “niches do not translate to riches for everyone”, pursuing niche markets while surviving online becomes very challenging, especially for small online stores [11, 37]. This is because the small online stores not only face <sup>fi</sup>erce competition from long-established and large sellers, and demanding customers that expect lower prices online [4,9], but also suffer from the insuf<sup>fi</sup>- cient <sup>fi</sup>nancial, human, and other resources, and lack of experience and formal management training [2].

Despite the practical pertinence of addressing the concern of online store survival, to our best knowledge, few prior studies have addressed it at the microeconomic level. Previous studies have either examined factors that lead to the survival of traditional organizations [39], or focused on the survival of Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms in general [31]. Among the few expectations that focus on small online store survival, Cabral and Hortacsu [11] attributed the exit of sellers to the negative feedbacks that they received. Lin et al. [37] studied the distribution of the reputation scores of sellers staying in or exiting the business. Although highlighting the importance of social capital, such as customer feedback and reputation score, on online store survival, none of these studies offered systematic and theory-grounded accounts of online store survival and its antecedents.

The objective of this paper is thus to <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap by proposing a theoretical framework of online store survival and validating the framework through an empirical investigation. This paper speci<sup>fi</sup>cally asks the question of “what explains the survival of online stores”. It adopts a social capital perspective to answer this question [1], and proposes that three dimensions of social structure wherein social capitals are embedded, social relations with peers and customers, hierarchical relations as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the obedience to online marketplace rules, and market relations as operationalized by online word-of-mouth (WOM), explains the survival of online stores. The social capital perspective is especially important online since the Internet is impersonal, and proper use of social tools can lubricate relations of various natures, thus help enhance the likelihood of online store survival. The visibility of online social structures also made the empirical tests feasible. A dataset covering 5772 sellers in two industries on a Chinese marketplace was collected and analyzed to validate the proposed model. This research makes theoretical contributions by integrating important social capital resources an online seller can mobilize in one framework, and therefore provides a more coherent account of online store survival.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant theories that ground the key hypotheses. Section 3 introduces the research contexts, research methods, and major <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The last section concludes the study by discussing the implications of this paper and prospects for further studies.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

This section reviews studies concerning the survival of organizations, as well as social capital theories, to develop a research model on online store survival.

## 2.1. Literature review

The question of why some <sup>fi</sup>rms die while others survive is one of the basic concerns of business scholars. Survival or longer-term viability has long been recognized as a basic goal for a business organization. It is not only a prerequisite for <sup>fi</sup>rm success in other terms such as market share and growth but also different from these success criteria. For example, the SME literature has noted that people start and operate their own <sup>fi</sup>rms for a variety of reasons other than maximizing economic returns, so that many small <sup>fi</sup>rms stay small over lifetimes, and owners' growth attitude signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects <sup>fi</sup>rm growth [59]. Firm survival has been addressed from multiple perspectives, such as industrial organization (see [39] for a review), <sup>fi</sup>rm capability [36], business model [31], dominant design [52], and organization ecology [13]. To date most research focuses on traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms, and few have studied Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms. Survival is also an important criterion for internet <sup>fi</sup>rm success, because <sup>fi</sup>rst, as widely known, it takes a long time for internet <sup>fi</sup>rms to breakeven [56], and the <sup>fi</sup>erce online competition makes <sup>fi</sup>rm exit and entry more frequently, so that initial survival can be an important milestone for success; second, online stores are SME niche players who may be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with operating in a niche rather than pursuing high speed growth by diversifying offerings.

Our research aims to explain the survival of online stores from a social capital perspective. The theory of social capital is originated from sociology, and has long been introduced into the management domain to account for the differential business performance of organizations [55]. Nahapiet and Ghoshal [41, p.243] de<sup>fi</sup>ned social capital as “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit.” The resources speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in their de<sup>fi</sup>nition can be physical resources such as materials, fund and information, or nonphysical resources such as trust, belief and norms. The central idea underlying the social capital theory is that valuable resources of the focal actor (called ego) are not necessarily possessed directly by the ego and may be embedded in the ego's social relations with others; when necessary, these resources are mobilized to meet desired outcomes.

Social relations that an individual/organization possesses are generally regarded as the source of social capital. In general, rich inter-personal connections foster mutual understanding, norms, and trust, thus facilitating cooperation and collective action among members. According to the strength of the interpersonal relationships [24], social relations are further classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as strong and weak ties that play different roles in affecting information <sup>fl</sup>ow: strong ties are more important in promoting information <sup>fl</sup>ow about activities within an organizational subsystem, whereas weak ties are more important in promoting information <sup>fl</sup>ow about activities outside an organizational subsystem. Adler and Kwon [1] extends the original social capital theory by including not only social relations, but also hierarchical and market relations, as key factors driving business success.

Relationships between social capital and the ego's performance have been empirically examined at the individual, team, and <sup>fi</sup>rm levels [7]. At the <sup>fi</sup>rm level, some researchers have examined the strength of social ties in relation to differences in <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. Granovetter [27] argued that although strong ties better serve the objectives of integrating and mobilizing resources, only weak ties can bring new information. Later studies on this area centered on contexts under which strong and weak ties can be optimally con<sup>fi</sup>gured to advance business performance. Bruderl and Preisendorfer [8] showed that both weak and strong ties can improve the sales performance of start-ups. In contrast, Rowley et al. [50] suggested that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of relational embeddedness (in particular, strong and weak ties) is contingent upon industry contexts.

More important in the case of our study, social networks can enhance <sup>fi</sup>rm survival probability. Raz and Gloor [49] found that Internet start-ups with larger informal communication networks have higher chances of surviving the dot-com bubble. When a social network is full of goodwill, members tend to be more cooperative and helpful, eventually improving the survival and success chances of start-ups [8, 28]. Some studies found that social networks of different natures have different impact on <sup>fi</sup>rm survival. In their study of Gujarati immigrant entrepreneurs in the lodging industry of the United States, Kalnins and Chung [30] found that the likelihood of survival of an immigrant entrepreneur's hotel increases when surrounded by branded hotels owned by individuals from their ethnic group, but the likelihood of survival is not affected by unbranded motels or by branded hotels owned by individuals from other ethnic groups. Uzzi [57] found that the probability of organization survival increases as the network with which the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm transacts tends toward an integrated network of embedded and arm's length ties; conversely, the probability of organizational survival decreases as the network with which the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm transacts tends toward either all arm's-length ties or all embedded ties.

In sum, social networks have been found to contribute to <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and survival. However, the relationship between online social networks and online store survival as an empirical question seems to be not addressed before.

## 2.2. Theoretical background

Because we base our research model on the social capital framework proposed by Adler and Kwon [1], their research is reviewed in this section. Social capital is broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the goodwill available to individuals or groups. The core of social capital is “appropriability”—the fact that ties of one kind can be used for other purposes [1, p.36]. The sources of social capital are from three dimensions of social structures: social, hierarchical, and market relations (see Table 1 for the differences among these three relations).

Social relations are characterized by favor and gift exchange. The favors exchanged through social relations are based on the tacit understanding that a favor one offers will be returned someday, which is yet to be determined, by somebody who is not necessarily bene<sup>fi</sup>ted directly. Social exchange is embedded in the informal relationships of an organization with its business partners, suppliers, research and development institutions, and customers, among others. Hierarchical relations represent connections through which obedience to authority is exchanged for “material and spiritual security” [1, p.19]. In line with this de<sup>fi</sup>nition, the relationships between a company and a government bureau, between a business unit and its parent company, or hierarchies within an organization, belong to hierarchical relations. Market relations refer to the exchange relationships between the focal organization and its customers. Market relations are remarkably important for commercial organizations. Unlike social relations wherein terms of exchange are often tacit and unspeci<sup>fi</sup>c, marketplaces feature exchange of goods and services for money, and therefore are explicit and speci<sup>fi</sup>c.

Although it naturally occurs to one that social capital is embedded in social relations, the other two sources of social capital, hierarchical relation and market relation, appear to be counter-intuitive and less well understood. According to Adler and Kwon [1], hierarchies, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as formal work process, business practices, and decision <sup>fl</sup>ows, actually help shape informal relationships, and therefore generate certain social capital. The role of government structure and legal rules in facilitating or impeding the emergence and maintenance of social capital and trust in civil society was also stressed by many authors [6, 33]. The relationship between market relation and social capital is more subtle. Although some authors suggest that market exchange corrode social capital, a more benign view of market exchange suggests that “commerce is a pacific system, operating to cordialise mankind, by rendering Nations as well as individuals, useful to each other [44, p.28]”. Based on Adler and Kwon's [1] social capital theory that we discussed above, this paper aims to explain three sources of social structure within the context of online store survival.

## 2.3. An integrated framework of online store survival

In our framework (see Fig. 1), social relations are online social networks of online sellers, operationalized as the number of ties in the online social network that an online seller possesses. Such relationships are enabled by various online communication media (e.g., instant messengers, forums, social networks, and blogs), and embodied in their connections with peers (other sellers) and consumers. Hierarchical relations are the relationships between sellers and online marketplaces because online marketplaces are not only facility providers, but also market process designers, rule makers and market regulators. Hierarchical relations generate social capital by providing structural assurance to sellers and consumers, as explained shortly in the model. Online market relations exist between online sellers and customers, who not only bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the trade but also leave online the expression of their gratefulness, online word of mouth (WOM), as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the online customer feedbacks about sellers' product and services. Therefore, online market relations are operationalized using online WOM. The following sections discuss each source of social capital and their relationship to online store survival in detail.

## 2.3.1. Social relations and social capital

Although operating in an impersonal space, sellers actively partici pate in online communication and build their online social networks. Online social media tools, such as SNS, online forums, and instant message software, are integrated into many online marketplaces. Sellers can exchange ideas in online forums, maintain social connections using instant messengers, and make friends via online social networks. By the nature of the online environment, sellers are more isolated than of<sup>fl</sup>ine sellers [30], thus the IT-supported social media become important for sellers to build and maintain social networks. Unlike face-to-face social networks, an IT-supported social network is less constrained by the number of social ties, which means that individual sellers can manage a large number of social ties with relatively less effort per tie [48]. The ties maintained by a seller, no matter strong or weak, facilitate the access to various resources that support online sellers to survive the <sup>fi</sup>erce online competition. These resources include:

(1) The knowledge of running an online store. Such knowledge includes basic skills of how to use the platform to set up a store, how to attract and convert online traf<sup>fi</sup>c, and how to manage the store operation. Such skills and knowledge are highly codi<sup>fi</sup>able, and can easily be communicated through online seller networks.

(2) The knowledge on competition dynamics, customer preference and supplies. The online environment is extremely dynamic, demanding constant monitoring of competitor actions, customer requirement changes and supply opportunities. In a highly dynamic environment, situational knowledge that relies on personal relationships, rather than systematic analysis and planning, is suggested as especially important [23].

(3) Just as entrepreneurial passion and enthusiasm are important in the pursuit of entrepreneurial effectiveness [12], emotion support can be important for online store survival. The online social network helps maintain store owners' entrepreneurial passion, enhances the sellers' sense of af<sup>fi</sup>liation and emotional attachment to e-Commerce and online marketplace, and encourages the longer survival of an online store. It also helps the owner to realize certain organizational goal beyond pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization such as enjoying product design, which discourages store closure in face of <sup>fi</sup>nancial dif<sup>fi</sup>culty.

(4) Because some friends/users are also buyers, the social network can help sellers to expand their market base [61]. For example, the authoritative status of a seller can help attract customers to his/her online store.

Based on the above discussion, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. The more socialization efforts online stores put in, the greater the possibility they will survive longer.

## 2.3.2. Hierarchical relations and structural assurance

Hierarchical relations between platform operators and sellers can take many forms. In this research, we are interested in platforms' structural assurance programs since they help online sellers to obtain a valuable social capital—trust. The concept of structural assurance was proposed by Gefen et al. [26] to classify three types of trust: knowledge-based, institution-based, and calculative-based trust. Institution-based trust is further conceptualized in two dimensions: situational normality and structural assurance [26]. Situational normality refers to the sense that the environment is in proper order and that success is likely because the situation is normal or favorable [34]. Structural assurance, on the other hand, refers to institutional structures [25], such as guarantees, regulations, legal recourse, or other policies, which are established to promote online exchange. Linking to our empirical study contexts (refer to Section 3), they are accreditation programs such as consumer rights safeguarding programs offered by third party shopping platforms.<sup>4</sup> The institutional structures are built and regulated by the platform, and sellers could determine their extent of obedience to such structures. However, once complied, such structures both constrain sellers' behavior, and enable trust between sellers and their customers [16].

Trust is the “subjective assessment of one party that another party will perform a particular transaction according to his/her con-<sup>fi</sup>dent expectations, in an environment characterized by uncertainty [4]”. In the social capital literature, trust is deemed to be the most important aspect of relational social capital [41]. Nevertheless, trust represents one independent stream of inquiry in the Information Management and e-Commerce research <sup>fi</sup>eld due to its importance in enabling transactions in the virtual environment [e.g., 4, 38, 53].

The adoption of structural assurance programs can enhance online store survival chance for the following reasons. Abiding by such programs sends buyers strong signals of seller attributes: higher product and service quality and their honesty and commitment to online transactions, which enhance the trustworthiness of the sellers as perceived by buyer groups. Furthermore, because such programs are endorsed by the platform, buyers are prone to transfer their trust toward intermediaries to sellers [16, 46, 53]. Therefore, enhanced seller reputation and consumer trust may make the adopting sellers stand better chances to survive online competitions. We propose:

Hypothesis 2. Compared to online stores that do not adhere to structural assurance programs issued by shopping platforms, stores adhering to the programs are more likely to survive longer.

## 2.3.3. Market relations and online word-of-mouth (WOM)

Online WOM, messages about a seller and its products/services communicated within customers' personal networks, is a product of market relations between sellers and buyers. Online WOM can be both positive and negative and properly leveraged WOM can help sellers to improve sales [63]. In the marketing literature, WOM marketing refers to purposefully designed messages about a particular product or service in a promotional campaign that operates through an individual's personal recommendations through his/her social networks. As the medium of WOM marketing changes from traditional face-to-face verbal communication to a variety of electronic media (e.g., e-mail, BBS, blog, and online forum), the scale of media networks has reached an unprecedented level.

E-tailing studies have examined the impact of consumer feedback systems and ratings (online WOM) on product sales opportunity, price premium, and sales growth [4]. Better reviews measured by a large number of reviews, positive ratings, and higher review quality can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly promote consumer purchase intention and product sales. Duan et al. [21] proposed that online WOM can engender two types of effects: awareness and persuasive effects. Using movie fans' postings and comments data collected from Yahoo!, they showed that box of<sup>fi</sup>ce sales are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the volume of online postings, suggesting the importance of the awareness effect. Using randomly selected data from Amazon.com and Barnesandnoble.com, Chevalier and Mayzlin [18] found that an improvement in review ratings for a book at one site leads to a relative increase in the sales of the book at that site. Park et al. [45] found that low-involvement consumers are affected by the quantity rather than quality of online product reviews, whereas high-involvement consumers are affected by review quantity mainly when the review quality is high.

Although the literature has focused extensively on the effects of online WOM on product sales, we propose that online WOM about sellers can also have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on online store survival. On the one hand, the sales premium generated by online WOM <sup>fi</sup>nancially supports sellers' longer survival; on the other hand, the appreciation given by grateful customers that are embedded in online WOM also encourages sellers to advance their businesses. In this regard, Cabral and Hortacsu [11] attributed the exit of sellers mainly to the negative feedbacks they received. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 3. The more positive the online WOM of online stores, the more likely they will survive longer.

## 3. Research design and results

## 3.1. Research site

We used a Chinese online marketplace as our research site. This decision was made for the following two reasons. First, e-Commerce in China is developing rapidly. The e-tailing sales were predicted to reach RMB 805.98 billion (USD 120 billion) in year 2012, an increase of 55% from the previous year [54]. In March 2010, the traf<sup>fi</sup>c on the biggest Chinese shopping platform, Taobao, has since surpassed that of eBay and Amazon, making Taobao the biggest e-Commerce site in the world in terms of online traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Second, shopping platforms in China have fully explored the applications of social network media in facilitating online sales. Due to the importance of the “Guanxi” culture in China, Chinese shopping platforms have integrated social network service, forums, and other social media into their trading platforms [43]. For example, Aliwangwang, a proprietary instant messenger tool of Taobao, is considered a key application that enables Taobao to defeat eBay in the Chinese market in 2003 [42]. Thus, Chinese shopping platform is considered proper for our research purpose: its prosperity allows us to draw a large sample, and its wide use of online social media supports the exploration of the role of social relations in online store survival.

## 3.2. Data

Testing our theoretical model is challenging because the life spans of sellers are not easy to accurately measure and empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings may vary by industry. With the assistance of a major shopping platform operator in China, we secured a dataset characterized by objective, longitudinal, and cross-industrial information.

We selected two representative industries in the online shopping platform (i.e., woman dress, and cell phone). They were among the best sellers in our research site.<sup>5</sup> A sample of 5772 sellers was randomly drawn from the whole population of the online trading platform in the beginning of December of 2009 (T1). Variables that are used to operationalize the key constructs of research interest (explained in Section 3.2) were collected. Six months later (T2), a second round of data collection was conducted. In addition to the variables collected in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round, we were especially interested in sellers who were still operating their businesses. The survival status of sellers was judged based on whether the online storefronts were still available to public in T2. According to the practices of this shopping platform, if sellers do not maintain their storefronts for more than two weeks and the number of products on shelves is fewer than a certain amount, their stores will be closed permanently (not available to public forever). Correspondingly, those sellers are deemed defunct.

The number of viable stores in the two time points is depicted in Fig. 2. At T1, our sample includes 4263 and 1509 stores in the woman dress and cell phone industries, respectively. A notable portion of the sellers in these industries exited the market within six months. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, 38.4% of the woman dress stores and 33.2% of the cell phone stores disappeared within six months. On average, 37.0% of sellers shut down their businesses, consistent with the view that the online market is characterized by a turbulent environment and <sup>fi</sup>erce competition.

As shown in Table 2, the average life span of woman dress stores in our sample is 720.75 days, whereas the life span of already closed business averages only 616.49 days. For cell phone stores, the average life period is 724.44 days (including those stores whose ages are right censored), while those failed stores averaged 607.58 days before closure. This further reinforces our early conjecture that online retailing business is not easy to sustain.

Seller survival according to different life spans is illustrated in Fig. 3. In general, for all stores and stores that have already closed their businesses, the life span histographs are skewed toward the right side, revealing that relatively fewer sellers survive for longer periods.

## 3.3. Measurement

## 3.3.1. Life span of online storefronts

As introduced earlier, we collect information about sellers during two time points that are six months apart. At T1, the date at which each storefront in our sampling frame was established was gathered. Six months later, we collected data about the accurate dates at which sellers permanently closed their stores. The differences between the dates of store closure and establishment thus indicate the life span of perished stores. Right censorship incurred for stores that were still under operation as of T2. For operationalization purpose, the life span for a store still alive was determined by taking the time differences between T2 (the second time point we collected data) and the date of its establishment.

## 3.3.2. Online social ties

On the shopping platform where we collected data, there were two major IT initiatives supporting online socialization activities: SNS personal space and discussion forum.

## 3.3.3. SNS friend number

The shopping platform we studied deems socialization supports as the key to enable sellers to communicate to peer sellers and buyers while staying in touch with existing friends. However, to avoid potential harassing behaviors of sellers, mutual acknowledgment of a relation is required to establish a SNS friendship. This is unlike the processes witnessed in many instant messenger tools for adding new friends. In addition, either party of a SNS friendship could terminate the relation without the consent of the other party. Such design signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces the risks of getting acquainted with sellers who try to abuse the SNS friendship. Hence, in alignment to our prior conceptualization, the number of friends in one's SNS personal space re<sup>fl</sup>ects one key facet of online socialization efforts. As long-lived sellers are prone to have more friends (since they have more times to socialize), we employed the number of friends divided by the age of an online store (in weeks) to operationalize the socialization efforts one seller makes on SNS personal space.

## 3.3.4. Socialization activities in discussion forum

In China, an online forum represents one essential part of online shopping platforms.<sup>6</sup> It is a place where sellers make friends, seek on-time market information, exchange ideas on addressing consumer complaints, and even look for emotional consolation. The major Chinese shopping platforms gather information on the overall number of postings, including the articles initiated by the seller and responses received.

As in any online forum, number of postings re<sup>fl</sup>ects community members' degree of activity. The larger the number of postings, the more active a seller is in online community. But, for sellers aiming at attracting attentions of peer sellers and potential buyers, the number of comments received (follow-up postings) may be equally important. By publishing in online forums, sellers try to send voices to the online community. The number of comments re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which others are interested in the topic of a posting. Therefore, in measuring sellers' socialization activities in online forum, both number of articles initiated and overall number of follow-up comments are taken into account. Similarly, as in the case of SNS personal space, seller age should be factored in to account for aging effects. As such, we use two proxies (number of articles published per week and number of comments/feedbacks received per week) to measure sellers' socialization efforts in online forum.

## 3.3.5. Structural assurance

Unlike shopping in physical stores, online shopping is characterized by uncertainties surrounding product quality and delivery processes. Not being able to examine the products physically, buyers may be concerned with the textual description of the products not being 100% re<sup>fl</sup>ective to the real products. Unsatisfactory shopping experiences might be triggered by the disagreement between seller and buyer on the features of the products (for reasons such as inaccurate/vague description, illegible pictures, incomplete information about product packaging and accessories, etc.). Furthermore, in China, buyers heavily rely on instant messengers to communicate transaction related information with sellers. Even if the products per se are described in suf<sup>fi</sup>cient details and faithfully, it is possible that sellers oversell their products (boasting) when chatting with buyers. Therefore, major online shopping platforms in China try to alleviate perceived risks for online transactions by enforcing Consumer Rights Safeguarding Programs.

## 3.3.6. Online WOM

After each transaction, online shopping platforms often provide buyers the chances to rate sellers on various service dimensions. On the platform where we collect data, three service dimensions are evaluated based on buyer feedbacks: faithful description of listing products, service attitudes throughout the transaction, and product dispatching speed. These dimensions are not unlike the Detailed Seller Ratings (DSR) on eBay and cover major aspects of online services. Buyer grading is gauged along a 5-level scale (1 to 5). One major issue with these DSR dimensions, however, is “common method bias”, which occurs because of the way the data are collected. In particular, seller services are evaluated by checking the radio boxes shown on the same webpage. Buyers may give very similar ratings for those dimensions, although they re<sup>fl</sup>ect different aspects of online services. In our dataset, we found that the DSR dimensions are highly correlated (with correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients ranged from 0.7 to 0.8).

Technically, this forbids us to use the three DSR dimensions individually as independent variables.

For the reasons above, we constructed two new scales to measure two important aspects of online services: overall service quality and the consistency of three DSR ratings. Overall service quality (mDSR) is measured by the average DSR rating across three dimensions. To measure consistency of DSR ratings, we used the variance of three DSR ratings (average squared deviations from the mean). In essence, the variance of DSR re<sup>fl</sup>ects the degree to which sellers could keep balance along multiple service dimensions. In particular,

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathrm{vDSR} & = \left(\left(\mathrm{DSR} _ {\text { descrip }} - \mathrm{mDSR}\right) ^ {2} + \left(\mathrm{DSR} _ {\text { dispatch }} - \mathrm{mDSR}\right) ^ {2} \right. \\ & \quad \left. + \left(\mathrm{DSR} _ {\text { attitude }} - \mathrm{mDSR}\right) ^ {2}\right) / 3. \end{array}
$$

In line to the H3 in Section 2.3, we expect higher overall DSR ratings to lead to better survival chances of sellers. As to DSR rating variance, we believe that consistent ratings (or smaller vDSR) are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to seller survival.

## 3.3.7. Control variables

Multiple variables that potentially affect store survival were controlled. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is ratio of positive ratings, the overall rating of the degree of satisfaction of past customers with a particular seller. Our experimental shopping platform adopts similar rating system to that on eBay [11]. In particular, after a transaction is completed, buyer can give the seller a grade of 1 (positive), 0 (neutral), or −1 (negative). Ratio of positive ratings is simply the sum of positive ratings divided by the total number of ratings for all buyers completing transactions with one seller. Conceptually, ratio of positive ratings is similar to average DSR ratings. However, we did not include it as a key measure of online WOM for two reasons. First, while DSR covers three major aspects of online services, the valence of feedback (positive, neutral, or negative) accommodates many other service dimensions not speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in DSR. For example, product price, product quality, ease of purchase, product delivery accuracy, shipping cost, and shipping options are also important service dimensions that buyers care about [47]. As a result, good services in terms of DSR do not always suggest good ratings. The difference between the two variables was also observed in our dataset; we found that the correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients between mDSR and ratio of positive ratings are 0.33 and 0.26 for women dress and cell phone stores, respectively (see Appendix A). Second, ratio of positive ratings is often characterized by high average value (more that 99%) and small variance (as small as 0.02). Thus, despite conceptually appealing, the lack of variance makes ratio of positive ratings inadequate as independent variables. We will include it as a control variable instead.

The literature on <sup>fi</sup>rm competitiveness and survival often includes <sup>fi</sup>rm size as a control variable [40, 60], since larger <sup>fi</sup>rms often have more resources to advance their business. To address <sup>fi</sup>rm size effects, we adopted “the number of products” as a proxy of scale of business. Besides focusing on product categories, some sellers also keep stock of digital products as a strategy of lifting overall credibility,<sup>8</sup> because digital products (e.g., phone cards and electronic tickets) have such virtues as low monetary values and cancelation of logistic services [5]. However, this diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy could undermine the core competency of sellers especially when their internal capacity is limited and focal product category is marginally related to the digital products they hosted. In this study, we control for the ratio of digital products that is measured by the percentages of digital products sold historically by a store. Finally, younger <sup>fi</sup>rms often bear signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher failure rate for reason of “liability of newness” [15, 28]. We thus control for the effects of store age in the analyses.

Of the models employed in survival analysis, Cox's proportional hazard regression model (Cox model) is one of the most frequently used and is adopted in this paper. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, suppose that T is the life span of sellers and $X _ { 1 } , \ldots X _ { k }$ is a group of explanatory variables. The Cox model takes the following form:

$$
h (t) = h _ {0} (t) e ^ {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} X _ {i} \beta_ {i}}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { h ( t ) = \operatorname* { l i m } _ { \Delta t  0 } \frac { P ( t \leq T \leq t + \Delta t | T \geq t ) } { \Delta t } } \end{array}$ is a hazard function; $P \left( t { \le } T { \le } t + \Delta t | \right.$ $T { \geq } t )$ denotes the event rate at time t conditional on survival until time t or later $( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , T { \geq } t ) ; h _ { 0 } \left( t \right)$ is the baseline hazard function; and $\beta _ { 1 } . . . \beta _ { k }$ represents a group of parameters to be estimated [32].

The central task of survival analysis is to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which independent variables $( X _ { 1 } , \ldots X _ { k } )$ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the lifetime of sellers. This is done by checking whether or not the corresponding parameter (β ) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from zero (or whether $e ^ { \beta _ { i } }$ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from 1.0). We use SPSS (Version 15.0) as the statistical tool in testing the proposed model.

## 3.5. Findings

The descriptive statistics of variables for each industry at T1 are shown in Table 3. Apart from the independent variables, we also provide statistical information for the three control variables. The correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of independent variables for both industries are presented in Appendix A1.

The analytical results of the survival analysis are presented in Table 4. In particular, for online social network variables, the SNS friendship did not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly help or undermine store survival. In other words, for both woman dress and cell phone industries, there are no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the probabilities of store failure, regardless of the number of SNS friends sellers maintain in their personal spaces. Similarly, for social networking indicators in online forum, both the intensity of posting and responding have no in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the survival chances of online stores, irrespective of the industries concerned. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that socialization in online forums is not helpful to online business. In sum, we didn't <sup>fi</sup>nd support for Hypothesis 1.

Second, enrolling into structural assurance plan was found to enhance survival chances of online stores. Particularly, for the woman dress industry, keeping all other variables constant, the probability of failure for stores that abide by the structural assurance plan is smaller than the chances of failure for stores that did not do so (i.e., negative Beta). For the cell phone industry, the results are similar, indicating that adhering to structural assurance plan does improve survival chances. Hence, we found support for Hypothesis 2.

Third, online WOM valence was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to store survival in both industries, but in reverse direction. Particularly, an increase in the average DSR leads to higher probability of store failure (i.e., positive Beta). This <sup>fi</sup>nding is different from that in the literature [18,62] and we will discuss possible reasons for this in the discussion section. In contrast, the more consistent the DSR evaluations along the three dimensions (as indicated by smaller vDSR), the better chances of store survival. Overall, empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide partial support for Hypothesis 3.

As for the control variables, higher ratio of positive ratings was found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly related to the lifetime of women dress stores. But such effects didn't emerge for cell phone stores. The number of products was found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly and positively related to the survival probability of online stores for both industries (negative Beta). In line to prior projections, ratio of digital products was negatively related to survival of cell phone stores but this effect did not emerge for the women dress industry (i.e., insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant effect). It seems that the image of cell phone stores is impaired if digital products contribute to a large portion of past transactions. As expected, the effects of liability of newness exist in online marketplace, meaning that younger stores are more likely to fail. Finally, analysis results utilizing pooled data (indicated in the right-most column) are generally consistent to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning either industry.

## 3.6. Additional analysis

To check the robustness of empirical results above, we conducted additional analyses as below. First, to measure online social ties, rather than using number of SNS friends established weekly, we regressed the number of friends on the seller age and calculated standard residuals (also called Pearson residuals). This new measure replaced the original measure (number of friends established per week) in the Cox regression and similar results were obtained. We applied similar techniques to other social tie measures, including number of articles published and the number of comments received in online forum per week, and replicate the analyses as above. The empirical results were insensitive to these different operationalization approaches.

Second, postings initiated and comments received were collapsed into one measure by calculating a ratio of comments received to articles published (directly measure the capability of a seller in drawing audience by postings). The new measure entered into the Cox regression function by replacing the two existing measures concerning article and comments. For both industries, we obtained insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant Beta, implying that feedback ratio has no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on store survival. In the meanwhile, <sup>fi</sup>ndings concerning other independent variables still hold, suggesting that our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust to different measurement speci<sup>fi</sup>cations of socialization activities in online forum.

Third, we plotted the average DSR ratings against store age at T1 and observe the patterns. Interestingly, when online stores were <sup>fi</sup>rst established, the DSR ratings are generally high, probably because they invest heavily in customer services when their stores are not well-recognized. As stores age, however, average DSR ratings show larger variances within seller group. When we regress average DSR ratings on store age, the standardized regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is − 0.372 (pb0.001), suggesting that seller population could not sustain high DSR ratings as before. The smaller average value and larger variance of DSR rating as time going implies that aged stores are more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed, further validating the empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding of positive Beta concerning mean score of DSR ratings (mDSR). We discuss the possible reasons for such <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the next section.

## 4. Discussion and conclusion

## 4.1. Discussion of findings

The current study highlighted a few aspects that are essential for the survival of online stores but somewhat different in nature from that of traditional businesses. First, although social network and social capital have long been noted to be critical to the success of traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms [55], our empirical study provides evidence that online socialization efforts not necessarily pay off and enhance the chances of online seller survival. For example, SNS friendship shows no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship with store survival. Why do intimate relationship with potential buyers/sellers in SNS personal space not readily transform to business? First, consumers might be alert to their paradoxical relationships with sellers that are characterized by false intimacy and commercial exchanges [20, p.90]. In a similar vein, Clemons [19] highlighted the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of adding an e-Commerce module in Facebook. Second, SNS integrated into a shopping platform is still new, and it takes time for sellers to enrich their account and relationships, and transform relationships into real business. Third, it could also simply because sellers lack experiences on how to market their products on SNS networks. More generally, this <sup>fi</sup>nding alerts e-Commerce giants trying to integrate SNS features into their online shopping platform (such as Taobao.com in China) and SNS giants endeavoring to introduce online commerce features (such as Facebook). We hope that this study could entice more research efforts along this line of inquiry.

Furthermore, online forum was recognized to be quite useful socialization functionality in the online marketplace where we collect data. Our empirical results, however, reveal that socialization on online forum is not helpful for online stores. Additional analysis using ratio of comments received to article published con<sup>fi</sup>rms this <sup>fi</sup>nding. Putting together, those <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that successful leveraging of online discussion forum demands careful calibration of the participation strategy. The laborious article posting might not be worthy of the efforts.

Second, our study conceptualizes structural assurance to represent an instance of the crucial hierarchical relations. Empirical results support the prediction that adhering to structural assurance plans improves the chances of online store survival. Complementing to the literature that institutional structures enhance consumer trust toward sellers, and consumer purchase intention [25, 26], this research vali dates that it also augment seller survival chances.

Third, our study shows that market relation, as manifested by online WOM, is helpful for online store survival. Many previous studies have examined the link between online ratings and sales performance [18, 21]. In terms of empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, while the majority of them found positive relations between online ratings and sales [17, 18], some did not identify any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relations [e.g., 21] and others took a contingency point of view [63]. In this research, average DSR ratings were found to negatively relate to survival chances of online stores. Additional analysis on average DSR ratings and store age con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the negative correlations. On the other hand, consistent DSR ratings (i.e., small DSR variance) were found to positively relate to store lifetimes. Overall, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are more complex than we hypothesized. Below we discuss possible reasons.

The inability of high average DSR ratings in advancing store survival could be partially due to the Web interface design of the shop ping platform from which we collected data. First, although three dimensions of DSR ratings of each store are visible to buyer groups, there is no anchoring point for buyers to evaluate the relative good ness of one store's ratings. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the average DSR scores of on line stores in a particular industry (for example, women dress and cell phone) are not available to public. Also, given the number of on line stores (often in the scale of ten thousands), it is hard for buyers to compare DSR ratings across online stores. As a result, the signaling effects of good DSR ratings might not able to manifest themselves. On the other hand, the variance among DSR rating dimensions is easily observable by buyers. The lack of capability in any dimension of DSR ratings might concern buyers and forbid them from patronizing the corresponding store. In light of this <sup>fi</sup>nding, it is advisable for on line stores to keep a balance among the three key dimensions of on line services, irrespective of how good their overall DSR ratings are. Second, aged sellers are often more active in organizing marketing campaigns, such as group buying, time-limited discount, bidding in seconds, and cumulative quantity discount with price cuts. It is also noted that those campaigns are often facilitated and audited by shopping platforms, meaning that young sellers are often not quali <sup>fi</sup>ed to participate. While effective in attracting buyers and boost sales, those marketing efforts often demand more service staffs to offer online communication services, package commodities, and manage deliveries. As a side effect, DSR ratings are undermined dur ing the period of marketing activities. In this regard, online sellers refer to such marketing campaigns as “DSR killers." Hence, although all stores want high DSR ratings, limited by resources, aged sellers cannot serve customers as well as young sellers, especially during the period of marketing campaigns. Third, from sellers' perspective, DSR ratings are not so important relative to another rating scale of ratio of positive ratings.<sup>9</sup> As is evident in Table 3, the average ratio of positive ratings is more than 0.99 and standard deviation is as small as 0.02 for both product categories.<sup>10</sup> The mean DSR rating, if standardized to a scale in percentage, is about 96.4% with larger vari ances (see Table 3). Such statistical results reveal the operation strategy of online stores that emphasizes ratio of positive ratings but underplays DSR ratings. In sum, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, notwithstanding how important the online word-of-mouth is to online store survival, its effect in real business contexts hinges on how important it is in comparison to other quality signals. Also, online WOM signals must be conceivable to buyers to manifest their effect on store survival. In this regard, the WOM design features on our experimental platform are not conductive to authentic customer voices.

## 4.2. Research implications

This research makes the following theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the social capital theory by enriching the substance of hierarchical and market relations [1]. While Adler and Kwon [1] provided clear de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of two kinds of relations and their relationships with social capital, no further examples were provided to elaborate the two constructs. This research operationalizes both hierarchical and market relations using structural assurance and online WOM, and tested the theoretical framework in online commerce contexts. Second, this research contributes to the <sup>fi</sup>rm survival literature by studying a new <sup>fi</sup>rm category, online stores, and suggests important antecedents of their survival. Third, by adopting the framework of [1], this research could integrate important sources of social capital together, and provide a systematic explanations of online store survival. To our best knowledge, the three elements of social capital, online social network, structural assurance and online WOM, have not been studied in relation to <sup>fi</sup>rm survival previously.

## 4.3. Practical implications

The empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study have important managerial implications for both online marketplace operators and sellers. First, the results on the impacts of online social ties on store survival suggest that the use of socialization tools is not effective in enhancing chances of store survival, whether it is SNS personal space or online forums. Therefore, both marketplace operators and store owners should recognize the importance of social media on the one hand, and be cautious in their usage of online social networking applications on the other hand, since not all socialization efforts are paid off in terms of prolonging <sup>fi</sup>rm survival. For e-Commerce platforms, the effectiveness of SNS in promoting online store business should also be questioned since it is awkward to sell products to “friends”. This alert also applies to SNS platforms such as Facebook and Myspace when considering setting foot in e-Commerce.

Second, our study suggests that practitioners should subscribe to structural assurance plans as long as their costs could be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. For instance, sellers are highly recommended to subscribe to the “Faithfull Description” option because it signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhances their survival. For online marketplace operators, given that the effect sizes of CRSP might differ across industries, operators should consider designing customized programs for different industries.

Finally, in the short run, sellers should make efforts to gain good WOM, to the degree that the WOM signals are discernible by buyer group. Taking DSR ratings as an example, having good WOM not only means high average ratings but also requires a balance among DSR dimensions. While the former might be well known to sellers, the latter is seldom documented in the extant literature. Our analysis results show that having good average DSR ratings does not help online store survival at all. In the long run, however, that does not mean sellers could be safe in maintaining low DSR ratings. As introduced earlier, online marketplace is characterized by dynamic competition. For online marketplace operators, resolving information asymmetry and identifying low quality sellers are in line with the interests of buyers and the marketplace as a whole. Hence, sellers must pay suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient attention to DSR ratings, in addition to existing quality signals such as seller credibility, ratio of positive ratings, and enrolment status of CRSP, among others. Overall, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study alert the sellers that having good WOM is indeed important. They also highlight essential capabilities for stores to survive online, for example, being sensitive to market environments and swift adaptation to changes.

## 4.4. Limitations and future studies

Although the analyses generally support the proposed theoretical model, they should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. First, the vanishing/shut-down of storefronts may not necessarily indicate the exit of a businessman. For example, because registering new online storefronts incurs marginal costs, some sellers register multiple accounts in an online marketplace. However, following the policy of the online marketplace where we collected data, a unique permanent resident ID number must be provided to register new ones. More importantly, to the extent that the transaction records of one online store cannot be transferred to alternative store identities, operating multiple stores means more physical and intellectual investments [37]. Overall, albeit not a major threat to the key results, this concern demands more future research efforts for the sake of rigor.

Second, we draw conclusions on factors contributing to store survival based on two rounds data collection that are six months apart, which could be not long enough. Actually, the selection of a six-month time frame was based on the balance between: (1) a longer time period that leads to smaller percentages of right censored samples (i.e., we expect that most of the stores closed when the study <sup>fi</sup>nishes) and more accurate statistical estimation, and (2) a shorter time period that prevents the independent variables from “outdated” (i.e., the validity of the measures is transient in a fast-changing marketplace). Also, we wanted a failure rate that is comparable to that in the literature. In [49], for example, the failure rate of Israeli software start-ups is 42% in a 5 year observation period. In another study, 20.5% of Gujarati hotels operating in Texas, U.S., exited the market 10 years later [30]. Similarly, Dunne et al. [22] observed a failure rate of U.S. manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms ranging from 30.8% to 39% in 5 years. Our study, although characterized by a short observation period, ends with failure rates of 38.4% and 33.2% for dress and phone sellers, respectively. Combining with another piece of information, the enormous growth of seller population in recent years on the shopping platform where we collected data, it appears that the online market is more turbulent than of<sup>fl</sup>ine market.

Third, the current study did not take into account other factors affecting seller survival. For example, competition is relevant to business survival. Con<sup>fi</sup>ned by our dataset, this study only focused on key factors in Adler and Kwon's framework [1]. Nevertheless, future studies should control for competition factors in validating the theory.

Fourth, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on data from a single online marketplace. Future studies are encouraged to collect data from other online marketplaces in different economic, social, and religious systems to enhance the generalization of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Despite the limitations discussed above, this paper represents one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical studies investigating seller survival and contributes to the literature by shedding light on how to excel in purely virtual business environments. We call for more future studies that aim at theoretically extending our knowledge to long tail, online entrepreneurship, online marketing, and online social structures and providing practical guidance to the online stores and online marketplace operators.
