## Abstract

This paper focuses on interdependencies between mobile Internet (MI) usage and mobile voice (MV) calling patterns among residential customers in one member country of the Arabian Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC). The analyzed data set contains usage data of 10,366 postpaid subscribers of a mobile network operator (MNO) in a GCC country over 25 months from July 2013 to July 2015. The results suggest a complementary MV–MI usage relationship. This relationship is more positive for users who (1) send fewer SMS, (2) are younger, (3) are foreign nationals, (4) use a smartphone, and (5) have a longer tariff tenure. The strength of MV–MI usage association in the GCC country sample is larger than relationships observed in earlier work, which analyzed MNO subscribers in Europe. This suggests that the development trajectory of a country’s telecommunications service market moderates the relationship between MI and MV calling use intensity.

## Keywords

GCC countries; mobile data; mobile Internet use; multilevel analysis; short message service (SMS); voice telephony

## 1. Introduction

Triggered by the advent of sophisticated “multipurpose” mobile devices along with the increasing data transmission capabilities and ubiquity of mobile communication networks, major developments, which affect the telecommunication industry worldwide, are an increased use of mobile networks to access the Internet (= mobile Internet (MI)), a stagnating use of mobile voice (MV) calls, and a decreasing number of text messages (= short message service (SMS)) sent (GSMA, 2016). While the aforementioned trends can be generally observed worldwide, there are distinct local preconditions, which make a more differentiated analysis a the regional or country level valuable. For developing countries<sup>1</sup>, the surge in MI usage is even steeper than that in the rest of the world with per person penetration rates of 4.5% in 2010 and of 39.1% in 2015. At the same time, fixed broadband subscriptions in developing countries only grew from 4.2% per capita (23.5%, developed; 7.6%, worldwide) in 2010 to a projected 7.1% (29.0%, developed; 10.8%, worldwide) in 2015 (ITU, 2015a). The infrastructure in densely populated and highly industrialized Asian countries, such as South Korea or Japan, as well as North America and countries within the European Union, allows users to complete many Internet-related tasks on both mobile and fixed-line networks (Hoernig, Bourreau, & Cambini, 2015; Nakamura, 2015; Vogelsang, 2010). In contrast, many developing countries, which achieved their current advanced economic states in fast motion, do not feature a well-established fixed-line infrastructure because of geo-deterministic factors (i.e., environmental, historical, and/or population density-related circumstances). In this study, the establishment of mobile broadband networks poses a more feasible alternative to investments in comprehensive fixed-line networks (Abbas & Hamdy, 2015; ITU, 2015a; Garbacz & Thompson, 2007). One region with such a predominance of mobile broadband infrastructures is the group of the six states, which form the Cooperation Council for the Arab States of the Gulf (known as the Gulf Cooperation Council = GCC). Members of the GCC are Bahrain,

Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates (UAE). To date, it is not clear how the dominance of mobile networks coupled with underdeveloped fixed-line infrastructure affects demand interdependencies between various categories of mobile communication services.

In general, many studies predict a strong substitution of MV and SMS use through Internet protocol (IP)-based “over-the-top” (OTT)-services (e.g., Cisco, 2016; GSMA, 2016; Kumar, Kamal, Varyani, & Barward, 2015; Rio & Malik, 2013). Hence, along with the uplift in mobile data traffic (Ericsson, 2015, 2016; GSMA, 2015a, 2015b), it can be expected that this substitution effect might be particularly pronounced in GCC countries. In fact, Arthur D. Little (2016) highlighted a substantial decrease in SMS sent between 2012 and 2014 in GCC countries and neighboring states, while at the same time, data volumes and OTT use within the region significantly increased. However, the aforementioned market research firms base their predictions on country-level data, which reflect the average use levels of all customers in a market. But a small group of heavy users, who utilize the services significantly more than the vast majority, may drive up the average consumption and, thus, skew the overall mean usage and disguise changes in the use amounts of MV, SMS, and mobile OTT-services at the in tra-individual level (Gerpott, 2015; Hox, 2010).

Hence, a number of studies have been conducted that explore demand interrelationships between established mobile communications services (especially MV and SMS) on the one hand and MI-access on the other not at the country level but at individual consumer level (Bouwman & Reuver, 2014; Cassidy, Colmenares, Jones, Manolovitz, Shen, & Viera, 2014; Cecere & Corrocher, 2012; Church & de Oliveira, 2013; Gerpott 2010a, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Gerpott, Thomas, & Weichert, 2014; Hsiao & Chen, 2015; Karikoski & Luukkainen, 2011; Karikoski & Soikelli, 2013; Lauricella & Kay, 2013; Lu, Watanabe, Liu, Uji, Shono, & Kitamura, 2011; Mäkinen, Luukkainen, & Karikoski, 2014; Skierkowski & Wood, 2012; Verkasalo, 2007). Unfortunately, the validity of prior work is hampered by several problems. First, numerous studies build on self-reported usage frequencies of various categories of mobile communications services (e.g., Cecere & Corrocher, 2011, 2012; Mäkinen et al., 2014). However, there is ample evidence indicating that such subjective estimates share little variance with objectively measured past usage (Abeele, Antheunis, & Schouten, 2014; Boase & Ling, 2013; Gerpott, 2011; Gold, Rauscher, & Zhu, 2015; Reuver & Bouwman, 2015). Second, with few exceptions (Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016), former studies do not account for intra-individual use changes over time and only look at cross-sectional usage data. Third, it is unlikely that work on telecommunications customer behaviors in countries with highly developed fixed-line networks (e.g., Hoernig et al., 2015; Nakamura, 2015) can be applied to GCC countries without limitations, because certain issues for mobile network operator (MNO) in highly developed countries, such as fixed-mobile substitution, are less relevant due to different historical preconditions.

In light of this state of research, the present paper sets out to remedy the highlighted short comings of many prior studies. This is performed in a two-level empirical investigation, which explores the impacts of the rise of the overall MI usage on MV calling over time and applies the statistical analysis techniques suggested by Gerpott (2015). However, in contrast to earlier studies, our geographic focus is on 10,366 residential postpaid MNO subscribers residing in a GCC country for which objective monthly usage data covering 25 months were obtained.

The remainder of this article is divided into five sections. Section 2 provides an overview of the mobile telecommunication markets in GCC countries. Section 3 introduces the conceptual background and develops respective research questions (RQs). Section 4 explains the empirical methods used to generate the results presented in Section 5. Section 6 discusses the empirical findings.

## 2. State of the mobile telecommunications markets in GCC countries

Table 1 provides an overview of some demographic and a few of the mobile and fixed-line telecommunications industry metrics in the six GCC countries. The striking demographic features of the region are a rather low median age ranging between 25.1 (Oman) and 32.8 years (Qatar) across all six countries compared to that in developed countries (e.g., 42.5 in the European Union); a high share of expatriates, ranging from 27% in Saudi Arabia to 90% in the UAE; and an above average share of males in the overall population (51% world average, 48% in the European Union) between 54% in Saudi Arabia and 77% in Qatar.

The markets for mobile telecommunications services in various GCC countries exhibit similar features. The services are provided by two (Oman, Qatar, and UAE) or three (Bahrain, Kuwait, and Saudi Arabia) MNO in each country. In Bahrain, the MNO with the most subscribers is Batelco, with a market share of 42%, followed by Zain (31%) and Viva (27%). In Kuwait, the operators’ market shares are close to parity with Zain serving 37%, Ooredoo attending 32%, and Viva handling 31% of the mobile customers. In Oman, 57% of the subscribers are customers of Omantel, whereas the remainder (43%) are served by Ooredoo. In Qatar, the subscriber market share of the largest MNO (Ooredoo) amounts to 69% and that of the second competitor (Vodafone) is 31%. In the largest GCC country Saudi Arabia, subscribers are serviced by STC (43%), Mobily (37%), and Zain (20%). In the UAE, Etisalat holds a market share of 57% of the mobile customers, 43% are subscribed to du. For all GCC countries, the smartphone adoption rate, which is the share of smartphone connections of the overall number of active SIM cards within a mobile network, exceeds the global average of 41%, ranging from 52% in Bahrain to 83% in the UAE and Qatar. All the aforementioned mobile communications networks offer their users access to 4G transmission technology (GSMA, 2015a, 2015b, 2016).

The SIM-card penetration rates vary between 1.75 (Oman) and 2.41 (Kuwait) per subscriber compared to the worldwide average of 1.79. Thus, the SIM-card penetration rate in the GCC countries is at an average or above average level. Nevertheless, users in GCC countries generate a markedly higher monthly average revenue per user (ARPU) ranging from 36.81 Euro

(Oman) to 78.38 Euro (Qatar) compared to a worldwide mean value of 18.16 Euro. In sum, the region stands out with a particularly young population, high ARPU, and smartphone adoption rates, as well as a large share of expatriate subscribers (GSMA, 2015a, 2015b, 2016). The share of the population connected to the countries’ fixed-line telecommunications network ranges from 9.6 fixed-telephone subscriptions per 100 inhabitants in Kuwait to 22.3 in the UAE. Thus, fixed-line telephony penetration in the GCC region is markedly low as compared to that in developed countries such as Germany (56.9) and the USA (40.1). Consequently, the number of fixed-broadband subscriptions per 100 inhabitants in the region is not high as well, with a minimum of 1.4 in Kuwait and a maximum of 12.4 in Bahrain. To interpret these figures, it is important to note that in GCC countries, average household sizes are relatively high and rather similar ranging from 5.02 in the UAE to 6.95 in Oman. However, the large number of persons within a household does not necessarily imply that fixed-line telephony and broadband usage is higher than suggested by the low penetration rates per 100 inhabitants. Theoretically, all members of a large household can use the respective fixed-line access; however, the actual individual amount of fixed-line telecommunications service consumption is low because of the capacity restrictions of a single access point. In any case, the aforementioned subscriptions also include access lines of business customers. Thus, Table 1 may provide an even somewhat exaggerated impression of the actual usage of fixed-line telephony and broadband among private users. In sum, in the GCC countries, there is a great disparity between well-endowed, heavily used mobile telecommunications services on the one hand and sparsely adopted fixed-line telecommunications networks on the other hand (ITU, 2015b).

Overall, governments in the GCC countries strive to keep the media state-controlled. Following the Arab Spring in 2011, only Qatar has proposed to loosen media legislation, while the other GCC states have either taken legislative action or announced to tighten control of the press and online platforms (Duffy, 2013). For the telecommunications sector and its customers, this mainly interferes with the accessibility of Internet-based contents to the extent that several websites, online platforms, and IP-based communication services are unavailable across different GCC countries. Furthermore, GCC countries are Muslim societies. Hence, the legislative systems and sociocultural values are shaped by Islamic principles. This manifests itself in the disparity in economic participation of men and women, as reported by the World Economic Forum (2015)<sup>2</sup>. Therefore, mobile postpaid contracts are usually signed by men, even if the respective SIM card is then used by women. This potentially leads to a distorted distribution of the male–female ratio in the MNO’s billing system, rendering the respective information useless for data analysis.

Although there are numerous practitioner publications dealing with telecommunications usage metrics in GCC countries (e.g., Cisco, 2016; GSMA, 2016), there is a dearth of scientific literature in this field with few exceptions. For example, Abbas & Hamdy (2015) examined a sample of 512 Kuwaiti MNO customers, who filled in a questionnaire on their satisfaction with these providers’ service quality. Ameen, Willis, & Thomas (2015) proposed a conceptual framework of the determinants of mobile phone usage in GCC countries based on the technology acceptance model. On the basis of the same theoretical framework, Isaid & Faisal (2015) analyzed an email-based survey sample of 406 Qatari residents to explain repurchase intentions of mobile phone users. Finally, Ziani, Elareshi, & Gunter (2015) examined MI use among 1221 students in all six GCC countries in a questionnaire-based convenience sample. However, none of the aforementioned studies analyze objective user data.

## 3. Conceptual background and research questions

There are three explanatory approaches in the scientific literature that offer a rationale for demand interdependencies in the use patterns of customers of mobile communications services. A first research stream builds on the allocation theory that focuses on implications of resource constraints on the demand side. The second strand is the uses and gratification framework, while the third line of argumentation builds on the activation theory. In brief, allocation theory points to a substitutional, uses and gratification theory to a null, and activation theory points to a complementary association between MV and SMS use on the one hand and MI use on the other. For each of the introduced conceptual lenses, there are a few prior empirical studies, which support them in non-GCC countries.

The first theoretical concept is based on the assumption that mobile customers consider temporal and financial resources available to them when making service usage decisions (Becker, 1965). If novel mobile communications offerings exhibit characteristics with substitutional relations to established services, then allocation theory suggests that customers optimize their consumption by assigning their disposable resources (Ghose & Han, 2011). Under the assumption that customers tend to rebalance their mobile service usage subject to the available money- and time-related resources, customers of MNO are incentivized to substitute MV and traditional SMS usage in case that is billed on a pay-per-use or bucket basis, with virtually unlimited MI usage. Hence, if this approach is valid, mobile customers should prefer MI-based text messaging and mobile Voice-over-IP services over traditional MV and/or SMS (Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Gerpott et al., 2014; Rio & Malik, 2013). Put differently, a considerable rise in MI usage should coincide with an observable substitution of traditional MV and SMS.

Cecere & Corrocher (2012) provided evidence that is in line with a substitutional relationship suggested by the resource allocation lens. In a sample of 10,438 Italian mobile service users, the authors found a negative impact of Voice-over-IP on traditional MV and SMS usage. However, the analysis is based on self-reported MI usage only and does not specifically distinguish between MV and SMS services or between fixed and mobile Voice-over-IP. Karikoski & Luukkainen (2011) also detected a significantly negative association between use of IPbased services and both traditional voice calls and texting by using objectively measured data generated with mobile handsets of 183 MI users in Finland between 2008 and 2010. Unfortunately, as the study was conducted in the early stages of MI adoption, it is uncertain to which extent its results can be considered representative for user groups other than early adopters. In addition, the study was limited to smartphones, which ran the eventually discontinued Symbian operating system, so that the findings are hardly suitable to draw generalizable conclusions for current market situations. Hence, empirical work that backs the resource allocation view on interdependencies at the individual level is scarce and methodologically less convincing. The second branch of literature draws on the uses and gratification framework (Palmgreen, 1984). The uses and gratification concept presumes that subscribers of mobile services seek different “gratifications” by fulfilling various personal needs. Individuals choose the respective service they use depending on a comparison of the extent to which their needs can be met by various mobile service categories (Jung, Kim, & Chan-Olmsted, 2014; Reid and Reid, 2007; Shin, 2009). On the one hand, it could be argued that MI and MV are not interchangeable services because MI offers a vast range of IP-based applications, which satisfy needs that are completely different from those triggering MV use. On the other hand, MV can be substituted by various Voice-over-IP services offered through MI (e.g., Google Hangouts). However, although both MI and traditional MV are capable of covering the need of one-on-one individual communications, MV is linked to a much narrower set of gratifications than MI. In other words, the uses and gratification theory suggests that there is only a minor overlap in the obtainable gratification of MI and MV services. Hence, according to the uses and gratification view, a significant association between MI and MV usage is unlikely.

The second theoretical lens that advocates a negligible relationship between the service types is in line with the results of a limited set of empirical investigations. Cassidy et al. (2014), Gerpott (2010b), and Verkasalo (2007) reported zero correlations between MV and MI usage. Cassidy et al. (2014) build their analysis on a student sample of 941 individuals at a US American university, who filled out an online survey about their preferences for information technology use in 2013. The results are compared to a similar survey conducted in 2010 to derive implications on usage changes between the two surveys. However, the findings are flawed because Cassidy et al. (2014) changed items in the survey and did not make sure to interview the same individuals at the two measuring occasions. Gerpott (2010b) used a sample of objective usage data of 443 residential mobile service customers provided by one MNO in Germany. He observed no significant correlation of MV and MI usage in a single period. The analysis of handheld-based data from 548 Finnish mobile subscribers in 2005/2006 of Verkasalo (2007) also does not reveal any significant correlations between the usage of different service categories. Again, the number of studies in the context of MV, SMS, and MI that supports the uses and gratification perspective is very limited. Furthermore, many of these investigations are cross-sectional and plagued by common method bias and therefore not fully convincing in terms of their research design.

The third view on the interdependencies of the usage of mobile communications service variants refers to the activation theory (Wei, 2008; also see “positive spillover,” Xu, Forman, Kim, & Ittersum, 2014, and “reinforcement effect,” Ongena, Bouwman, & Gillebaard, 2012). The theory assumes that the use of one mobile communications service category will have a positive effect on the use intensity of other categories, or in other words, heavy users of MV are likely to exhibit an above average usage of MI as well. Individuals who already are more inclined to use their mobile devices for MV calls and, thereby, communicate with others may also be more prone to resort to MI-based information exchanges (Ghose & Han, 2011; Wei, 2008; Xu et al., 2014; also see Lu et al., 2011 for the relation of SMS and MI usage). Therefore, the activation theory implies that the correlation between MV and MI use should be significantly positive.

The results of the study by Ghose & Han (2011), Gerpott et al. (2014), and Gerpott & Meinert (2016) tend to support activation theory predictions. Ghose & Han (2011) analyzed objectively measured data of 180,000 customers of a Korean MNO over a 15-week period starting

March 2008. The authors found a positive correlation between the users’ inclination to upload and download content through MI on the one hand and MV calls on the other hand. Gerpott et al. (2014), who assessed data of 8312 private customers drawn from the billing system of a German MNO in a 2-month period in 2011, also detected a significantly positive, albeit small correlation between MV calls and MI usage. Hence, the analysis of these objectively captured data sets that partially enable longitudinal analyses effectively support the activation theory. However, the analyses were conducted in the early stages of MI adoption, and although both studies are longitudinal, the examined study periods are still too short to draw implications for long-term effects. Gerpott & Meinert (2016) found a significantly positive correlation between MI and MV usage in a sample of 11,614 postpaid customers of the German branch of a multinational MNO. This study captured usage over a considerably longer period of 25 months between October 2011 and October 2013. Because of its focus on German customers, it is unclear to what extent the results can be transferred to nations without highly developed fixed-line telecommunications networks.

The aforementioned empirical studies use mobile customer samples in developed countries in Asia, Europe, and North America. Hence, the transferability of their results onto differently developed geographical regions in general and GCC countries in particular is questionable. At least, there is some scholarly literature on user behavior of private telecommunications service customers in GCC and neighboring countries (e.g., Al-Mamary, Shamsuddin, & Hamid, 2015; Ameen et al., 2015; Isaid & Faisal, 2015; Ziani et al., 2015). However, the studies do not comprise any of the three introduced strands but commonly draw on variants of the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989; Venkatesh, Morris, Davis, & Davis, 2003) or merely present descriptive rundowns of self-reported usage assessments. Furthermore, the GCC markets exhibit several distinct features, which have to be considered. For instance, the general population in GCC countries is significantly younger than the population in developed regions such as the European Union (42.5 years) or the USA (37.8 years) (CIA, 2016). In addition,

MNOs in GCC countries are confronted with substantially higher SIM card penetration and smartphone adoption rates on the one hand, and exceptionally low fixed-line broadband penetration on the other hand. Therefore, the usage behavior of subscribers in GCC markets is likely to vary from mobile telecommunications customers in other (developed) countries. To the best of our knowledge, there are no precedent studies on individual usage behavior of private customers, which analyze objectively measured longitudinal data in Arab countries. As the first objective, the present analysis seeks to shed light on the interdependencies of MI and MV usage against the background of a scientifically untapped geographical region. Against this background, we formulate our first RQ as follows:

RQ1: How do changes in the amount of an MNO customer’s MI data volume relate to variations in the amount of outgoing MV minutes per person over time in a GCC market?

According to prior work (Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Xu et al., 2014), an MNO’s customer base is not homogenous, but instead consists of various groups of subscribers that exhibit diverse features, which may have an impact on MI usage intensities and habits on the individual level (e.g., Abeele et al., 2014; Gerpott et al., 2013; Hsiao & Chen, 2015). Potential distinctive individual characteristics are socio-demographic factors, contractual circumstances, and device-related features. For example, at first glance, users of a large hybrid device combining smartphone and tablet characteristics (i.e., a phablet) may be more inclined to use MI applications than users of conventional smartphones with smaller screens because certain tasks are easier to execute when the screen of the device is larger (Ma & Chen, 2015). However, given that the three aforementioned research theories explaining the relationship between MI usage volumes and MV calls or SMS do not at all discuss the relevance of potential moderators on the individual level, it is not surprising that most preceding studies omit these factors (e.g., Cassidy et al., 2014; Cecere & Corrocher, 2012; Gerpott, 2010a, 2010b;

Gerpott et al., 2014; Karikoski & Luukkainen, 2011; Mäkinen et al., 2014). Exceptions are the studies by Gerpott (2015) and Gerpott & Meinert (2016), who found significant moderating effects of individual characteristics on MI–SMS- or MI–MV-substitution, respectively. Because these two studies are based on data of users in Germany, it is questionable to what extent the results can be transferred onto subscribers in GCC countries. Hence, we formulate our second RQ as follows:

RQ2: How do socio-demographic, contract-, and device-related features as well as the study period moderate the relationship between MI data volume and the amount of outgoing MV minutes in a GCC market?

Previous scholarly contributions imply that the number of MV calls and the use frequency of SMS are interrelated at the level of the individual customer. Some studies suggest them to be substitutes (e.g., Anderson, Foros, & Steen, 2009; Gonzales, 2014; Karikoski & Luukkainen, 2011; Kim Telang, Vogt, & Krishnan, 2010; Pinchot, Douglas, Paullet, & Rota, 2011). Other work found a complementary relationship between MV calls and SMS usage (e.g., Gerpott, 2010a, 2015; Gerpott et al., 2014). However, with only one exception, there are no examinations that account for potential effects of the complementary relationship of these two service on MI usage volume. Gerpott & Meinert (2016) suggested that there is a significant moderating effect of SMS usage on the relationship of MI and MV. Therefore, the third RQ closely follows the trajectory of the respective study and can be stated as follows:

RQ3: How does the monthly number of outgoing SMS moderate the relationship between MI data volume and MV call minutes generated by a residential MNO customer in a GCC market?

## 4. Empirical methodology

## 4.1 Sample generation

Our analysis uses information retrieved from the billing system of one of the MNO’s active in the GCC countries. The raw data set contains the usage records and socio-demographic profiles of postpaid customers and the cooperating MNO as of July 31, 2015. From this population, we drew a random sample of approximately 31,500 residential mobile communications service customers, who were subscribed to a postpaid tariff between July 2013 and July 2015. From the initial random sample, 21,134 subscribers were excluded for a number of reasons. First, we dropped customers whose devices are not designed to process all the three examined services, namely, MI, MV, and SMS. Such devices are, for example, mobile broadband modems, tablets that can only be used for MI applications, or basic phones that have no MI functionality. Second, we omitted persons who did not use both MV and MI services at least once during the 25-month observation period. Third, we also discarded customers whose call records or socio-demographical profile contained missing values. The selection procedure resulted in a final random sample of 10,366 postpaid subscribers who either used a smartphone or phablet to access MI applications, made MV calls, and sent SMS. Customers in our final sample were not subscribed to a usage-independent flat-rate tariff. Instead, their prevailing tariffs were based on a bucket structure (Schlereth & Skiera, 2012). Customers choose between packages that contain certain amounts of MI data volume, SMS, and MV minutes for a fixed price. In case that the respective monthly allowance is used up, subscribers either opt for the next higher bucket or are migrated to a linearly usage-dependent tariff.

## 4.2 Variable measurements

## 4.2.1 Use intensity of mobile communications services

To measure the individuals’ MV calling intensity, instead of counting the number of calls per user, which is the approach of some of the preliminary research (e.g., Ghose & Han, 2011; Wei, 2008), we accounted for the overall duration of outgoing MV calls. Following various other studies (e.g., Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Gerpott et al., 2014; Karikoski & Luukkainen,

2011; Verkasalo, 2007), we captured monthly observations of the individuals’ number of outgoing minutes. This measurement was preferred to the number of calls because the call numbers do not adequately describe the individuals’ actual usage.

Table 2 provides an overview of the distribution statistics on average monthly MV calls in our sample. The average total amount of outgoing MV call minutes per user is 678.37 with a standard deviation of 482.06 min. The lowest average individual monthly usage was 13.76 min, and the highest was 5985.36 min. The fourth quartile, which has a class minimum of 852.04 min, makes up about 53.13% of the overall call volume in our sample. This shows a distortion toward users who exhibit a disproportionally heavy usage.

To operationalize MI use intensity at the individual level, we captured each customer’s IP traffic in megabyte (MB) at the end of each study month, following the approach of earlier studies (e.g., Gerpott, 2010a, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Gerpott et al., 2014; Verkasalo, 2007). More specifically, our variable “MI data volume” includes IP traffic that was transmitted through the MNO’s cellular network. The measure for MI data volume cannot be interpreted as straightforward as the more narrowly defined value for MV minutes because the MI use intensity captures different IP-based services, which differ significantly in their fields of application and intensity of data volume consumption. Nevertheless, the variable offers a distinct picture of individual usage of IP-based services (Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Ghose & Han, 2011). According to the distribution statistics on MI data volume in Table 2, the average monthly MI volume per user is 2260.063 MB with a standard deviation of 1762.167 MB. The user with the lowest (highest) average MI usage used 0.04 MB (16,422.84 MB) per month. Similar to the distortion in MV usage, the heavy users in the fourth quartile caused 52.88% of the overall MI traffic of the sample.

The changes in the monthly average MV and MI usage over the 25-month study period are shown in Figure 1. Although the trend in the development of outgoing MV minutes over the study periods is not obvious, the data suggest a weak growth trajectory for MV. In stark contrast, the average MI volume exhibits a marked increase of 387.0% from an average 855.03 MB in July 2013 to 4164.30 MB in July 2015. It is worth noting that in all study periods, the average amount of MI volume in our sample is considerably higher than comparable measures. reported in prior studies for developed countries (e.g., Germany, as described in Gerpott, 2015 and Gerpott & Meinert, 2016). This deviation can be explained by the lower fixed-line broadband penetration rates in GCC countries compared to many developed countries. The poor fixed-line broadband access options drive customers to perform a larger share of their Internet-related tasks through wireless networks.

In the observed timeframe, both medians of the MV minutes and MI volume increased from 448 to 538.5 min or 667 to 2766 MB, respectively. The observation that the values for the mean usage among all usage indicators are consistently well above the median usage across all study periods highlights the skewness of the usage distributions in the sample. It is caused by few heavy users, whose consumption over-proportionally amplifies the average values. This implication is backed up by previous research, which drew the same conclusion in cases where the sample was similarly skewed (Gerpott, 2010a; Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016; Gerpott et al., 2014; Verkasalo, 2008).

The validity of the statistical procedures, which examine intra-individual use changes (Section 4.3), may be severely hampered by the strongly distorted usage distributions of the sample. Therefore, we calculated the natural logarithm for both usage variables (MI volume, MV minutes). The logarithmized usage variables are much closer to a normal distribution than the originally measured values (see Table 2).

## 4.2.2 Potential moderators

Five customer-related characteristics and monthly outgoing SMS were treated as potential moderators in our analysis (see Tables 3 and 4; Figure 1). Two socio-demographic, one device-related, and two contract-related characteristics were derivable from the collaborating

MNO’s database. The two socio-demographic variables were age and nationality<sup>3</sup>. On July 31, 2015, the median age in our sample was 34 years (see variable #4 in Table 4). This is considerably above the officially documented median age in the GCC countries, which varies between 25.1 (Oman) and 31.8 (Bahrain) (CIA, 2016). However, this discrepancy is not surprising, as the sample only includes data on postpaid customers, who must have reached the legal age of maturity. Thus, there are no underage persons in the sample.

The second socio-demographic characteristic “National citizenship” is binary. A total of 877 individuals (= 8.5%), who have a different citizenship than the nationals of the observed country, received a score of 0. Given that only 2 of 50 (foreign) countries were represented by more than 100 individuals, we refrained from generating further variables for various foreign citizenships. The remaining 9489 subscribers (= 91.5%) with the nationality of the GCC country in which they were residing were scored as 1.

The device-related characteristic measures whether the individual used a smartphone (65.8% of all individuals; coded as 1) or a phablet (34.2%; coded as 0) in July 2015. To qualify as either one of the two device types, the phone must be capable of the three focal service types (MV, MI, and SMS) and have a touchscreen. Tablet computers, because they commonly lack MV and SMS functionality, were excluded from the sample. The criterion that determined whether the device was a smartphone or a phablet was its screen size. Although a phablet’s functionalities resemble that of a smartphone, phablets have screens somewhat larger than those of smartphones but smaller than those of fully fledged tablets. Even though the basic functions do not differ between smartphones and phablets, we distinguish between these two categories. We do this to account for potential usage variations, which have been observed between device types as a function of their screen sizes (Ericsson, 2015, 2016; Gerpott et al., 2013; Hsiao & Chen, 2015; Kim & Sundar, 2014; Müller, Gove, Webb, & Cheang, 2015). All devices with a screen size smaller than 6.5" were counted as a smartphone. Gadgets with a screen size equal to or greater than 6.5" and smaller than 7.5"<sup>4</sup> were defined as phablets because between these boundaries most devices are formally labeled as a tablet computer but at the same time still enable subscribers to use all three focal services (Device Atlas, 2016).

The first contract-related variable “Tenure with MNO” was calculated by subtracting the date at which the customer first signed up with the MNO from the end of the study period, i.e., July 31, 2015. One precondition to include a customer in our sample at the time of extraction was that there were at least 25 months of observable usage of the respective individual. Consequently, there are only persons in the sample, who exhibit more than 24 months of MNO tenure. The maximum amount of time a customer has had a contractual relationship with the MNO at the time of data extraction was 11 years. The median tenure with MNO is 6.92 years. The second contract-related factor was the participants’ “Tenure in current tariff.” It reflects the timespan between the commencement date of the customers’ current tariff and the last day of the study period. The customers in the sample switched from their previous tariff to a specific postpaid tariff type within the last 18 months of our 25-month overall study period. The respective tariff option served as a filter variable to ensure that the individuals in the sample are postpaid customers. It was introduced exactly 1.5 years before the extraction date to eventually replace prior postpaid tariff options. As depicted in Table 3, the sample is almost evenly distributed over the three tenure intervals (1) up to 6 months, (2) more than 6 months and up to 1 year, and (3) more than 1 year.

As explained in prior studies (e.g., Gerpott & Meinert 2016; Gerpott et al. 2014; Kim et al., 2010), one potential moderator of the MV–MI association is the number of SMS, which subscribers send from their devices. Hence, a person’s SMS use intensity was captured by recording the number of SMS sent by every customer during each of the 25 months in our study period. Figure 1 graphs the number of average outgoing SMS, which decreased substantially by approximately 41.5% from 108.14 to 63.26 SMS sent per customer per month in July 2013 and July 2015. In the same timeframe, the median number of monthly outgoing SMS went down from 63 to 31. Although the overall mean number of SMS sent was 78.33 per month, the median was at only 48.44. Again, this points to a substantial skewness of the SMS distribution caused by the dominance of heavy users in the sample. Therefore, for the number of outgoing SMS we computed, the natural logarithm for each individual in the same vein as for the other two usage variables, MV calls and MI volume.

Table 4 provides the Pearson and Kendall correlation measures for the introduced potential moderators. The low and/or insignificant coefficients suggest that multicollinearity was not a problem in the present study.

## 4.3 Statistical procedure

The present data set includes the duration of MV calls as the criterion and two types of independent variables, namely (1) time-variant usage variables that can change differently from month to month over the 25 measurement occasions and (2) characteristics that do not vary for each individual over time (e.g., nationality) or change by the same absolute values for all individuals (e.g., tenure with MNO).

The main thrust of our analysis is to reveal the extent of the connection between MV calling minutes and MI traffic over the course of the 25 examined measuring occasions. Another objective is to determine the invariant features, which constitute parts of the variance across the individual regression lines of the MI and MV measures. To handle variables that are repeated ly measured in the same group of individuals on multiple occasions on the one hand and individual-level characteristics that remain constant on the other, we apply multilevel (or mixed/hierarchical) regression modeling (Bell & Jones, 2015; Diggle, Heagerty, Liang, Zeger, 2013; Garson, 2013; Goldstein, 2011; Hox, 2010; Moskowitz & Hershberger, 2012; Raudenbusch & Bryk, 2002; Shen, 2016; Snijders & Bosker, 2012; West, Welch, & Galecki,

2015). The two-level model, the basis for our analysis, enables us to spot intra-individual changes in the usage behavior (Raudenbusch & Bryk, 2002). Particular changes in the individual MI usage intensity are observed at level 1 of the analysis by capturing each subscriber’s personal usage development at every measuring occasion. A further level 1 variable is SMS usage as it varies for individuals at each measuring occasion. At level 2 of the analysis, there are moderator variables such as time-invariant individual characteristics (e.g., age or tenure with MNO).

Multilevel modeling is a variance-based statistical procedure, which captures the differences in individual regression lines by random intercepts and slopes. The idea is to test whether the variances of the individuals’ intercept and the slope parameters are significantly above a value of zero. In the context of our analysis, the intercepts serve to describe the variation in MV minutes over all sample subjects in the first of the 25 study periods. These two random components are a means to explain the inter-individual variance that is nested within subjects. This study examines the intensity of MI usage as the main level-1 predictor, which is complemented by the two level-1 variables, SMS usage intensity and study period, as well as the individual-level (level-2) parameters and interaction terms between MI intensity and potential moderators. Every significant interaction term indicates that the slope of the MI use intensity exhibits variation subject to each additionally included variable (Aguinis & Culpepper, 2015, pp. 157-158; Aguinis, Gottfredson, & Culpepper, 2013, p. 1493; Hox, 2010, pp. 63-68, 88-91, Shen 2016, pp. 951-952). We applied the “MIXED” procedure of the statistics software IBM SPSS (version 23) to calculate two-level models. To fully depict the association between MI use intensity as the main predictor and outgoing MV minutes as the criterion variable, we included fixed (inter-individual) regression coefficients and (intra-subject) random variance components in the individual regression equations. To determine both fixed and random effects, we used the full maximum likelihood approach (Aguinis et al., 2013, p. 1501; Garson, 2013, p. 32; Heck, Thomas, & Tabata, 2014, pp. 25-31). We modeled the residuals (within-

subject error structure) with a scaled identity matrix. At the second analysis level, the intercept, slope, and their covariance were determined with an unstructured covariance matrix for the random effects. (Diggle et al., 2013, p. 335; Garson, 2013, pp. 41-42; Heck et al., 2014, pp. 186-188; West et al., 2015, pp. 19-22).

## 5. Empirical results

To address the RQs mentioned in Section 3, Table 5 reports six multilevel models (A–F) for the logarithm of the number of outgoing MV minutes as the dependent variable. Model A is the null model that depicts the random intercept for MV minutes that describes the variations between persons while any predictors are excluded. It provides an estimate of the shares of within- and between-person variance. Thus, we test the significance of the between-person variance in the number of MV calling minutes by examining the level-2 intercept (see variance “RI” in Table 5) and the residual level-1 variance. The “interclass correlation” (ICC), which reflects the share of the total variance of the dependent measure that is explained by the between-person (level-2) variance, is 61.09% (= (0.429562/ (0.429562 + 0.273565)  100) in the first instance of the model (Heck et al., 2014, pp. 6, 73-74; Hox,2010, p. 15; Snijders & Bosker, 2012, pp. 17-18). Hence, in the present sample, 38.91% of the variance is attributed to the within-person variation in MV usage between the 25 measuring occasions. This result implies that although the MV calling behavior varied considerably within the subjects over the 25 observed periods, the subscribers were still heterogeneous with regard to their MV calling levels. As Garson (2013, pp. 27-28) and Heck et al. (2014, p. 74) argued, a large ICC may distort the estimate for the MI–MV association between persons. Hence, it is advantageous to perform a multilevel analysis with MV calling predictors at the first and other explanatory factors at the second level, as well as cross-level measures.

Model B reveals the impact of the three time-variant characteristics, namely, study period, MI use intensity, and SMS usage, on MV calling minutes. The inclusion of the level-1 predictors reduces the unaccounted variance by $1 3 . 5 5 \% ( = ( 0 . 3 7 1 3 7 7 - 0 . 4 2 9 5 6 2 ) / 0 . 4 2 9 5 6 2 \times 1 0 0 )$ . Although the measure for study period is statistically significant $( \mathtt { p } \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and slightly positive (0.002387), it is very small thus indicating that MV calling intensity does not systematically change over time. This finding underpins the intuitive conclusion from Figure 1 in Section 4.1 that there is no obvious trend in MV calling development during the 25-month study period. The highly significant estimates for MI data volume and outgoing SMS (variables 2 and 3 in model B) are both positive, thus indicating a complementary relationship between each of the two usage variables and MV calls.

Model C incorporates the fixed effects of the level-2 variables (age, nationality, device type, as well as MNO and tariff tenure) to provide insights for $\mathrm { R Q } 2 .$ . The inclusion of the five components reduces the share of unexplained variance by another 3.74% (= (0.357482–

0.371377)/0.371377  100) and hardly affects the magnitude and statistical significance of the three time-variant measures. The slightly negative value for age is significant at the 3.1% level (variable 4 in Model C in Table 5). The negative value for the subscribers’ nationality reveals that expatriates generate more MV calling minutes than national citizens of the observed GCC country. The remaining three level-2 variables included in model C are positive, which indicates that MV calling intensity is more likely to be high if subscribers use a smartphone, have long MNO tenure, and switched into their current tariff longer ago (variables 6–8 in model C).

In models A to C, the slope of outgoing MV minutes was fixed across all 25 study periods. Starting in model D, we allow for random variations in the slope. According to a likelihood ratio test (i.e., the difference between “Deviance” in models C and D in Table 5), model D provides a better fit with the underlying data because it explains significantly $( \mathtt { p } \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ more variation than the previous model C (Heck et al., 2014, pp. 95, 204; Snijders & Bosker, 2012, pp. 97-98; West et al., 2015, pp. 34-35). The between-person variation of the random slope of MI data volume (see “RII.” in Table 5) is highly significant $( \mathtt { p } \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . This indicates that subscribers are heterogeneous with regard to the impact of MI data volume changes on variations in the number of outgoing MV call minutes.

To learn more about the heterogeneity of individual regression slopes, we computed a bivariate regression with MI volume as the predictor and outgoing MV calling minutes as the dependent variable for each of the 10,366 individuals in our sample over the 25 measuring occasions (Gerpott, 2015, pp. 818-819; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016, pp. 71). Only 1.68% of the sample (174 persons) have a markedly negative b-coefficient below −0.3 (minimum: −2.92), which points to a potential substitutional effect of MI usage on the number of MV calls. More than two-thirds (68.36%) of the individuals exhibited usage interdependencies close to zero, i.e., a b-coefficient between –0.3 and 0.3. For the remaining 29.96% of the subscribers, the analysis revealed slope coefficients of 0.3 or higher (maximum: 2.89) indicating a positive interrelation of the two usage variables. Hence, for this group of the subscribers, growing MI usage is likely to coincide with an increase in outgoing MV minutes.

There is a significantly negative covariance/correlation between the random intercept and the slope (see “RIIIa” and “RIIIb” in Table 5). The larger the amount of outgoing MV minutes at the beginning of the study period in July 2013, the less positive is slope of MI use intensity at the end of the study period (July 2015), (Hox, 2010, p. 90; Snijders & Boser, 2012, p. 79;

West et al., 2015, pp. 356-357). This relationship implies that subscribers, who generally have a high (low) MV usage intensity, are less inclined to further increase their MV call minutes as they expand their MI usage. Reversely, the number of MV minutes of individuals who rarely made voice calls in July 2013 tends to rise more steeply with every unit of MI usage they consume. According to former work (Shek & Ma, 2011, p. 58; West, 2009, p. 221), this pattern is most commonly observed in longitudinal studies.

Predictors 9 to 15 in models E and F (Table 5) provide additional insights on how MI usage intensity affects the regression slope of outgoing MV minutes. The interaction of the two time-varying predictors, study period and MI data volume, are represented in variable 9 of Table 5. The remaining five variables (11–15) depict the cross-level interactions of MI usage (level-1) and the level-2 variables, which vary only between persons and aim at addressing RQ2. The interaction terms reflect the extent of intra-individual differences of the MI–MV relationship over time unobservable with direct effects alone. In other words, unexplained variations in the level-1 model can be explained by the level-2 variables (Heck et al., 2014, p. 15). Of all interaction terms, only variable 14, which is the interaction variable for MI usage and MNO tenure, is unambiguously insignificant.

As proposed by prior work (Hox, 2010, p. 57-59), we therefore drop the insignificant term in the last iteration (model F) to adequately analyze RQ2. According to the statistical literature, the remaining significant interaction terms and the direct effects of the explanatory variables cannot be viewed isolated from each other, but have to be interpreted as a system (Hox, 2010, p. 63).

Following this principle, the indications of the coefficients in model F (Table 5) are that the MI–MV relationship is less positive in the later study periods (interaction 9), for users send ing lots of SMS (interaction 10), for older customers (interaction 11), for national citizens (interaction 12), for phablet users (interaction 13), and for people who most recently changed their tariff (interaction 15).

The findings in Table 5 were put through several robustness checks. First, we separated the study period into two periods consisting of the first 13 months (July 2013–July 2014) and the last 12 months (August 2014–July 2015). Second, we changed the structure of the covariance matrix of the residuals according to other covariance structures that are commonly used for multilevel, i.e., compound symmetry, diagonal, Toeplitz, and first-order autoregressive structure (Hox, 2010, pp. 99-10; West et al., 2015, pp. 21-22). Finally, we excluded all 186 heavy users from the sample, whose monthly mean MV minutes and/or MI usage intensity exceeded the sample mean by at least three times the value of the standard deviation (Gerpott, 2015; p. 820; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016, p. 73). None of the reported variations provided any results that are substantially different from our findings reported in Table 5. Therefore, our results are unlikely to be an artifact of the particular assumptions of our baseline modeling approach. To summarize, there is substantial heterogeneity in the individuals’ MI–MV association. 79.77% (20.23%) of the analyzed individuals exhibited a positive (negative) relationship between the two service types. Hence, even though our findings point to the suggestion that an increase in MI data volume usage over time has a complementary effect on the amount of outgoing MV minutes, the first RQ cannot unequivocally be answered. Regarding RQ2, our multilevel analysis reveals the MI–MV relationship varies over study periods and is affected by an individual’s age, nationality, device type, and tenure in the current tariff. With regard to the third RQ, we find a moderating effect of the number of SMS sent on the MI–MV relationship, namely, subscribers with high SMS usage are less inclined to complement increasing MI usage with a rise in MV calling minutes than their counterparts with a low SMS use intensity.

## 6. Discussion

The present longitudinal work analyzed interdependencies of MV calling intensity and MI usage and the moderation of the focal relationship by SMS use and time-invariant individual subscriber characteristics (i.e., socio-demographic, device-related, and contractual variables). The data set, which we retrieved from an MNO active in one of the GCC countries, contains 10,366 private postpaid mobile communications services customers, who either used a smartphone or phablet over the 25 months from July 2013 to July 2015. The empirical results revealed skewed distributions of the mean MI and MV consumption with a few very heavy users, who uplifted the mean values of our study’s core variables. However, compared to earlier studies of subscribers in an EU country, MI and MV usage was less unevenly distributed among subscribers residing in a GCC country (Gerpott, 2015; Gerpott & Meinert, 2016). The empirical findings revealed that customer heterogeneity with regard to the association between MI and MV usage over the study period was large. We therefore conducted a two-level analysis of individual MV calling usage changes.

Across the 25 study periods, at the sample level, the average monthly MV calling intensity did not exhibit any distinct decreasing or increasing trend. However, at the individual level, our results point to a small but significant association between the study period and MV calling intensity, meaning that individuals in our sample slightly increased the amount of calling minutes over time. At the same time, the average monthly MI data volume almost quintupled, while the third usage variable, number of SMS sent, declined by 41.5% between July 2013 and July 2015 (Figure 1). Judging from the association between the two focal usage variables, MI use intensity and MV calling minutes, a substitutional MI–MV relationship is unlikely. On the contrary, our data suggest a strong complementary relationship of the usage of the two services. Intra-individual regressions revealed a positive correlation between MI and MV usage among nearly four-fifth of the subscribers. This can be partially attributed to the bucket pricing tariff structure of the cooperating MNO: It provides proportional increases in MI volume and MV calling minutes with each next higher bucket. Subscribers who opt for a larger MI volume automatically receive more MV minutes and can therefore increase their consumption of the two services to the same extent without incurring additional costs.

A second complementary relationship was observed regarding MV calls and the number of SMS sent, i.e., on average, subscribers who increased their SMS usage were more likely to rise the number of MV calling minutes. In conclusion, the results of our study provide strong support for the activation theory, which implies positive correlations between usage changes for different types of mobile communications services (Wei, 2008). Overall, this finding is in line with the observations of Gerpott & Meinert (2016). However, the strength of the MI–MV calling usage association in the GCC country under study is approximately 36% larger than in the one detected by Gerpott & Meinert (2016) among mobile communications subscribers in Germany.

The aforementioned results with regard to a substitutional or complementary effect of different mobile communications services can be partially explained by deviant conditions that are present in GCC in comparison to Western country markets. In the GCC countries, it is likely that customer usage of information and telecommunications technology has not reached a point at which a substitution of the various services occurs. The less-developed fixed-line network paired with the comparatively much higher adoption rates of mobile network services is to be considered as a major driver of MI data volume increase (cf. Table 1). At the same time, the low penetration rate of fixed-line networks has the consequence that users who get more convinced with regard to the benefits of MI access simultaneously see the usefulness of voice calls, which they had hardly placed in the past because they had no fixed telephone line. Hence, the main scholarly implication of our results is that the maturity of a national telecommunications service market moderates usage interdependencies between established services and MI. The relationship is more likely to be complementary (substitutional), if the overall maturity of a telecommunications service market is low (high).

Our findings carry strategic implications for practitioners. First, MI data traffic and the number of MV minutes are likely to continue its overall increase in the future because of the worldwide increase in MI use (cf. Section 1) and due to the circumstance that the MV services markets in the GCC countries are not yet saturated (cf. Section 2). Hence, MNOs are well advised to further expand their wireless networks and to regularly review their tariff offers to include larger data volume and MV calling buckets to anticipate growing demand and maintain a complementary usage relationship of MI and MV. Second, as the MV–MI relationship is significantly more positive for expatriates (cf. Section 5), MNOs should consider to develop offers that account for the peculiarities in the usage behavior of both nationals and non-nationals. More specifically, in the focal GCC country, MNOs may benefit from emphasizing “bundled packages” including substantial MI, SMS, and MV allowances (e.g., 1 GB data and 100 call minutes and 100 SMS) in the expatriate, but not in the national segment. In contrast, nationals should be targeted with tariff plans, which make it possible to book allowances for each service category separately (e.g., 1 GB data or 100 call minutes or 100 SMS). Notwithstanding that our study is the first longitudinal analysis of actual usage behaviors of a sample of mobile communications service customers in a GCC country, our work is not without limitations. The objectively captured data, which were derived from the cooperating MNO’s data warehouse, have several shortcomings. First, because of the typical contractua preconditions in GCC countries, we were unable to incorporate the gender variable in our analysis. Furthermore, information on other demographic characteristics such as education, income, or marital status, was unavailable. Second, the unique identifier of a subscriber was his or her SIM card. Therefore, we were unable to identify customers who hold multiple SIM cards and who, consequently, split their services usage over multiple devices. Third, compared to the general population in the GCC countries, the group of expatriates was underrepresented in the sample. Fourth, we focused on a single GCC country, which raises the question whether our results hold in the remaining five GCC nations. As depicted in Section 2, although the preconditions in network technology and development are generally comparable between the six GCC countries, there are still differences with regard to the countries demographical characteristics, legal systems, and economic status. Therefore, to provide a more distinct picture of mobile subscribers in the GCC countries, research is needed that compares and distinguishes the usage behavior of the respective countries. Fifth, we only assessed the total volume of MV call minutes. A more in-depth analysis of the subscribers’ usage behaviors could also include the number of monthly calls and/or the mean call duration because short calls are more eligible to be replaced by SMS or MI short messages than long conversations. Sixth, it was not possible to consider qualitative and/or subjective factors that reflect the subscribers’ perceptions of mobile service qualities. Seventh, the provided data only contained information on postpaid customers of a single MNO. Therefore, there might be other factors, which are fundamentally different for prepaid subscribers or customers of other MNOs. For example, MNO tenure could be significantly lower because prepaid customers may be more inclined to change providers than their postpaid counterparts because of lower switching costs. In addition, the overall mobile service consumption levels may be lower be-

cause prepaid customers may be more price sensitive and, thus, choose tariff options which include lower MI data volume and/or less MV minutes.

Finally, the system-captured data provided information on the monthly data volume of the MNO’s private customers. The MI use intensity measure neither accounts for the various applications that generate MI usage nor for the individual involvement or personal arousal caused by the data provided. To more appropriately examine the MV–MI relationship, the actual MI usage could be dissected into OTT voice app usage, which is the part of the MI usage that provides the same or similar functionality as MV calls on the one hand and other IPbased services on the other. Such a fine-grained approach enables scholars to obtain a more detailed understanding of the substitutional relationship between traditional mobile communications and OTT services.

## Biographical notes

Dr. Torsten J. Gerpott is a Full Professor in the Mercator School of Management at the University of Duisburg-Essen in Germany. His research interests include the diffusion of innovative Internet-based services and business/marketing strategies as well as regulatory economics in network-based industries. Furthermore, he has more than 25 years of top management consulting expertise. He has published about 420 scientific articles in a broad range of scholarly journals such as Telecommunications Policy, Information Economics and Policy, Journal of Media Economics, Energy Policy, Technological Forecasting and Social Change and Journal of Management Studies.

Sebastian May is a PhD student and a research assistant of Prof. Gerpott at the University of Duisburg-Essen. His major field of research is the use of mobile telecommunications services among private customers. He studied economics at the University of Duisburg-Essen and the University of California, Berkeley.

Gokhan Nas is a telecommunication professional with over 12 years of international experience in the areas of consumer marketing, investment and technology planning. He completed his MBA at the University of Hull, UK following his BSc in Electrical and Electronics Engineering at Eastern Mediterranean University, Cyprus.
