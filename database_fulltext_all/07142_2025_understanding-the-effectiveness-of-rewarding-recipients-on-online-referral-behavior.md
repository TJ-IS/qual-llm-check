# UNDERSTANDING THE EFFECTIVENESS OF REWARDING RECIPIENTS ON ONLINE REFERRAL BEHAVIOR<sup>1</sup>

Angelika Dimoka Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami Coral Gables, FL, U.S.A. {dimoka@miami.edu}

Jinghui (Jove) Hou Decision and Information Sciences, C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston Houston, TX, U.S.A. {jhou@bauer.uh.edu}

Xitong Li Information Systems and Operations Management, HEC Paris Jouy-en-Josas, FRANCE {lix@hec.fr}

Paul A. Pavlou Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami Coral Gables, FL, U.S.A. {pavlou@miami.edu}

Online referral programs predominantly focus on incentivizing referrers to make referrals but largely ignore the potential of rewarding recipients to motivate referral behaviors. In this paper, we systematically examine whether, how, and when rewarding recipients could effectively motivate referrers with five experiments across a variety of settings. The results demonstrate that rewarding recipients is effective in motivating referrers to behave prosocially and make referrals. Interestingly, referrers behave prosocially when they are not rewarded, but the effectiveness of rewarding recipients is reduced if referrers are rewarded. We also reveal that referrers’ prosocial behaviors are primarily driven by altruistic motivation, rather than the pursuit of either reputational or collective benefits; notably, offering rewards to referrers dampens their altruistic motivation. Finally, we find that referrers’ prosocial behaviors are inhibited with an increased action cost of their referral behaviors, while their prosocial behaviors persist, even with an increased interpersonal cost associated with the referral behaviors. We conclude by offering theoretical and managerial implications for designing effective referral strategies that leverage the potential of rewarding recipients.

Keywords: Online referrals, prosocial behaviors, altruistic motivation, action cost, interpersonal cost

## Introduction

Online referral programs are recognized as one of the most effective and prevalent word-of-mouth promotion strategies (Van den Bulte et al., 2018). To incentivize consumers to participate in online referral programs, retailers have traditionally offered rewards to referrers (those who send out referral messages for referring a product) and/or recipients (those who receive the referral messages and decide whether to buy the referred product). Referral SaaSquatch, a leading marketing referral agency, summarizes the most recommended referral reward programs. First, 45% of referral programs use the strategy that only rewards referrers (hereafter referred to as the rewarding-referrers strategy).<sup>2</sup>

Second, 51% of the programs use the referral strategy that rewards both referrers and recipients (referred to as the rewarding-both strategy).<sup>3</sup> Only 4% use the referral strategy that only rewards recipients (referred to as the rewardingrecipients strategy).<sup>4</sup> From a homo economicus perspective, it is expected that rewarding referrers is the most effective way to motivate referral behaviors, which explains its prevailing business practice in industry. Accordingly, academic research has also largely focused on studying the strategies that reward referrers (Hong et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2020).

Until recently, researchers have begun to pay attention to the potential advantages of rewarding recipients in online referral programs (Gershon et al., 2020; Jung et al., 2021). Contributing to this emerging literature, our research aims to investigate whether, how, and when rewarding recipients is effective in motivating referrers to make referrals. Building on the prior work on referral reward programs (Ryu & Feick, 2007) and prosocial motivations (Heyman & Ariely, 2004), we theorize that rewarding recipients could induce referrers’ altruistic motivation, i.e., referrers’ concern toward recipients’ welfare (Batson & Powell, 2003), motivating referrers to behave prosocially (that is, making referrals despite not being rewarded). Notably, we conjecture that referrers’ altruistic motivation will arise when recipients are rewarded, as under the rewarding-recipients strategy, but their altruistic motivation will be dampened when referrers are also provided with rewards, as under the rewarding-both strategy.

We tested these propositions in five experiments across a variety of products (i.e., mobile app dictionary, photo-editing app, food delivery app) and reward types (i.e., Amazon gift card, product voucher) and in different countries (i.e., U.K., U.S.). We show that referrers behave prosocially under the rewarding-recipients strategy, which is mainly driven by altruistic motivation, instead of egoistic motivations (i.e., referrers’ concern toward their own benefits; Batson & Powell, 2003), such as pursuing reputational or collective benefits. However, once referrers are also provided with rewards (i.e., the rewarding-both strategy), their altruistic motivation, which is induced by rewarding recipients, is dampened (referred to as the “dampening” effect). Consequently, referrers’ referral behaviors largely depend on the rewards given to them rather than the rewards given to recipients, leading to a similar referral likelihood as under the rewarding-referrers strategy.<sup>5</sup> We further explored the moderating roles of two types of referral costs: (1) action cost and (2) interpersonal cost. Action cost refers to the cost, such as effort and time, needed to complete a referral task, whereas interpersonal cost refers to the potential risk of damage to the social relationship between referrers and recipients due to a potentially poor referral (such as referring a low-quality product that recipients may deem questionable). We found that referrers’ prosocial behaviors are inhibited by a higher action cost of making referrals, while their prosocial behaviors persist, even when facing a relatively high interpersonal cost. This indicates the asymmetrical moderating roles of action cost versus interpersonal cost in the relationship between reward strategies and referral behaviors.

Our research makes several contributions to the literature on online referrals, referral incentive schemes, and prosocial motivations. First, our research systematically examines the effectiveness of rewarding recipients in online referral programs. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that rewarding recipients could be effective in motivating referrers to behave prosocially, even when referrers are not rewarded (as under the rewarding-recipients strategy). Interestingly, the effectiveness of rewarding recipients is attenuated if referrers are also rewarded, as under the rewarding-both strategy. Second, our study shows that the effectiveness of rewarding recipients under the rewardingrecipients strategy is primarily driven by referrers’ altruistic motivation, but their altruistic motivation is dampened when they are also rewarded (as under the rewarding-both strategy). Finally, our study examines the relationship between cost and prosocial behaviors with empirical evidence from the context of online referrals. Notably, our research distinguishes two types of referral costs (action cost and interpersonal cost) and uncovers the asymmetrical effects of action cost and interpersonal cost in moderating referrers’ prosocial behaviors, thereby shedding light on the relative effectiveness of different reward strategies.

## Related Literature and Hypothesis Development

## The Potential of Rewarding Recipients

The extant literature on online referral programs focuses on whether providing rewards can effectively motivate customers to participate in such programs (e.g., Orsingher & Wirtz, 2018), increase their loyalty (e.g., Garnefeld et al., 2013), reduce the potential risk of customer churn (e.g., Armelini et al., 2015), or increase the matching between referred customers and firms (e.g., Van den Bulte et al., 2018). Prior research has also shown that the effects of providing rewards are contingent on various factors, such as reward type (e.g., Jin & Huang, 2014), reward size (e.g., Kornish & Li, 2010), and customer characteristics (e.g., Kumar et al., 2010).

While prior literature on referral reward strategies has focused on examining the effectiveness of rewarding referrers on motivating referrers to make referrals (e.g., Biyalogorsky et al., 2001; Hong et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2020; Lobel et al., 2017), it has largely overlooked the potential of rewarding recipients. Notably, although Ryu and Feick (2007) used the rewarding-recipients strategy in their Study 3, they excluded the rewarding-recipients strategy in their Study 4 by explaining that “this strategy is much less common in practice” (p. 90). The field experiment by Hong et al. (2017) did not incorporate the rewarding-recipients strategy as a treatment group because, as explained by the authors, the industry partner believed that the strategy would be unlikely to be accepted by referrers (p. 797).

However, the potential of rewarding recipients has received some attention in recent literature. Jung et al. (2021) provided evidence that the rewarding-recipients and rewarding-both strategies could outperform the rewarding-referrers strategy in terms of generating product adoptions. While Jung et al. focused on examining the effects of different reward strategies on generating product adoptions, our research differs from their work in three important aspects. First, our research aims to explore whether and how rewarding recipients could effectively motivate referrers to make referrals. Second, whereas Jung et al. (2021) showed that the effectiveness of rewarding recipients in generating product adoptions is partially driven by “better-targeted” referrals, we theorize and empirically show a different underlying mechanism through which rewarding recipients promotes referrers’ referral behaviors—that is, referrers’ altruistic motivation. Finally, we explore how two types of referral costs (action cost and interpersonal cost) moderate the effects of rewarding recipients on referral behaviors.

Gershon et al. (2020) also explored the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy in generating referrals and found that the rewarding-recipients strategy can motivate referrers to behave prosocially. Despite the existing evidence for the potential of rewarding recipients in motivating referrers to make referrals, Gershon et al. stated that their research mainly focuses on comparing the rewardingrecipients strategy and rewarding-referrers strategy because such comparison offers a clean conceptual separation. In addition, although Gershon et al.’s Study 1 and Study 4A also involved the rewarding-both strategy, the authors did not focus on examining the effect of rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy on motivating referral behaviors.<sup>6</sup> Indeed, rewarding recipients occurs not only under the rewarding-recipients strategy but under the rewarding-both strategy as well. It is yet unclear whether the effectiveness of rewarding recipients varies, depending on whether referrers are rewarded. The investigation has not only theoretical implications for academic research but also important managerial implications since the actual marketing expenditure of the rewarding-both strategy is twice that of the rewarding-recipients strategy—which, if successful, could be a cost-effective referral strategy for practitioners.

The motivational mechanisms that explain how rewarding recipients could motivate referrers to behave prosocially and make referrals remain underexplored. Gershon et al. (2020) argued that seeking reputational benefits (such as appearing generous) is likely a potential mechanism that drives referrers’ prosocial behaviors. However, extensive literature documents that one’s prosocial behavior is likely driven by coexisting motivations (e.g., Malti et al., 2009; Wentzel et al., 2007). Altruistic motivation, defined as concern toward others’ welfare (Batson & Powell, 2003), is considered a basic motivation that drives prosocial behavior. Surprisingly, little research has examined the potential role of altruistic motivation in driving referrers to make referrals. It is unclear whether altruistic and egoistic motivations (such as seeking reputational or collective benefits) drive referrers’ prosocial behaviors through a dual motivational mechanism, or whether altruistic motivation plays a primary role in the motivating process. Further, assuming altruistic motivation could drive referrers to make referrals, it is important to explore how referral costs affect referrers’ altruistic motivation and thus the effectiveness of only rewarding recipients. Addressing these nuanced questions not only has theoretical implications but could also help managers understand how to leverage the strategies that reward recipients in an effective and economical way.

## Altruistic Motivation and the Dampening Effect

Building on Fiske’s (1992) relation theory, Heyman and Ariely (2004) introduced the “dual-market model” to explain the relationship between compensation level and effort. In the dual-market model, there are two types of markets: the monetary market, in which effort increases with compensation, and the social market, in which effort is shaped by altruistic motivation and thus sensitive to others’ welfare, instead of one’s own rewards. In the social market relationship, people seek to increase others’ welfare due to altruistic motivations. Prior research has shown that people have an intrinsic motivation to act altruistically, such as sharing resources with others and helping others without extrinsic compensation (for a review, see Warneken & Tomasello, 2009). The burgeoning literature on referral incentives documents preliminary evidence that altruistic motivation may drive referrers’ referral behaviors. A notable finding by Jung et al. (2020) shows that under the rewarding-both strategy, referrers are more likely to make referrals when the rewarding-both strategy is framed in altruistic terms (i.e., highlighting the benefits to recipients) rather than in egoistic (i.e., highlighting the benefits to referrers) or equitable terms (i.e., highlighting the benefits to both referrers and recipients). Under the rewarding-recipients strategy, no reward is given to referrers, and it is thus less likely to activate the monetary market relationship. Instead, rewarding recipients in this strategy is likely to induce referrers’ concern about recipients’ welfare, thus activating the social market relationship. Accordingly, referrers under the rewarding-recipients strategy may want to make referrals simply because their referrals provide recipients with an opportunity to receive rewards. In sum, we propose the following two hypotheses:

H1a: Referrers’ referral likelihood under the rewardingrecipients strategy will be higher than that under the noreward strategy.

H1b: The effect of the rewarding-recipients strategy on referrers’ referral likelihood will be mediated by referrers’ altruistic motivation.

Heyman and Ariely’s (2004) dual-market model also suggests that for markets with signals of both monetary-market and social-market relationships, economic incentives are likely to suppress altruistic motivations; consequently, the moneymarket relationship would be expected to dominate mixed markets. Consistent with the dual-market model, prior research has shown that giving rewards undermines people’s altruistic tendencies in various contexts, such as blood donation (Lacetera & Macis, 2010) and environmental protection (Frey & Oberholzer-Gee, 1997). This is because people attribute their reasons for engaging in prosocial activities to the rewards offered to them (Warneken & Tomasello, 2008). According to the dual-market model, under the rewarding-both strategy, referrers’ focus on their own rewards will likely undermine their altruistic motivation, which would be induced by rewarding recipients. Thus, referrers’ referral likelihood under the rewarding-both strategy is likely to be largely determined by the rewards they can gain, as under the rewarding-referrers strategy.<sup>7</sup> In other words, compared to the rewarding-referrers strategy, rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy may not provide any additional value in motivating referrers to make referrals. Following this logic, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2a: Referrers’ referral likelihood under (i) the rewardingboth strategy and (ii) the rewarding-referrers strategy will be higher than that in the no-rewarding strategy.

H2b: Referrers’ altruistic motivation under the rewardingboth strategy will be lower than that under the rewardingrecipients strategy (i.e., the dampening effect).

In contrast to the theorized altruistic motivation and the dampening effect based on the dual-market model, there are two alternative egoistic motivations that may drive referrers’ prosocial behaviors: (1) maximizing the collective benefit and (2) pursuing reputational benefits. First, Clark and Mils (1993) show that when people share helpful materials with others, they tend to view themselves as having communal relationships with others and self-other boundaries are blurred. Thus, they are likely to view themselves and others as belonging to a collective and will focus on the collective’s total benefit (Dawes et al., 1988). Accordingly, referrers may treat the rewards given to recipients as rewards given to the collective of referrers and recipients, which would therefore maximize the total benefit for the collective. Thus, the rewards given to recipients could motivate referrers to make referrals in both the rewarding-recipients and the rewarding-both strategies. Second, building on the literature on the image motivation of prosocial behavior (Ariely et al., 2009), a desire to appear generous may also drive referrers’ prosocial behaviors in strategies that reward recipients only (Gershon et al., 2020). Therefore, pursuing a reputational benefit is another alternative egoistic motivation that could drive the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy. As such, we need to empirically examine these two potential alternative mechanisms related to egoistic motivations (in Study 1a).

## Asymmetrical Effects of Action Cost and Interpersonal Cost

We propose that referral costs, including action cost (the cost, such as effort and time, needed to complete a referral task) and interpersonal cost (the potential risk that might hurt the social relationship between referrers and recipients due to a potentially poor referral), affect the effectiveness of the reward strategies in an asymmetrical fashion.

A considerable literature shows that although people often behave prosocially without considering extrinsic benefits, their motivation to help others decreases as the cost of the altruistic behavior increases (e.g., Clark & Mils, 1993). Intuitively, people often give strangers road directions by virtue of altruistic motivation, but they are less likely to help when asked to donate a large amount of money. Batson et al. (1983) showed that a high cost of helping shifts people’s attention from being concerned about others to being concerned about themselves, thereby weakening their altruistic tendencies. Rational altruism theory (Andreoni & Miller, 2002) posits that people’s altruistic behaviors are bounded by rational thinking; people act altruistically only if the cost of such behavior is not overly high. Therefore, we propose that referrers are more likely to behave altruistically under the rewarding-recipients strategy when their referral costs (including action cost and interpersonal cost) are relatively low, compared to when their referral costs are relatively high. Note that we compare referrers’ altruistic tendencies between the conditions of relatively low and high referral costs within the rewarding-recipients strategy.

On the other hand, under the rewarding-both and rewardingreferrers strategies, referrers are given rewards; thus, their referral behaviors are largely motivated by rewards, as theorized by the dual-market model (Heyman & Ariely, 2004). Building on exchange theory (Walster et al., 1978), we propose that the rewards given to referrers encourage them to consider engaging in an exchange relationship and behave in accordance with the monetary market. Therefore, referrers’ referral behaviors would largely depend on a cost-benefit analysis, and referrers’ referral likelihood under the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies would decrease as referral costs (either action cost or interpersonal cost) increase. Note that we compare referrers’ referral behaviors that are driven by rewards with increased referral costs within the rewarding-both and the rewarding-referrers strategies.

While referrers’ referral behaviors in the three reward strategies may be driven by different motivational mechanisms, we conjecture that when referral costs (including action cost and interpersonal cost) are relatively low, all three reward strategies could be similarly effective in motivating referrers to make referrals. However, when referral costs increase, some reward strategies may become less effective than others. Below, we compare referrers’ referral likelihood across the reward strategies in terms of their relative effectiveness in conditions of relatively low and high referral costs.

We first discuss the situation where the action cost is relatively high. As the dual-market model suggests, under the rewardingboth and rewarding-referrers strategies, the likelihood of referrers making referrals primarily depends on a cost-benefit analysis—weighing the reward offered against the action cost needed to make the referrals. Given a certain level of reward, referrers’ referral likelihood would decrease as the action cost increases. In contrast, under the rewarding-recipients strategy, referrers’ referral behavior is largely driven by altruistic motivation (concern toward recipients’ welfare); thus, referrers referral likelihood would be influenced by both referrers altruistic motivation and the action cost involved. According to rational altruism theory, referrers’ altruistic motivation under the rewarding-recipients strategy diminishes when the action cost increases. Thus, referral likelihood under the rewardingrecipients strategy decreases, even to a greater extent, compared to that under the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies since altruistic motivation is reduced with an increased action cost. As such, with a high action cost, the decrease in referrers’ referral likelihood would be greater under the rewarding-recipients strategy than it would under the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies. Therefore, we propose that when the action cost of referrals is relatively high, the rewarding-recipients strategy will become less effective than the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies in motivating referrers to make referrals. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3a: The relative effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy on referrers’ referral likelihood, compared to the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies, will be reduced with a high action cost of making referrals.

Next, we discuss the situation where the interpersonal cost is relatively high. First, under the rewarding-referrers strategy, making referrals only rewards referrers. Thus, referrers may be concerned about their reputation, as recipients may think the referrals are made by referrers solely in the pursuit of their own benefits. The reputational concern may be particularly salient in the case of a relatively high interpersonal cost—for example, when the potential risk of a poor referral is relatively high (e.g., referring a poor or questionable product, say of a relatively low average review rating). Consequently, referrers’ reputational concern may lead them to be reluctant to make referrals. Note that reputational concern is the negative reputational consequence of making referrals, whereas reputational benefit is the positive reputational consequence.

In contrast, however, when the interpersonal cost is relatively high, the rewarding-recipients strategy could still motivate referrers to make referrals due to their altruistic motivation because the referrals would offer recipients an opportunity to receive a reward. Similarly, the rewarding-both strategy may also motivate referrers to make referrals; since recipients can receive a reward under this strategy, referrers’ reputational concern may be alleviated. Therefore, when the interpersonal cost of referrals is relatively high, the rewarding-recipients and rewarding-both strategies would likely be more effective than the rewarding-referrers strategy in motivating referrers to make referrals. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3b: The relative effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients and rewarding-both strategies on referrers’ referral likelihood, compared to the rewarding-referrers strategy, will be increased with a high interpersonal cost of making referrals.

## Empirical Overview

We conducted five experimental studies to examine whether, how, and when rewarding recipients is effective in motivating referrers to make referrals. Figure 1 illustrates our conceptual framework. Table 1 presents an overview of the five experimental studies. The details of the measurements, manipulations, and additional analyses of all the studies are provided in the transparency material.<sup>8</sup>

Study 1a examines the effectiveness of the different referral reward strategies in driving referral likelihood and the underlying motivational mechanisms. The results show that the primary mechanism driving referrers’ referral behavior under the rewarding-recipients strategy is altruistic motivation rather than alternative egoistic motivations (i.e., pursuing reputational or collective benefits). The results also demonstrate that referrers’ altruistic motivation is dampened under the rewarding-both strategy. Study 1b further examines altruistic motivation by manipulating the framing of the strategy that highlights different motivations. The results substantiate that altruistic motivation is the primary driver of the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy. Study 1c further examines the proposed dampening effect by manipulating the value of the reward offered to referrers and recipients. The results show that once referrers are rewarded, their referral likelihood depends on the reward they are offered rather than the reward offered to recipients. Studies 2a and 2b examine the moderating effect of action cost and interpersonal cost on the relative effectiveness of the reward strategy. The results show that referrers’ prosocial behaviors are inhibited as the action cost increases (Study 2a), but they persist when the interpersonal cost is relatively high (Study 2b).

## Study 1a

Study 1a aims to test the effectiveness of different reward strategies in driving referral behavior, and it explores whether referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy are primarily driven by altruistic motivation or alternative egoistic motivations and whether referrers’ altruistic motivation is dampened under the rewarding-both strategy.

## Study 1a Method

We randomly assigned 702 U.S.-based participants from a Prolific online panel (467 women, $M _ { a g e } = 3 9 . 4 5 )$ to conditions in a one-factor, four-level, between-subjects design (reward strategy: no-reward, rewarding-recipients, rewarding-referrers, and rewarding-both). Participants were informed that a company is promoting a mobile app dictionary, and we invited them to participate in its referral program. If they referred the mobile app dictionary to their friends and their friends downloaded and installed the mobile app dictionary, then (1) nobody (no-reward strategy), (2) their friends (rewardingrecipients strategy), (c) they (rewarding-referrers strategy), or (d) both they and their friends (rewarding-both strategy) would receive a \$5 Amazon gift card.

To enhance scenario realism, we used a real company selling a professional multilingual dictionary, and we introduced participants to the company and the mobile app dictionary (Appendix A). Participants in all conditions were told that they could refer the app dictionary to as many friends as they desired. After reading the referral scenario, participants were required to answer a comprehension question to check if they truly understood who would be rewarded in the referral program. If participants answered incorrectly, we prompted them to reread the program until they gave the correct answer. This comprehension check was used in all our studies. Next, participants were required to indicate the number of friends, if any, they would like to refer to the mobile app dictionary (i.e., 0, 1, 2, etc.). Then, they rated the following motivations for their referral behaviors (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree): altruistic motivation (e.g., making the referral would ultimately benefit my friend/make my friend happy/reflects my genuine concern for my friend’s welfare without expecting some sort of “payoff”; six items, $a \ = \ 0 . 9 0 ;$ Batson & Powell, 2003), reputational benefit (e.g., making the referral would make my friend think that I am a generous/helpful/friendly/wellintentioned/likable person; five items, α = 0.95; Gershon et al., 2020), and collective benefit (e.g., making the referral would benefit/increase the collective of me and my friend; two items, $\alpha = 0 . 8 7 \mathrm { ; }$ ; Tu et al., 2016).

## Study 1a Results

Referral likelihood: Among the participants who indicated that they would refer the app dictionary, the vast majority referred it to no more than two friends. Thus, we tested the effects of the reward strategies with a binary outcome: whether a participant chose to refer the app to any friend (1 = yes, 0 = no). We coded each of the strategies using a dummy variable as the independent variable. Logistic regressions showed that participants’ referral likelihood under the rewarding-recipients (74.29%), rewarding-referrers (75.28%), and rewarding-both (75.42%) strategies did not differ between each other (ps > 0.81), while all three referral likelihoods were significantly greater than that in the no-reward strategy (56.47%, ps < 0.001), thereby supporting H1a and H2a.

Motivational mechanisms: One-way ANOVA with reward strategy as the independent variable and altruistic motivation as the dependent variable revealed that the main effect was significant $( F ( 3 , 6 9 8 ) = 7 0 . 7 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Participants’ altruistic motivation under the rewarding-both strategy (M = 4.20) was higher than that under the rewarding-referrers strategy $( M = 3 . 2 5 , t ( 3 5 5 ) =$ $7 . 7 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and the no-reward strategy (M = 3.50, t(322.14) $= ~ 5 . 0 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 ) ;$ ; the latter two strategies were not significantly different from each other in this regard $\left( t ( 3 4 6 ) = - \right.$ 1.80, $p = 0 . 0 7 3 )$ . Specifically, participants expected to receive greater reputational benefit under the rewarding-recipients strategy $( M = 4 . 8 3 , S D = 1 . 4 2 )$ than under the rewarding-referrers $( M = 3 . 7 0 , S D = 1 . 3 7 , t ( 3 5 1 ) = 7 . 5 9 , p$ $< 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , rewarding-both (M = 4.06, SD = 1.35, t(352) = 5.22, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and no-reward $( M = 4 . 0 3 , S D = 1 . 5 0 , t ( 3 4 3 ) = 5 . 1 0 ,$ p < 0.001) strategies.

The main effect on collective benefit was also significant $( F ( 3 , 6 9 8 ) = 8 5 . 6 4 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Participants’ expected collective benefit under the rewarding-recipients strategy $( M = 3 . 5 7 , S D =$ 1.57) was significantly lower than that under the rewardingboth strategy $( M = 5 . 3 2 , S D = 1 . 3 7 , t ( 3 4 3 . 5 4 ) = - 1 1 . 1 3 , p <$ 0.001); it was not significantly different from that under the rewarding-referrers strategy (M = 3.60, SD = 1.37, t(351) = - 0.17, p = 0.86) and was significantly higher than that in the noreward strategy $( M = 3 . 0 3 , S D = 1 . 6 9 , t ( 3 4 3 ) = 3 . 3 7 , p = 0 . 0 0 1 )$

Mediation analyses: First, we conducted a mediation analysis with reward strategy (1 = rewarding-recipients vs. 0 = noreward) as the independent variable; altruistic motivation, reputational benefit, and collective benefit as the potential mediators (included in the model simultaneously); and the binary referral choice as the dependent variable, using Hayes’s (2017) PROCESS Model 4. The results reveal a significant effect of the reward strategy on altruistic motivation $( \beta = 0 . 7 7 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , reputational benefit $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and collective benefit $( \beta = 0 . 2 7 , p = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The results also show that the effects of altruistic motivation $( \beta = 0 . 7 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and collective benefit $( \beta = 0 . 4 0 , p = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) were significant, while the effect of reputational benefit $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 , p = 0 . 2 8 )$ was not significant. In addition, the direct effect of the reward strategy was not significant $( \beta = - 0 . 1 9 , p = 0 . 2 6 )$ . Bootstrapping with 5,000 resampling confirmed that the indirect effects of altruistic motivation ( = 0.56, 95% CI [0.2997 to 0.8709]) and collective benefit ( = 0.11, 95% CI [0.0364 to 0.2323]) were significant, whereas the indirect effect of reputational benefit $( \beta = 0 . 0 6 ,$ 95% CI [-0.0478 to 0.1849]) was not. These results suggest that the positive effect of the rewarding-recipients strategy on referral likelihood, compared to the no-reward strategy, is mediated by increased altruistic motivation and collective benefit, while reputational benefit does not mediate the effect.

Second, although the three different reward strategies led to similar main effects on referral likelihood, their main effects may have been driven through different routes (Hayes, 2009). On the one hand, rewarding referrers under the rewardingreferrers and rewarding-both strategies is likely to increase referral likelihood due to referrers’ egoistic motivations. On the other hand, rewarding recipients under the rewarding-recipients strategy is likely to increase referrers’ altruistic motivation and thus their referral likelihood. To further uncover the motivational mechanisms, we conducted a set of mediation analyses by comparing the rewarding-recipients strategy (coded as 1) with the rewarding-both strategy (coded as 0) or the rewarding-referrers strategy (coded as 0), using Hayes’s (2017) PROCESS Model 4. Similarly, we simultaneously included altruistic motivation, reputational benefit, and collective benefit as the potential mediators in the model.

Specifically, when comparing the rewarding-recipients to the rewarding-both strategy, the effect of the reward strategy on altruistic motivation (β = 0.83, p < 0.001), reputational benefit (β = 0.77, p < 0.001), and collective benefit (β = -1.75, p < 0.001) was significant in all cases. The effects of altruistic motivation (β = 0.72, p < 0.001) and collective benefit (β = 0.46, p < 0.001) on referral likelihood were significant, while the effect of reputational benefit (β = 0.25, p = 0.073) was not. Furthermore, the direct effect of the reward strategy was not significant (β = -0.03, p = 0.95). Bootstrapping with 5,000 resampling confirmed that the indirect effects of altruistic motivation ( = 0.60, 95% CI [0.2787 to 0.9824]) and collective benefit ( = -0.80, 95% CI [-1.3786 to -0.2961]) were significant, yet the indirect effect of reputational benefit ( = 0.20, 95% CI [-0.0210 to 0.4853]) was not.

Similarly, when comparing the rewarding-recipients strategy to the rewarding-referrers strategy, the effect of the reward strategy on altruistic motivation (β = 1.79, p < 0.001) and reputational benefit (β = 1.13, p < 0.001) was significant, while the effect of collective benefit (β = -0.03, p = 0.86) was not. Whereas the effect of altruistic motivation on referral likelihood was significant (β = 0.64, p < 0.001), the effect of reputational benefit (β = -0.03, p = 0.81) was not. The direct effect of the reward strategy was significant (β = -1.20, p = 0.002). Bootstrapping with 5,000 resampling again confirmed that only the indirect effect of altruistic motivation ( = 1.15, 95% CI [0.5859 to 1.7704]) was significant, whereas the indirect effects of reputational benefit ( = -0.036, 95% CI [- 0.3849 to 0.2556]) and collective benefit ( = -0.011, 95% CI [-0.1722 to 0.1171]) were not significant.

Together, these results reveal that altruistic motivation is a persistent and dominant motivational mechanism driving referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy, thus supporting H1b. The mediation results indicate that the rewarding-both strategy, compared to the rewardingrecipients strategy, suppresses altruistic motivation, thus offering empirical evidence supporting the “dampening effect” proposed in H2b.

## Study 1a Discussion

The results of Study 1a show that rewarding recipients motivated referrers to behave prosocially and make referrals under the rewarding-recipients strategy. However, rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy did not further increase referrers’ referral likelihood, compared to the rewarding-referrers strategy that only rewarded referrers. Furthermore, the results reveal that rewarding recipients boosted referrers’ altruistic motivation under the rewarding-recipients strategy, but rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy did not increase this motivation as much because referrers’ altruistic motivation appears to be dampened under the rewarding-both strategy. The mediation results indicate that the primary motivational mechanism driving referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy is altruistic motivation rather than either pursuing reputational or collective benefits.

We acknowledge that the empirical evidence on the motivational mechanisms relied on participants’ self-reports; thus, we cannot rule out the possibility of social desirability bias in the participants’ self-reported responses. In Study 1b, we aimed to obtain additional evidence on whether altruistic motivation is the primary motivation that drives referrers’ prosocial behavior under the rewarding-recipients strategy by manipulating the framing of the strategy to highlight different motivations. In addition, the results of Study 1a provide initial evidence that referrers’ altruistic motivation, which is potentially induced by rewarding recipients, is dampened under the rewarding-both strategy. Study 1c provides more direct empirical evidence about the proposed dampening mechanism.

## Study 1b

Self-perception theory suggests that one’s prosocial behavior remains under the control of altruistic motivation when it is not perceived to be under the control of egoistic motivations (Batson et al., 1987; Bem, 1972). If altruistic motivation is the primary driver, then the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy should be undermined when the framing of the strategy highlights egoistic motivations, such as pursuing reputational or collective benefits. Study 1b aims to test this proposition.

## Study 1b Method

We randomly assigned 298 U.S.-based participants from a Prolific panel (165 women, M<sub>age</sub> = 36.97) to conditions in a one-factor, three-level, between-subjects design (framing: altruistic, reputational-benefit, and collective-benefit). In Study 1b, we only used the rewarding-recipients strategy, while manipulating the slogan of the referral program to highlight different motivations as follows:

Altruistic slogan: Invite Your Friend, Send Reward to Her/Him!

Reputational-benefit slogan: Invite Your Friend, Show How Great a Person You Are!

Collective-benefit slogan: Invite Your Friend, Get Reward for Your Group!

Participants read the same referral scenario used in Study 1a and viewed one of the slogans. To capture the behavioral outcome of participants’ referral choice, we used a deception technique in this study where participants were led to believe that if they chose to refer the mobile app dictionary, they would need to provide their name, their friend’s name, and their friend’s email address on the next page, and that we would send referral invitations to their friends using the provided information. <sup>9</sup> Participants then answered the question, “Would you like to refer the mobile app dictionary to any friend?” (yes/no).

A pretest confirmed that participants (N = 52) indeed perceived that the altruistic slogan (M = 5.17, SD = 1.88) highlighted the point of “making the referral to increase your friend’s welfare” more than the reputational-benefit slogan (M = 3.21, SD = 1.84, t(51) = 5.51, p < 0.001) and the collective-benefit slogan (M = 4.33, SD = 1.62, t(51) = 2.54, p = 0.007). Participants perceived that the reputational-benefit slogan (M = 5.50, SD = 1.60) highlighted the point of “making the referral to present your positive image so as to increase reputation” more than the altruistic slogan (M = 3.19, SD = 1.72, t(51) = 7.53, p < 0.001) and the collective-benefit slogan (M = 3.71, SD = 1.75, t(51) = 5.23, p < 0.001). Participants perceived that the collectivebenefit slogan (M = 5.83, SD = 1.58) highlighted the point of “making the referral to increase the group welfare of your friend and you” more than the altruistic (M = 2.92, SD = 2.02, t(51) = 9.09, p < 0.001) and reputational-benefit slogan (M = 3.17, SD = 1.94, t(51) = 8.06, p < 0.001).

## Study 1b Results

Logistic regressions with reward strategy as the independent variable and the binary referral choice as the dependent variable showed that participants’ referral likelihood in the altruistic framing (45.54%) was significantly higher than that in the reputational-benefit framing (30.61%, χ<sup>2</sup>(1) = 4.65, p = 0.031) and the collective-benefit framing (32.32%, χ<sup>2</sup>(1) = 3.65, p = 0.056, marginally significant), while the latter two framings did not differ from each other (χ<sup>2</sup>(1) =.067, p = 0.796).

## Study 1b Discussion

The results of Study 1b support our proposition that highlighting egoistic motivations in the framing of the rewarding-recipients strategy reduces the effectiveness of the strategy by undermining referrers’ altruistic motivation. The findings of Study 1b again support our theorizing that the primary motivation that drives referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy is altruistic motivation (thereby further supporting H1b).

While Study 1a provides evidence for the proposed dampening effect under the rewarding-both strategy, more direct evidence is needed. If referrers’ altruistic motivation, induced by rewarding recipients, is dampened when referrers are also rewarded under the rewarding-both strategy (as shown in Study 1a), then it would be expected that referrers’ referral behaviors would be largely affected by the reward provided to referrers. Study 1c seeks to test this prediction.

## Study 1c

In Study 1c, we sought further evidence for the dampening effect by manipulating the value of the reward provided to referrers and recipients. According to the dual-market model, referrers, when offered no reward, are in a socialmarket relationship and exhibit altruistic motivation. Thus, their referral likelihood will depend on the reward offered to recipients. However, offering rewards to referrers may potentially lead them into an economic-incentive mindset that could dampen their altruistic motivation. As a result, we expect referrers’ referral likelihood to be affected by the value of the reward offered to them, regardless of the reward offered to recipients.

## Study 1c Method

We randomly assigned 510 U.S.-based participants from a Prolific panel (244 women, $M _ { a g e } = 3 6 . 4 9 )$ to conditions in a 3 (value of the reward given to referrers: no, small, big) × 3 (value of the reward given to recipients: no, small, big) between-subjects design. ## Study 1c Discussion

The results of Study 1c show that referrers’ referral likelihood increased with the value of the reward given to recipients when referrers were not rewarded, but once referrers were provided with a reward, they became self-interested and their referral behaviors became insensitive to the value of the reward given to recipients. The results imply that offering rewards to referrers could suppress their altruistic motivation, thereby providing consistent evidence for H2b.

Study 1a found that referrers’ altruistic motivation increased substantially under the rewarding-recipients strategy but increased to a much lesser extent under the rewarding-both strategy, in which referrers were also rewarded. This suggests that rewarding referrers dampened their altruistic motivation, which would otherwise be expected because recipients received a reward under the rewarding-both strategy. Study 1a presents process evidence that altruistic motivation is the primary and persistent mechanism driving referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy, rather than the pursuit of reputational or collective benefits. Study 1b provides further evidence for the primary motivational mechanism of altruistic motivation by showing that the framings highlighting egoistic motivations, compared to an altruistic framing, undermined the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy. Study 1c presents additional evidence for the dampening effect by showing that once referrers are rewarded, their referral likelihood largely depends on the reward offered to them rather than the reward offered to recipients. Next, we examine how the action cost and interpersonal cost of the referrals moderate referrers’ prosocial behaviors, thus incorporating additional parameters on the relative effectiveness of the reward strategies.

## Study 2b Discussion

The findings of Study 2b show that referrers’ referral likelihood generally decreased with a relatively higher interpersonal cost, but referrers’ altruistic tendencies under the rewarding-recipients strategy persisted regardless of whether the interpersonal cost was relatively lower or higher. The results further show that when the interpersonal cost was relatively high, the rewarding-recipients and the rewardingboth strategies led to greater referral likelihood than the rewarding-referrers strategy. This relative effectiveness was driven by rewarding recipients (under the rewardingrecipients and the rewarding-both strategies), which enhanced referrers’ altruistic motivation and reduced referrers reputational concern. Thus, H3b is supported.

In sum, the findings of Studies 2a and 2b show that the action cost and interpersonal cost of making referrals are important factors that affected referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy and, accordingly, the relative effectiveness of the reward strategies. Importantly, the results show that the effects of action and interpersonal costs are asymmetrical in the sense that referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy were inhibited as the action cost increased, but they persisted even when the interpersonal cost was relatively high.

## General Discussion

## Theoretical Contributions and Implications

First, our research contributes to the literature on online referrals and referral incentive schemes. An emerging literature focuses on understanding the potential of rewarding recipients (versus rewarding referrers, which earlier literature has primarily focused on). For instance, Jung et al. (2021) provided evidence that rewarding recipients under the rewarding-recipients and rewarding-both strategies could outperform the rewardingreferrers strategy in terms of yielding product adoptions, but it is unclear whether rewarding recipients could influence referrers’ referral likelihood. In contrast, our research focuses on exploring whether and how rewarding recipients could induce referrers to make referrals. Our results reveal that referrers’ altruistic motivations and the dampening effect are primary underlying mechanisms that explain the effectiveness of rewarding recipients. We also demonstrate how the action cost and interpersonal cost moderate the relative effectiveness of rewarding recipients in an asymmetrical manner.

Second, although Gershon et al. (2020) hinted at the potential of rewarding recipients only, they overlooked the effect of rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy. Recognizing that both the rewarding-recipients and rewarding-both strategies reward recipients, we focus on understanding whether, when, and why rewarding recipients would be effective in motivating referrers to make referrals. While Gershon et al. examined the egoistic motivational mechanism of seeking reputational benefit, our research uncovers a different, yet overlooked, motivational mechanism—that is, referrers’ altruistic motivation and the dampening effect. We offer a unified account to explain (1) why rewarding recipients under the rewarding-recipients strategy could be effective (due to referrers’ altruistic motivation) and (2) why rewarding recipients under the rewarding-both strategy does not lead to an additional boost in the referral likelihood (due to the dampening effect), compared to the rewarding-referrers strategy. Finally, Gershon et al. did not examine the interpersonal cost of referrals, whereas our research explores both action and interpersonal costs; interestingly, we find that the moderating effects of these two types of referral costs are empirically asymmetrical.

Third, our research contributes to the literature on prosocial motivations by providing evidence in the context of online referral programs. Our research shows that it is altruistic motivation, rather than the promise of reputational or collective benefits, that plays a primary and persistent role in motivating referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewardingrecipients strategy. Pursuing reputational or collective benefits is not the primary motivation of referrers’ prosocial behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy, likely because online referral programs are a commercial context where referrers do not necessarily seek to enhance their reputational or collective benefits. Our study also shows that once referrers are rewarded, as under the rewarding-both strategy, referrers’ altruistic motivation, which is potentially induced by rewarding recipients, is suppressed. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first to examine referrers’ prosocial tendencies from the perspective of the relationship between altruistic motivation and rewards. Our research builds upon and extends the dual-market model (Heyman & Ariely, 2004) (monetary, social, mixed) to explain consumer behavior by comparing the effort that consumers devote to a certain task when they are provided with monetary (e.g., \$5), symbolic (e.g., candy), or monetized-symbolic (e.g., candy that is worth \$5) rewards. While Heyman and Ariely found that reward types (monetary vs. symbolic vs. monetizedsymbolic) can prime a given market relationship, we advance the dual-market model by showing that the different reward strategies (rewarding-referrers vs. rewarding-recipients vs. rewarding-both) can also influence which mindset market— monetary versus social—consumers enter.

Finally, our research provides new insights into the relationship between cost and altruistic tendencies in the context of online referrals. We are the first to show the asymmetrical effects of the action cost and interpersonal cost of referrals in moderating referrers’ prosocial behaviors. Specifically, we find that referrers’ referral behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy are inhibited by a relatively high action cost, yet their referral behaviors persist even when facing a relatively high interpersonal cost. Our research contributes to rational altruism theory (Andreoni & Miller, 2002) by providing new evidence suggesting that prosocial behaviors are more sensitive to the action cost than to the interpersonal cost of behaviors, at least in the context of online referral programs. More broadly, by integrating perspectives of the dual-market model (Heyman & Ariely, 2004) and rational altruism theory (Andreoni & Miller, 2002), our work advances the theorizing on the (de)motivating effects of monetary rewards by identifying the distinct and nuanced roles of popular reward strategies in promoting individuals’ behaviors that may have undesirable downsides for them (such as the expenditure of time and effort) or for their friends (such as dissatisfied experience with a product).

## Managerial Implications

Our research offers important managerial implications for online retailers in terms of when the rewarding-recipients strategy (rewards recipients only) may be effective. First, when both the action cost and interpersonal cost are relatively low, the rewarding-recipients strategy, the rewarding-referrers strategy, and the rewarding-both strategy are similarly effective in motivating referrers to make referrals. The rewarding-recipients strategy provides recipients with rewards and is thus more likely to lead recipients to accept referrals, compared to the rewardingreferrers strategy. Therefore, while both generate a similar referral likelihood for referrers, the rewarding-recipients strategy is likely to generate more accepted referrals than the rewardingreferrers strategy. Comparing the rewarding-recipients strategy and the rewarding-both strategies, although the two strategies may have a similar referral likelihood for referrers and a similar acceptance likelihood for recipients, the rewarding-recipients strategy may be the most cost-effective referral strategy, given that the associated marketing expenditure is only about half that of the rewarding-both strategy.

Second, our findings show that when the action cost is relatively high, the rewarding-recipients strategy becomes the least effective referral strategy. However, when the interpersonal cost is relatively high, the rewarding-recipients strategy becomes more effective than the rewarding-referrers strategy, although not as effective as the rewarding-both strategy. Nonetheless, rewarding recipients only might still be the most cost-effective referral strategy, again considering that the associated marketing expenditure is only about half that of the rewarding-both strategy.

Third, given that consumers under the rewarding-recipients strategy appear to be more sensitive to the action cost than the interpersonal cost of referral behaviors, online retailers should first consider the action cost of referral behaviors. To maximize the relative effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy, online retailers should reduce the action cost incurred at the referral stage. For example, online retailers should minimize the steps and simplify the procedure of sending out referral messages. Online retailers should also consider designing user-friendly interfaces for consumers to participate in online referral programs. In addition, if online retailers are uncertain about whether the product referred can satisfy recipients’ needs, the rewardingrecipients strategy would likely be preferable to the other reward strategies, as long as the action cost is not overly high.

Finally, understanding the motivational mechanisms behind referrers’ prosocial behaviors sheds light on designing appropriate campaigns for the rewarding-recipients strategy. When retailers design referral programs, they often explicitly highlight the egoistic benefits that consumers can gain to motivate them to participate in online referral programs (Jung et al., 2020). However, since referrers’ referral behaviors under the rewarding-recipients strategy are primarily driven by altruistic motivation, framings that highlight egoistic benefits may divert referrers away from altruistic actions, thus decreasing their referral likelihood. Online retailers could enhance the effectiveness of the rewarding-recipients strategy by conveying the social norms of helping and altruism in their referral campaigns. Retailers should also be cautious with campaign framings that stress referrers’ egoistic benefits.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our research has a few limitations, which point toward several further research directions. First, our research focuses on examining the effectiveness of rewarding recipients in online referrals, largely because online referrals are becoming increasingly popular in practice, and there is a growing literature that focuses on exploring the different reward strategies in online referrals. The extent to which the findings of our research can be generalized to referrals in offline settings is an interesting question for future research. Second, the strength of social ties between referrers and recipients is an interesting factor that may moderate the effects of rewarding recipients on referrers’ referral likelihood. Future research could investigate how tie strength moderates the relative effectiveness of rewarding recipients in real-life field settings. Third, while Study 1b highlighted that the reputational benefit in the framing of the rewarding-recipients strategy undermines referrers’ altruistic motivation, referrers may also be concerned that recipients might perceive their referrals as solely intended to project a good image, and this could potentially reduce referrers’ willingness to make referrals. We cannot rule out this possibility and acknowledge this as a limitation of Study 1b. Future research could aim to rule out this alternative explanation.

## Concluding Remarks

While the academic literature and industry practice predominantly favor the strategies of rewarding referrers in online referral programs (i.e., the rewarding-both and rewarding-referrers strategies), in this research we show that, in most cases, the rewarding-recipients (only) strategy can be at least as effective as the rewarding-both strategy in motivating referrers to make referrals. However, the rewarding-recipients strategy only requires half of the marketing expenditure for online retailers. Notably, the rewarding-recipients strategy could also be superior to the rewarding-referrers strategy, especially when the interpersonal cost of referrals is relatively high. We seek to inform the academic literature and industry practice on how to leverage the approach of rewarding recipients in designing effective prosocial reward strategies.
