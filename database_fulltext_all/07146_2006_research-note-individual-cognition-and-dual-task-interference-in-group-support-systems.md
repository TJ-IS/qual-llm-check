# Individual Cognition and Dual-Task Interference in Group Support Systems

This study examined one poten tial cause for this failure: Dual-task interference caused by the need to concurrently process new information from others while also contributing one’s own information to the discussion. Although prior research argues that dual-task interference should be minimal, we found that it significantly reduced participants’ information processing and led to lower decision quality. ## Introduction

Most prior group support systems (GSS) research has focused on applying social psychology theories to computer-mediated decision making. One consistent result of this research is that although GSS enables a more complete exchange of information, there is often a failure to process the new information received, which can result in poor decisions (Dennis 1996, Hilmer and Dennis 2001).

We propose that the root of this failure lies not in social processes, but in the deeper processes of individual cognition (Nagasundaram and Dennis 1993, Robert and Dennis 2005, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998). In this study, we examine one possible cause for this failure: dual-task interference. Dual-task interference may occur when team participants perform two tasks simultaneously: (1) contributing to the discussion and (2) processing new information they receive from the discussion. This interference may induce participants to fail to process the new information because they are engaged in contributing their own information (Pinsonneault et al. 1999, Paulus 2000). Because dualtask interference affects individual cognition, our unit of analysis is the individual within the team. Therefore, we focus on individual cognition and decision making within a team that is working on an informationbased decision-making task.

## Dual-Task Interference

Dual-task interference is a well-known, well-studied phenomenon (Pashler 1994; Meyer and Kieras 1997a, b; Shaft and Vessey 2006). Research on dualtask interference has focused primarily on elementary tasks (e.g., extremely simple, one-step tasks such as pressing buttons in response to seeing different lights or hearing different sounds that occur near simultaneously) but can also be considered in complex, nonelementary tasks (Pashler 1994).

There are several theoretical underpinnings for dual-task interference, but the most common is that individuals have a limited cognitive capacity that can be shared among tasks. This limited amount of attention creates a “bottleneck” when an individual attempts to perform two tasks at the same time, which can result in poor performance of one or both tasks (Pashler 1994, Rithruff et al. 2003, Shaft and Vessey 2006, Tombu and Jolicoeur 2002).

Some tasks can be performed pseudosimultaneously because one task (often called a secondary task) can be performed with only limited cognitive attention, while the other task (often called the primary task) receives the lion’s share of the available capacity (Pashler 1994; Meyer and Kieras 1997a, b; Sarno and Wickens 1995; Wickens and Seidler 1997). If the secondary task suddenly needs attention, then the primary task can be put on hold while cognitive resources are switched to the secondary task. For example, most people can carry on a conversation while driving because for most experienced drivers, driving can be done with minimal cognitive effort, enabling attention to be focused on the conversation. If a situation arises that requires the driver to focus attention on driving (e.g., an animal runs into the road), the driver can stop talking (or listening) and focus on driving.

Thus, individuals can choose to allocate their attention to one task or the other in a sequential manner (or in a near-simultaneous manner) that effectively enables both tasks to be performed in a given period (Garcia-Larrea et al. 2001; Gopher et al. 1982; Hancock et al. 2003; Meyer and Kieras 1997a, b; Sarno and Wickens 1995). However, individuals can hold only so much information in working memory, so as the demands from the two tasks increase beyond some point, dual-task interference will occur. At this point, individuals often will attempt to use cognitive simplification strategies (Beach and Mitchell 1978, Streufert 1973) that often result in decreased performance (Chewning and Harrell 1990, Johnson and Payne 1995, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998,

Speier and Morris 2003). Individuals typically are not aware of interference unless the tasks are physically incompatible—e.g., typing and driving—or cognitively demanding—e.g., talking and adding a check in a restaurant (Pashler 1994).

## Dual-Task Interference Using Group Support Systems

Dual-task interference has long been recognized as a problem in verbal discussions, although the term “dual-task interference” has not been used to refer to it (e.g., Diehl and Stroebe 1987, Jablin and Seibold 1978, Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973, Nunamaker et al. 1991, Pinsonneault et al. 1999). The term “attention blocking” has been used by some authors (Nunamaker et al. 1991, Lam and Schaubroeck 2000).

One potential advantage theorized for GSS over verbal discussion is that it may reduce dual-task interference. GSS enables participants to turn what would be two simultaneous tasks in verbal discussion (contributing and processing information) into two consecutive (but near-simultaneous) tasks because participants can briefly ignore the discussion to think about an issue or type their contributions, and then rejoin and contribute to the discussion without missing anything because all discussion is recorded and viewable on-demand. This enables participants to selectively focus their attention on contributing information or processing information from others without missing anything (Dennis 1996, Dhaliwal and Tung 2000, Nunamaker et al. 1991, Satzinger et al. 1999, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998). In theory, there should be little dual-task interference in GSS (Nunamaker et al. 1991, Pinsonneault et al. 1999, Robert and Dennis 2005).

However, empirical studies have found that information processing in GSS is still impaired (Dennis 1996, Dennis et al. 1997/1998, Hightower and Sayeed 1996, Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Straus 1996). Contrary to prior theory (including our own works), we argue in this paper that dual-task interference remains an important process loss in GSS and that it acts to impair information processing. Indeed, the need to write or type comments in a GSS does not come as naturally and reflexively as speaking does in a face-to-face meeting, which we argue would decrease an individual’s ability to process and contribute new information.<sup>1</sup> Simply put, participants engaged in GSS attempt to focus on two activities (contributing information and processing information from others), and this creates dual-task interference, which acts to impair the performance of both tasks.

In this study, we focus on the impact of dual-task interference on information processing. Individual information processing, or cognition, in decisionmaking teams can be considered a two-step process (Driver and Streufert 1969, Gouran 1982, Guzzo 1982, Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Patton et al. 1989).

Once others have provided information, the first step is for participants to attend to the information— that is, direct their cognitive focus to the new information. When dual-task interference occurs, it would be easy for participants to ignore information because, unlike verbal discussion, no one actively directs their attention to it (cf. Harkins and Petty 1987, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998). Important information is present, but participants do not attend to it.

The second step of information processing is to evaluate the information, determine its relevancy, and then integrate it into one’s decision framework. Empirical evidence has found that unique new information received during discussions is likely to be ignored, both in verbal discussions (e.g., Stasser et al. 1989) and in GSS (e.g., Dennis 1996). During discussions, participants may not actively process the information they attend to because of the dual-task interference introduced by the need to contribute information. They neglect to process new information because they devote attention to developing their own contributions (Lamm and Trommsdorff 1973). Thus, dual-task interference causes unique information first received by participants during GSS to be processed only superficially and not integrated as fully as information studied before the discussion began (Dennis 1996).

The key question is, then, does the need to contribute to discussions induce dual-task interference that impairs an individual’s ability to process information received from others? We argue that the need to interact introduces dual-task interference. Without the need to actively participate, a participant could focus all of his or her cognitive resources on information processing. Once he or she is expected to participate in a discussion, his or her cognitive resources must be split between two tasks (processing the available information and interacting with other participants), thus reducing resources for information processing (Anderson 1983, Dabbish and Kraut 2003, Treisman and Davies 1973). Therefore, we hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1. Participants interacting in a GSS discussion will experience dual-task interference that will lead them to process less information than participants only monitoring a GSS discussion.

## Impacts on Decision Making

For an individual team member to make a decision, he or she must identify and evaluate information about various decision alternatives (Burnstein and Vinokur 1973, Kleindorfer et al. 1993). In cases where individual team members do not have all relevant and important information prior to team discussion (often called a hidden profile task: Stasser and Stewart 1992), the sharing and consideration of information from other team members is critical to making the right decision (Dennis 1996, Stasser and Stewart 1992, Thompson 1991). Unless this “new” information is attended to and integrated into an individual’s decision framework, he or she is more likely to make a poor decision because this information is needed in the evaluation of alternatives (Dennis 1996, Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Stasser and Stewart 1992).

As we argued above, dual-task interference will reduce the processing of this new information. Because information processing—critical to decisionmaking performance in hidden profile tasks—is reduced, individuals are less likely to make good decisions. Therefore:

Hypothesis 2. Participants who experience dual-task interference by interacting in a GSS discussion will be less likely to make an optimal decision than participants only monitoring a GSS discussion.

## Methods

The unit of analysis was the individual participant, not the team as a whole. We could have conducted this study by having participants interact with each other in the traditional manner for team experiments and then measure the data at the individual level. However, this could introduce greater error variance into the process, because each GSS discussion is likely to have a different trajectory, with different information entering the discussion at different times.<sup>2</sup>

Therefore, a simulator was used (see Garfield et al. 2001, Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Satzinger et al. 1999). The simulator behaved essentially like a series of automated confederates (cf. Connolly et al. 1990). The simulator was a software tool that looked and acted like a regular GSS tool such as Instant Messenger (IM). Participants could type comments into the system, but instead of sharing those comments with others, the simulator presented comments that appeared to be from other participants but were in fact drawn from a previously prepared script. The script contained the task information and discussion about the information, as well as nontask comments typically found in GSS discussions, such as “Are we done yet?” The script used comments from real teams who performed the same task in a previous study (Dennis et al. 1997/1998). To increase the realism of the experimental session, groups of 10 to 15 participants were randomly assigned into one of two adjoining rooms. Participants were placed apart so that they could not read other screens. To not bias the outcomes, participants were not informed they were using a simulator until after the experiment.

We conducted two treatments, one with dual-task interference and one without. In the no dual-task interference treatment, participants read the information supplied by “other participants” before making a decision. In the other treatment, dual-task interference was introduced by instructing the participants to share the information they learned from the presession information sheet in addition to reading the information supplied by “other participants.” Thus, participants were asked to do two things at once (Meyer and Kieras 1997a, b; Pashler 1994; Shaft and

Vessey 2006). By using the simulator to deliver identical scripts to both treatments, we could control the information load: Both treatments received the same comments from the simulator in the same order and at the same time intervals.

## Participants

A total of 102 participants completed the study. Participants were undergraduate business students drawn from a large core course who received course credit for participating. The average age was 20.1 years. Approximately 51% were female. Participants were randomly assigned into one of two treatments, with 49 and 53 participants, respectively.

## Task

We used a hidden profile student admissions task developed by Dennis (1996), which asked participants to select one of four (fictitious) candidates for admission to the university. We chose this task because it was easily understood by participants as they had all been through the admissions process. We adapted the facts in the task to our university’s current admissions process with the help of the university admissions office. The revised task had 16 different categories or attributes of admission information collected about each candidate such as GPA, SAT scores, advanced placement courses taken, etc. We validated the task with the university’s Director of Admissions to ensure that, with complete information, the candidate designed to be the best choice (Candidate 3) was indeed the best choice.

With a hidden profile task, participants first receive information that leads them to choose a suboptimal choice, and only by processing information received from other team members can they identify the optimal choice. The prediscussion information was designed to influence participants to choose Candidate 1. We validated the task through several rounds of pilot tests with a total set of 60 students drawn from the same subject pool as those in the experiment (but these 60 students did not participate in the final experiment).

## Treatments

There were two treatments. Each treatment presented identical comments through the simulator, but the way in which participants interacted with the simulator was different. In both treatments, participants were first given a sheet of paper with incomplete information about each candidate, and were informed that this information was incomplete. After participants had read this information, the information sheets were collected and participants were asked to select the best candidate for admission to the university based on their incomplete information.<sup>3</sup> Next, one of the two treatments was administered.

No Dual-Task Interference. This treatment was designed to have no dual-task interference by asking participants to watch the replay of a team discussion. Participants were told that they would read the comments from a previous 15-minute GSS discussion about the four candidates, and then be asked to make another individual decision. They were reminded that their initial decision had been based on incomplete information and were instructed that to make a good decision, it was important that they read the information in the discussion. After 15 minutes, the discussion ended, and participants were asked to select the best candidate based on all the information they now had. They were told that their decision could be the same as or different from their original selection.

In this treatment, the simulator gave participants information about the four candidates at varying time intervals in the way in which they would appear in a real GSS discussion. Once displayed, a comment remained accessible on the screen so that participants could view all currently available information by scrolling up and down through the comments. Participants received a total of 61 comments (with an average length of 19.1 words) during the 15-minute treatment, or an average of about 4 comments per minute—typical of GSS discussions (e.g., see Tyran et al. 1992). Participants did not type any comments.

Dual-Task Interference. This treatment was designed to introduce dual-task interference by placing the participant in what appeared to be a GSS discussion. Participants were informed that they would participate in a 15-minute GSS discussion with other participants and then make another individual decision. This treatment was identical to the no dual-task interference treatment, except that the instructions asked participants to contribute information. This treatment acted like a “real” GSS tool. Participants typed comments in a text entry area at the bottom of the screen.<sup>4</sup> An area at the top of the screen displayed the discussion comments “from other participants” and the comments typed by the participant (so that these comments appeared to be available to “other participants”—but were never actually shared with other participants).

## Measures

Dual-task interference has not been measured directly because attempts to directly measure it without biometric instrumentation would distort the phenomenon. Instead, it is measured by a change in performance in one task due to the imposition of a second task. Thus, dual-task interference is seen in the measurement units of the empirical performance data of the first task after the second is introduced. Previous studies have measured dual-task interference by the increase in time taken to press a button in response to a flashing light after a second task was introduced (e.g., the need to press a different button in response to a noise), or the increase in the number of driving errors committed when a second task is introduced (e.g., carry on a cell phone conversation) (Meyer and Kieras 1997a, b; Pashler 1994; Shaft and Vessey 2006). In this study, we examine the decrease in information processing and decisionmaking quality caused by the introduction of a second task (the need to contribute information to a discussion). Therefore, we examine the measurement units of the first task (information processing and decision quality) for changes after a second task (contributing information) is introduced.

Information Processing. Information processing was measured by the information recalled by each participant on a postsession questionnaire (cf. Ariely 2000, Dennis 1996); participants were asked to list all task information they remembered. While not a perfect measure of information processing, this is a widely used proxy because all information recalled must have been processed, although not all information processed is recalled (Cacioppo et al. 1983, Stasser and Titus 1987).

Two independent raters counted the number of pieces of information remembered correctly. Interrater reliability was acceptable (79%). This information was classified as either known information recalled (i.e., information contained in the initial, prediscussion task information sheet given to participants prior to using the simulator) or new information recalled (i.e., new information only received from the simulator). The known information recalled was used to check whether participants in both treatments had similar recall abilities. The new information recalled measured the extent to which the information received during the treatment was affected by dualtask interference.

Decision Quality. Decision quality was coded as a zero-one variable. Participants choosing the correct alternative (Candidate 3) on the postsession questionnaire received a one, otherwise they a received a zero.

## Procedures

Procedures followed the general approach of Dennis (1996). Participants reported to the experimental session and were randomly assigned into one of two treatments. Participants first completed a background questionnaire (giving their gender and age) and signed an experimental consent form. They then received a task information sheet that provided the prediscussion (incomplete) information and were asked to make an initial individual decision.

Participants were trained in the use of the software and then participated in the experimental treatment (either dual-task interference or no dual-task interference). Participants then completed a postsession questionnaire that asked them to make their final decision and recall the information discussed. Finally, participants were debriefed and released.

## Discussion

Prior research has shown that although GSS may increase the information available to participants, they often do not incorporate this new information into their decision making. This study suggests that one possible cause of this problem is dual-task interference. Participants in the dual-task interference treatment (i.e., those required to both read the comments of others and contribute their own comments) failed to process as much new information as did participants in the no dual-task interference treatment (i.e., those required to only read). Participants in the no dual-task interference treatment recalled about 50% more new information (8.11 pieces of information versus 5.26). Participants who were asked to provide their own information to the discussion failed to effectively process the new information they received from the discussion. This decreased information processing was associated with worse decisions: Only 35% of participants in the dual-task interference treatment made the correct decision compared to 62% in the no dual-task interference treatment. We conclude that the dual-task interference from the need to both process information from others and contribute information to the discussion is one important factor that may explain why individuals in GSS discussions often make poor decisions.

Dual-task interference is often ascribed to a “bottleneck” caused by the need to spread an individual’s limited attention resources across simultaneous tasks (Pashler 1994, Rithruff et al. 2003). Prior research has argued that GSS should reduce potential dualtask interference caused by the need to both process new information and contribute to the discussion because it enables participants to disengage from the discussion, pause to think, and reengage in the discussion with little effort (Dhaliwal and Tung 2000, Nunamaker et al. 1991, Satzinger et al. 1999, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998). Our results show that this is not the case: Dual-task interference does exist in GSS.

We see the most plausible explanation as coming from a cognitive perspective. Managing the flow of information in discussions is important (Ariely 2000), and managing information flow in GSS discussions can be especially difficult (Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998) because the ability to simultaneously contribute information often leads to a multiway free-for-all, which makes it difficult for participants to direct and focus the discussion (Herring 1999). Thus, scarce cognitive resources may be diverted away from information processing and allocated to managing and contributing to the discussion. If this is the case, then better-designed GSS tools that enable participants to more easily control and organize information flow might reduce dual-task interference (cf. Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Tombu and Jolicoeur 2002).

Dual-task interference is likely to occur only when there are moderate or high demands for cognitive attention. Thus, dual-task interference should become more acute with more complex information or when the volume of information approaches an overload level, because in these situations an individual’s cognitive capabilities are sorely tested (Grise and Gallupe 1999/2000, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998, Streufert and Streufert 1978). If information is very simple or if there is very little information, then the additional cognitive demands from needing to participate in a GSS should pose little interference—the individual would have sufficient cognitive resources available for both discussion participation and information processing with little impairment in performance.

In this study, we used a task with information we considered to be neither simple nor complex and provided information at a rate we considered to be neither very low nor at overload levels. Nonetheless, the participants’ information processing and decisionmaking performance were significantly impaired by the need to participate in the discussion. If this phenomenon is observed in the research laboratory with a modest task, then we speculate that it also occurs in organizational environments that have potentially more complex information competing for attention—especially when synchronous GSS tools such as IM are often used pseudosimultaneously with other work.

## Implications for Future Research

To date, much research on GSS and computer-mediated decision making has focused on social psychology, pursuing the social issues around the development and use of new collaborative technologies. This research is important. However, our results suggest one key explanation for why teams that use GSS fail to perform well lies not in the social processes, but in the deeper processes of individual cognition. The effect was large with about a 50% difference in informationprocessing performance and in decision-making performance.

We believe that the first and most important implication for future research is the need for more— much more—research on the cognitive foundations of computer-mediated decision making (cf. Nagasundaram and Dennis 1993) and the increasing importance of the sociocognitive lens such as the collective mind (Weick and Roberts 1993) and transactive memory (Wegner 1987). Social psychology is important, but we need to emphasize cognitive psychology and the ways in which individual cognition can be affected (positively and negatively) by software tools and their uses.

Our study was conducted with the use of a simulator. The use of confederates with prepared scripts and simulators (effectively automated confederates) has a long history in the study of GSS discussions (e.g., Connolly et al. 1990, Garfield et al. 2001, Hilmer and Dennis 2001, Satzinger et al. 1999) and in management and psychology research in general. The precision provided by prescripted comments enables highly controlled investigations but may lack the natural spontaneity of normal team discussions and limit the duration of the task due to the risk of discovery. Simulators may also have limited applicability in the study of established teams whose members are sufficiently familiar with each other that they could realize that they were interacting with a simulator instead of their team members. Past research suggests that few subjects detect the use of simulators, even when prompted (Garfield et al. 2001), but nonetheless the use of simulators remains a limitation of this study. Future research is needed to examine the dual-task interference phenomenon in real teams.

Research is needed to understand the fundamental source of dual-task interference. Is it due to the inherent difficulty of managing information flow in GSS, the importation of social norms from verbal discussion that constrain participants from taking advantage of synchronous discussion tools’ capabilities, or some other factor(s)?

Research also is needed to develop new GSS tools and new work processes to mitigate the impact of dual-task interference (cf. Grise and Gallupe 1999/

2000). Simple interventions such as a pause to think about the issues may prove useful, or perhaps a structured process in which participants first engage only in monologues, posting information, followed by a period devoted to thinking about and processing information, only then followed by an opportunity for dialogue.

New discussion tools also could be developed to induce participants to attend to all new information by encouraging them to deliberately sort incoming information into important and unimportant folders (Hilmer and Dennis 2001). If tools could be developed to help participants mitigate some of the potential information overload experienced in GSS (Grise and Gallupe 1999/2000, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998), this might free up more cognitive resources and lessen the effects of dual-task interference.

Dual-task interference may be amplified by GSS because all communication is written and more information can be exchanged (leading to overload). Williams and Wilson (1997) found that those who believed they possessed good written communication skills felt that they could make stronger and better arguments through GSS than through verbal discussion. Likewise, those who perceived themselves as possessing poor written communication skills felt that GSS was not an effective communication tool, and thus they may be less engaged in GSS discussions. In addition, task complexity or uncertainty of the task may induce dual-task interference. Thus, further research is needed in how written communication skills and task characteristics such as complexity and uncertainty affect dual-task interference in GSS.

## Implications for Practice

In a day of around-the-clock projects with virtual teams comprised of professionals at home and abroad, we believe that the findings of this study can also benefit the use of GSS-based synchronous text discussion in practice. Such discussions are a key part of IM conversations, both in the workplace and personal lives of millions of IM users. We found that dualtask interference from the need to respond to others reduced both information-processing and decisionmaking quality. One possible solution would be to remind users that it is simple (and appropriate behavior) to disengage from the discussion to stop and think about the issues.

Another suggestion would be to introduce formal norms or procedures for GSS discussions that incorporate agenda items such as explicit times for thinking and responding. One example might be to promote asynchronous discussion prior to synchronous discussion (either using GSS or verbal communication). Another example would be to devise agendas for synchronous meetings with formal stages for information sharing, thoughtful contemplation, and general discussion prior to making decisions.

In the past, we have looked to social psychology to better understand team processes and outcomes in computer-mediated environments. We believe that our results call for creative research built on cognitive psychology that examines individuals within teams to better understand team processes and outcomes. Improving social interactions is important, but improving cognitive processing offers an entirely new dimension by which information technology can improve GSS in ways that may not be possible for verbal discussion. Perhaps new team technologies can change not only the way in which participants interact with each other, but also the ways in which participants think.
