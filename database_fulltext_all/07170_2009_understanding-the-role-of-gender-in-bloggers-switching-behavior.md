## a b s t r a c t

Blog has become an increasingly popular form of social networking technology in recent years. Many world famous web sites (e.g., Microsoft, Yahoo!, and Google) are providing blog services on the Internet. Bloggers, therefore, can easily establish and maintain their blogs through such services. However, research on blogs adoption and diffusion is rare. In this study, we attempt to study bloggers' post-adoption behaviors. We examine bloggers' intention to switch their blog services by building upon studies in the marketing and IS literature, Particularly the role of gender is explored and an online survey is conducted to test the research model. Findings con<sup>fi</sup>rm that bloggers' intention to switch their blog services is strongly associated with three factors: satisfaction, sunk costs, and attractive alternatives. Meanwhile, moderating effects are found in gender, but not in sunk costs. Implications for both researchers and practitioners are provided.

## 1. Introduction

Blogs are web pages that allow owners to publish their content online in a reverse chronological order [29]. Appearing in the mid-1990s, it has become an increasingly popular form of social networking technology in recent years [60]. Bloggers can use their blogs to present themselves and interact with others [32]. According to Pew Internet & American Life Project, 55% of online teens use social networking sites [43], and more than 8% of Internet users are bloggers in the US [42]. As a widely recognized blog tracking site, Technorati claims that it is tracking 112.8 million blogs on the Internet [62] and the number is growing fast. Many world famous web sites, such as Microsoft, Yahoo!, and Google, are providing blog services. Bloggers, therefore, can easily establish and maintain their blogs through these blog services [31]. For example, Google's Blogger.com is one of the most popular sites providing blog service [42]. A user can create a blog and post the <sup>fi</sup>rst entry within minutes. Also, it is very easy to pick from various design templates, to decorate the blog with a drag-and-drop layout system, and to control visitors' permissions to read or comment on the blog [47].

Compared with blog's rapid development, research on its adoption and diffusion is rare. Blog researchers in Information Systems (IS) have only recently begun to pay their attention on blog acceptance. For instance, Ma et al. [48] built upon the Uni<sup>fi</sup>ed Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model of Venkatesh et al. [66] to examine bloggers' acceptance of blogs; Hsu and Lin [31] integrated factors from the literature of technology acceptance, knowledge sharing, and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence to study the motivations of participating in blog activities. It has been suggested that adoption and acceptance of technologies is an important domain in IS <sup>fi</sup>eld [1,64]. Considerable attention has been paid, and many researchers have devoted to this area [10]. In recent years, post-adoption research is gaining more and more interest as well (e.g., [2,7,8,33]). Some researchers argue that the success of information technology relies more on individuals' continuance usage rather than on the initial usage or adoption of IT [8,30]. This would be particularly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for subscription-based online services, because the continuance usage of these services is closely related to the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts [7,55,59]. In the case of blog service, although it is usually provided for free, blogs can attract a number of visitors. As the saying of “where there are eyeballs, there are business models” [61, p. 25], advertisements in these blogs could be an important income source for the blog service-hosting sites. It is estimated that, in the US, blog advertising revenue will increase from \$283 million in 2007 to \$768 million in 2012 [67]. Therefore, in this study, we follow the trend of post-adoption research, and study bloggers' switching intention in blog services. It refers to the bloggers' intention to switch to other blog services.

Gender issue is another emphasis in this paper. It has been claimed to play an interesting role in blogs [63]. Many blog studies have implied that the number of female bloggers is usually larger than that of male bloggers in their data collection results [25,46,48,50]. Herring and Paolillo [28] explicitly indicated that diary blogs have more female stylistic entries while <sup>fi</sup>lter blogs have more male stylistic entries. The writing of female bloggers is suggested to be more interpersonal, and that of male bloggers is more informative [5]. Other gender-related topics include self-presentation [63], gender identity [17], and gender inequity in the blogosphere [27]. As a result, gender differences may also exist in bloggers' switching behavior. Therefore, we incorporate gender into the research model and examine its role through this study.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In the following section, research on switching behavior in the marketing and IS literature, as well as several aspects of gender studies are reviewed. Afterwards, we introduce our research model and hypotheses. Then, our research design is presented. Finally, we conclude our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and provide implications for both researchers and practitioners.

## 2. Theoretical background

Switching behavior has been studied in both the marketing and the information technology usage literatures. The role of gender has also been investigated in these contexts, often focusing on the effect of gender on social behavior through socialization processes.

## 2.1. Switching behavior in the marketing literature

Research on switching behavior has been conducted over a decade in the marketing literature. The work of Ping [56] was one of the early studies that addressed switching behaviors between companies. They proposed that the relationship between retailers and suppliers have <sup>fi</sup>ve stages: loyalty, voice, opportunism, exiting, and neglect. They further suggested that satisfaction, attractive alternativeness, investment, and switching costs have impacts on each stage. After that, customers' switching behavior became an important topic in the <sup>fi</sup>eld and important determinants of switching behavior were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by many researchers. For instance, Keaveney [38] proposed and classi<sup>fi</sup>ed eight categories of factors affecting switching behavior, including pricing, service failures, attraction by competitors, and the like. In the study of Wieringa and Verhoef [69], they focused on the liberalizing service market, and suggested that cognitive and affective antecedents in prior loyalty literature, and the concept of switching costs can be applied to address the importance of inertia. Following this stream of research, many service studies on customers' switching behavior have been conducted in recent years, including car-insurance services [3], auto-repair and hairstyling services [6], and <sup>fi</sup>xed-line telephone services [45].

## 2.2. Switching behavior of information technology

The number of studies on switching behavior of information technologies increases in recent years. Keaveney and Parthasarathy [39] investigated the customers' switching behavior between Internet service providers, which arouses researchers' interest on switching behavior of information technologies. Chen and Hitt [12] measured the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of customer characteristics and <sup>fi</sup>rm attributes on online brokers' switching and attrition. In the context of e-commerce websites, a buyer–seller relationship perspective [70] was adopted to compare the website stayers and switchers [44]. New technologies like mobile services' [37,58] and email services' [41] switching behavior were also analyzed. Along with the aforementioned research stream on post-adoption studies in the IS literature, Ye et al. [71] examined prior customers' switching behavior studies in the marketing literature and focused on Internet users' switching behavior of web browsers. Factors, which are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to both the current web browser (e.g., satisfaction) and the new one (e.g., relative advantage), were empirically examined.

## 2.3. The role of gender

## 2.3.1. Gender research in switching behavior

Demographic variables like gender also attract attention from IT switching researchers. Chen and Hitt [12] examined customers' demographic characteristics and concluded that only weak effects were found on predicting their switching behaviors. In the mobile service context, Ranganathan et al. [58] considered both age and gender in their research. It is suggested that technological facilities are more ‘masculine’ [9], and females may show more anxiety toward the usage of mobile technologies [24]. Therefore, they proposed that this anxiety may suppress the switching behavior, which means that female customers would be less likely to switch than male customers. The results supported this hypothesis and further showed that gender exerts stronger effects than age on switching.

## 2.3.2. Gender research in technology acceptance

In IS research, Gefen and Straub [23] incorporated gender into the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). They argued gender as one of the socio-culture factors in IT context and concluded that perceptions toward email technology diffusion differ by gender. Similarly, in the UTAUT model [66], gender and other factors (age, experience, and voluntariness of use) are included as key moderators. Recently, in a post-adoption research on the theory of trying, Ahuja and Thatcher [2] also found that gender moderates the relationship between perceptions of work environment factors and trying to innovative.

## 2.3.3. Social role theory

Social role theory (SRT) posits that gender differences in social behavior stem from the socialization process [18]. Women and men develop their social roles from the division of labor as well as the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of societal expectances [4]. According to SRT, many researchers have proposed that women are characterized as more communal while men as more agentic [4,19,21]. Communal traits are described as “friendly, unsel<sup>fi</sup>sh, concerned with others, and emotionally expressive,” whereas agentic traits refer to “independent, masterful, assertive, and instrumentally competent” [19, p. 309]. It is suggested that many aspects of gender differences can be explained through the dimensions of communion and agency [4]. For instance, in the study of Dittmar [15], women were proposed to value material possessions more in an emotional view, while men are more in an instrumental perspective. Similarly, Djamasbi and Loiacono [16] adopted SRT in the decision-making context, and suggested different reactions from women and men in terms of their usage of feedbacks.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Instead of investigating all possible factors, this study only includes three salient factors to explain bloggers' Intention to Switch (INT) blog services: satisfaction (SAT), sunk costs (SC), and attractive alternatives (AA), which <sup>fi</sup>t the two categories of independent variables on switching behavior of Ye et al. [71]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two factors are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the current blog service while the third one is related to the new service. Furthermore, by examining moderating effects of sunk costs and gender, we bring more speci<sup>fi</sup>c perspectives on bloggers' switching research. The research model is illustrated as follow, where dotted lines refer to moderating effects ( Fig. 1).

## 3.1. Satisfaction

Oliver [53, p. 29] de<sup>fi</sup>ned satisfaction as “the summary psychological state resulting when the emotion surrounding discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed expectations is coupled with the customer's prior feelings about the consumption experience.” It is widely held that satisfaction/dissatisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences customers' switching behavior in the marketing literature [22,38]. Low levels of satisfaction will result in the increase of customers' switching intention [3,6].

In post-adoption studies, satisfaction is found to be positively associated with IS continuance intention [8]. Keaveney and Parthasarathy [39] proposed that customer continuers are more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed than switchers. In the study of Ye et al. [71], they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed satisfaction as the factor pertaining to the incumbent web browser usage, and suggested that it will negatively affect users' switching behavior. Therefore, if bloggers keep using a blog service, certain level of satisfaction will be formed in terms of the discrepancy between their expectancies and usage experiences. We propose that when bloggers hold low level of satisfaction, they will have a higher intention to switch to another blog service:

H1. Bloggers' satisfaction with current blog services is negatively associated with the intention to switch their blog services.

## 3.2. Attractive alternatives

Perceived attractive alternatives are positive perception toward competing products or services [6]. Keaveney [38] identi<sup>fi</sup>ed that attraction by competitors is one of eight reasons that will drive customers to switch services. Interestingly, to some extent, the notion of attractive alternatives is similar to relative advantage, which is used for predicting IS diffusion and adoption [51]. They both share the meaning of seeking for something “better.” In the study of Ye et al. [71], they suggested that relative advantage, as a factor speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the substitute product, will positively affect users' switching behaviors. Hence, we hypothesize that if bloggers discover better, more innovative, functional or attractive alternative blogs, they will be likely to try the new blogs and have a higher intention to switch the blog service:

H2. Bloggers' perception of attractive alternative blog services is positively associated with the intention to switch their blog services.

## 3.3. Sunk costs

Sunk costs are derived from switching costs [36]. Based on the research of Jones et al. [36], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne sunk costs as the perception of irrecoverable time and effort that are invested in using current blog services. Studies have found that the more investments customers have made, the less likely they will switch to use another services [45,58]. In IS literature, Keil et al. [40] introduced the importance of sunk costs on IT project's continuance research. Ye et al. [71] argued breadth of use through the concept of sunk costs and proposed its negative effect on switching behavior.

Some researchers have proposed that switching costs or switching barriers may have moderating effects [6,35]. When customers perceive high levels of switching costs, even if they are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their current service provider or <sup>fi</sup>nd out some better substitutes, they may still be reluctant to switch. Indirectly, in occupation studies, research has found employees who are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their jobs may show fewer propensities to change due to high cost or trouble perceptions [49]. As for the blog service context, we anticipate that if bloggers perceive high level of irrecoverable time and effort invested in the blog service, the impacts from dissatisfaction and attractive alternatives toward their switching intention may be weakened as well. It means that sunk costs not only have direct effect but may also have moderate effects.

Taking these into account, we propose that when bloggers' sunk costs is high (e.g., they may have already written many posts, uploaded many pictures, and built up many friendship ties with other bloggers), they will be less likely to switch to new blogs, less sensitive to the satisfaction with current blog services, and less responsive to attractive alternative blog services:

H3a. Bloggers' perception of sunk costs for switching is negatively associated with the intention to switch their blog services.

H3b. Stronger perception of sunk costs will weaken the association between satisfaction and intention to switch.

H3c. Stronger perception of sunk costs will weaken the association between attractive alternatives and intention to switch.

## 3.4. Gender

Earlier discussions indicate that gender issue varies in many aspects of blogs. Research on switching behavior and IT post-adoption also suggest its importance. In this study, the role of gender on bloggers' switching behavior is explained through different emphases between emotionality and instrumentality.

Oliver [54] posited that satisfaction is a cumulative evaluation, which captures both cognitive and affective meanings. In advance, Bhattacherjee [8] postulated that satisfaction is an affective condition based on cognitive evaluation of the discrepancy between performance and prior expectation. It implies that satisfaction can capture one's emotional state. On the contrary, alternative attractiveness or relative advantage emphasizes more on users' pursuit of increased utility.

In IS discipline, men are believed to be task-oriented, and concern more on perceived usefulness of an information technology [64–66]. In general, research has found that women tend to care more about interpersonal and emotional aspects, whereas men focus more on advancement and problem solving issues [23]. This is consistent with social role theory, different social roles are established in the socialization process: women are more characterized with emotional traits (communal), while men are more with instrumental traits (agentic). Thus, we believe that in terms of blog services switching behaviors, women are more sensitive to satisfaction and less responsive to alternative attractiveness rather than men:

H4a. The association between satisfaction and intention to switch is stronger in women than in men.

H4b. The association between attractive alternatives and intention to switch is weaker in women than in men.

## 4.1. Data collection

An online survey was employed for data collection. As pointed out by Trammell and Keshelashvili [63, p. 973], “a complete directory of blogs does not exist, and it is impossible to select a random sample.” Thus, many blog studies conducted surveys through convenient samples and usually adopted snowball-sampling techniques [34,48,57,68]. In this study, we adopted similar techniques and distributed the invitation messages, which contained the URL of our online questionnaire, to many popular online forums and online blog communities in Hong Kong. The prevalence of blogging in Hong Kong starts from 2005, although several years lagging behind the western countries, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of this technology has gained considerable attention in local magazines, newspapers, TV, and the web [52]. Xanga, one of the most popular websites providing blog services worldwide [11], has as much as 44.7% users coming from Hong Kong according to the statistics from Alexa (www.alexa.com, 4 March 2009). Some recently published empirical studies on blog have also drawn their conclusions based on samples of Hong Kong bloggers [32,48]. Under these circumstances, we believe that sampling in Hong Kong is suitable for this study. Further, in order to increase response rate, incentives of local supermarket coupons were offered as lucky draw prizes. At the end of the survey, 316 people completed our questionnaires. After initial examinations, 17 responses were deleted for inconsistent answers in the questionnaire (e.g., in the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the questionnaire, they may <sup>fi</sup>rstly choose Xanga as their primarily used blog service. However, in the <sup>fi</sup>nal part, they chose Yahoo instead.). Therefore, a total of 299 usable data were collected. Demographics of female (N=169) and male (N=130) groups are listed in Table 1.

## 4.2. Measures

We adapted validated items of constructs from prior studies [8,36,41]. Slight wording modi<sup>fi</sup>cations were applied to <sup>fi</sup>t the blog research context. In particular, two types of scales were used for the measurements of items. Satisfaction used seven-point differential semantic scales, while attractive alternatives, sunk costs, and intention to switch adopted seven-point Likert scales. Details of measures are listed in Appendix A.

## 4.3. Data analysis and results

We used Partial Least Squares (PLS) to analyze the research model. PLS is a robust and frequently adopted technique in IS literature [2,23,65]. It is a component-based structural equation modeling approach, which requires a relatively small sample size, and has no restriction on normal distribution [13]. These make PLS highly suitable for the data analysis of our study. Therefore, we used PLS-graph version 3.0 for Windows and followed the two-step analytical procedures [26]. The measurement model was <sup>fi</sup>rst examined and then the structural models were assessed.

## 4.4. Measurement model

In each subgroup, we calculated the convergent validity and discriminant validity to assess the measurement model. To achieve suf<sup>fi</sup>cient convergent validity, items with Composite Reliability (CR) values higher than 0.7 and with Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values higher than 0.5 are required [20]. After deleting one item of attractive alternatives for its low factor loading, we obtained CR values ranging from 0.810 to 0.944, and AVE values ranging from 0.692 to 0.850 for both female and male groups (Table 2), which meant that the results were adequate. Meanwhile, to ensure the discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE for each construct should be greater than the correlation between constructs [20]. As shown in Table 3, discriminant validity of the data was also suf<sup>fi</sup>cient.

## 4.5. Structural model

In this study, we adopted PLS product-indicator approach [13] and subgroup analysis method [2,40,65] to examine the moderating effects of sunk costs and gender respectively. To perform these, we tested three structural models for the full, female, and male groups. Before estimating such models, we <sup>fi</sup>rstly standardized the items of each construct and then produced two interaction terms: sunk costs⁎satisfaction and sunk costs⁎attractive alternatives.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst model, as the baseline model, was to assess the effects of control variables on bloggers' switching intention. Since days of blogging per week is conceptually related to sunk costs, we took years of blogging, possession of blogs, primarily used blog service, age, and education level as the control variables. Results indicated that all variables, except for years of blogging and age, have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on intention to switch. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant variables were then included into the following two models. In the second model, we tested the direct effects of satisfaction, sunk costs, and attractive alternatives on intention to switch. Finally, the third model incorporated two interaction terms to examine moderating effects of sunk costs. Table 4 provides the path loadings and corresponding t values of these structural models.

## 5. Conclusion and discussion

Motivated by the need to understand the post-adoption switching behaviors of bloggers, this study attempts to investigate bloggers' switching intention between blog services. The research model incorporates three salient factors, namely satisfaction, sunk costs, and attractive alternatives, drawn from the marketing and IS literature. The role of gender is further explored to understand the differences between women and men in terms of their switching decision. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that satisfaction and sunk costs will negatively affect bloggers' switching intention, while attractive alternatives exert a positive effect. In addition, moderating effects are found in gender, but not in sunk costs. Female bloggers, rather than males, are more sensitive to satisfaction and less responsive to attractive alternatives.

## 5.1. Implications for theory and research

This study addresses two academic needs. First of all, compared with many switching behavior studies in the marketing literature, research on switching behavior of information technologies is not fully explored. On the other hand, researchers from the post-adoption area also call for more studies on new IT settings and more theoretical perspectives to enrich our understanding. Bearing these thoughts in mind, we believe that switching behavior is an important topic in the post-adoption domain. Intention to switching the blog service is therefore explored under the blog technology context, where previous studies had only just begun to shed light on the initial blog acceptance phenomenon [31,48]. As another key contribution, we investigate the role of gender in the research model. Although prior studies on switching behavior have addressed the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of gender [12,58], few have advanced any theories to support their arguments. In this study, we draw on the social role theory and suggest that gender plays moderating roles in the research model. Results of the data analysis reveal that most of our hypotheses are supported.

Direct impacts from satisfaction, sunk costs and attractive alternatives are found to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect bloggers' switching intention. These are consistent with previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings (e.g., [3,6,71]). It also implies that both user factors toward the incumbent (e.g., satisfaction and sunk costs) and the substitute services (e.g., attractive alternatives) of the same technology innovation are important predictors of bloggers switching behavior.

Moderating effects from sunk costs and gender are also examined in this study. To our surprise, only the hypotheses of gender are supported. Results indicate that sunk costs do not weaken the in<sup>fl</sup>uences from satisfaction and attractive alternatives toward bloggers' switching intention, which are inconsistent with <sup>fi</sup>ndings from prior studies (e.g., [6]). A possible explanation is that, in light of the blog context, bloggers may not perceive sunk costs as important as that in the marketing context, where individuals usually suffer monetary loss. The sunk costs in using a blog can be represented in terms of two forms. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is materials uploaded, including posted entries, uploaded pictures, and accessories or gadgets used to decorate the blog. The other is relationship ties established through the blog, where the blogger can interact with his friends. Currently, there are tools that can move blog archives from the old blog service to the new one, or can download them into the computer. Also, if the blogger has switched to use a new blog service, he canpost and highlight his new blog's URL address on the old blog, or he can inform his friends through email and instant messenger. As a result, the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of sunk costs may be weakened, and may not be able to prevent bloggers from switching away, when they have high levels of dissatisfaction and perceived attractive alternatives. Therefore, sunk costs fail to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effects.

Finally, the study results indicate that women tend to care more about satisfaction with regard to switching intention, whereas men focus more on attractive alternatives. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the communal-agentic dimension in the social role theory (e.g., [4,18,19]). We believe that these <sup>fi</sup>ndings can broaden our understandings of the sex differences in bloggers' behaviors, as well as enrich the gender research in the emerging IT post-adoption literature.

## 5.2. Implications for practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may also inform practitioners, especially designers of web sites that provide blog services. Because satisfaction and sunk costs negatively affect bloggers' switching intention, blog service designers should not only collect bloggers' feedbacks and complaints instantly, but also provide interesting activities for them to communicate with each other, thus increasing their time spent on blogs. Concerning the positive effect of attractive alternatives, blog service designers can apply two strategies. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one is to prevent the decreasing of their current blog memberships. They can keep track on the development of blog services from other web sites, and try to make their own blog services as comparable as others at the minimum. The second one is to attract new bloggers. One suggestion is to provide more innovative features and useful functions according to the feedbacks and complaints collected from current blog membership, because it will increase the satisfaction of these bloggers as well. Finally, blog service designers should be aware of the differences between females and males, since female bloggers care more about satisfaction with their current blogs and male bloggers tend to look for new blogs that are better or more attractive. Accordingly, different strategies on females and males could be carried out.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

The main limitation of this study is related to our sample, which is conveniently collected in Hong Kong with 299 usable respondents. Both the sampling region and the sample size could affect the generalizability of this study. According to the social role theory [18], as well as gender studies on IT acceptance (e.g., [2,23,65]), gender is closely related to culture. This indicates that the moderating effects of gender may not be generalized to other regions (e.g., from eastern to western regions). Another limitation is that the R square of the <sup>fi</sup>nal model is 24.1% for female bloggers and 28.4% for males. As Cohen [14] indicated, an R square of 15% explains a moderate amount of variance, while 35% is believed to be large. Our relatively moderate R squares suggest that additional variances could be explained by other important factors, which may include bloggers' perceived enjoyment or social in<sup>fl</sup>uences from their friends (e.g., [31]). In sum, future research could be conducted by considering all the above discussions.
