# How do mediated and non-mediated power affect electronic supply chain management system adoption? The mediating effects of trust and institutional pressures

Weiling Ke <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Hefu Liu <sup>b</sup>, Kwok Kee Wei <sup>c</sup>, Jibao Gu <sup>d</sup>, Huaping Chen <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Business, Clarkson University, Clarkson Ave., Potsdam, NY 13699, USA

<sup>b</sup> USTC-CityU Joint Advanced Research Center, University of Science and Technology of China, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China

<sup>c</sup> Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China

<sup>d</sup> School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China, China

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 20 November 2008

Keywords: Supply Chain Management Systems Trust Power Institutional pressures

## a b s t r a c t

Electronic Supply Chain Management systems (eSCMS), as instances of information technologies spanning organizational boundaries, have the potential to provide operational and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, the high uncertainty of adoption consequences impedes <sup>fi</sup>rms from adopting eSCMS. Research on how to facilitate eSCMS adoption is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. This study investigates how institutional and socio-political factors in a dyadic trading partner relationship affect the dependent <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption intention. In contrast to prior research, we examine the con<sup>fl</sup>uence of institutional pressures, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's power. In particular, we differentiate power of different sources, and examine the mediating effects of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures on the relationship between different types of power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption. Data collected from executives in China largely support our research hypotheses. Theoretical contribution and managerial implications of this research are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic Supply Chain Management systems (eSCMS), as one kind of inter-organizational systems (IOS), enhance communication, coordination and collaboration between trading partners, and are thus suggested to be critical for <sup>fi</sup>rm performance [17,32,33,65,86,98,102]. Different from early forms of IOS, eSCMS, especially the Internetenabled SCMS, have enhanced features (e.g., shared database and joint decision making support) that allow the integration of fragmented, silo-oriented supply chain processes with low cost and rich content [32,86]. Thus, eSCMS enable seamless information, as well as physical and <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fl</sup>ow between <sup>fi</sup>rms [65,82,102]. However, despite their greatly purported bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the consequences of eSCMS adoption can be highly unpredictable [102]. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from eSCMS can be distributed unevenly and skewed in favor of players with power in the supply chain [88,98]. That is, it is likely that powerful players will bene<sup>fi</sup>t at the expense of dependent <sup>fi</sup>rms [21]. The uncertainty of eSCMS adoption consequences impedes <sup>fi</sup>rms from adopting this innovation, and the insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciency of eSCMS adoption is regarded as a major critical failure factor of supply chain management. Research on how to facilitate eSCMS adoption is of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in enriching our understanding and providing guidelines for promoting eSCMS adoption.

A recent literature review indicates that researchers study IOS adoption from the lens of socio-political theories, examining the effects of trust and power on IOS adoption [e.g., [43,44,48,84]. Power refers to relative independence in a dyadic inter-organizational relationship. That is, if Company A's dependence on Company B is higher than B's dependence on A, then B has power over A. In this paper, we coin the <sup>fi</sup>rm with power as the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm with higher dependence the target (or focal) <sup>fi</sup>rm. To date, the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the effects of power on IOS adoption are mixed and even controversial [44]. While many studies have found that dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms can exercise their power and promote trading partners' IOS adoption [e.g., [2,19,43,47,83,84,90], others report weak and even negative relationships between power exercised by dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms and dependent partners' IOS adoption [e.g., [40,44,95]. Such mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings impede the advancement of this line of research, and thus it is imperative to scrutinize the nuances of power. Indeed, according to marketing channel theories, power has various sources and thus can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into different types, i.e., mediated and non-mediated power [15,36,46]. These different types of power can lead to varying, even opposite, results of inter-organizational cooperation [15]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, mediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may incur con<sup>fl</sup>icts, reduce the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's (we use “focal <sup>fi</sup>rm”, “target <sup>fi</sup>rm” and “dependent <sup>fi</sup>rm” interchangeably in this paper to make the delineation clear) trust, and lead to a negative attitude toward inter-organizational cooperation, while non-mediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm can increase the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's positive appraisal of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm cooperation, enhance its trust, and motivate the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to cooperate with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm [15,43].

Another stream of research applies institutional theory to investigate how the seeking for legitimacy and recognition drives <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt IOS [47,94,100]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, institutional pressures, i.e., coercive, mimetic and normative pressures, have been proposed and found to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors leading a <sup>fi</sup>rm to conform to norms and expectations regulated by other constituents in the institutional environments [26,54,80]. Going against these institutional pressures may incur questions about the <sup>fi</sup>rm's legitimacy, and thus affect their capabilities to gain resources and social support [26,66,100,101]. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will comply with institutional pressures and adopt IOS [47,94,100].

These two streams of research have examined the effects of institutional and socio-political factors independently in separate models [e.g., [43,100]. However, according to dependence theory, a dominant player that controls scarce and important resources as an important constituent, will proactively exercise its power to reshape institutional rules in the environment to serve its own interests [48,75,78]. That is, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised power is a critical source of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived institutional pressures [4]. As such, how different types of power affect the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived pressures warrants scrutiny. Hence, we contend that integrating the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's different types of power and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived institutional pressures in a single model may shed new light on our understanding of drivers for eSCMS adoption.

This research intends to bridge the gaps in the extant literature. We seek to understand how socio-political factors and institutional pressures facilitate eSCMS adoption. In particular, we investigate the con<sup>fl</sup>uence of the dominant trading partner's power, as well as the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures. Our research questions thus are: (1) What are the mechanisms underlying the in<sup>fl</sup>uencing process of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's power in affecting the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption? and (2) How are the in<sup>fl</sup>uencing processes different between different types of power?

To address our research questions, we develop our research framework by drawing upon institutional, socio-political, marketing channels and information system theories. We propose nine hypotheses on the relationships between the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust, perceived pressures, mediated and non-mediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm, and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption. The research model is tested by data collected from executives in China.

This paper is organized as follows. In the following section, we describe the theoretical background of the current study. In Section 3, we present the research model and articulate our research hypotheses. Then we outline our research methodology in Section 4, followed by our data analysis and results in Section 5. We conclude the paper with contributions to research and implications for practice.

## 2. Theoretical background

Electronic Supply Chain Management Systems (eSCMS), as information systems integrated across organizational boundaries, enable trading partners to orchestrate the operation of value chains [19]. Among the different types of eSCMS, the Internet-enabled SCMS have been widely taken as the new important trend of eSCMS [32]. Numerous Internet-enabled SCMS, such as SAP, Oracle and IBM ebusiness have been introduced to <sup>fi</sup>rms as powerful strategic weapons for supply chain management [32,41]. Unlike early forms of IOS, such as EDI, the Internet-enabled SCMS have different structure, technology, usage and information exchange capabilities [102]. They have a range of features for information sharing [63,65,86], joint decision making [67] and business process integration [67,82,86]. In addition, the Internet-enabled SCMS allow trading partners to share real-time information on demand, such as inventory and new product ideas, enable training aids and technical knowledge sharing, and thus allow synergistic effect generation [22,32,33,98,102,104]. Therefore, it is suggested that eSCMS, especially the Internet-enabled SCMS, are able to provide operational and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [98,102].

However, reaping these purported bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS is challenging [11,20,60]. Kumar and Dissel [60] have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed economic, technical and socio-political risks in IOS adoption. Due to these various risks, the consequences of IOS adoption can be highly unpredictable. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from IOS can be distributed unevenly and skewed in favor of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm [88,98]. Stated alternatively, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm will bene<sup>fi</sup>t at the expense of the dependent <sup>fi</sup>rm [21,98]. Therefore, IOS adoption by the dependent <sup>fi</sup>rm in the market, is best explained by socio-political and institutional theories [e.g., [43,44,50,100]. According to socio-political theory, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust toward the trading partner and power exercised by the dominant partner are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant antecedents of IOS adoption [43,44]. However, according to institutional theory, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm adopts IOS because of perceived institutional pressures [19,100]. In the following sections, we delineate these constructs and articulate their effects on IOS adoption. In particular, we focus on investigating how different types of power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm affect the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption through their effects on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures.

## 2.1. Power

In a dyadic relationship, power is derived from asymmetric dependence which refers to the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's capability to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the target <sup>fi</sup>rm to act as the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm desires [25,44]. There are six different types of power sources: coercion, reward, legitimate, expert, referent, and information [31]. In marketing channels literature, there are three ways to classify these six in<sup>fl</sup>uencing strategies: coercive/ non-coercive, economic/non-economic and mediated/non-mediated [62]. The mediated and non-mediated power grouping has gained consistent empirical support. Indeed, a meta-analysis of the 16 major published studies on channel power supports the mediated and nonmediated power classi<sup>fi</sup>cation [49]. Therefore, it is proposed that this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation offers a more desirable method for examining power than other classi<sup>fi</sup>cation approaches [e.g., [36,43,91].

Mediated power includes reward, coercion and legal legitimate power sources. Coercion power is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to mediate punishments if a target <sup>fi</sup>rm is not cooperative. Reward power refers to a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to mediate rewards if a target <sup>fi</sup>rm complies with its demands. Legal legitimate power means that a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm has legitimate rights to exert in<sup>fl</sup>uence on a target <sup>fi</sup>rm [14]. These power sources are external to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, and are provided upon the condition that the target <sup>fi</sup>rm does what the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm wishes [15]. Such contingency controlled by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm will generate the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's negative feelings on losing autonomy although it may bring about the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's compliance [10,52]. Therefore, the use of mediated power will decrease the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's social satisfaction and damage the dyadic relationship in the long run [13,15,30,93] even though the reward power may allow the target <sup>fi</sup>rm to obtain some economic gain in the short run [87].

Non-mediated power refers to the power sources whose enforcements guiding the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision making and behaviors are not mediated by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm [15]. Rather, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm mediates those reinforcements for itself, and the success or failure of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's behaviors is attributed to itself [15]. This type of power includes the sources of referent, expertise and information provided by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. Referent power is based on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's identi<sup>fi</sup>cation with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm or its hope to be closely associated with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. Expert power refers to a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's holding expertise or knowledge that is valued by the target <sup>fi</sup>rm [97]. An example of such knowledge is the know-how of supply chain management. Information power is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to provide information not previously made available to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, and interpret existing information in ways that are meaningful but not yet known by the target <sup>fi</sup>rm [14]. An example of such information is the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS. The difference between expert and information power is subtle. Possessors of information power must be up to date and be con<sup>fi</sup>dent and persuasive in debating. But they do not have to be perceived by their images of expertise to show credibility [55]. Generally, the nonmediated power sources are long-term oriented. By using nonmediated power, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm aims to build in the target <sup>fi</sup>rm a sense of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and then have the target <sup>fi</sup>rm internalize the ideas and norms provided [52]. Due to such internalization and its non-contingency nature, non-mediated power produces fewer con-<sup>fl</sup>icts in inter-organizational relationships, and thus enhances the quality of the dyadic relationship [15,105].

In short, the exercise of power allows the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm to exert in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision making and behaviors. Firms may use different types of power to lead the target <sup>fi</sup>rm to enter into cooperation ties, such as eSCMS adoption. However, different types of power affect inter-organizational cooperation in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, yet contrasting ways [15,46]. Therefore, it is necessary to be speci<sup>fi</sup>c and clear about the type of power that is in the study, and to operationalize the construct accordingly. Most prior studies on the effects of power on IOS adoption (with the exception of Hart and Saunders [43]) have treated power as a simple construct and failed to examine the various effects of different types of power. This may explain the mixed and even controversial research <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the effects of power in previous studies.

## 2.2. Institutional pressures

Institutional pressures refer to the pressures that emanate from the institutional environments that can induce <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt shared norms and routines [26]. The agents that may exert pressures include a <sup>fi</sup>rm's key customers, suppliers, competitors and government agencies. According to institutional theory, institutional environments refer to the processes of institutionalization and mechanisms of in<sup>fl</sup>uence that pertain to legitimacy in a particular societal context [26]. The legitimacy is in regards to the expectations for appropriate organizational structures, behaviors, and practices [92]. These expectations act as unwritten rules of proper social conduct [42]. Violating these rules may bring a <sup>fi</sup>rm's legitimacy into question and jeopardize its access to scarce resources and social support [26,66,100,101]. Thus, a <sup>fi</sup>rm embedded in social networks would perceive high pressures to adhere to these institutional expectations [26]. Institutional pressures can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into three categories: normative, mimetic, and coercive pressures. Mimetic pressures mainly stem from perceived success of competitors' actions [26] and act through structural equivalence [100]. Since mimetic pressures do not operate in buyer–seller relationships, we focus on the effects of coercive and normative pressures.

Coercive pressures stem from political in<sup>fl</sup>uences and legitimacy in the institutional environments [61]. Coercive pressures can be exerted on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm by the institutional environments formally through rules or laws, or informally through certain cultural expectations [3,26,100]. Firm dependence on external resources, and fear of being left out of its industry make coercive pressures in<sup>fl</sup>uential in driving the <sup>fi</sup>rm to adhere to expectations of the environment. As a constituent in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's environment, a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm can directly or indirectly affect the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressure. In particular, when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm foresees the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c inter-organizational practice, it will reshape institutional rules to make the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm perceive a high cultural expectation or legitimate requirement for adopting this practice [4,24,78]. To secure access to scarce resources from the environment, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will submit to pressures and comply. Indeed, the positive effect of coercive pressures on IOS adoption has gained support from some previous studies [e.g., [26,61,100].

Normative pressures result from expectations regarding how work should be conducted professionally [26]. Through inter-organizational channels, these expectations are transferred and gradually become shared norms. In particular, norms are created and strengthened by the prevalence/extent of some new practices in the market, and they impose great normative pressures on a <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision-making and behaviors [12,57,66,100]. To appear legitimate and progressive, a <sup>fi</sup>rm tends to adopt new practices which are consistent with the norms and values of its constituents in the institutional environments [26,57,66,96,100].

## 2.3. Inter-organizational trust

Inter-organizational trust refers to one party's intention or willingness to depend on another party, based on the anticipated bene<sup>fi</sup>cial behavior of that party [23,58,72,76]. According to social exchange theory, inter-organizational trust can determine the nature of inter-organizational cooperation and social orders [34,37,76]. It helps reduce the perceived risk of cooperation [70] and therefore allows <sup>fi</sup>rms to collaborate with each other in an uncertain environment that makes explicit contracts too expensive, if not impossible [9,35,44,76,103].

Lewis and Weigert [64] identify two dimensions of trust: affective trust and cognitive trust. Affective trust is based on an organization's sensibility judgments and is established through emotion and feelings in a dyadic inter-organizational relationship. Cognitive trust is based on organizations' rational judgments and is developed through a <sup>fi</sup>rm's understanding and conviction of its partner's competence [23,64,71]. It is suggested that research on trust should take both dimensions of trust into consideration [64]. Missing cognitive trust could incur blind faith or <sup>fi</sup>xed hope, and removing affective trust could induce coldblooded prediction or rationally calculated risk. Therefore, we treat trust as a construct with these two dimensions in this research.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Fig. 1 depicts our research model. The model describes how the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures and different types of power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm affect the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption. It shows the process that in<sup>fl</sup>uences mediated power and nonmediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm, and how the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures mediate the relationships between different types of power and eSCMS adoption. The research model focuses on eSCMS adoption intention, rather than actual adoption because “the role of intention as a strong predictor of behavior has been well-established in IS and reference disciplines” [56].

## 3.1. Power and institutional pressures

In a hypercompetitive condition, a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm, as an important constituent of the environment, exercises its power to reshape institutional rules and models in the environment to serve its own interests [4,48,75]. In the view that eSCMS enables information sharing, joint decision making, business process integration, and knowledge sharing [67,86,98], we expect that, to derive these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm will exercise its power, affect its trading partners' perception about the rules and expectations in the institutional environments, and drive these partner <sup>fi</sup>rms to adopt eSCMS. Therefore, we propose that a target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived institutional pressures (i.e., coercive pressures and normative pressures) are affected by the different types of power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm.

In general, a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power increases a target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures. The coercive pressures perceived by the target <sup>fi</sup>rm may emanate from its suppliers, customers and other institutions that have the power to regulate the industry, such as government agencies [26]. These pressures, as formal rules or laws, exert in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision making and behaviours [3,26,100]. In the context of eSCMS adoption, to promote the integration with trading partners, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may exercise its mediated power to put pressures on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm. For instance, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may set the rules of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm practices and transactions by standardizing the inter-organizational interfaces of data, applications, and processes in the channel relationships [44,86,89]. Then it may impose these rules on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, coupled with reward promises, punishment or legal action threats to induce the target <sup>fi</sup>rm to adopt eSCMS. In such an in<sup>fl</sup>uencing process, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, with its dependence on the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm, will regard eSCMS adoption as a necessity to do business with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm and will thus sense a high level of coercive pressure to adopt the system.

In contrast, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised mediated power will decrease the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative pressures. A dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm usually can use its mediated power to achieve a target <sup>fi</sup>rm's short-term compliance [44]. However, such an operation, i.e., using rewards, punishments and legal legitimacy, will generate the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's negative feelings from losing autonomy [30,91]. Although the target <sup>fi</sup>rm complies with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's request, it does not identify with or internalize the value of adopting eSCMS [14]. For instance, when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm indicates that it will punish the target <sup>fi</sup>rm if the target <sup>fi</sup>rm does not comply, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will feel a low level of solidarity in the dyadic relationship and will consequently view the situation as the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm achieving its own goal at the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's expense. Similarly, if the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm promises rewards for the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's compliance, it may undermine the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's sense of autonomy and cause it to be more concerned about the cost of compliance. With these negative feelings, coupled with an understanding of the risk involved in inter-organizational cooperation [60,85,98], the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will question the practice of adopting eSCMS, regard the purported bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS as rhetoric, and doubt the actual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that the dependent <sup>fi</sup>rms can appropriate. Therefore, mediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm has a negative impact on shared norms and values in the dyadic relationship, and consequently diminishes the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative pressures. We thus propose the following hypotheses:

H1a. The target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures toward eSCMS adoption are positively related to the exercised mediated power of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm.

H1b. The target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative pressures toward eSCMS adoption are negatively related to the exercised mediated power of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm.

The dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised non-mediated power has an impact on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures. Although nonmediated power does not set up formal rules to compel a target <sup>fi</sup>rm to comply, it helps nurture a cultural expectation in the institutional environments, thereby affecting the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures [3,26]. According to Hofsted [45], certain parts of cultural expectations are formulated based on norms and values shared in the institutional environments. When the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm exercises non-mediated power, i.e., information, expert and referent power, it shares its understanding and expertise of eSCMS. For example, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may emphasize the cost of not adopting eSCMS and share stories of companies losing business due to the inadequacy of integration with their trading partners. When such information and knowledge are shared with the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, they in<sup>fl</sup>uence the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perception of the cultural expectations in the institutional environment. With a long-term cooperation orientation, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will take eSCMS adopion as a requirement imposed by the institutional environments and regard eSCMS adoption as a necessity to conduct business [13,15]. Thus, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm perceives coercive pressures to adopt eSCMS.

On the other hand, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised non-mediated power strengthens the shared norms and values in the inter-organizational relationship and has a positive effect on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative pressures. According to market channel theories, the more the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm uses its non-mediated power, the more it focuses on constructing shared norms and values, and the target <sup>fi</sup>rm is then more inclined to internalize and identify with these norms and values [15]. These internalized and identi<sup>fi</sup>ed values and norms serve as the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's reference for decision making and behaviour [26]. This notion can be extended to the context of eSCMS adoption. For instance, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm can share its understanding of potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS and information on the prevalence of eSCMS in the industry. The target <sup>fi</sup>rm will assimilate the shared information and knowledge, recognize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS adoption, and regard eSCMS applications as a professionally appropriate business practice [15,27]. As a result, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will sense a high level of normative pressure. Thus, we propose the following hypotheses:

H2a. The target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures toward eSCMS adoption are positively related to the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised nonmediated power.

H2b. The target <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative pressures toward eSCMS adoption are positively related to the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised nonmediated power.

## 3.2. Power and inter-organizational trust

Following Morgan and Hunt [74], we de<sup>fi</sup>ne trust as con<sup>fi</sup>dence in a trading partner's reliability and integrity. Trust and power, as two dyadic relationship constructs, may interact with each other in affecting inter-organizational cooperation. For example, Ke and Wei [51] posit that trust moderates the relationship between power and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's intention to collaborate with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, trust strengthens the effect of reward, information and expert power. However, empirical studies have found that a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's exercised power has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust [e.g., [10,15,43,68]. In the view that the current study is aimed to investigate the effects of power, we follow previous empirical studies and focus on power's effect on trust.

It is established that different types of power are related with trust in different ways [15,43]. When a dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm exercises its coercive and legitimate power, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm will view it as the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm forcing its one-sided advantages on others [6]. Especially when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm imposes punishment on an uncooperative partner by ceasing to do business with or passing additional processing costs to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, it signals that the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm regards the trading relationship as expendable [43]. Also, when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm exercises reward power, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's sense of autonomy is undermined [30]. Although the reward power provides the target <sup>fi</sup>rm with some economic satisfaction, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm does not regard the interaction with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm as ful<sup>fi</sup>lling and gratifying [59]. Therefore, mediated power has a negative effect on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's affective trust, which is based on sensibility and is established through feelings [30]. Indeed, meta-analytical results demonstrate that increased use of rewards by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm is negatively correlated with the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust [38]. This notion can be extended to the current research context. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power may be interpreted as overt attempts to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to adopt eSCMS, or cause it to be viewed with considerable disfavour, triggering the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to question its goodwill and lowering its affective trust toward the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm [43].

Similarly, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power has a negative impact on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's cognitive trust. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm uses rewards, punishment and legal legitimacy to get the target <sup>fi</sup>rm to comply with its eSCMS adoption request, it foregoes the opportunity to demonstrate its competence to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm [15,43,44]. Given that competence is the origin of cognitive trust, mediated power does not help to enhance the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's cognitive trust. Instead, mediated power makes the target <sup>fi</sup>rm realize that the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm succeeds by taking advantage of other companies' dependence [30]. In a way, it re<sup>fl</sup>ects the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's incompetence and thus reduces the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's cognitive trust.

In contrast, exercised non-mediated power by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm can enhance the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust [43]. According to market channel theories, exercised non-mediated power of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm can positively affect the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's attitudes [30] and enhance relational norms in the channel relationship [13]. The favourable attitudes and strong relational norms are conducive for inter-organizational cooperation [93]. In particular, when sharing with the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm the information and expertise on how to leverage bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm focuses on common norms and values, as well as the relationship itself [15,30]. It is a signal that the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm cares about the mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the channel relationship, rather than being eager to pursue its own bene<sup>fi</sup>ts at the expense of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm [98]. Therefore, the exercised non-mediated power has a positive effect on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's feelings about the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm and elevates the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's affective trust.

In addition, since eSCMS require trading partners to integrate their IT infrastructures and business processes [86], non-mediated power sources allow the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm to help the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm get over the learning curves, recognize the value creation opportunities offered by eSCMS, identify with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm and assimilate the technology [98]. Meanwhile, by sharing information and expertise, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm demonstrates its competence in applying the advanced technology [16]. In the view that competence is an important source of cognitive trust, non-mediated power exercised by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm elevates the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's cognitive trust toward the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm [43]. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H3a. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust toward the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm is negatively related to the exercised mediated power of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm.

H3b. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust toward the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm is positively related to the exercised non-mediated power of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. 3.3. Institutional pressures and eSCMS adoption

Coercive pressures are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as formal or informal pressures exerted on a <sup>fi</sup>rm by other constituents in the environment upon which the <sup>fi</sup>rm is dependent [26]. It has been found that a <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived coercive pressures are built into exchange relationships and may stem from a variety of sources including its resource-dominant trading partners, regulatory bodies and parent corporations [66,100]. In particular, when a dominant party with control over scarce and important resources demands trading partners to adopt business practices or structures to serve its interests, a target <sup>fi</sup>rm will comply with the demand to secure its access to these resources [78,94]. In the context of IOS, the positive effect of coercive pressures on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's adoption has gained consistent empirical support in the literature [e.g., [19,53,61,100]. We expect that this notion can be extended to the adoption of eSCMS, an advanced form of IOS. That is, when the target <sup>fi</sup>rm perceives a high level of coercive pressures, it will comply with the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's request for eSCMS adoption.

H4. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption is positively related to its perceived coercive pressures for eSCMS adoption.

Normative pressures mainly stem from values and norms of professionals regarding how work should be conducted [26]. Through inter-organizational channels, these values and norms are transferred and shared, and thus in<sup>fl</sup>uence the <sup>fi</sup>rm's behaviors, and facilitate them to reach consensus [12,66,94,100]. Meanwhile, these values and norms can be strengthened by the prevalence/extent of innovations among partners in a network [57,66,81,100]. Stated alternatively, <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to adopt a business practice when they identify with the values of this practice, especially when this practice prevails [26,57].

In the context of eSCMS, we expect that normative pressures will have a similar effect on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's adoption. Being embedded in the social networks, a <sup>fi</sup>rm is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the values and norms about eSCMS adoption that are prevalent in the institutional environments. It may eventually regard eSCMS as an innovation, allowing <sup>fi</sup>rms to derive operational and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and thus establish a professionally appropriate practice that warrants managers' attention and adoption [67,82,86,98,102]. Therefore, when the <sup>fi</sup>rm perceives a high level of normative pressures, it will be more likely for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to adopt eSCMS. Thus, the hypothesis:

H5. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption is positively related to its perceived normative pressures for eSCMS adoption.

## 3.4. Trust and eSCMS adoption

Trust, as a critical lubricant of inter-organizational relationship, is a central mechanism for governing inter-organizational coordination expectations and interactions [6,65,76]. In particular, trust mitigates the uncertainties related to the vulnerabilities in inter-organizational relationships [44]. eSCMS allow adopters to transform their fragmented, functional, silo-oriented supply chain processes into seamless, integrated systems [86]. Such integration, and the ensuing joint decision making and modularized business processes make the adopters more dependent on the relationship and more vulnerable to trading partners' opportunistic behaviours [73,76,85]. When faced with this highly uncertain cooperation opportunity, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm with a high level of affective trust has positive expectations about the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's future behaviours [44,65]. Also, cognitive trust makes the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm not only con<sup>fi</sup>dent about the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's business sense and judgment, but also become more willing to make joint decisions [60]. Such belief allows the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to be more optimistic of realizing the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of eSCMS. Therefore, trust enables the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to get over the psychological barriers and adopt eSCMS. Thus:

H6. The focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption is positively related to its trust toward the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm.

We include three control variables: the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's size, dependence on the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm and perceived mimetic pressures in our research model. These variables are selected because they may have an impact on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption intention. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, <sup>fi</sup>rm size, to a certain extent, re<sup>fl</sup>ects the <sup>fi</sup>rm's resources and thus may affect the <sup>fi</sup>rm's intention to adopt eSCMS. Similarly, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's dependence on the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may affect its eSCMS adoption intention, especially when it is <sup>fi</sup>rst initiated by the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. While we focus on the effects of coercive and normative pressures, it is the mimetic pressures, manifesting the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived by competitors, that may facilitate the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision to adopt the system.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

We collected data in collaboration with a Chinese institute that is famous for its executive training programs. This institute had many training programs on supply chain management and information systems that are related to our research topic. The institute helped us identify the <sup>fi</sup>rms that met the criteria of (1) had not yet adopted eSCMS, (2) had been approached by at least one of its dominant trading partners for eSCMS adoption, and (3) were currently evaluating whether to adopt eSCMS. Further, senior executives of these <sup>fi</sup>rms identi<sup>fi</sup>ed needed to have taken supply chain management training with this institute. It was appropriate for this study to collect data with these senior executives for the following reasons. First, the training programs made these executives knowledgeable about the issues examined by the current research, such as the supply chain management and eSCMS. Second, as senior executives, they had good knowledge about the dependent structure of dyadic relationships with their trading partners. Finally, these executives were required to be familiar with the institutional environment so that they could make strategic decisions such as eSCMS adoption on behalf of their <sup>fi</sup>rms.

We sent out 202 questionnaires addressed to the senior executive ONLY ONE OR SHOULD IT BE PLURAL? from each of the <sup>fi</sup>rms identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. To ensure that respondents would have the same understanding of eSCMS as the authors, we provided a clear de<sup>fi</sup>nition and description of eSCMS at the beginning of the questionnaire. The respondents were required to <sup>fi</sup>rst think of a dominant supplier or customer that had been working with their <sup>fi</sup>rm on eSCMS adoption, and then answer the questionnaire from that <sup>fi</sup>rm's perspective, focusing on the dyadic relationship with this speci<sup>fi</sup>c dominant partner. We received 151 returned questionnaires (17 incomplete questionnaires were discarded) resulting in 134 useful questionnaires and a response rate of approximately 66%. We tested the non-response bias by the method suggested by Armstrong and Overton [5]. That is, the chi-squares of the measurement items of the responses from the <sup>fi</sup>rst 25% of the respondents were compared with that of the <sup>fi</sup>nal 25%. Also, we compared the possible differences in <sup>fi</sup>rm size, as well as IT department and industries size within these two groups of samples. Our results indicated no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between these two groups on key measures. The demography of these samples is shown in Table 1.

## 4.2. Measures

We <sup>fi</sup>rst developed an English questionnaire based on previously validated measures. All items were measured with 7-point Likert scales, ranging from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree.” The measurement items for the dependent variable, namely eSCMS adoption intention, were adapted from Ajzen and Fishbein [1]. Noteworthy is that the Internetenabled SCMS was in its infancy in the Chinese market and that these items focused on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's Internet-enabled SCMS adoption intention. However, with the prevalence of the Internet and the high potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the Internet-enabled SCMS, more and more <sup>fi</sup>rms were evaluating whether to adopt this innovation. Thus, we considered it more appropriate to measure <sup>fi</sup>rms' adoption intention rather than actual adoption.

The items measuring the six power sources were adapted from Brown et al.'s [15] work, and the items for measuring inter-organizational trust were adapted from Cummings and Bromiley [23]. We used the scales from Teo et al. [100] and Khalifa and Davison [53] to measure institutional pressures. In particular, coercive pressures were measured by two subconstructs: customer pressures and supplier pressures, with three items for each pressure type. Normative pressures were measured by three formative items on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived prevalence of eSCMS in its industry. For the control variable, <sup>fi</sup>rm size, we used the total number of full-time employees for measurement. Also, dependence was measured using a three-item scale adapted from Premkumar and Ramamurthy [84], which assessed the level of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's dependence on the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm. Given that the survey was conducted in China, we translated the questionnaire into Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rst and then back-translated it into English so as to ensure equivalence of meaning between the English and Chinese versions. The measurement item numbers and sources are provided in Table 2.

Meanwhile, the <sup>fi</sup>rst factor of these ten factors did not account for the majority of the variance (only 20.88%). ## 5.2.2. Test of mediating effects

To test the mediating effects of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived coercive pressures and normative pressures, we adopted the threestep method suggested by Baron and Kenney [8]. As Table 7 shows, the direct links between mediated power and adoption intention, and between non-mediated power and adoption intention were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and thus satis<sup>fi</sup>ed the <sup>fi</sup>rst condition for mediating effect. The links between mediated power and both perceived coercive and normative pressures were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. The second condition for mediating effect was thus not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed; therefore, coercive pressures and normative pressures did not mediate the relationship between mediated power and adoption intention. In contrast, the links between mediated power and trust, between non-mediated power and trust, between non-mediated power and coercive pressures, and between non-mediated power and normative pressures were all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. As such, they satis<sup>fi</sup>ed the second condition for the existence of mediating effects. Furthermore, the direct relationships between mediated power and adoption intention, and between non-mediated power and adoption intention became insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant when we added the link between trust and adoption intention, between coercive pressures and adoption intention, and between normative pressures and adoption intention, respectively, while the latter links were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Therefore, the results show that the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust fully mediated the relationship between the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption intention. Also, the relationship between the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's non-mediated power and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption was fully mediated by the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust, perceived coercive pressures and normative pressures.

## 6. Discussion and conclusions

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the effects of trust, power and institutional pressures are not only consistent with prior studies, but also offers new empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the association between power and trust and institutional pressures in driving the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm to adopt eSCMS. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we show that the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures, both coercive and normative, have a positive impact on eSCMS adoption. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with previous research [e.g., [44,61,100]. Also, the results indicate that the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's non-mediated power affects the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption through its positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived coercive and normative pressures. In contrast, the effects of dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption are mediated by its negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with what has been proposed by prior studies [e.g., [19,43]. The <sup>fi</sup>nding of the negative relationship between the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's eSCMS adoption intention is in accord with Hart and Saunders [44] and Son et al. [95].

Our hypotheses on the relationship between the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power and both the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative and coercive pressures are not supported. There are two possible explanations. First, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived institutional pressures are based on its evaluation of the expectations, practices and requirements imposed by its institutional environment, which includes all its suppliers, customers, competitors, professional associations and government agencies. As a constituent in the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's institutional environment, the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm may affect the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived pressures. However, its exercised mediated power arouses the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's negative feelings and the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm may be resistant to include the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's request in its evaluation of the environment. Another explanation is that, with the presence of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm does not internalize the value of integrating its system with that of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's. As such, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm does not sense the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's request as a valid requirement or professional practice of the institutional environment. We propose that future research further should investigate the linkage between the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's mediated power and both the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived normative and coercive pressures.

Before we discuss the theoretical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance and managerial implications, it is important to highlight the limitations of the current study. First, the demography of our research sample might limit the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. To reduce random errors caused by the differences of industries, we purposely chose to study <sup>fi</sup>rms in the manufacturing and services industries. Consequently, our research <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be applicable to other contexts. We recognize that generalizability troubles all survey-based research because it is very dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, to obtain a sample that could be claimed to be truly representative of the population. However, we do hope that future research will be conducted with samples from diverse backgrounds and different industries.

Second, we used the convenient list of executives identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the institute; this might have caused a bias in our research results. Nevertheless, the particular list is appropriate for the purpose of the current research for the following reasons: (1) all respondents were from <sup>fi</sup>rms that had not adopted eSCMS but were making the adoption decision; (2) the institutes trained many senior executives on supply chain management in general, and leveraging IT to enhance supply chain agility in particular; (3) executives identi<sup>fi</sup>ed were in good positions to <sup>fi</sup>ll out the questionnaires as they made critical interorganizational decisions on the behalf of their organizations; (4) there were other constituents that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perception of institutional pressures. Given that the current study focuses on the effects of the dominant <sup>fi</sup>rm's power, we did not include other possible antecedents of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm's perceived pressures in our research model. As indicated in the data analysis results, the variances explained of both coercive and normative pressures were relatively low, 0.136 and 0.068, respectively. Future research may consider incorporating other factors affecting perceived pressures in the research model.

A <sup>fi</sup>nal limitation is that the items we used to test the six types of power sources were adopted from western studies. It is acknowledged that a Chinese <sup>fi</sup>rm's understanding of these types of power may be different from that of a western <sup>fi</sup>rm due to cultural differences, such as Chinese high collectivism, high power distance and guanxi culture [105]. Such differences may explain why the weights of legitimate and referent power are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in our model. We appeal to future research to further investigate this issue and develop instrument scales that better serve the Chinese context.

From the theoretical perspective, our research extends current understanding of drivers for eSCMS adoption. In particular, we clearly differentiate different types of power and examine their varying effects on eSCMS adoption. Prior studies in information systems tend to treat power as a simple construct and ignore its different sources and types [e.g., [2,44]. Indeed, our research helps explain the mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the effect of power on IOS adoption. As an IOS that spans organizational boundaries, eSCMS can be a double-edged sword, and trading partners' power may have a critical effect on the adoption of eSCMS. Our research indicates that such effect is contingent on types of power exercised by the dominant partner. Hence, studies treating power as a unitary construct may lead to inaccurate research results.

This study also contributes to the literature by integrating the perspectives of institutional and socio-political theories. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine the mediating effects of the relational exchange factor (trust) and institutional factors (coercive and normative pressures) in the relationship between power and eSCMS adoption, and purport that these mediating effects vary between different types of power. To the best of our knowledge, our research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to conduct such a theoretical and empirical examination. Prior studies have focused on the direct effect of these factors and have examined them in separate models, thus forgoing the opportunity to have an in-depth understanding of the in<sup>fl</sup>uencing processes of these factors. Therefore, the current study enriches literature on IOS adoption in general and eSCMS adoption in particular by providing insights into the interaction of the factors that are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as signi<sup>fi</sup>cant antecedents of IOS adoption.

This study has several implications for practice. Millions of dollars have been invested in information systems to help <sup>fi</sup>rms gain competitive bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, these investments may not reach their highest level of ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency if <sup>fi</sup>rms do not integrate their systems across organizational boundaries, and leverage IS to achieve operational and strategic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in order to derive competitive advantage [44,86,99]. Therefore, it is critical for managers of dominant <sup>fi</sup>rms to exercise their power to motivate their partners to adopt eSCMS. Our research offers them guidance and knowledge.

It is important for managers to realize the different effects of mediated power and non-mediated power on partners' decision making. Based on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the opposite effects of different types of power, <sup>fi</sup>rms should apply non-mediated power and avoid using mediated-power. Using mediated power would increase con<sup>fl</sup>icts and have a negative effect on their partners' trust, thus damaging the trading relationship [44]. Even worse, mediated power would have a negative impact on trading partners' eSCMS adoption. We hope that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the positive effects of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm's trust and perceived institutional pressures will guide the dominant players in the market to leverage their social and relational capital in formulating cooperative relationships with trading partners.
