## Abstract

In general, works on information system (IS) project success focus on either project success factors or project success criteria, neglecting the relationships (mechanisms) between these concepts. Accordingly, it is mainly unclear how success factors actually contribute to success criteria. We attempt to illuminate this black box by conducting semi-structured qualitative interviews with experienced IS project managers by applying Repertory Grid and Laddering. Our exploratory study yields numerous success mechanisms, revealing project-related motivation to be the central hub in the factors-criteria network. Factors preceding projectrelated motivation such as transparency, certainty, and management attention are described.

Keywords: Information systems, Project success, Success criteria, Success factors, Repertory Grid, Laddering

## 1 Introduction

Information system (IS) project success has a long research tradition (Agarwal and Rathod 2006; Davis 2016; Ginzberg 1981; Narayanaswamy et al. 2013; Pankratz and Basten 2013; Wateridge 1995; Yetton et al. 2000). In general, works on project success focus either on achieving or measuring success (Ika 2009). In the former case, emphasis is placed on investigating project success factors, that is, the independent input variables that contribute to the success of IS projects. Researchers have made substantial effort investigating (IS) project success factors (Barclay and Osei-Bryson 2009; Belassi and Tukel 1996; Cooke-Davies 2002; Davis 2014; Hyväri 2006; Kendra and Taplin 2004; Pankratz and Loebbecke 2011; Petter 2008; Pinto and Slevin 1988; Yetton et al. 2000). Many factors emerged from this research, for example, communication, top management support, and the management of user expectations. In the latter case, measuring IS project success, the focus is on identifying IS project success criteria, that is, the dependent output variables by which success is judged. Much attention has been paid to this topic as well (Agarwal and Rathod 2006; Aladwani 2002; Davis 2016; Karlsen et al. 2005; Nelson 2005; Savolainen et al. 2012; Thomas and Fernández 2008; Wateridge 1998). As the traditional approach to measure success by using planning-related criteria, such as meeting time, budget, and requirement targets, has been deemed insufficient, additional criteria have been proposed by numerous authors. Among others, satisfaction of the various stakeholder groups is said to be critical for project success assessments (Baccarini 1999; Karlsen et al. 2005). This is in line with the prevalent view in research that success is a matter of perspective rather than an objective concept (Ika 2009).

However, while these two research streams on project success have received much attention, considerably less is known about the relationships between factors and criteria. Researchers have pointed out that many works on (IS) project success focus on the input and output variables of this concept but do not address the relationship between them (Doherty et al. 2012; Ika 2009; Siau et al. 2010a; Tan 1996). Following Tan (1996), in his review of works on project success, Ika (2009, p. 9) emphasizes that “very few articles discuss both success factors and success criteria, and few empirical studies have sought to examine important links between [critical success factors] and success criteria.” Siau et al. (2010a, p. 88) similarly conclude that “The IS [development] process is either skimmed through in literature or mated as a black box.” Accordingly, it is mainly unclear how success factors actually contribute to success criteria. Only if those impacts are known and understood can project managers take effective measures to purposefully affect success criteria in a concrete project. In this paper, we attempt to open the prevailing black box by investigating the mechanisms of IS project success, that is, by examining the impacts of success factors on other success factors and ultimately on success criteria (for a detailed definition of the term mechanisms, see Section 2.1). We thus set out to answer the following research question:

## How do IS project success factors impact IS project success criteria?

We address this question by conducting an empirical qualitative study among experienced IS project managers. We focus on this particular stakeholder group as IS project managers have deep insights into projects and are directly involved in both achieving and measuring project success. The Repertory Grid Technique (RepGrid) and Laddering are applied to uncover the mechanisms between the success factors and criteria and to generate theoretical insights from data. Illuminating the black box of IS project mechanisms with regard to factors-criteria relationships, we contribute to research and practice in the following ways. Researchers obtain a detailed picture of the impact of success factors, including interdependencies among factors and their impact on success criteria. Analyzing factor interrelations and the resulting chains to success, we uncover factors that are the key nodes in these chains, representing the

#

pivotal factors for project success. These findings add to a more advanced understanding of the elusive concept of IS project success and enable subsequent inquiries, for example, indepth investigations of success factors that are pivotal to IS projects. Practitioners gain insights into the perspective of IS project managers on the mechanisms of IS project success and can use our findings to lead their projects more successfully. In addition, a view on the actual input and output variables, including success factors and criteria, emerges as a byproduct of our investigation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section provides the theoretical background on IS project success. We then describe our research approach by presenting the fundamentals of RepGrid and explaining its design in our context. Afterward, we present our results, including numerous quotes from our interviews. Subsequently, we discuss our results by suggesting propositions for future research, discussing implications for researchers and practitioners, and pointing out the limitations of our research. The article ends with a short conclusion.

## 2 Theoretical Background on IS Project Success

To provide an extensive, up-to-date theoretical background on IS project success, we conducted a literature review in IS (AIS Top 50 List; see https://aisnet.org/general/custom.asp?page=JournalRankings) and project management outlets (Project Management Journal and International Journal of Project Management). Because this concept has a long research tradition, we did not restrict the timescale of the considered studies but use both older and newer works to present a comprehensive picture. We used the terms information system\*, information technology (IT), software, project, success\*, performance, factor\*, criter\*, and mechanism\* in our search, which was then complemented with a forward search based on the identified studies. In the following, we define and distinguish the relevant concepts and present related works on IS project success.

## 2.1 Relevant Concepts

A project is generally defined as “a temporary endeavor undertaken to create a unique product, service, or result” (Project Management Institute 2013, p. 3). An IS can be defined as “a work system whose processes and activities are devoted to processing information, that is, capturing, transmitting, storing, retrieving, manipulating, and displaying information. Thus, an IS is a system in which human participants and/or machines perform work (processes and activities) using information, technology, and other resources to produce informational products and/or services for internal or external customers” (Alter 2008, p. 451). An IS includes both information technology artifacts, that is, hardware and software, and business artifacts, that is, processes, organizational structures, informational flows, and so on (Aier et al. 2011). Combining these definitions, an IS project can be seen as a project in the above terms to develop, extend, or adapt an IS. It should be noted that researchers use the terms IS (development) projects (e.g., Siau et al. 2010a), IT projects (e.g., de Bakker et al. 2010), and software (development) projects (e.g., Savolainen et al. 2012) synonymously without stressing the differences between these denotations. Some studies even use combinations of these terms (“IS/IT projects,” de Bakker et al. 2011; Wateridge 1998). In this study, we use IS projects even when referring to studies that use a different term.

At this point, we emphasize the conceptual difference between IS success and IS project success (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014), which follows from the distinction between an IS and a project to develop an IS. While IS success denotes the success of an IS as a product, IS project success refers to the performance concerning the unique and temporary process of

#

creating an IS. Regarding the assessment of the former, the model suggested by DeLone and McLean (1992) is widely acknowledged (Petter and McLean 2009). In the updated version of this framework, information quality, system quality, service quality, use/intention to use, user satisfaction, and net benefits are suggested as the six interdependent constituents of IS success (DeLone and McLean 2003). Numerous studies have been conducted using IS success as a dependent variable and illuminating this topic from different angles. Selected examples include investigating the effect of user participation and involvement on IS success (Abelein et al. 2013), exploring the current state of IS success research through a literature review (Urbach et al. 2009), exploring and enhancing factors that contribute to effective realization of benefits from system developments (Doherty et al. 2012), and extending the DeLone & McLean model by suggesting additional dimensions (business value, institutional trust, and future readiness; Green et al. 2014). Because many scholars (Baccarini 1999; Basten and Pankratz 2015; Collins and Baccarini 2004; Liu et al. 2011; Nelson 2005) consider the concept (IS) project success as consisting of process and product success as the two major components (a more detailed discussion of the concept IS project success is provided in Section 2.3), product success assessments might be part of or overlap with project success assessments. However, IS success and IS project success should not be equated because IS success does not capture one of the essentials of any IS project—the development process. Furthermore, the criterion of product success should not simply be adopted for IS project success to avoid potential deviations. The success assessments of the product as a whole and the product as part of a project might, for example, take place at different points in time and differ accordingly. Substantiating the argument not to equalize IS success and IS project success, DeLone in one of his works uses multiple IS project success criteria (Espinosa et al. 2006), only four of which originate from the updated DeLone and McLean (2003) model. Moreover, most scholars do not refer to that model at all when analyzing IS project success (Nelson 2005; Savolainen et al. 2012; Wateridge 1998). Taking the conceptual distinction of these two concepts into account, this study places emphasis on the concept of IS project success.

The vast majority of works on IS project success focus on either achieving success or assessing success. The distinction between these two concepts is commonly emphasized in literature (Cooke-Davies 2002; Davis 2014; Ika 2009; Savolainen et al. 2012). Research focusing on achieving success investigates success factors (independent input variables), which are project characteristics that directly or indirectly contribute to the success of the project. In contrast, assessing success is a matter of criteria (dependent output variables), which are measures by which the success of a project is judged. Factors do not necessarily contribute to success directly; rather, there is a multilevel network of factors that ultimately lead to success criteria (e.g., Pankratz and Loebbecke, 2011). We refer to these multilevel relationships as mechanisms, defining this term as follows.

From an ontological perspective, mechanisms refer to recurrent processes that link conditions to specific outcomes (Mayntz 2004). Studies in IS research refer to mechanisms in diverging ways and define the concept, for example, as “causal structures that generate observable events” (Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013, p. 911) and “processes composed of entities, actions, and events that produce change” (Avgerou 2013, p. 429). Based on a review of definitions for the concept, it is concluded that “it is difficult to propose a mechanism definition that would both be informative and cover all examples of mechanisms” (Hedström and Ylikoski 2010, p. 50). Accordingly, an ambiguity exists in the use of the notion. Mechanisms are typically referred to either as causal processes leading to an effect of interest or as critical elements of these causal processes (Hedström and Ylikoski 2010). However, most definitions incorporate the idea that mechanisms are identified by the type of effect they produce (here: IS project

#

success) and have a causal notion that leads to the effect (here: IS project success factors). Moreover, mechanisms form a hierarchy, that is, mechanisms at lower levels explain mechanisms at higher levels (here: factor chains). Finally, mechanisms have a structure that is disclosed when “a mechanism-based explanation opens the black box […] and turns the black box into a transparent box” (Hedström and Ylikoski 2010, p. 51). In line with these general characteristics, we refer to mechanisms of IS project success as the causal relationships that link success factors to other success factors and, ultimately, success criteria.

We present the theoretical background on success factors, success criteria, and, ultimately, the research gap on IS project success mechanisms in the following sections.

## 2.2 Success Factors

Project success factors have exercised the minds of researchers and practitioners for decades. In striving to understand how to make projects more successful, researchers have undertaken major efforts to investigate these factors, which produced various streams in literature.

One research stream identifies and groups project success factors in general (e.g., Belassi and Tukel 1996; Cooke-Davies 2002; Davis 2014; Hyväri 2006). Prominent success factors such as top management support (Pinto and Slevin 1988), communication (Hyväri 2006), and goal commitment of the project team (Baker et al. 2008) emerged from this research stream. While many of these factors are also applicable to IS projects, this should be done with care because IS projects exhibit peculiarities that distinguish them from other projects (Fuller et al. 2008). For example, IS projects’ progress is less transparent, they are more prone to technological changes, and they differ from previous IS projects, resulting in a lack of experience data. Additionally, the establishment of project objectives is impeded by complicated user requirements specifications (vom Brocke and Lippe 2010).

Consequently, a more specific research stream focuses on success factors in IS projects, considering their peculiarities. Here again, different types of studies exist. First, there are works that identify and group success factors in IS projects in general (Aladwani 2002; Kendra and Taplin 2004; Nelson 2007; Wateridge 1995). While many of the earlier works on both project success factors in general and IS project success factors in particular attempted to derive generic lists of factors that would apply to a wide range of projects (e.g., Baker et al. 2008; Pinto and Slevin 1988; Wateridge 1995), the discussion of contingency theory in the project context challenged this perspective in the 1990s. The basic argument of the advocates of contingency theory is that there is no one-size-fits-all approach; rather, the project context needs to be taken into account (Dvir et al. 1998; Shenhar and Dvir 2007). Accordingly, researchers increasingly tend to apply a specific focus when investigating sets of IS project success factors. This focus can be a specific stakeholder perspective (e.g., IS project managers (Pankratz and Loebbecke 2011), software developers (Procaccino et al. 2005), senior IS executives (Doherty and King 2001), and comparisons of different stakeholder groups (Davis 2014)), the type of contractor-client relationship (e.g., in-house development (Verner and Evanco 2005)), or the type of IS that is being developed (e.g., enterprise resource planning systems (Ram et al. 2014), global IS (Biehl 2007), and integrated healthcare IS (Hung et al. 2014)).

On a more specific level, a large number of studies have been conducted by scholars who preselect single factors and scrutinize their impact on project success. Examples of some common, widely acknowledged factors include top management support (Young and Jordan 2008), user training (Sharma and Yetton 2007), and management of user expectations (Petter 2008). Some of the more recent studies address IS project success factors such as status

#

reporting (Keil et al. 2014), the influence of executive sponsors (Kloppenborg and Tesch 2015), organizational culture and environmental pressures (Gu et al. 2014), monitoring and shirking (Mahaney and Lederer 2010), and software process diversity (Ramasubbu et al. 2015). Often, researchers investigate preselected factors in specific contexts, narrowing the focus even more. For example, one study examines coordination within the project team as well as between the client and the vendor organizations from the vendor’s perspective (Gopal et al. 2011), while another investigates the impact of coordination in consultant-assisted IS projects (Liberatore and Luo 2010). Another example is the study of the impact of project team attributes on ERP system implementations, which suggests that established beliefs regarding project success might not apply in an ERP adoption context (Rothenberger et al. 2010).

Overall, while this considerable body of research produced success factors that are widely acknowledged, there is still no clear set of universally agreed-on project success factors (Cooke-Davies 2002; Davis 2014; Hyväri 2006). This is not surprising considering that success is a matter of perspective (Basten et al. 2011; Ika 2009; Shenhar and Dvir 2007) and given the contingency perspective outlined above. In this study, we focus on one specific stakeholder perspective—IS project managers.

Factors contributing to project success are, by their nature, dependent on how success is judged. Therefore, investigating factors that influence project success goes hand in hand with the definition of the latter. As stressed over 20 years ago, “[i]t does little good to measure various independent or input variables […] if the dependent or output variable […] cannot be measured with a similar degree of accuracy. […] Without a well-defined dependent variable, much of I/S research is purely speculative” (DeLone and McLean 1992, p. 61). For this reason, a discussion of the constituent components of project success, the success criteria, is needed at this point.

## 2.3 Success Criteria

As (IS) projects are typically defined with regard to cost, schedule, and requirements (Nicholas and Steyn 2012), their success is traditionally assessed in terms of adherence to planning (ATP), that is, adherence to budget, adherence to schedule, and conformance with specified requirements (Ika 2009; Turner and Zolin 2012). However, numerous scholars express serious doubts concerning ATP’s sufficiency as the sole criterion for IS project success for various reasons (Agarwal and Rathod 2006; Basten et al. 2011; Judgev and Müller 2005; Pinto 2004; Turner and Zolin 2012). For example, the lack of adequate methods to estimate budget and schedule (Agarwal and Rathod 2006) often results in inaccurate estimates as the basis for project plans (Moløkken-Østvold et al. 2004), and negotiations or political actions often lead to biased plans (Lederer et al. 1990). The insufficiency of ATP is substantiated by many projects that meet the plans but are perceived as failures and projects that exceed the planned budget and schedule many times over but are still considered a success (Ika 2009; Nelson 2005; Savolainen et al. 2012).

A common approach to resolve this mismatch is the differentiation between project management success and project success (Cooke-Davies 2002; Ika 2009; Judgev and Müller 2005; Savolainen et al. 2012). While the former, narrower concept reflects the traditional measures of ATP, the latter concept is assessed against the overall objectives of the project, thus incorporating the long-term perspective. Project success goes beyond “the strict adherence to the ‘time/cost/quality triangle’—the most common objective of project management” (Ika 2009, p. 8). For example, Turner and Zolin (2012) address the question of how stakeholders of large projects perceive success months or years after the project output is delivered. The authors argue that in large projects, the long-term perspective is all the more relevant since the outputs of such projects have ultimate impacts that last for years or even decades. In this context in particular, “the wretched golden triangle of project success (time, cost, and quality) is an inadequate indicator of project success, and that success is not just related to completion of the project’s scope of work, but also to the achievement of business objectives” (Turner and Zolin 2012, p. 87).

A related approach to defining the overall concept project success is to differentiate between process success (corresponds to project management success) and product success as its two major components (Baccarini 1999; Basten and Pankratz 2015; Collins and Baccarini 2004; Liu et al. 2011). Other researchers make a similar differentiation by considering (process) efficiency and (product) effectiveness as project success’ two key measures (Liu et al. 2011). Overall, as these approaches show, there is a consensus in literature that ATP is important but insufficient for project success assessment: “Project success is measured not just by completion of the scope of work to time, cost, and quality, but also by performance of the project’s outputs, outcomes, and impacts, and thereby the achievement of the desired business objectives, as assessed by different stakeholders over different timescales” (Turner and Zolin 2012, pp. 87-88).

Hence, further criteria such as process efficiency (Baccarini 1999; Thomas and Fernández 2008) and stakeholder satisfaction (Baccarini 1999; Karlsen et al. 2005) have also been proposed over the years. In a review of the literature concerning external IS projects from the contractor’s perspective, customer satisfaction, short-term business benefits, and long-term business benefits have been revealed as success criteria (Savolainen et al. 2012). However, while researchers agree on the multi-dimensionality of project success and process and product as its components, the overall set of criteria is inconsistent, as diverging approaches show (Baccarini 1999; Karlsen et al. 2005; Nelson 2005). As Davis (2016, p. 481) concludes, “[t]here are many literature reviews, which comprehensively discuss project success […], but the definition of success is inconsistent.” Taking into account that success is a matter of perspective as described above, this is not surprising. In line with studies of success factors, we observe a tendency toward investigations of success criteria in specific contexts and from specific stakeholder perspectives (Basten and Pankratz 2015; Davis 2016; McLeod et al. 2012; Savolainen et al. 2012).

## 2.4 IS Project Success Mechanisms—Relationships between Success Factors and Criteria

While both success factors and success criteria have received much attention in research, significantly less emphasis has been placed on the relationships between them. Considering that project management has its roots in the middle of the 20th century, it is remarkable that “it was only in 1995 that concerns began to emerge for discovering links between project success factors and project success criteria” (Ika 2009, p. 12). With regard to projects in general, “[v]ery little research has touched upon […] how the success factor is related to success criteria. […] Empirical study linking success criteria and success factors is even harder to find” (Tan 1996, pp. 46-47). Over a decade later, when conducting a review of research on project success, Ika (2009, pp. 11-12) still concludes that “studies on project success are too often focused on success factors and not often enough on the criteria or, more specifically, the links between success factors and success criteria.” Two of the studies identified in that review, which do touch on both criteria and factors, name factors that contribute to project management success (thus, focusing on ATP criteria as described above) and factors that contribute to the overall success (Munns and Bjeirmi 1996; Westerveld 2003).

Similarly, Clarke (1999) shows how, by focusing on four success factors, project management can become more effective. She follows Belassi and Tukel (1996) by emphasizing that success factors are interdependent and their interaction is of critical importance for project success. Belassi and Tukel (1996) themselves provide a framework that includes factor groups and the relationships between them. However, while linking the two concepts and considering factors or criteria groups, the existing works still do not examine in detail how factors are related to each other and to the criteria. Referring to the more specific field of IS projects, a similar observation is made: “The IS [development] process is either skimmed through in literature or mated as a black box” (Siau et al. 2010a, p. 88). Along these lines, Doherty et al. (2012, p. 3) remark, “all too often there is a serious disconnect between success factors and project success, so that it becomes difficult to discern any clear causal relationships.” One earlier work, for example, provides lists of both success factors and criteria in IS projects but does not analyze the relationships between them (Wateridge 1998). Treating the relationships between factors and criteria as a black box prevents practitioners from taking effective measures when it comes to purposefully influencing success criteria. In particular, mediating effects (i.e., indirect instead of direct effects that help to clarify the nature of relationships) remain uncovered and limit the understanding of mechanisms underlying IS project success. Selected mediators such as benefits realization management (Serra and Kunc 2015) and expectation management (Basten et al. 2016) have been subject to previous investigations. However, a holistic approach to illuminating the black box between the input variables (success factors) and output variables (success criteria) of IS project success (schematically illustrated in Figure 1) is missing.

While the insights into preselected IS project success mechanisms are valuable, we follow the researchers’ call and attempt to investigate the mechanisms of IS project success by taking a holistic and exploratory approach. For this purpose, we conduct the Repertory Grid Technique complemented by Laddering. This combination not only helps the interviewees elaborate on project success factors starting with whatever aspects they consider relevant, but first and foremost, it allows for tracing the impact of those mentioned factors via other factors all the way to the highest levels, that is, to success criteria.

## 3 Research Approach

We relied on qualitative research because it fits our purpose to explain mechanisms of IS project success and is more suitable compared to quantitative approaches when it comes to complex issues and understanding the respective context (Flick 2014). For data collection, we used RepGrid (Kelly 1955) and its extension, Laddering (Hinkle 1965), which are in line with grounded theory (Glaser and Strauss 1967, 2009; Corbin and Strauss 2015) because insights emerge from the data rather than from researchers’ preexisting theoretical concerns (Rogers and Ryals 2007; Urquhart and Fernández 2013). The combination of RepGrid and grounded theory is particularly effective for studies in under-researched areas (Edwards et al. 2009; Siau et al. 2010b). In the following, we introduce RepGrid and Laddering before providing indetail information about the interview process, our sampling approach, and data analysis.

## 3.1 Repertory Grid Technique and Laddering

RepGrid is an interview technique that George Kelly (1955) developed based on his personal construct theory (for a detailed elaboration of this theory, cf. Fransella et al. 2004). According to this theory, constructs in people’s mental models of the world possess several important qualities. Because people “never affirm anything without simultaneously denying something” (Fransella et al. 2004, p. 7), constructs are bipolar in nature, consisting of an emergent pole

#

and a contrast pole: tall – small (people); innovative – outdated (technology); and innovative – established (technology). As demonstrated by the two latter examples, the contrast pole is crucial to capturing a construct’s whole meaning. Moreover, constructs are hierarchically related, that is, individuals’ personal construct systems are unique in their hierarchical structure of super- and subordinate constructs. Fransella et al. (2004, p. 3) additionally emphasize that constructs are not equivalent to their verbal labels. While constructs exist in people’s minds, their verbal labels describe and communicate these constructs. The distinction is essential when considering Shaw and Gaines’ (1989) distinction between semantic constellations: consensus (the same terminology for the same concepts), correspondence (different terminology for the same concepts), conflict (the same terminology for different concepts), and contrast (different terminology for different concepts). The awareness of the semantic ambiguities and coping with them in an adequate way (e.g., by Laddering; see below) is decisive for the validity of qualitative studies.

Kelly (1955) developed RepGrid to explore people’s personal construct systems. RepGrid is a tool for creating cognitive maps (Eden 1992). When applied in studies of a qualitative nature such as the present study, RepGrid is used by comparing elements (which can be any objects of thoughts, e.g., items, functional departments, or IS projects; cf. Smith 1986) to elicit constructs (which are elements’ qualities) by identifying similarities and/or differences between the elements. For this purpose, various design alternatives need to be considered. Taking the method of triads as an example, the researcher chooses three elements and asks the respondent to think of characteristics in which two of these elements are similar but different from the third. As an alternative approach, two elements are compared for the identification of a similarity or difference between them when using dyads. The method of triads is based on Kelly’s original understanding of how constructs are initially formed. Taking into account that triads are more cognitively exhausting and should preferably be applied to straightforward domains, Fransella et al. (2004) argue that no reason exists for using three elements to elicit constructs that are already established in one’s personal construct system. Tan and Hunter (2002) provide a comprehensive overview of RepGrid’s numerous design alternatives and their corresponding applications.

Among RepGrid’s manifold qualitative applications within the IS field are topics such as examining business and IT thinking (Tan and Gallupe 2006), exploring the skills of successful IT project managers (Napier et al. 2009), and investigating the characteristics of team members (Siau et al. 2010b). This technique provides multiple advantages:

Comparing and characterizing elements eases the elicitation of personal constructs (Curtis et al. 2008). As interviewees are supported in becoming aware of what they know, tacit knowledge is converted to explicit knowledge during the interviews (Jankowicz 2001; Stewart et al. 1981).

RepGrid does not aim to confirm the researcher’s view (Curtis et al. 2008). Rather, it is used to discover how participants construct their world, which is essential for our exploratory study and differentiates this method from traditional survey instruments. In line with this objective, the researcher bias during the conversation is minimized: “[RepGrid] enables one to interview someone in detail, […] and to do this in such a way that the input from the observer is reduced to zero” (Stewart et al. 1981, p. 5). The individual knowledge is captured in an untainted way since constructs emerge and remain in the words of the interviewees.

RepGrid is often complemented by a process called Laddering, which provides a repertoire of additional questions (Rugg et al. 2002). This allows not only to obtain more detailed information (ensuring an accurate understanding of the individual constructs, which is critical due to the potential semantic ambiguities) but also to uncover hierarchical relations between the identified constructs.

The subsequent content analysis of the gathered data (Jankowicz 2001) enables consolidation and categorization of knowledge from individual construct systems, thus reaching a shared understanding of the researched phenomenon.

Overall, RepGrid, in conjunction with Laddering, is particularly suitable for our research objective of uncovering project success mechanisms as described in the following.

Hinkle (1965) developed Laddering as an extension to RepGrid. When applying Laddering, the researcher asks follow-up questions regarding each identified construct. Ladders can thereby move in different directions (Rugg et al. 2002): downward, eliciting explanations or members of classes (by asking questions such as “How could you tell that something was $X ? ^ { \dag }$ or “Can you give me examples of $X ? ^ { \dag }$ with X being the construct pole in question); upward, eliciting information about higher level constructs (“Why would you prefer $X ? ^ { \dag }$ or “Why does X contribute to $\mathrm { Y } ? ? { }$ with Y being the target variable); and sideways, eliciting further constructs at the same hierarchical level (“Can you think of more aspects like X?”). Downward Laddering aims at gathering refined information about the given construct, thus clarifying its meaning (Jankowicz 2004). In this manner, Downward Laddering counteracts the potential semantic ambiguities described above. Upward Laddering is applied to uncover hierarchical relations by identifying superordinate constructs. This procedure quickly leads to top-level constructs, which represent respondents’ personal core beliefs. Sideways Laddering helps respondents identify similar additional constructs. In anticipation of our interviews description, our respondents had no difficulty thinking of new constructs; thus, we did not need to apply Sideways Laddering. However, Downward Laddering and Upward Laddering were crucial in our inquiry as described below.

## 3.2 Interview Process

The application of RepGrid requires multiple design decisions, which depend on the purpose of the study. Table 1 provides the numerous design alternatives of RepGrid studies and our choices, respectively. This overview of the design alternatives is collected from several works on designing RepGrid studies and Laddering (Fransella et al. 2004; Jankowicz 2001; Rugg et al. 2002; Stewart et al. 1981; Tan and Hunter 2002). Figure 2 illustrates (1) the RepGrid- and Laddering-based interview phases that we used to explore our respondents’ subjective views concerning relationships between the factors and criteria of IS project success and (2) the theoretical saturation, which is further explained in Section 3.3.

Having briefly introduced RepGrid and Laddering, we asked the interviewees to name at least four IS projects on which they had worked in the past and of which they had a good recollection. Considering that project management is a complex and cognitively challenging field, we chose the method of dyads (two projects) for element comparison. We asked each project manager to sort all projects according to their degrees of success. To allow for an “easy” start with many constructs for the first pair of projects, we first asked the respondents to compare the most and least successful projects. Our leading question was “Projects can differ in various factors which contribute to project success, for example, human, organizational, technical, methodical factors, general conditions etc. In terms of what such factors do these two projects differ?” Following our data-driven research approach, we paid special attention to not plant any answers but to allow all information to emerge from the interviewees. For each factor identified by the respondents, we applied Downward Laddering to ensure its clear understanding. Subsequently, we applied Upward Laddering to the identified construct by asking “Why does [factor pole positively related to success] contribute to project success?” We then applied Upward Laddering to the emergent hierarchically superordinate construct as well. Continuously following this approach, we finally reached top-level constructs, which are direct sub-constructs of project success (success criteria). The end of the ladders was reached when a respondent answered along the lines of “…this factor leads to X, resulting in project success” (X representing a success criterion). Returning to the dyad at hand, we then asked for additional factors. If a respondent could not think of further constructs as starting points of new ladders for the given dyad, we chose another dyad and repeated the procedure for all project combinations. This approach yielded a multitude of ladders from factors at the bottom of the emerging chains to project success criteria. In this study, such ladders represent the mechanisms of IS project success by illustrating the impact of subordinate factors to superordinate factors and, ultimately, to success criteria.

## 3.3 Sampling Approach

To gain meaningful insights into our research question, we used the approach of purposeful sampling by defining that the respondents needed to name at least four projects that met the following inclusion criteria:

\- In the projects, the respondents worked as project managers on behalf of the contractor;

The projects needed to be completed by the time of the interview, be commissioned by a customer, have the objective of developing an IS, and contain all typical development phases;

\- The respondents remembered the projects well;

Among the projects were both successful and less successful ones (because such contrasting experiences are likely to deepen the understanding of success).

We first interviewed two project managers who could provide at least four projects that met the above inclusion criteria. These two managers were former graduates of our IS department at the university and worked in leading positions with numerous IS project managers as their subordinates. After their interviews, these two respondents helped us identify other project managers who would also be suitable to participate in our study (i.e., snowball approach; see Morse 2010). More precisely, they suggested colleagues who should be able to provide at least four projects according to our criteria and would be willing to be interviewed. We confirmed the inclusion criteria with each potential respondent.

As noted by Glaser and Strauss (2009), a researcher “trying to discover theory cannot state at the outset of his research how many [participants] he will sample during the entire study; he can only count up the [participants] at the end” (p. 61). While the numbers of RepGrid interviews considered appropriate in literature differ (Dillon and McKnight 1990; Dunn and Ginsberg 1986; Hassenzahl 2002), researchers agree that the main objective is theoretical saturation (Glaser and Strauss 1967, 2009; Corbin and Strauss 2015) regarding the identified constructs. Theoretical saturation is reached once no new findings arise in subsequent interviews. It is also referred to as the “point of redundancy” (Siau et al. 2010b), a term introduced by Lincoln and Guba (1985, p. 235). Striving for this point, we analyzed the emerging constructs in consecutive interviews. In particular, after each set of 2-3 interviews, we considered new Laddering constructs that emerged in each interview and compared them to those already identified in previous interviews. Laddering constructs are factors identified using Upward Laddering in the interviews (see also Section 3.2). We chose Laddering constructs as indicators over regular constructs due to our focus on the mechanisms of IS project success. Because Upward Laddering reveals constructs on higher hierarchical levels, these factors are at the very heart of the black box illustrated in Figure 1. Figure 2 depicts both the total amount of emerging Laddering constructs and the amount of new Laddering constructs in the interviews. Because no new Laddering constructs arose in the last six interviews, we are confident of having reached theoretical saturation.

Our final interview sample consists of 11 experienced IS project managers—two females and nine males—working in Germany for the IT service departments of three large organizations in the logistics, IT consulting, and insurance industries. Each interviewee worked on behalf of the contractor on IS projects with an average of 14.3 years in IS development. Table 2 lists our respondents (the names are altered to ensure confidentiality).

## 3.4 Data Analysis

Two researchers, the interviewer plus one, examined the transcripts with the objective of analyzing the ladders collected in the interviews as follows (see also Figure 3). First, the ladders from the interviews were combined by adding ladder segments if two or more ladders shared a factor. For example, two ladders, A-B-C and D-B-E, were combined to the four ladders A-B-C, A-B-E, D-B-E, and D-B-C since we identified two successors of B (C and E) in this schematic example. This combination step yielded a total of 223 ladders, which we consider our data basis across all interviews. It is worth noting at this point that a combination of personal models of different respondents is a valid part of the qualitative analysis in a RepGrid investigation (Jankowicz 2004; Stewart et al. 1981). In this regard, our study conforms to investigations that aggregate cognitive maps from different individuals (Eden 1992; Lee et al. 1992). Furthermore, the context was narrowed by our underlying set of elements, which were predefined to be contracted, completed projects containing all typical phases of developing an IS. Second, we excluded all factors that were only mentioned once by a single interviewee to focus on the most relevant ladders. This reduction led to 202 ladders remaining. Third, we excluded all ladders that had only one factor left as a result of the previous step, leaving 124 ladders. Fourth, we removed all duplicates as well as ladders that were part of other ladders (e.g., ladder A-B-C was removed if another ladder, A-B-C-D, existed). These analysis steps led to a final set of central ladders, which are described and discussed in the following sections.

## 4.2 IS Project Success Mechanisms

## Central ladders

With regard to our research question, our analysis yielded central ladders as illustrated in Figure 4, which we accordingly consider essential for IS project success. In all of them, project-related motivation of team members is found at the top of the ladders as a predecessor of project success criteria. The importance of this factor is further emphasized in several ways. First, it was the only criterion mentioned by all 11 respondents. Second, in several interviews, project-related motivation was the central factor because it was included in almost all emerging ladders. For example, Michael named eight raw constructs, and each ladder to project success included project-related motivation. Third, counting the appearances of the factors in the total scheme of 223 combined ladders (see Section 3.4), project-related motivation is the leading factor with 28 occurrences, followed by transparency with 6. Accordingly, we consider project-related motivation to be the pivotal success factor in the mechanisms of IS project success. Note that the arrows pointing from factors to other factors and, ultimately, to criteria represent the positive impact of a factor on the following factor (criterion) according to the Upward Laddering responses of our interviewees. For example, higher certainty in a project (i.e., reliable plans) was said to contribute to the higher projectrelated motivation of the team members. We accentuate at this point that, as illustrated in Figure 4, the identified central ladders and project-related motivation as a common denominator only show a part of the complete picture. There are other (important) factors contributing to IS project success (e.g., proper project planning) that were mentioned by our respondents. While these other factors and relationships are indicated by the empty boxes and small dots in Figure 4, the portion shown represents the most important mechanisms of IS project success according to our analysis.

As a result of our data analysis, the central ladders that emerged and are depicted in Figure 4 are a combined scheme of all interviews. Therefore, the presented ladders were not necessarily mentioned in their entirety in one of the interviews.<sup>1</sup> In the following, we thus elaborate on the factors in the ladders that emerged by taking a factor-oriented rather than a ladder-oriented perspective. However, because we provide substantiating quotes from the interviews, including answers to Upward Laddering, the arrows between the factors are illuminated as well. Subsequently, we also present all other factors that were identified in the interviews to have an impact on project-related motivation.

## Project-related motivation

So what exactly is project-related motivation? According to our respondents, a distinction needs to be made between project-related motivation and the personal motivation of team

#

members. While the former concerns the concrete project and achieving its success, the latter refers to team members’ motivation related to work in the given enterprise, industry, and environment. As Frank stated, “On the one hand, it is the joint bearing of the project, which includes its goals and approach, not so much the personal attitude towards the work, which is the motivation: what does it look like in the enterprise? How does the work environment motivate me? Do I feel connected with this enterprise?” While it is conceivable that one of these types of motivation is a specific type of the other, we consider them to be at the same hierarchical level because it was the image portrayed by our respondents.

As described in Section 3.1, personal constructs have two poles, which are essential to capturing the whole meaning. For project-related motivation, the pole associated with higher success was, unsurprisingly, high motivation. The contrast pole was mentioned as being a “lack of motivation” or “routine business.” Downward Laddering revealed concrete insights into this central construct of our respondents. For example, when answering “How could you tell that people were motivated with regard to the project? Can you give me examples?” Frank explained, “Everyone is 100% willing to play a part in achieving success and doing their jobs. To phrase it more concretely, highly motivated people are still sitting here at 10 pm if it is required. [...] Unmotivated people throw the keyboard at your feet at 5:30 pm and are gone. Motivated people who really want to find the solution stay and keep looking.” Thomas added, “People identify with the project, that is, they perceive, take on, and implement the tasks given to them.”

Regarding the impact of high project-related motivation on project success, all success criteria identified in our study except for system is used by end users<sup>2</sup> are influenced by this central factor. As an example of its impact on non-functional requirements (Upward Laddering: “Why does project-related motivation contribute to project success?”), Michael explained: “Higher motivation leads to more precise, responsible actions. When I am motivated, I pay more attention and want to play a larger part. I know that my work is appreciated and respected. For it to be this way, the work must be good – and not just good, but excellent. That is, with motivation, I am willing to produce excellent results, that is, I do not just want to achieve the goal – I want to exceed it. I do not just want to code something – referring to systems development – that works somehow; I strive to make it really nice, that is, to look a little more to see whether there are error sources, whether everything was considered, whether I coded solidly on all levels. Straightforward code can be written down quickly. I do not need to display high motivation or be particularly diligent for that. Quality appears when I can push myself to ensure that all potential errors are intercepted, making my application robust. Robustness makes the product stable and fail-safe. I only manage to do that when I am confident that what I am doing is meaningful. If I am not motivated, I just work through my requirements. This can work, but there should be no malfunctions or everything collapses.”

## Certainty

Certainty was one of the often-mentioned factors that contribute to project-related motivation. It refers to the certainty that team members feel in the project, which is given when plans are reliable. Elaborating on the impact of a clearly defined scope (Upward Laddering: “Why does a clearly defined scope contribute to project success?”), Michael stated: “It is reliable, stable over the project course, and thus predictable, which reduces uncertainties. Uncertainties always lead to discomfort and anxiety.” In response to the Upward Laddering (Why is certainty important for project success?), Michael explained: “I can rely on the plans and do not need to worry about planning, that is, I can put 100% of my work performance into the task and do not need to think about what can go wrong, what uncertainties there are in the project. My thoughts would be there instead of with my work. [With certainty,] there are no distractions, and higher motivation and better results are seen.”

## Progress is measurable

Another factor contributing to project-related motivation is measurable project progress. When progress is measurable, it directly and indirectly (increasing certainty; see Figure 4) contributes to project-related motivation. Explaining the impact of a structured project approach (Upward Laddering: “Why does a structured approach contribute to project success?”), Frank clarified: “[Following a structured approach,] the results that you achieve become predictable. You can predefine them, predict them, and ensure their quality. Did I really get the result that I need in the next step? Then you obtain a clear sequence of deliverables. […] These deliverables are clearly defined: what has to be there, what should be there, and what I need for my next step. I can review that and say, ‘Either I do a little rework here or I proceed with the next step.’ The whole thing becomes observable, verifiable, comprehensible, and clearly leads to project success. That does not mean that there are no changes, however. […] In every project, there are changes, but still, those projects are conducted in a clearly defined, systematic, documented way, including the willingness of all involved and without reducing motivation.”

## High project value

Next, our respondents emphasized the value of a project as a driver for motivation. Bernd explained, “This was a project that was not really connected to the value creation of the organization; it was rather a necessary renovation. You have to make investments to reduce costs for the infrastructure and operations. […] It was a project, yet not a project where I say ‘I open up a new market or introduce an innovation’ […] but ‘we make our lives easier by tidying up our room’ […] High project value vs. low value. In this project, I reorganized an organization or part of it to pursue a whole new strategy. It is a glossy brochure. In comparison, it is like here I renovated the house by installing thermal performance and a solar panel system, and here I cleared out the basement.” David noted in this regard that higher value contributes to the prominence and prestige of the project, increasing motivation: “These are common, boring projects. This is coupled with motivation. It seems to be the central aspect for me when I think about it; since it is, of course, nice for somebody to say, ‘I am on this project that everybody knows about’, and everybody says ‘Wow, these are the best guys’ – of course it increases motivation. […] Every human is looking for something that offers prestige. Professionally, I can increase the motivation with it, which then increases the success of my project. That is, I can use prestige as a means to lead the project to success.”

## Management attention and commitment

Our respondents described management attention and commitment as two related factors in which both contribute to project-related motivation. Attention is given when management shows interest in the project, for example, by regularly requesting information about the

#

progress. Commitment refers to management’s willingness to support the project, for example, by providing resources and making critical decisions when necessary. Describing the difference in management attention between two projects, Frank stated, “This one was a project on a small scale. The management did not pay much attention to it. They said, ‘Just do it’, whereas this one was a project that was tracked even at the highest management level, and it was monitored accordingly – project status reports and so on. It was also clearly communicated who was in charge. Therefore, management attention is important to quickly lead projects to success.” In response to the following Upward Laddering (“Why does management attention contribute to success?”), he further explained, “Because you get the support you need. There are always problems and obstacles that you have to crawl through, and with attention and support, you do not need to crawl; you just jump over it. There is quickly a statement like this: ‘This is how we are going to do it. Decision made.’” Along these lines, Christian reported on lower management attention and commitment in a project that had to be conducted for legal reasons, compared to another project that was desired and initiated by upper management. “As a project manager,” he said, “you cannot just act. You need better support, that is, getting resources more easily, getting resources more quickly, decisions being made quickly, which is very important.”

It is important to clarify at this point that the presented arrows in Figure 4 do not preclude other arrows or arrangements of factors. For example, one may argue that management attention and commitment are the same factor or that the latter leads to the former instead of the other way around. We emphasize in this regard that our analysis is based on the statements of our respondents only. Since management attention was said to lead to management commitment (cf. Frank’s quote above), this impact is presented in our scheme accordingly.

## Transparency

Several respondents emphasized the importance of creating transparency for all stakeholders. Torben defined it and described its implications as follows: “My customer and my project team, and suppliers if we have any, know the plan and know the current project situation. Not in every aspect but in areas relevant to them. And they can contribute to make the project successful. If there is an issue, a problem that we currently face, they can work on it and fix it. Also, as the case may be, they spot dependencies that only they can see. Another aspect is that transparency leads to openness and trust. A counterexample is that for six months, everything is green, and then it switches to red and nobody knows why. With transparency, I can create trust when I say: ‘We have a potential delay here. We will do everything we can to prevent it’, but if it happens, then it is a known problem. If I only say ‘everything is great’ prior to that, then the affected people are, of course, very surprised.” Regarding the impact of transparency on team members, Torben elaborated further: “With transparency, I give the team members more responsibility. They are involved, informed of the whole project and not just their part of it. Seeing the whole picture, they usually feel and work more responsibly for the project. They think, ‘there is a problem, maybe not in my activities, but I might have an idea about how to solve it’.” Bernd mentioned good, high-quality communication as an important enabler of transparency. Explaining the impact of such communication, he stated, “All affected people are better informed; there are fewer misunderstandings, and people are happier since they feel involved. They do not feel ignored.” Regarding the method of communication to create transparency, he mentioned several aspects: “You can quantify the frequency of communication. Are there newsletters? Do I see people talk to each other or do they just sit

#

and work at their desks? Frequency is important, but quality, too. Do I think about who needs information via which communication channel, or do I just do it ad-hoc?”

## Early-warning system

As mentioned in the description of transparency, it is important to recognize problems at early stages of the project. Our respondents often reported on projects where an early-warning system did not exist and problems seemingly arose from nowhere, often resulting in delays, dissatisfaction, and eventually failure. Measurable progress (see above) forms the basis for such an early-warning system, which in turn contributes to transparency since all concerned stakeholders know of relevant problems and the real state of the project. As Stephen explained, “[…] one notices earlier if something goes wrong and can take countermeasures to get back on track.” Katarina emphasized the importance of direct contact with the customer in this regard as opposed to projects with intermediate levels: “In project 2, we had direct contact with the customer, that is, people who would use the product afterwards. In project 3, there was always someone in between, a service provider who passed the customer requirements to us. [Upward Laddering – why is direct contact important for project success?] Because you catch the nuances, too. I take notice of that, and I get feedback on what the customer really thinks about certain aspects much earlier. He can tell me, ‘This is fine’, and in a sub-clause, ‘but if it goes on like this, it will be awful’. The service provider will just tell me, ‘everything is green’. That means I do not have this early warning system. I only have it when I work directly with the customer.”

## Fewer misunderstandings

Another factor contributing to transparency that was mentioned in multiple ladders is fewer misunderstandings between people involved in the project. This can be achieved, in turn, by different means. For example, Torben elaborated on the effects of a shared location of team members (as opposed to a distributed team): “It simplifies the communication and has an impact on quality since team members have a better understanding of the subject matter. […] On the other hand, there are fewer misunderstandings, and team members are considerably more engaged since they work together on a topic and mutually inspire each other.”

## Team members’ competence

Finally, many respondents pointed out the importance of having team members with high professional competence. Thomas defined this factor as follows: “You have a team with certain expertise, and they are capable of performing the required tasks – that is, the requirements and professional competence match.” Bernd realized during the interview that, on an abstract level, two aspects were most important for him: people and transparency: “For me, the two most important factors for success are that I need the right people, and you must know what happens – transparency.” Further clarification revealed that in his view, people refers to both team members’ competence and their motivation, whereby competence also contributes to creating transparency.

## 5 Discussion

Our study yields three major findings. First, having linked the success factors of IS projects to corresponding success criteria and having identified 223 ladders from factors to criteria, we emphasize the high extent and complexity of IS project success mechanisms. Second, we extracted the most central ladders, revealing project-related motivation as the pivotal factor of IS project success. Finally, we provide insights into the ways to achieve this type of motivation by presenting the preceding factors from all identified ladders. On the basis of these findings, we discuss implications for researchers and derive three propositions concerning the management of IS projects that need to be tested by future research. Additionally, we provide implications for practitioners and discuss the limitations of our study.

## 5.1 Implications for Researchers

Based on RepGrid and Laddering, our study can be seen as a contribution to the field of IS project success research by applying a cognitive mapping technique to the mechanisms that link success factors and criteria. As an outcome of our study, we provide research insights into causal relations that explain IS project success as proposed by our interviewees. Considering our study’s explorative character, the factors within the identified ladders (see Figure 4) can be seen as hypotheses proposing a positive influence on other factors or success criteria, which should be tested in future research. In fact, the factors’ actual effectiveness has yet to be determined. While Figure 4 generally comprises a set of testable propositions, we discuss our findings in light of extant research and derive core propositions subsequently. In deriving these core propositions, we searched for patterns on a higher level of abstraction – following the idea of thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006). This analysis led to three themes (i.e., centrality of project-related motivation, the mediating role of project-related motivation, and the mediating role of transparency for project-related motivation), which build the foundation for our core propositions. Moreover, we discuss further general implications.

Motivation is considered the single most important factor for software development productivity (Verner et al. 2014). In turn, low motivation can cause failure of software engineering endeavors. As a consequence, motivation is a recurrent theme in research on project success factors (Chow and Cao 2008; Dvir et al. 1998; Turner and Müller 2005). Nevertheless, Beecham et al. (2008, p. 860) conducted a systematic literature review on the topic of motivation in software engineering and found that “there is no clear understanding of […] what motivates Software Engineers, how they are motivated, or the outcome and benefits of motivating Software Engineers.” An update of the review concludes that there seems to be no significant advancement concerning the overall understanding of how software developers are motivated (França et al. 2011). However, the context of a project is suggested to be decisive for whether stakeholders engage in the project (Beecham 2014; Purvis et al. 2015). Among others, motivating contexts are those that involve technically challenging tasks that have an impact on the organizations involved in a project (da Silva and França 2012; Sharp et al. 2009). Such contexts require developers to exercise all their technical skills during the different project phases (da Silva and França 2012) and make the project a meaningful one (Dwivedula et al. 2017). Since software developers have been shown to be interested in intrinsic rather than extrinsic factors (Beecham and Noll 2015), teams that perform routine tasks might experience lower productivity due to reduced motivation. It is thus important that developers identify themselves with a project (da Silva and França 2012; Sharp et al. 2009). Our results corroborate this relevance of working conditions in projects. Furthermore, we

#

emphasize the important difference between personal motivation and project-related motivation (Schmid and Adams 2008). As such, our study is in line with well-acknowledged research emphasizing the role of task-related motivation on task performance (Bills and Brown 1929; Locke 1968). Since characteristics of individuals are central in explaining differences in motivation, a fundamental understanding of motivation should be rooted in each individual’s context (França et al. 2014). However, such contexts are insufficiently understood yet (Purvis et al. 2015) and qualitative research might contribute a clear and grounded understanding of motivation in software engineering (França et al. 2011). Here, insights from our interviews reveal that project-related motivation plays a crucial role for IS project success. Previous research has addressed factors that influence project-related motivation in general project management such as top management commitment, project scope, personal conflicts (Schmid and Adams 2008), and project incidents (Gällstedt 2003). Additionally, more recent research has identified a set of six motivational factors for software developers common to projects in varying cultural contexts, including a good working environment and having a pleasant experience working on the project (Verner et al. 2014). Yet, our study is the first to provide in-depth insights into the embeddedness of the success factor project-related motivation in the mechanisms of IS project success. Considering the multi-dimensionality of IS project success (see Figure 4), our interviewees positively associated project-related motivation with IS project success. Based on these insights, we suggest our first proposition.

## P1: The project-related motivation of team members contributes to the success of IS projects.

Furthermore, the distinction between personal motivation and project-related motivation is important because different aspects contribute to these different types of motivation, as suggested by our respondents. While these two types of motivation might amplify each other, they might also considerably differ in specific projects. For example, while the personal motivation of employees is likely to be related to aspects such as the fit of personal goals and career perspective in the given enterprise, project-related motivation is directly linked to certainty, transparency, high project value, management attention, and management commitment (see Figure 4). A personally low-motivated employee might still be highly engaged in a concrete project (and vice versa) if, for example, the employee is assigned an interesting task or enjoys the spirit of the given project due to the right team mix (see Table 4). Accordingly, to develop effective guidelines for practitioners to increase project success, we suggest that scholars focus on the type of motivation related to the project. While Schmid and Adams (2008) focus on the project manager’s role in teams’ motivation toward a project (e.g., managers’ ability to communicate effectively), we provide a perspective on project-related motivation that covers a broader set of contributing factors. Considering the centrality of project-related motivation in IS project success mechanisms, our findings thus suggest a new perspective on IS project success. Previous research has identified management commitment as the most important factor for project success (Young and Jordan 2008). However, the influence of this factor is indicated to be mediated by team members’ projectrelated motivation. Additionally, the mediating effect of project-related motivation can also be presumed for other central factors in IS project success mechanisms, that is, certainty, progress is measurable, high project value, management attention, and transparency. Regarding transparency, our insights are in line with those of practitioners (Böhm and Haselberger 2012; Cohn 2009) and other researchers (Joosten et al. 2014; Pankratz and Loebbecke 2011). In a previous work, we have shown that transparency contributes to IS project success without investigating this mechanism in greater detail due to the different focus of the study (Basten and Pankratz 2015). Based on both the research and insights from our interviews, we thus suggest our second proposition.

P2: The project-related motivation of team members mediates the influence of project success factors on IS project success.

As shown in Figure 4, project transparency is a construct that mediates the influence of other factors on team members’ project-related motivation (i.e., progress is measurable, earlywarning system, fewer misunderstandings, and team members’ competence). At their core, these factors are related to the development process and contribute to the early detection and solution of problems concerning IS development. As such, these factors help to ensure an IS development process that is perceived as frictionless. Considering the general differentiation between process success and product success in IS projects (Baccarini 1999; Collins and Baccarini 2004; Liu et al. 2011), these factors focus on the former concept. In line with insights from research concerning the management of expectations in IS projects (Basten et al. 2016; Diegmann et al. 2017), we emphasize the relevance of process success and argue for the criticality of process performance in the context of IS projects. Concretely, we suggest our third proposition.

P3: Project transparency mediates the influence of success factors related to the development process on the project-related motivation of team members.

In addition to the propositions, there are general implications for research. The great majority of extant research is dedicated to identifying lists of project success factors, success criteria, or investigating the impact of single factors. While these perspectives are crucial, our study sheds light on the mechanisms of IS project success rather than focusing on factors or criteria only. Given the interrelatedness of success factors between each other and the relationship to success criteria, adding this perspective is indispensable for arriving at a holistic and accurate understanding of IS project success. Our results are thus a first, yet crucial step for a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of IS project success. In our context of IS project management, the respective influences (see Figure 4 and Table 4) on project-related motivation should receive attention because tasks or rather their characteristics are determining factors for behavior (Ryan 1958). As noted in previous research on general project management, “[w]hether a project team is highly motivated to attain a project’s goal can make the difference between a smooth project progress and one that is bogged down with complications. […] yet, understanding how motivation works from the project manager’s perspective remains unclear” (Schmid and Adams 2008, p. 60). We invite future researchers to continue our work and focus on this pivotal factor. The preceding factors identified in this research can be seen as a starting point for further inquiries. Although these factors were frequently mentioned by the respondents and can consequently be seen as important, future research needs to systematically identify factors with the greatest impact on project-related motivation.

Our study shows the effectiveness and manifold applicability of RepGrid and Laddering in the IS domain. RepGrid has been shown to be a suitable means to identify tacit knowledge by making it explicit since many respondents were surprised by the high number of factors of which they became aware during the interviews. Regarding Laddering in particular, it has seen relatively little application in the IS community (Kutsch et al. 2013) and, if applied, rather serves to clarify the meaning only (e.g., Napier et al. 2009). Our application of Upward Laddering exemplifies the great potential of this way of designing RepGrid studies to identify hierarchical relations. As one example, project-related motivation as the central factor frequently emerged from Upward Laddering, but has been named as a factor that served as a basis for ladders only a few times. This example shows that crucial insights might emerge from using interview techniques that provide in-depth insights from interviewees’ implicit knowledge that otherwise would remain undiscovered. We have used Laddering to shed light

#

on one of the major black boxes in project management, the mechanisms of IS project success. We thus advance the portfolio of existing RepGrid studies in IS research and guide scholars toward a broader (additional domains) and more in-depth (within a particular domain) application of this research method in our field.

## 5.2 Implications for Practitioners

Considering the central role of project-related motivation suggested by our respondents, the main implication for practitioners is the prospect of greater success of their projects by focusing on increasing the motivation of the employees for the project. Multiple suggestions for doing so have been presented in this paper. First, we suggest focusing on the predecessors of project-related motivation included in the central ladders (see Figure 4). According to the interviewees, ensuring that these factors are in place has a considerable effect on projectrelated motivation. The detailed description (see Section 4), substantiated with quotes from the interviews, can help practitioners effectively address those factors. Furthermore, the given list of other predecessors of project-related motivation can be used as a checklist (see Table 4). Some of the factors in this list have been emphasized in previous research as success factors (however, not explicitly as predecessors of project-related motivation), for instance, communication (Hyväri 2006), clearly defined scope (Pinto and Slevin 1988), and responsibility (Belassi and Tukel 1996). Other factors are less common, for instance, success participation and positive external presentation. Considering the former, we suggest practitioners to hold out the prospect of it during the project and invest resources to make it happen after project completion. An organizational culture of established project success participation of the employees is likely to contribute to higher motivation and success on a long-term basis. This notion is in line with research on success concerning projects in the open-source domain, where recognition is seen as “a primary motivation for involvement” (Crowston et al. 2003, p. 10). Considering the latter, practitioners should create a positive image for the project to the outer world. Elaborating on a project with negative external presentation, Michael recalled: “In this project, the reputation was quickly established: ‘This is crap and will not work anyway’ […] This resentment of the outsiders was quickly picked up by the team: ‘What, our project is going wrong?’ That is not motivating.” To consider two more examples, we first suggest that practitioners bear in mind that an interesting task contributes to motivation and better performance of team members and thus, whenever possible, consider personal preferences when assigning work packages to developers. Second, and regarding feasibility, we advise practitioners to learn whether the communicated project plans are perceived as being realistic by the team members. It might be difficult to see the real picture because employees might not be willing to admit that they perceive their task as being too ambitious; anonymous surveys could help in this regard.

It is important to note that the presented factors can be influenced by the various project stakeholders to a different degree. Management attention and commitment are foremost in the realm of the top management. Project managers can, however, call management’s attention to the importance of these factors for project success, which has been previously emphasized (Young and Jordan 2008). High project value, on the other hand, is rather a condition that a project might or might not have and that is likely to be related to the project’s benefits concerning organizational strategic goals (Cleland 1986). Similarly, it is often not easy to reduce the complexity or size of a project. In the absence of important motivational conditions, for example, in a low-value project, the importance of other factors increases. In

#

general, however, practitioners should bear in mind that they can and should influence the motivation of team members in IS projects. As noted by research on projects in general, “team motivation can be heavily influenced by the project manager, especially during the early stages of the project. It appears that project managers have the ability to create a subculture within an overarching organization in which team dynamics can lead to higher levels of motivation than in the encompassing organization” (Schmid and Adams 2008, p. 70).

Managers might also consider means to increase motivation that are not included in this paper. In general, different motivation types can be found with varying degrees of autonomy, that is, the extent to which behavior is self-determined (Ryan and Deci 2000). Based on this differentiation, a variety of incentives have been identified that can be used by the management, including career advancements, economic rewards, and autonomy (Basten et al. 2013).

## 5.3 Limitations

First, not all mechanisms of IS project success that emerged from the 11 interviews were able to be reported in this paper due to the vast amount of identified ladders (223). However, this amount alone reflects the high extent and variety of IS project success mechanisms. Factors and ladders that are not included in our results sample are still important for IS project success but are not as central as project-related motivation and respective ladders in the view of the respondents, according to our analysis. Focusing on the central ladders and presenting all other predecessors of project-related motivation, we lay the groundwork for achieving this type of motivation. Furthermore, given the manifold stakeholders typically engaged in IS projects, our study is limited by considering the perspective of project managers only. Another limitation of our study pertains to our sample size and sampling approach. With only 11 respondents and a mixed sampling approach that partially relies on convenience sampling, the meaningfulness of the given quantitative statements (e.g., regarding the importance of project-related motivation based on the 28 occurrences in ladders) is limited. Nevertheless, we see the extent to which project-related motivation stands out from the other factors as a strong indicator of its central role in achieving IS project success. Moreover, RepGrid does not provide universally valid results – the relationships illustrated in Figure 4 represent the consolidated view of our respondents and need to be confirmed in a quantitative manner. Finally, the external validity of our results needs to be considered. We acknowledge that our results are primarily applicable to the context in which they have been elicited. This context is determined by the experience of our respondents, which has an emphasis on contracted IS projects conducted by IT service departments of three major German companies. While projects in other countries, companies, and domains might exhibit additional peculiarities, we believe that our results are representative of the majority of projects. Accordingly, the IS project success factors identified in this study, especially project-related motivation, are likely to play a central role in most IS projects.

## 6 Conclusion

In this study, we investigated project managers’ views on IS project success mechanisms. These mechanisms were uncovered by applying RepGrid and Laddering. While not all identified mechanisms of IS project success could be presented, our given results indicate that project-related motivation is a central hub in the factors-criteria network. In other words, team members’ motivation to achieve project goals is pivotal to success and has an impact on all but one success criterion identified in our study. Having presented and discussed factors that were identified as being the predecessors of project-related motivation, we enhance the understanding of project success mechanisms and shed light on an area that is often treated as a black box. Researchers obtain detailed insights into managers’ views of project-related motivation and related factors and can use these insights to further investigate these central aspects. Practitioners can use our results, including all identified predecessors of projectrelated motivation, to increase the motivation of team members on their projects, thus considerably increasing the likelihood of IS project success.

## ABOUT THE AUTHORS

Oleg Pankratz is a Postdoc Researcher at the Department of Information Systems and Systems Development at the University of Cologne, Germany. His research focuses on IS project success and has been published in journals such as Project Management Journal, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, International Journal of Information Sys tems and Project Management, and the proceedings of conferences such as HICSS, ECIS, AMCIS, and ICIS.

Dirk Basten is an Assistant Professor at the Department of Information Systems and Systems Development at the University of Cologne, Germany. His research focuses on IS project success, software development effort estimation, knowledge management in project and program contexts, and Gamification. His research has been published in journals such as Project Management Journal, Journal of Computer Information Systems, Communications of the Associa tion for Information Systems, IEEE Computer, IEEE Software, International Journal of Information Technology Project Management, and the proceedings of conferences such as HICSS, ECIS, AMCIS, and ICIS.
