## ABSTRACT

On-demand ride (ODR) services are expanding and becoming more influential globally because of the rapid growth in the use of mobile devices. ODR services are creating new business models and opportunities and reshaping transportation markets. This study used KakaoTaxi, a popular ODR service in Korea, to examine how customers evaluate an ODR service and determine whether customers are willing to reuse such a service. In the first-stage qualitative study, we explored decisional factors by conducting interviews with customers of the ODR service. In the second-stage quantitative study, we interpreted these previously explored decisional factors and developed a research model by applying the customer value theory. Going beyond previous research, the meta-inferences from the qualitative and quantitative studies explain how customers decide to reuse an ODR service. This study also informs ODR service providers of marketing strategies to leverage these key decisional factors in the reuse of an ODR service.

Keywords: On-demand ride (ODR) service, KakaoTaxi, customer value theory, qualitative study, quantitative study

## 1. Introduction

The number of mobile devices in the world has been increasing steadily, a change in communication that creates new business models and opportunities [30]. These devices enable ubiquitous communication and allow instant interaction, with features that are reshaping our everyday lives. For example, the rapid growth in the use of mobile devices underlies the global spread and influence of on-demand ride (ODR) services. ODR services such as Uber, Lyft, and KakaoTaxi are transforming the way we hail a taxi and travel in cities [78]. All services are new business models that attract customers comfortable with information and communication technologies. These technologies are relatively new and evolving rapidly. They create a new value by seamlessly combining online and offline services [12,27].

As part of these changes, ODR services are creating new business models and reshaping transportation markets. Entrants such as Uber, KakaoTaxi, and Lyft are a new phenomenon that is changing user behavior and business procedures and represent a huge commercial market. For example, Uber’s and Lyft’s ridership has increased by as much as 25% monthly.<sup>2</sup> In South Korea, KakaoTaxi, an ODR service based on the KakaoTalk app used by 93% of the nation’s smartphone users,<sup>3</sup> has attracted more than 200,000 of a total of 250,000 registered drivers in South Korea and receives more than 700,000 requests daily.<sup>4</sup> Therefore, it is important to understand this new phenomenon and how its specific characteristics relate to its success with consumers. However, despite continuous interest in, and creation of, such ODR services, little research has been devoted to understand the ODR services and identify their specific characteristics.

Prior research on ODR services has mainly addressed the adoption of a call taxi app [49], especially the problems and methods of solving rideshare matching problems [2,3] and comparisons of taxi and ridesourcing trips [56]. Given that ODR services are an emerging phenomenon and the lack of comprehensive theories about factors that might help individuals reach decisions related to the use of such services, an exploratory study is needed to offer a rich mechanism for discovering these factors. Moreover, previous research lacks theoretically grounded approaches with quantitative empirical validation. Therefore, an exploratory approach is needed to unearth the motivational factors related to individuals’ perceptions of ODR services. Further, such an approach needs to develop a theoretical model based on the decisional factors explored in the first stage and then test the model by using a confirmatory quantitative study.

Our study is motivated by the gap noted above in research on ODR services as well as the practical problems ODR service providers face in leveraging the sale of their services. From their perspective, it is essential to understand the motivational factors in how customers decide to use an ODR service. Equipped with such an understanding, service providers might provide better ODR services that in turn could generate additional revenue from increased repeat usage by both first-time and repeat riders. In the contexts of online commerce and general marketing, however, it has been observed that in comparison to the value of attracting potential new customers or retaining current customers, repeat sales to returning customers is essential for survival and long-term profitability [57]. Many business firms have recently started ODR services in intense competition with other transportation service alternatives. These new entrants into the market are exploring ways to increase their revenue. Therefore, examining the reuse of ODR services by repeat customers would be very meaningful for the business management of ODR service providers.

This study seeks to answer three research questions: (1) What are the decisional factors for reuse of an ODR service (i.e., re-purchase of ODR services)? (2) Do these decisional factors explain ODR service reuse? (3) In what way do the results from the quantitative data collection support or refute the results from the

#

qualitative data collection? On the basis of these research questions, the purposes of our study are developmental (i.e., to ensure the questions from one strand emerge from the inferences of a previous one or that one strand is used to develop hypotheses the research will test in the next one) [68] with an emergent element of triangulation (i.e., to assess the credibility of inferences obtained from one approach). Therefore, on the basis of these guidelines [68,69], we applied a mixed-methods approach through qualitative and quantitative research. The first stage (qualitative study) of this study explores the decisional factors of ODR service reuse. In this initial stage, we extract factors from interviews with users of KakaoTaxi as an ODR service. The second stage (quantitative study) of this study interprets decisional factors explored in the first stage based on the customer value theory [59,66] and then develops a research model. We have thus used a theoretically grounded approach in the second stage of the quantitative study. The model is validated by using survey data on an ODR service. Therefore, this study contributes to research by exploring customers’ decisional factors and examining how these factors lead to the repurchase of ODR services. Moreover, this study informs ODR service providers of marketing strategies to leverage these key decisional factors in the reuse of an ODR service.

## 2. On-demand ride service

ODR services are relatively new and evolving rapidly. ODR services rely on a mobile device to promptly match customers’ calls to suitable rides. These services are commonly known as ride sourcing, app-based rides, and call-a-taxi applications. These services are part of the burgeoning sharing economy that uses online platforms to connect seekers and providers of specific services. It serves its main purpose of providing boarding or riding services through the use of the latest technologies such as global positioning systems (GPS), smartphones, and network techniques. This business approach is becoming a new paradigm for the global economy [78]. The widespread use of mobile devices that made the development of ODR services possible and practical illustrates the potential of mobile devices to reshape transportation services.

Transportation services such as car sharing, ride sharing, and ODR sourcing are proliferating all over the world [26]. According to the Deloitte University Press<sup>5</sup>, use of ODR services has accelerated since 2012, and Uber, a type of ODR service, currently has a market share of approximately 45% of the entire taxi market in the world. In addition, diverse ODR services are being introduced globally. For example, Uber and Lyft Southeast Asia, Line taxi operates in Japan, Ola Cabs operates in India, and KakaoTaxi and T Map Taxi both operate in South Korea. Some ODR services such as Uber and KakaoTaxi have been extremely successful.

The procedure to use ODR services is as follows. First, the prospective passenger opens a mobile app and enters departure and destination points. Then, the prospective passenger calls a taxi to connect to a nearby taxi driver in his or her vicinity. When a matching taxi is detected, the ODR service app sends the taxi’s vehicle information, driver’s information (e.g., driver’s name, photo, and contact number), and realtime location to the caller. When the customer receives this information, he or she is able to contact the driver to deliver a pick-up location. In addition, passengers have an opportunity to rate their driver, meaning that the passengers and drivers have an opportunity to communicate. This provision makes it possible to continuously manage the service. We can use an ODR service to call a taxi and receive vehicle and driver information and their real-time location.

ODR services rely on a mobile app that links drivers and potential users. This implies that the passengers and drivers can easily interact. The use of such an app underlies several differences between traditional taxi services and ODR services. First, in ODR service, passengers use their mobile phones to call for a ride. Once this call is made (a caller has the option of also giving a destination), the app notifies drivers available nearby. When a driver accepts the ride, the app enables the caller to see a display of minutes until the driver arrives and track his or her progress. This contrasts with regular taxi service in which passengers typically hail a taxi on the street. Second, in ODR service, passengers and drivers can interact before

If the passenger exercised the option of providing the destination information during the initial call, then the driver, unlike a traditional taxi service driver, already knows the passenger’s destination. By contrast, typically, passengers do not know any information about a taxi before they get into the car. Third, ODR services have a range of mechanisms to ensure passengers are safe. Once a driver driver’s phone. The passenger is also able to share his or her location with friends or family at all times, which also permits these persons to track the passenger’s whereabouts throughout the ride. Moreover, before passengers enter a vehicle, they have information about it as well as the driver’s name, rating, photo, and contact number, and a record is made of the trip. These unique characteristics of ODR services change people’s behavior compared with their conduct with typical traditional taxi services.

However, despite the growing interest in ODR services because of their unique attributes, theorygrounded empirical studies on the unique factors that affect customers’ behavior and the relationships between these factors are lacking. Previous research on ODR services has mainly addressed the adoption of a call taxi app [49], especially the problems and methods of solving ride-share matching problems [2,3] and comparisons of taxi and ride-sourcing trips [56]. Agatz et al. [3] developed optimization-based approaches to minimize total systemwide vehicle miles and individual travel costs. Their study presented methods to solve ride-share matching problems and used computer simulations based on travel demand data to test the performance of a practical dynamic ridesharing system. Later, Agatz et al. [2] adopted an operations research perspective in examining the characteristics and problems of dynamic ride-sharing to support the matching of riders and drivers in real time.

However, previous research did not explain the specific characteristics of ODR services that affect customers’ decisions to reuse an ODR service. We examined ODR services from the perspective of an qualitative study explored specific decisional factors for reuse. The second-stage quantitative study interpreted those factors and examined the corresponding research model from the perspective of the customer value theory. A theory explains a phenomenon by specifying what variables are related to what variables and how they are related to its justification (i.e., why) [73]. Thus, in the second-stage quantitative study, we applied the customer value theory in interpreting the decisional factors that we explored and in developing the theoretical framework used to develop a research model.

## 3. Qualitative study

In the first-stage qualitative study, we explored specific decisional factors for ODR service reuse, and in the second-stage qualitative study, we tested these factors with a theoretical interpretation from the customer value theory perspective. By using the guidelines for the development of mixed-methods studies [68], we determined the five steps as follows (see Appendix A): (1) appropriateness of mixed-methods research, (2) strategies for mixed-methods research design, (3) strategies for collecting and analyzing mixed-methods data, (4) drawing meta-inferences from the mixed-methods results, and (5) assessing the quality of metainferences.

## 3.1. Research method

We first adopted a qualitative research method in which we interviewed KakaoTaxi users to explore their reasons for deciding to reuse the service (i.e., re-purchase of ODR services). KakaoTaxi is an example of a

#

successful ODR service application in South Korea. It is integrated with KakaoTalk, which has evolved into a global service with social networking features; therefore, KakaoTaxi users are suitable samples for our study. An interview approach is highly reliable and can be used to explain a phenomenon because it provides a comparatively precise observation of the nature of the phenomenon and collects data from the perspective deliver perceived causal inferences [33,74]. In recruiting interviewees, we used the snowball sampling technique [8]. Qualitative studies tend to use a nonprobability sampling approach because relevance to the research topic determines the data sampling [45]. The snowball sampling technique has been used to create sampling frames with nonprobability methods of selection [8]. According to the sampling strategy, we recruited 10 interviewees and then asked for the names and contact information of other members of the target population through the use of snowball sampling. At that time, we tried to recruit and ask for participants who use KakaoTaxi frequently, especially office workers and persons in their 20s. They were targeted because as more frequent users of KakaoTaxi, they had a better understanding of the service than infrequent users.

For interviewees, we first recruited 10 people and recruited an additional 50 interviewees for a total sample size of 60. We selected only interviewees who had used KakaoTalk at least twice in the last month. Because the users had used KakaoTaxi recently, the retrospective recall problem should not occur [33]. It took approximately 20 days for us to recruit our sample through our referral method. We determined the optimal number of interviewees by confirming theoretical saturation [63], which indicates that no new code will emerge from additional data collection and analysis. In this study, after conducting five interviews per stage, we analyzed the interview data based on the coding approach used in each stage. After having interviews at 12 stages with 60 subjects, we could no longer find new indicators and codes. Each of the 60 one-on-one interviews lasted for 10-15 minutes to permit gaining detailed responses from each person. We conducted a structured interview that consisted of questions related to decision-making factors for (1) reusing KakaoTaxi, (2) the time of day KakaoTaxi was most frequently used, and (3) frequency of use. The male-to-

#

female interviewee ratio indicated a fairly equal distribution of 53.3% to 46.7%, and the majority of the interviewees were in their 20s (48.3%) and 30s (31.7%). Sixty percent of the interviewees were undergraduate students; the others were employees (28.3%).

We recorded the interviews and later transcribed them. Then the significant phenomena within the data extracted from the interviews were conceptualized and coded to categorize similar or related phenomena. We used open and axial coding [16] to analyze the transcripts, a coding approach that is a part of the grounded theory. This theory is a general methodology, a way of thinking about and conceptualizing data [64]. Open coding is the initial step of theoretical analysis that pertains to the initial discovery of categories and their properties [23]. It is an interpretive process by which data are broken down analytically [17]. Axial coding is a process of exploring the relationships among categories [62]. In axial coding, researchers relate categories to their subcategories and test the relationships against data [17]. That is, the purpose of open coding is to help the researchers get new insights into the data and to delineate concepts and group data into meaningful categories. Then, the axial coding categories are related to the categories to develop causal relationships explaining the reuse of the ODR service. That is, a category can be related to other categories.

During the open coding, each coder examined the interview responses line by line to identify codes within the textual data that could explain interviewees’ decision-making. The coders then discussed each concept identified and named it after arriving at a consensus. We grouped these concepts into broader categories that reflected commonalities among the codes. Thus, we used open coding to delineate concepts and group them into meaningful categories. We related the categories to develop causal relationships that during axial coding could explain the reuse of the ODR service. To validate the data coding results, three other researchers conducted a peer review of the interview details and keyword classifications. The coders then discussed each concept identified and interacted with each other. Then, to settle any inter-rater disagreements, we recruited a coder who was uninvolved with either the data collection or discussion of it. In the confirmatory study, we applied the categorized concepts to the theoretical framework of the customer value theory to extract factors that influence decisions on the reuse of the ODR service.

## 3.2. Data analysis and results

More than half of the interviewees said they typically use KakaoTaxi between 10 p.m. and the early morning of the next day because of the difficulty of using public transportation and regular taxis during those hours (38 people, 63.3%). Twelve interviewees said they mainly use KakaoTaxi in situations such as rush hours when they must reach somewhere in a hurry (12 people, 20.0%). As for the frequency of KakaoTaxi use, 37 interviewees said they had used it fewer than five times (37 people, 61.7%), and five interviewees had used it more than 15 times (five people, 8.3%).

We identified the 10 concepts from the open coding of the interview transcripts related to the decisional factors for the reuse of an ODR service. After initial coding, we grouped similar concepts into broader categories that were intended to evolve into theoretical constructs. As a result, in categorizing responses concerning motivational factors driving the reuse of KakaoTaxi, we extracted seven main concepts: ubiquitous connectivity, ODR service convenience, perceived informativeness of ODR, reassurance of ODR, relative attractiveness of ODR, monetary value of ODR, and two-way communication. The inter-rater agreement scores averaged 0.82, and Cohen’s Kappa scores [13] averaged 0.81. Inter-rater disagreements were reconciled through discussion and by another coder who was uninvolved in the data collection. Next, we grouped relevant codes into the seven main concepts. Consequently, 87 codes were grouped into the seven concepts.

In the context of this study, ubiquitous connectivity, at 27.6%, was the highest motivation for reuse, thus reflecting customers’ expressed satisfaction with access to ODR service regardless of time or place. Ubiquitous connectivity refers to the extent to which users can access an ODR service anytime and anywhere. ODR service convenience was rated as the second highest motivation at 21.8%. With regard to this category, interviewees said they decided to reuse an ODR service because of the convenience derived from the capability of location recognition; in fact, it is possible to call a taxi without actually giving one's own location. We defined ODR service convenience as the extent to which users can use the KakaoTaxi without their location information, not from the general convenience perspective but the specific convenience in the use of an ODR service related to location information.

Perceived informativeness of an ODR service registered the third highest motivation at 12.6%. This KakaoTaxi sends its customers vehicle information and basic information to identify the driver (e.g., the driver’s contact number) as soon as the matching process is complete. In this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to reuse an ODR service because they know up-front the driver’s name, location, and number; they also can easily recover lost property because they have the driver’s information. Reassurance of an ODR service registered the fourth highest motivation at 11.5%. Reassurance represents the degree of assurance of safety that an ODR service conveys to customers through the safe messages that can be sent to their acquaintances to inform them of their whereabouts and activity. With regard to this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to reuse an ODR service because they felt safe with this KakaoTaxi safety feature; they felt this because the calling/ride-related information remains on the server. Moreover, they had confidence in the Kakao brand.

Relative attractiveness of ODR and the monetary value of ODR registered identical rates of 8.0%. Relative attractiveness represents attractiveness recognized by customers under difficult spatiotemporal circumstances and in comparison with other transportation services. KakaoTaxi can be conveniently used late at night at isolated locations after public transportation is discontinued. More than half of the interviewees said they mainly use KakaoTaxi late at night or during the early morning. For this category, interviewees mentioned that they decided to reuse an ODR service because of its availability when public transportation is discontinued late at night and also because it is possible to call a taxi to an isolated location. Monetary value of ODR service represents the benefit or value customers perceive they receive for the money they paid. Although KakaoTaxi is similar to the call taxi process, it provides additional services without calling costs. The customers said that the cost–benefit of using KakaoTaxi is higher than that with other services. That is, interviewees mentioned that it provides better service than the call taxi system at no extra cost. Finally, twoway communication registered a rate of 5.7%. The two-way communication between a would-be passenger and a driver refers to the reciprocal communication and messages in a sequence related to each other. Accordingly, in this study, the two-way communication was used to indicate the level of reciprocal communication among customer, driver, and other customers.

There were some other responses related to linguistic savings and automation. However, because such responses were factors not significantly differentiated from other riding services and only registered at an insignificant rate, they were excluded from the main factors used in this research. We then related the identified categories through axial coding. Designating ODR reuse intention as the core category (dependent variable), we were able to relate this category with the other seven categories representing the decisional factors that are its antecedents. The seven main concepts extracted from the interviews serve as decisional factors that were interpreted based on the customer value theory and tested in a subsequent confirmatory study. The customer value theory is a cognitive expression of the most basic and fundamental desires, as well as purposes, that consumers pursue. We used it to derive interrelationships among the factors and also with the dependent variables that together form the theoretical framework and research model.

## 4. Quantitative study

## 4.1. Customer value theory and theoretical framework

The marketing and e-commerce literature considers customer value an important predictor of customer behavior and decision-making [59,66]. In customer behavior, value is a cognitive expression of the most basic and fundamental desire as well as a purpose that consumers try to pursue. In this study, customer value is to provide consumers with a superior value; it is expressed with constructs of value such as service quality

#

and special features. As value maximizers, consumers are thought to consummate exchanges with providers that provide maximal value [60]. Customer value has been used as a theoretical foundation in explaining customers’ decisions in making purchases (i.e., initial purchase made by a potential customer and repeat purchase made by a returning customer) (e.g., [31]). Customers tend to maximize value in transactions with a service provider across phases (i.e., initial purchase and repeat purchases). Accordingly, customer value has been used in explaining customers’ repeat purchases (e.g., [48]) specifically and their relationship development with a service provider (i.e., loyalty) (e.g., [47]). For this reason, we adopted the customer value theory to interpret the decisional factors derived through the exploratory study and made the connection between the customer value theory and reuse intention.

The theoretical framework of this research was developed based on the explored factors from the qualitative study and the customer value theory (see Figure 1). We proposed five dimensions of customer value based on a combination of five dimensions [59] and three dimensions [66]. Sheth et al. [59] expressed the subvalues of the customer value theory with emotional, conditional, functional, social, and epistemic values. Sweeney and Soutar [66] expressed it with functional, social, and hedonic values. We did not apply the social value dimension because there was no social value component in the context of ODR service. Therefore, we adopted the emotional, functional, conditional, and epistemic values in interpreting these explored decisional factors based on the interaction of the two value approaches of Sheth et al. [59] and Sweeney and Soutar [66].

As for the mixed-methods research design, based on existing guidelines [69], we adopted a sequential explanatory design (i.e., exploratory investigation of study 1 and confirmatory investigation of study 2). These explored factors were used for the development of hypotheses and quantitative tests. We explored these decisional factors on the basis of interviews with experienced ODR service users. For the theoretical interpretation of the explored factors (i.e., theoretical research model development), we applied the customer value theory because this theory fits well with the factors that explain customers’ decisions to reuse ODR services. The selected mixed-methods research design (i.e., sequential explanatory design) allows us to interpret these explored factors in terms of the subtypes of customer value, not vice versa (i.e., identifying factors on the basis of the subtypes of customer value). Therefore, five decisional factors (interactivity, perceived informativeness, relative attractiveness, reassurance, and monetary value) of ODR services were derived from the exploratory study results, and we linked them to the dimensions of the customer value theory [59,66].

Functional value is a quality and utility recognized through using a product or service [59]. Ubiquitous connectivity, ODR service convenience, and two-way communication extracted from the primary interviews may serve in this study as characteristics of interactivity to explain the functional aspects of an ODR service. Interactivity has been frequently explained from the perspective of multidimensional concepts: (1) the perspective of information processing and message exchange processing [54,61], (2) the perspective of the technical/functional characteristics of media [25,46], and (3) the awareness level of users [40,42], an integrated perspective process and technical/functional characteristics [36,76]. In this study, we applied a perspective of interactivity that integrates the ODR service process, technical/functional characteristics, and awareness level. Accordingly, on the basis of the primary interview results as well as the previous literature [36,54,55], ODR service-related interactivity was established by characterizing its three different aspects. Therefore, ODR service-related interactivity refers to an interaction among drivers, users, and information about the service. In other words, the interactivity is that service users communicate with each other and access information sequentially.

We used a reflective second-order construct in that it is reflected by its three first-order dimensions. The three dimensions are ubiquitous connectivity, service convenience, and two-way communication. Ubiquitous connectivity represents the capability to access information anytime and anywhere; service, convenience represents using the service without passengers’ location information, and two-way communication represents reciprocal communication in a sequence. That is, these three dimensions represent the concept of interactivity. In turn, each of the three dimensions of interactivity of an ODR is reflected by its indicators. It is distinct between the indicators of first-order constructs and second-order superordinate constructs [53]. When modeling first-order reflective constructs, indicators (i.e., measurement items) are direct manifestations of the construct and considered interchangeable. By contrast, when modeling a secondorder superordinate construct, we may expect that the dimensions covary [53]. Therefore, we conceptualize these three dimensions (ubiquitous connectivity, service convenience, and two-way communication) as a first-order construct that constitutes the interactivity of an ODR. We propose the interactivity of an ODR as an antecedent of intention to reuse an ODR service.

Epistemic value is a usefulness-related value acquired from an alternative’s capacity to provide novelty and satisfy a desire for knowledge [59]. It includes obtaining pieces of information as well as satisfying a desire for knowledge. Users want to receive appropriate information, and this desire affects consumers’ knowledge. In this study, this category includes the perceived informativeness of an ODR service that signifies its capability to provide customers with essential information, such as a taxi’s location and its driver’s contact number, while they are connected to the KakaoTaxi service. This new experience that includes obtaining information provides an epistemic value. In addition, customers can trace and recover lost property easily with the driver’s contact information that the KakaoTaxi offers. Accordingly, the perceived informativeness of an ODR service signifies the necessary information that an ODR service provides to its customers. Therefore, we propose the perceived informativeness of an ODR service as an antecedent of the intention to reuse an ODR service.

Conditional value is a product or service efficiency-related value recognized when faced with a specific situation or set of circumstances [59]. It is related to the conditional effects of a specific situation on value perception [66]. Consideration of choice contingencies accounts for the main difference between functional value and conditional value [59]. Although ODR service convenience has no choice contingencies, there are choice contingencies for the relative attractiveness of an ODR service such as “when the public transportation is discontinued late at night” and “isolated locations where other alternative transportation services are difficult to use.” The relative attractiveness of an ODR signifies the attractiveness of the KakaoTaxi service, in comparison with alternative transportation services, that can be recognized under special circumstances such as late at night and at an isolated location when and where it is difficult to use other means of transportation. Therefore, we propose the relative attractiveness of an ODR as an antecedent of intention to reuse an ODR service from the conditional value perspective.

#

Emotional value is a value recognized when specific emotions are felt or triggered [59]. It means that customers can recognize specific emotions by using the service. KakaoTaxi service provides a safe message to reassure its customers of the safety of their situation. In other words, people feel secure by using safe message services when they take an ODR service. In this study, reassurance by an ODR service signifies the reassurance level it conveys to its customers and represents an emotional value among its customers’ values. Therefore, we propose reassurance by an ODR as an antecedent of intention to reuse it. Monetary value is a benefit, including commercial profit, related to the purchase of a product or service [66]. Although KakaoTaxi uses a fare system same as that of a regular taxi, it provides additional services. Accordingly, because the monetary value of an ODR service is associated with monetary value, it signifies a benefit or value from the money paid by customers to use an ODR service [43]. Therefore, we propose the monetary value of an ODR as an antecedent of intention to reuse such a service.

## 4.2. Comparison with user acceptance research

To explain information systems (IS) use, technology acceptance research has focused mainly on individuals’ evaluations of using a system. For example, TAM identifies two types of beliefs that influence user behavior, namely, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use [18]. The unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) identifies four main determinants of IS use — performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions [70]. Moreover, user satisfaction has long been recognized as an antecedent of IS use. The IS continuance model identifies user satisfaction and perceived usefulness as the two main determinants of IS continuance [7]. The three models, namely, TAM, UTAUT, and IS continuance model, are general models that can be applied to many IS.

Whereas past literature extends significantly our understanding of individuals’ reaction to an information technology (IT) artifact, the three models (TAM, UTAUT, and IS continuance model) ignore the cost of technology adoption and usage. By contrast, monetary payment for the service is an important decisional factor for users of ODR services. Consumers must pay for the service each time they use it. In our study, therefore, reuse intention of ODR services reflects repurchase intention of ODR services for ODR service users as consumers. According to marketing and economy literature (e.g., [67,75]), service consumers make decisions and behave with the goal of maximizing value. Because of that outlook, consumers are thought to consummate exchanges with those service providers who provide a maximal value [60].

Although it is meaningful to investigate ODR service reuse by extending previous research on TAM, UTAUT, and IS continuance, continued reliance on the same perspectives poses risk of limiting the creation of new knowledge. All three models (TAM, UTAUT, and IS continuance) also are limited in their capability to explain how users of services make their decisions when those users act as value maximizers. Furthermore, the level of specificity between general IT use/continuance and ODR service reuse is quite different. The IS literature suggests that the optimal level of specificity at which a concept should be assessed is a function of the specificity of the inquiry of interest [1]. A specific model is necessary to examine the reuse of an ODR service when service consumers bear the cost of the monetary payment. Thus, the present study explores in its first-stage qualitative study specific decisional factors for repeat consumption of an ODR service. Then, these decisional factors are tested in the second-stage based on a theoretical interpretation from the perspective of the customer value theory.

## 4.4. Research Method

We developed a survey instrument that adopts existing validated scales wherever possible. The research variables verified in the previous literature were adjusted to fit the context of the ODR service. In this study, the perspective of interactivity that integrates an ODR service’s process, technical/functional characteristics, and awareness level was applied from previous studies [36,54,55]. The interactivity of an ODR service was constructed based on the three concepts, namely, ubiquitous connectivity, service convenience, and two-way communication. We modeled the interactivity of an ODR service as a second-order reflective construct based on the primary interviews and previous literature [36,54,55]. The three concepts were set as first-order reflective indicators of the interactivity of an ODR service. These three indicators serve as the manifestation that displays the ODR service-related interactivity. Another reason is that any change made to the properties of these indicators or any addition of other indicators would not influence the interactivity of the ODR service itself [28].

To assess ubiquitous connectivity and service convenience, we adapted scales from Lee [36]. A scale for two-way communication was adapted from Liu [40] and Gao et al. [21]. We also developed other items communication: “The ODR service helps immediate communication between me and the driver,” “ODR service delivers my opinion well,” and “ODR service helps me smooth communication between me and other users.” We adapted the items for perceived informativeness of an ODR service from Lederer et al. [35] and Kim et al. [34]. Scales for the relative attractiveness of an ODR service were adapted from Kim and Gupta [32]. Scales for monetary value were adapted from Chen [10], and scales for intention to reuse an ODR service were adapted from Dodds et al. [19]. To measure the reassurance of an ODR service, we adapted scales from Leung and Wei [38]. Although reassurance was mentioned primarily in literature on communication and mass media, it is a newly extracted variable in research on an IS-based ODR service. Accordingly, the measurement items used in previous literature were adjusted to fit the context of this research [38]. To assess and verify the content validity of the measurement items, three experts in this field reviewed them and provided feedback. We then conducted a sorting exercise with four graduate students as judges. The sorting results indicated that the average overall placement ratio of items within the targeted constructs was 0.93. After this sorting of items, two experts were engaged to review the items again. The final measurement items were anchored on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree).

## 4.6. Hypotheses testing

We examined the structural model with some control variables (age, gender, and profession). We tested the hypotheses using the pooled data by applying a bootstrap resampling technique. The bootstrapping technique provided in SmartPLS 2.0 was used, and 500 samples were constructed to verify the hypotheses. The results are shown in Figure 3.

The results of testing our hypotheses indicate that the monetary value of an ODR service (H2), relative attractiveness of an ODR service (H3), reassurance of an ODR service (H4), and interactivity of an ODR service (H5) have significant effects on the intention to reuse, thus explaining 41% of its variance. However, we did not find a significant effect of the perceived informativeness of an ODR service (H1) on intention to reuse it. We also did not find a significant effect of any control variable (age, gender, and profession) on ODR service reuse intention.

In addition to the direct effects of the five decisional factors on ODR reuse intention, we conducted a post-test analysis to examine the relationships among the decisional factors. Compared with other types of customer values, the main characteristic of conditional value is the consideration of choice contingencies [59]. Regarding conditional value, customers perceive a certain basic value (e.g., functional, monetary, emotional, and epistemic value) under a certain condition (e.g., in comparison with other transportation services), i.e., choice contingency. As the level of these specific basic values increases, customers may perceive a higher conditional value in the corresponding choice contingency.

Conditional value is the result of a specific situation confronting the choice maker [59]. Situational factors have the capability to predict behavior [5] and enhance other values [59]. In this study, situational

factors can be isolated locations and late nights when people prefer to use an ODR service because they are at some place where other transportation services are difficult to use or less attractive to use compared to an ODR service. Therefore, we have used conditional value as a way to enhance the other factors that influence ODR service reuse intention. We have examined the effects of the interactivity of ODR (functional value), reassurance of ODR (emotional value), perceived informativeness of ODR (epistemic value), and monetary value of ODR (monetary value) on the relative attractiveness of ODR (conditional value). ## 5. Overall discussion and implications

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

Based on the guidelines [20], we considered three levels of integration (i.e., “mixing”) across three levels (i.e., design, methods, interpretation, and reporting) regarding the integration (i.e., “mixing”) of our qualitative and quantitative studies. There are three basic design approaches for the integration at the design level: exploratory sequential, explanatory sequential, and concurrent. We applied an exploratory sequential design in this study. We thus began with a qualitative data collection and analysis phase, which builds to the subsequent quantitative phase. Next, there are four different approaches to the integration at the methods level: connecting, building, merging, and embedding [20]. We applied the integration through the building approach for the method-level integration. That is, the results of the first-stage qualitative study informed the data collection and analytical approach in the following quantitative study. Finally, there are three different approaches for the integration at the interpretation and reporting levels: integration through narrative, integration through data transformation, and integration through joint displays. We applied the integration through joint display approach (see Appendix C).

#

Regarding the meta-inferences (see Appendix C), we focused on using inductive and deductive theoretical reasoning to develop and test hypotheses. We developed the qualitative inferences first. This sequence helped to triangulate the results from the qualitative and quantitative studies into a coherent conceptual framework. Additionally, we examined inference quality including design quality, explanatory assessed the credibility of inferences obtained from analyzing both qualitative and quantitative data (i.e., triangulation with the emergence of complementarity). We also assessed the quality of meta-inferences by checking several criteria such as design suitability, design adequacy, analytical adequacy, and integrative correspondence [69]. Overall, the quality of meta-inferences is satisfactory in terms of design quality and explanation quality (see Appendix A(5)).

Appendix C integrates the qualitative and quantitative results through a joint display and reports the meta-inferences. We found that four decisional factors (relative attractiveness, monetary value, reassurance, and interactivity) have significant effects on the intention to reuse such a service. This study thus explains that four factors take significant roles in consumers’ ODR service purchase decision-making, as metainferences, consistently across both the qualitative and quantitative studies.

As a result of adopting the customer value theory [59,66], we confirmed the value associated with the interactivity of an ODR service from the perspective of functional value, its reassurance from the perspective of emotional value, its relative attractiveness from the perspective of conditional value, and its monetary value from the perspective of monetary value were all significantly influential on the intention to reuse an ODR service. We confirmed that customers are able to (1) access the ODR service regardless of time and place, be provided with services optimized to their circumstances, and to communicate and interact with the driver and with other customers. In addition, we found the customers are willing to reuse the ODR service because (2) an ODR service’s attractiveness is comparatively high, especially under circumstances in which other means of transportation are difficult to use; (3) an ODR service reassures customers; and (4) an ODR service is considered a great monetary value.

##

We further found that some decisional factors have relationships with other factors. We found that both the interactivity of an ODR service and the reassurance of an ODR service have positive effects on the relative attractiveness of ODR. The ubiquity of mobile devices and constant access to them translates into near-constant opportunities for interaction because of familiarity with these devices and with the convenience especially through its access under specific spatiotemporal circumstances. By providing an ODR service that is always accessible, interactive, and optimized to a customer’s circumstances and has rider–driver two-way communication, KakaoTaxi has gained recognition as a better value than other means of transportation. Moreover, safety is the most important single factor in attracting customers to choose an ODR service compared to a traditional taxi service [72]. One way that an ODR service conveys this sense is that safety is through an immediate smartphone connection between a driver and his would-be passenger. The increasing availability and sophistication of GPS-enabled mobile devices can use location information to reassure users that their location is known and confer a sense of safety that allows them to continue their activities [9]. Similarly, an ODR service communicates to customers information on the vehicle and driver that not only reassures the rider but also permits the rider to notify others of his or her whereabouts and reassure them of his or her safety. Because of these forms of reassurance, customers may prefer to use an ODR service instead of a regular taxi or other alternative transportation services.

Finally, we found that three factors — ubiquitous connectivity, service convenience, and two-way communication — were the subordinate dimensions of the interactivity of an ODR. Previous researchers into diverse contexts devoted considerable discussion to a multidimensional concept and proposed diverse subordinate concepts [21,36,40]. In our research, the concept of the interactivity of ODR was recombined, on the basis of the interviews and the customer value theory, to cope with the newly recognized characteristics of an ODR service. We confirmed that the recombined interactivity of an ODR service exerted a significant influence on the intention to reuse an ODR service.

#

There is, however, one discrepancy between the qualitative findings and the quantitative findings. Although the qualitative study found the informativeness of an ODR service as an important decisional factor, the quantitative study did not agree with it. We have conducted additional interviews with three KakaoTaxi users who participated in the first-stage qualitative study in an effort to learn the potential reason for this inconsistency. Those interviewees mentioned informativeness (i.e., providing the taxi number and the driver’s information) of KakaoTaxi in their decision-making, a result same as that obtained in the qualitative study. We further asked how informativeness affects their decision-making. They replied that they feel relief if they receive the driver’s name and the vehicle information, especially when they take a KakaoTaxi at night or at an isolated location, because they can share it with family members or friends in a certain situation. Most of them agreed that information from an ODR service reassures a user about using the service. Previous research also demonstrated this informational effect on reassurance in the context of a medical service [50].

Thus, the potential reason for the insignificance is the indirect effect of perceived informativeness of an ODR service on ODR service reuse through the reassurance conveyed. That is, the information provided by KakaoTaxi enables customers to feel secure. We have formulated an ad-hoc hypothesis and tested it to check the validity of this potential reason. A bootstrapping approach for a mediation test based on the ODR service on reuse intention through reassurance from the service (standard error (SE) = 0.038, lower confidence interval $\mathrm { ( C I ) = 0 . 0 7 }$ , and upper $\mathrm { C I } = 0 . 2 2 )$ . It explains that the reassurance of an ODR service mediates the effect of the perceived informativeness of an ODR service on reuse intention.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research direction

Our research explored the motivational factors for reusing an ODR service and, by adopting the customer value theory, empirically analyzed the influence of each factor on this reuse intention. Despite the significant findings of this study, it has some limitations, and its results outline directions for future research. First, this study considered only KakaoTaxi users in obtaining interviews and survey data. This likely limits the generalizability of our findings. Other ODR services operate around the world, and each service has its distinctive features. Moreover, awareness of ODR services probably varies from one nation to another, and this presumed diversity offers a fertile field for research into their differences and similarities.

Second, the possibility exists that factors we did not explore also influence decisions on reuse intention. Just as our focus on KakaoTaxi may have affected our results, so may have our methods by excluding other influential factors. We extracted the motivational factors for using an ODR service through interviews with users of KakaoTaxi, and such factors were applied to the customer value theory to conduct empirical research. However, factors other than those extracted through such a process may be influential as well, especially for potential first-time users weighing the use of an ODR service. In future research, other potential factors relating to using the ODR service will need to be considered, and the influential relationships among such factors need to be examined. Those factors can be compared between potential customers (i.e., initial adoption) and repeat customers (i.e., continuance of ODR service use). Additionally, different groups of customers may have different motivators for using the service. However, our research did not examine such differences between customer groups. In future research, we need to discuss finding new and different motivators in relation to each occupation. Third, this study used intention to reuse an ODR service to examine how consumers use the service. In other words, our study is based on perceptual data. Future studies should seek to identify more significant results through objective data, such as ODR service costs and frequency of ODR service use, all of which would document the actual behavior of consumers.

## 5.3. Implications for research

This study has several implications for research, especially because it is the first to address these four aspects of an ODR service: (1) examining reuse decision-making from the perspective of a mixed-methods approach; (2) extending the customer value theory in the context of an ODR service; (3) taking a comprehensive approach to the study of the interactivity of an ODR service and its three subtypes; and (4) examining the relationship between value factors as ODR service characteristics.

##

First, this study contributes to IS research by using a mixed-methods approach to study an ODR service. The previous research lacked a theory-based empirical explanation of what factors lead to the reuse of an ODR service. By contrast, this study adopted the mixed-methods approach to examine decisional factors involved with the use of ODR services. We adopted the mixed-methods approach particularly because neither a qualitative nor a quantitative method alone is adequate to answer the proposed research questions. We followed the guidelines for mixed-methods research [68,69] to decide on the appropriateness of mixedmethods research, develop strategies for mixed-methods research designs, develop strategies for collecting and analyzing mixed-methods, draw meta-inferences, and assess the quality of meta-inferences. A mixedmethods approach provides an opportunity to develop great insights into the phenomenon involved and to develop an outstanding theoretical model [68,69]. In this study, the ODR service users were primarily interviewed based on the qualitative research as a way to extract the factors for using the ODR service. These extracted factors were then applied to the customer value theory to develop a theoretical framework and research model for the empirical study. This study contributes by conceptualizing the relationships between the factors identified in the qualitative study, developing the research model based on the customer value theory, and validating the model with ODR service users.

Second, this study extends the customer value theory [59,66] and empirically analyzes the connection between value factors and ODR reuse intention in the context of an ODR service. A theory explains a phenomenon by specifying what variables are related to what variables and how they are related with its justification (i.e., why) [73]. Thus, we applied the customer value theory in interpreting the decisional factors we explored in the qualitative study and in developing the theoretical framework in the second-stage quantitative study. The same approach has been adopted by previous researchers (e.g., [33]) who explored decisional factors for mobile app purchases in a first-stage qualitative study and then the applied mental accounting theory to interpret these factors and develop a theoretical framework in a second-stage quantitative study. We further applied the customer value theory in explaining the rationale of each hypothesis (i.e., how each explored decisional factor affects ODR service reuse). Therefore, the use of the

#

customer value theory improves the validity of the quantitative study and of the results. This study is the first to examine the factors influencing intention to reuse an ODR service from the perspective of customer value. Thus, the study contributes to the literature by identifying factors and generating new insights into how these identified factors lead to the reuse of an ODR service.

Third, this study applies interactivity, especially an ODR service. We applied the perspective of interactivity in an ODR service process as well as its technical/functional characteristics and awareness level. The present study is the first to examine the three subdimensions (i.e., ubiquitous connectivity, service convenience, and two-way communication) of the interactivity of an ODR as a second-order reflective construct from previous literature [36,54,55] and interview results. This study is to examine the three subdimensions of the interactivity of an ODR as a second-order reflective construct. Thus, it expands the literature on ODR service by incorporating its conceptual model into a comprehensive set of the interactivity of ODR and thereby stimulating the other customers’ values and customers’ decisions.

Fourth, in this research, the factors influencing the ODR reuse intention were newly proposed, and the relationships between such factors were examined. This research confirmed that interactivity, reassurance, Moreover, it confirms that interactivity and reassurance influence the relative attractiveness of an ODR service. As for the comparison between the current and previous studies, the previous studies on ODR services [2,3,49,56] did not explain the specific characteristics of ODR services that affect customers’ decisions and the relationships of these characteristics. Although Peng et al. [49] found key factors (i.e., perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, compatibility, subjective norm, and perceived risk) that affect user adoption, these factors are not unique to ODR service use. We developed a research model with the specific characteristics of ODR service and their relationships to examine ODR service reuse. Among the identified decisional factors, reassurance of an ODR service from the perspective of emotional value, a factor we extracted through interviews, has not been previously examined [38]. It is a newly identified factor in the ODR service literature. This study is significant in that it proved that the reassurance features of an ODR service influence the intention to reuse it. Specifically, there is lack of research that examines the main effect of the functional value of an ODR service as well as the role of the conditional value of an ODR service and its relationships with other ODR service decisional factors. Therefore, the present study is the first to examine the specific decisional factors of ODR services and their relationships.

## 5.4. Implications for practice

This study offers several practical implications for practictioners. It suggests that in promoting their service, ODR service providers and practitioners should consider interactivity, reassurance, relative attractiveness, and monetary value as the four factors that influence intention to reuse an ODR service. First, in terms of interactivity, it is necessary to provide an innovative future ODR service that lets customers and drivers interact even more conveniently and actively. The service interface also needs improvement from the perspectives of customers and drivers. Specifically, the current use of a credit card payment limited to a specific ODR service (e.g., KakaoTaxi Black) should be extended to other ODR services to make payments easier and more convenient. In addition, there are opportunities to enhance value even further through such added functions as displays in real time of departure and destination times, real-time location notification, and fare predictions.

Second, service providers should be alert to features and innovations that can further reassure users and potential users of their safety. Because anxiety about crime seems higher in association with taxis than in association with other transportation forms, this reassurance can increase usage and increase the overall culture of the ODR business. Although KakaoTaxi has been making efforts to alleviate such anxiety through its safe message feature, clarifying the taxi driver information through added functions such as “directly selecting the highly evaluated vehicles” and “real-name driver system” will reinforce the service’s commitment to passenger safety. Third, it is necessary to establish a specialized plan so that the ODR service can be accessed even more easily in situations in which it is difficult to use other means of transportation. Our research confirmed that availability at difficult times and places is a major attraction of an ODR service.

#

However, from time to time, drivers decline to accept riders in these circumstances and generate controversy. A strategy that would ensure availability in all circumstances would benefit and further enhance the status of an ODR service.

Finally, although KakaoTaxi enjoys high status as a good value, additional features, services, and service with a company or service business could be considered. From the perspective of the service providers, such an affiliation could easily generate higher revenues and greater profits. From the customers’ perspective, the ride service could be provided as a perk or benefit to customers or employees. Such complementary relationships should come easily for KakaoTaxi as an ODR service. Moreover, launching such an affiliated service would have the potential to not only benefit customers and drivers but also expand the overall market for ODR services. Other ODR services in other nations also can establish tailored strategies to develop and expand the markets for ODR services.
