# THE VOICE OF COMMERCE: HOW SMART SPEAKERS RESHAPE DIGITAL CONTENT CONSUMPTION AND PREFERENCES<sup>1</sup>

Yoonseock Son Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame Notre Dame, IN U.S.A. {yson@nd.edu}

Wonseok Oh College of Business, Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology Seoul, SOUTH KOREA {wonseok.oh@kaist.ac.kr}

Il Im School of Business, Yonsei University Seoul, SOUTH KOREA {il.im@yonsei.ac.kr}}

The present study investigates the effects of smart speaker usage on consumers’ digital content search, purchase, and consumption behaviors. Using a unique panel data set comprising information on household patterns of digital content (e.g., video on demand [VOD]) transactions and consumption and smart speaker usage, we found that the adoption of smart speakers is positively associated with the increased purchase of digital content but negatively related to the average rate of content completion. More specifically, we found that VOD content-related expenditures increased by 21.5% following smart speaker adoption but the average consumption of VOD content purchased decreased by 3.0%. We also examined millions of data points on TV remote-control use and conducted a survey via MTurk to support the validity of the findings. Smart speaker usage can reduce search costs, which subsequently increases search incidence and conversion rates, behavioral changes that can lead to a rise in purchases. We further show that the use of smart speakers for purposes other than information seeking is positively associated with purchases. We develop insights on how to elicit economic value from voice recognition technologies and provide implications for the design and implementation of effective voice commerce strategies

Keywords: Voice channel, search, smart speaker, digital content, econometrics

## Introduction

The rapid advancement of natural language processing and voice recognition has transformed how consumers interact with machines as they search for digital products and services (Simms, 2019). Certain conventional technologies restrict humanmachine interplay to the pressing of buttons or swiping on mobile phones, but more advanced technology enables consumers to use verbal commands to, for example, check the weather or order products from online shopping platforms (Hoffman & Novak, 2018). The considerable attention devoted to voice technology indicates that it has the potential to revolutionize commercial paradigms (The Economist, 2021).

Despite such attention, it is unclear how smart speakers affect user behaviors and translate into revenue (Kharpal, 2017). Although the unique characteristics of voice channels are understood, the influence of voice-assisted transactions on consumer behaviors during search, purchase, and consumption has received limited attention. Previous studies have shown the effects of behavioral changes induced by the introduction of new consumer channels (e.g., mobile) in terms of the demand structures (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 2009) and search and purchase behaviors (e.g., Ghose et al., 2013; Son et al., 2020), but research on how the voice channel affects search cost structures and subsequent behaviors is limited (Kemp, 2019). Given the different cognitive processes associated with the voice modality compared to manual modalities (Klesse et al., 2015), firms need to understand how the voice channel affects consumers’ search and purchase behaviors to adapt their business strategies accordingly (Mayer & Harrison, 2019).

To the best of our knowledge, our research is among the first to examine the efficacy of voice search channels in driving sales transactions and content consumption in the digital content market, where voice-driven technologies are becoming widely adopted to reduce search costs and increase convenience. We began by inquiring about the extent to which using a smart speaker positively influences consumers’ purchase volumes and content consumption rates. We then investigated the changes in consumers’ search patterns to understand the underlying mechanism of subsequent behavioral changes. Smart speakers also are used for other functional purposes, such as informational (i.e., to obtain information such as weather) and conversational purposes that do not require the smart speaker to respond with meaningful information. To extend empirical insights, we also explored how customers’ different functional uses of smart speakers influence their purchase behaviors after they adopt these voice-based devices.<sup>2</sup>

We explored these issues using a unique panel data set on household-level smart speaker usage and video-on-demand (VOD) consumption. We acquired data from a firm that offers various services, including VOD, through its smart speaker platforms. We also analyzed millions of observations related to consumers’ TV remote-control usage patterns to assess changes in search-related behaviors before and after smart speaker adoption. Finally, we administered a survey through Amazon MTurk to provide insights that were not directly observable from the data.

The key findings indicate that smart speaker adoption positively influences consumers’ VOD purchases but negatively affects the completion of VOD consumption (i.e., how much VOD content a consumer watches before abandoning it or switching to a different content). Specifically, the econometric estimates showed that smart speaker adoption led to a 21.5% increase in the monthly purchase of VOD content and a 3.0% decrease in the average completion rate per purchased content. That is, a consumer who, on average, consumed about 70% of a VOD content prior to smart speaker adoption will consume an average of 67.9% of a VOD content following smart speaker adoption. Using the data on remote-control usage, we found that changes in purchase and consumption behaviors are associated with a greater number of search incidences, higher conversion rates, and less labor-intensive and detailed searches. Upon further examination, we found that the use of conversational functions amplifies the positive relationship between smart speaker adoption and purchase volume.

This study contributes to the growing body of literature on the use of voice technologies in digital environments in a number of ways. First, despite the increasing attention paid to voice channels, scant research has explored their value in generating business benefits (e.g., Luo et al., 2021). Whereas prior research is limited to an examination of the differences in voice modality in expressing one-time preferences (e.g., Klesse et al., 2015), we determine how voice modality as a search channel can affect consumers’ behaviors during the purchase process. Second, we assess how the functional use of smart speakers affects the relationship between smart speaker adoption and purchase behaviors. Despite the multiple purposes of smart speaker usage beyond search and purchase, this is the first study to empirically investigate how different functional uses of smart speakers affect purchase behaviors. Third, our findings expand the understanding of how the voice modality changes consumers’ search costs and effort. Whereas the literature on channel adoption has primarily focused on how purchase behaviors vary, we examine changes in search costs and the subsequent consumption of purchased online content.

## Related Literature

## Effects of Different Modalities on Consumer Behaviors

Previous studies indicate that different modalities can result in changes in purchase decisions and consumer behaviors, even when choices are kept constant (e.g., Klesse et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2016), and that the interfaces used to access products can be as important as the products themselves (e.g., Liu et al., 2016). With the exception of studies by Klesse et al. (2015) and Sun et al. (2021), there is limited research on the comparative effects of spoken and manual (e.g., pressing a button) decision-making. Klesse et al., (2015) conducted a series of laboratory experiments to compare the differential effects of verbal versus manual preference. Sun et al., (2021) empirically validated the effects of smart speaker adoption on users’ purchase behaviors and how the effect varies for different user demographics and product categories. The current work extends this nascent stream of literature by using a voice usage panel and remote-control usage data to investigate the temporal dynamics of consumer behaviors in terms of the search, purchase, and consumption process. Further, we categorize commands to understand the heterogeneous effect on changes in purchase behaviors of different functional uses of smart speakers.

## Effects of Consumer Channels on Search Costs and Search Behaviors

The introduction of different consumer channels affects search costs and transaction frictions, resulting in changes in consumer behaviors (e.g., Ghose et al., 2013; Jung et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2017). Search costs and frictions, in turn, influence price dispersion, product sales concentration, and product variety (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 2009; Brynjolfsson et al., 2011). The low search costs associated with voice channels are related to different modalities used in the search process. Voice channels are considered efficient and convenient, requiring minimal mental workloads (D’Mello et al., 2010) and lower search costs (Sun et al., 2021). They are also associated with cognitive processes that are different from other manual modalities (e.g., touching, pushing), thus producing different decision outcomes (e.g., Klesse et al., 2015). Although the research discussed provides valuable insights, it does not address how people’s search behaviors change with the introduction of voice technology and how such variations affect business performance. We extend the literature by examining changes in consumers’ digital content search behaviors (e.g., effort, quality) after the adoption of smart speakers, using voice and remote-control data.

## Research Setting and Data

We used a panel data set from a large telecom company that provides VOD services across diverse market segments. To maintain anonymity, the firm is referred to as the “focal firm.” The focal firm provides VOD and smart speaker services, among others. There is a clear distinction between smart speakers and voice-enabled remote controls, as the functions embedded in the former are not limited to searching and purchasing VOD content. That is, the smart speaker provided by the focal firm is not exclusive to the VOD platform; rather, it is similar to other smart speakers in that it offers various other functions (e.g., ordering products, weather forecasts). Further, the voice-based search attribute for VOD content is only one of the numerous functionalities available on the smart speaker, but no additional features are provided regarding the VOD service when using the speaker. The same recommendation algorithms were used for smart speaker users and nonusers. In addition, the voice command recognition error rate was below 10% for all commands and significantly lower for commands related to the content search. Thus, behavioral changes following smart speaker adoption were unlikely to be affected by errors associated with voice-based commands or different recommender systems.

The data set for the VOD service consisted of the start and end times of VOD viewing, as well as the genre, total runtime, and price of the content. The VOD data cover the period from September 2016 to September 2018 (25 months). The data set on the smart speaker service included the date and time of each use, speech-to-text data on users’ verbal commands, and the categories of each command. Specifically, the focal firm grouped each verbal command into one of three categories (i.e., VOD-related, informational, and conversational commands). The most popular VOD-related subcategories were controlling TV functions (e.g., adjusting volume) and searching for VOD content. Popular informational subcategories included inquiries about news headlines, current time, and weather. Finally, conversational commands include those that do not belong to the first two categories and encompass verbal commands that do not require an informative answer. Examples of conversational commands are “I think I caught a cold,” “I don’t want to do the laundry,” and, “I had a really tough day today.” These speaker data are from June 2017 to September 2018 (16 months).

In collaboration with the focal firm, we obtained data from its promotional campaign, wherein households were offered smart speakers at no cost. We chose this particular period to alleviate the potential endogeneity concern that might arise from the selfselection of smart speakers. Given that smart speakers were randomly offered to consumers free of charge, selection bias could be kept to a minimum. When users called the focal firm’s customer service center during the promotional period, operators randomly offered callers an opportunity to own a smart speaker at the end of the call. The firm used a random selection approach for the promotion to ensure the representativeness of the sample. Furthermore, the offers were extended to households that did not inquire about the smart speakers, as the main objective of the promotion was to encourage the trial or use of a smart speaker. Most of the inquiries from these households concerned other issues, such as mobile phone plans. Hence, the adoption decision of each household was not necessarily a self-directed behavior but rather stimulated by the acceptance of an exogenous offer. To validate our argument, we administered a survey (see “Survey-Based Validation” section) to ascertain how likely participants would be to accept the offer with no prior intention to purchase or use the technology. Only 5% of the respondents stated that they would reject the offer.

We derived a random sample of 3,062 households that were offered and accepted the smart speaker between August and September 2017. These households were then matched with households that called customer service at least once during the promotional period but did not adopt the smart speaker during the data collection period. ## Empirical Specifications

## Propensity Score Matching

Propensity score matching (PSM) is a widely used technique for estimating the influence of a certain treatment through the elimination of incomparable observations with regard to common support for an estimated propensity score distribution (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). We employed PSM to select households among smart speaker adopters (treatment group) and nonadopters (control group) that resembled each other in relevant observable attributes before the adoption took place. We executed the matching procedure by using nine months of data spanning the period before our first observations of smart speaker adoption. We employed a logistic regression model to obtain estimated propensity scores and used one-to-one matching without replacement below a caliper size of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the propensity score. The procedure yielded 1,502 pairs of households (Table 2).

## Difference-in-Differences Analysis

Using the matched household data set acquired via PSM, we conducted a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis; this approach has been widely used to infer causal relationships in certain treatments (Bertrand et al., 2004). In constructing the DID model, we employed the following equation to estimate the treatment effect of smart speaker adoption on the outcome variables $\left( \boldsymbol { \mathsf { y } } _ { i t } \right)$

$$

$$

where i denotes household, and t represents month. The outcome variables $\left( \boldsymbol { \mathsf { y } } _ { i t } \right)$ are purchase amount and completion rate. We used a log-transformed value of purchase amount to interpret the effect size in terms of percentage. $\mathrm { A f t e r } _ { i t }$ is a binary variable that equals 1 for periods after each treated household has adopted a smart speaker, and 0 otherwise. The vector ${ { \mathrm { X } } _ { i t } }$ represents a set of control variables and includes the amount of new content uploaded in month $t ,$ the amount of downloadable content in month t, and the time trend.

Next, we conducted a relative time model to address concerns about non-homogeneous time trends observed in the treatment and control groups (e.g., Greenwood & Wattal, 2017). $$

## Results

## Effects of Smart Speaker Adoption

The main results of the DID analysis are presented in Table 3. As shown in Column 1, expenditure significantly increased among households that adopted smart speakers, relative to those that did not adopt such devices. Specifically, contentrelated expenditures increased by 21.5% following smart speaker adoption. If the average monthly expenditure of each household were \$46, a 21.5% increase would translate into a \$9.89 increase in sales per month for each household. Although the total effect size depends on the user base, our estimate suggests that a 100,000-user base would lead to about a \$989,000 increase in monthly revenue. This may be a simplistic calculation, as various factors (e.g., platform characteristics, profit structure) were disregarded. Nonetheless, our results point to a considerable increase in revenue associated with providing smart speaker adoption. Column 2 presents evidence of a reduction in completion rates, with the average consumption decreasing by 3.0% following smart speaker adoption in the treatment group.

We then validated that most of the estimates in the pre-treatment period were insignificant (Table 4), which indicates that there was no imbalance between the two groups, implying a homogeneous time trend during the pre-adoption period. Treatment effects were also observed immediately following smart speaker adoption for both outcome variables. The coefficient signs during the post-adoption period are consistent with those of the coefficients in the main analyses, further confirming the treatment effect found in the main analysis.

## Robustness Checks and Falsification Tests

Alternative matching procedures—PSM using alternate sample: To examine whether our results remained consistent with different sample compositions, we obtained data from 31,043 randomly sampled households with at least one VOD order record during our data collection period. Among these households, 23,358 did not adopt the focal firm’s smart speaker service during the data collection period, whereas the remaining 7,685 adopted the service between June 2017 and October 2017. Of the households that adopted the service, we sampled 4,316 households that had records of smart speaker usage at least once a month after adoption. We then replicated our PSM procedure, which yielded 2,762 treated and 2,762 matched households (details of the sample and the matching procedure are available upon request). As shown in Panel A of Table 5, our results remain consistent with those found in the main analyses.

Alternative matching procedures—Look-ahead PSM: If households that adopted smart speakers were to systematically differ from nonadopting households in ways that could not be accounted for by the observables, this would indicate that treatment effects may suffer from potential bias from unobservables. To address this potential threat to validity, we followed previous literature (e.g., Bapna et al., 2016) and replicated our main analysis by using only the treatment group that shared inherent attributes related to smart speaker adoption. As our treatment group described in the previous section consisted of households that adopted smart speakers between June and October 2017, we treated those that adopted the devices in June 2017 as the new treatment group (?????????? equals 1) and those that adopted in October 2017 as the new control group $( T r e a t _ { i }$ equals 0). Hence, our new control group consisted of households that had not adopted smart speakers at that time but would do so in the future. We also replicated our PSM procedures using the same variables in our main analysis, leaving us with 809 household pairs. We then modified the values of our adoption variable $( A d o p t i o n _ { \mathrm { i t } } )$ to 1 for periods between June and September 2017, and 0 for periods prior to June 2017. As shown in Panel B of Table 5, an alternative matching specification yielded consistent results.

We also conducted additional robustness checks by running the Rosenbaum sensitivity analysis and a coarsened exact matching procedure, using alternative settings (e.g., different measures of dependent variables, aggregating the data set into two time periods, i.e., before and after smart speaker adoption, and using the block bootstrap technique). The results are all consistent with our main findings. The details are provided in Appendix C.

Influence of inaccurate searches on treatment effects: Although our focal smart speaker had a low voice command recognition error rate for commands related to VOD content searches, we further evaluated whether inaccurate searches may have led to any bias in our estimates. We considered content with extremely quick discontinuations (i.e., played for less than a minute) to be either content purchases or views from inaccurate searches and created two new dependent variables— namely, the percentage of paid and unpaid content play with extremely quick discontinuations. These variables were then used to replicate Equation (1). The purpose of the additional analyses is twofold: to assess how a voice channel affected extremely quick discontinuations for paid and unpaid content and to evaluate whether and how much extremely quick discontinuations affected our main results.

As presented in Panel A of Table 6, the percentage of extremely quick discontinuations did not significantly change for paid content following smart speaker adoption. These results indicate that the main findings derived on the basis of paid content were not affected by erroneous searches due to technical limitations. To clarify whether extremely quick discontinuations biased our findings, we removed the purchase and consumption records related to paid content with extremely quick discontinuations and replicated our main analyses. As shown in Panel B, the results remain consistent with the main findings. Finally, we assessed whether our results were indirectly affected by the extremely quick discontinuations of unpaid content by dividing the sample into two groups: households that showed higher (HUD—i.e., high unpaid discontinuations—equals 1) and lower (HUD equals 0) unpaid content plays with extremely quick discontinuations compared to the median frequency. Panel C shows no difference in the treatment effects between the two groups, indicating that inaccurate searches of unpaid content did not affect our results.

## Effect of Smart Speaker Usage Pattern

In this section, we consider how each household’s smart speaker usage patterns were related to the treatment effects on the purchase amount. Users may have multiple intentions for engaging with smart speakers. Although more emphasis is placed on smart speakers’ functional and informational features, more than half of users treat such technologies as “companions” and use them for conversational purposes (e.g., requesting jokes and asking fun questions) (Handley, 2019; Purington et al., 2017). Hence, it is equally important for service providers to understand not only the informational and functional effects of smart speakers but also those related to their conversational features (Kemp, 2019).

## Explanation of Underlying Mechanisms

## Remote-Control Usage Data

Our remote-control usage data set covers August 2017 to August 2018 and consists of more than 34.6 million clicks made by 4,035 randomly selected households that made more than one purchase during the data period. The data set comprised the time of remote-control manipulation, the remote-control button that was clicked (e.g., “down,” “okay,” “home”), the type of page viewed (e.g., home menu, search menu), and the content viewed. Among the 4,035 households, 317 had records of using smart speakers during the data period, and 257 households purchased content between November 2017 and May 2018. We used the latter households as the treatment group in our analyses. Next, we conducted PSM in accordance with the months in which the households adopted the smart speakers. To illustrate, for households who adopted the smart speakers in November 2017, we used a monthly average of matching variables before November 2017. We include the matching and search-related variables in Table 2, resulting in 257 pairs of matched households.

## Variable Operationalization

We used four approaches to examine search cost reduction as the underlying mechanism of increased purchase volume. We created total clicks, operationalized as the total number of remote-control clicks (before smart speaker adoption) as well as the sum of the total number of remote-control clicks and smart speaker commands used (after smart speaker adoption). Further, as the effort involved in remote-control clicks versus voice commands may not be comparable, we considered the number of episodes, which was operationalized as the number of remotecontrol clicks until the user (1) purchases VOD content (total episodes[purchase]) and (2) enters the detailed page of VOD content information (total episodes[detailed search]). Detailed search occurred when a user clicked on certain VOD content to access in-depth information, such as cast, genre, summary, and run time. We also considered multiple consecutive remotecontrol clicks as an episode (e.g., two clicks as an episode, three clicks as an episode, four clicks as an episode) to provide more descriptive evidence on the relationship between remote-control clicks and voice commands and assessed when the treatment effect became insignificant.

Next, we referred to search day and conversion rate to determine whether reduced effort or search cost ultimately created higher demand. Search day was operationalized as the number of days in a month that a customer searched for VOD content, and conversion rate was operationalized by dividing the number of purchases in days by the number of search incidences in days for each month. We also categorized the types of buttons into two groups: basic and advanced. Basic buttons include the down, up, left, right, and “okay” keys, whereas advanced buttons are those that feature more sophisticated functions (e.g., the latest uploaded content).

Then, we operationalized the variable basic click percentage, which was obtained by dividing the total number of basic clicks by the total number of clicks. Through this process, we looked into whether the smart speakers offered a high level of convenience (e.g., a low proportion of basic click usage). Next, we explored whether customers made less detailed searches when using the smart speakers. During the search process, users could begin to seek content from a TV screen with thumbnails of multiple offerings displayed on a grid-like structure, in which only basic information about the given content was provided. A screen with detailed information on the selection (e.g., cast, summary) was displayed on the screen if users clicked on the content. In this analysis, we categorized the former as a nondetailed search and the latter as a detailed search and operationalized detailed search percentage as the number of instances of detailed search over the total number of searches.

Although we were able to identify which type of interface was shown on the TV screen when the remote control was clicked, we could not identify which type of interface was shown on the TV screen when a smart speaker was used during the search process. To address such data constraints, we took the most and least conservative approaches and assumed that all voice commands were related to basic clicks and nonbasic clicks for the variables basic click percentage upper bound (UB), and basic click percentage lower bound (LB), respectively. The same approach was employed for detailed search percentage in constructing the boundaries.

Results: Table 9 presents the results of our analysis for changes in different search behaviors. The results indicate a statistically significant decrease in total clicks, total episodes (purchase), and total episodes (detailed search) after smart speaker adoption. In addition, we found a statistically significant increase in search day and conversion rate after smart speaker adoption. It follows then that intensified purchase is associated with less vigorous search behaviors, greater search incidences, and higher conversion rates. Furthermore, we found that following smart speaker adoption, households generally engaged in less labor-intensive search behaviors and were more likely to purchase content without considering the options in detail. More specifically, as shown in Panels A and B of Table 9, the signs of the coefficients for basic click percentage remain consistent and statistically significant, even considering the upper and lower bounds, with a decrease in the range of 2.21 to 13.1%. Next, as demonstrated in Panels C and D of Table 9, we observed statistically significant decreases in detailed search percentage LB and detailed search percentage UB, ranging from 1.74% to 16.1%.

Our results also show that the treatment effect is significant until the search cost of one voice command is considered equivalent to the search cost of nine remote-control clicks (Table 10). Taken together, these results support our argument that the adoption of a smart speaker reduces the total search cost during a VOD content search.

## Survey-Based Validation

Next, we conducted a survey to understand the correlation between smart speaker usage and changes in user perceptions that may not have been directly observable. We recruited 700 smart speaker users from Amazon MTurk who had used our focal smart speaker and had experience searching for VOD content using the smart speaker. The questionnaires are provided in Appendix D.

The responses indicate that 74%, 70%, and 70% of the survey participants, respectively, provided positive answers (i.e., 5, 6, and 7 out of a 7-point Likert scale) regarding whether they were able to search more easily, conveniently, and with less effort, after smart speaker adoption. Further, 82% and 79% of the respondents, respectively, indicated that they searched and purchased more after adopting the smart speaker. These responses support our argument that smart speakers reduce the effort demanded in searches and encourage more searches and purchases. Finally, 57%, 55%, and 64% of the respondents, respectively, answered that they purchased content less carefully, purchased more spontaneously, and paid less attention to searches. These findings support our argument that users conduct less detailed searches after smart speaker adoption.

## Discussion and Implications

Considering the nascence of the service and the lack of understanding regarding the economic impact of smart speaker usage, understanding its value to practitioners is important (Koksal 2018). Hence, we address whether managers should embrace smart speakers in their offerings and what they should expect from smart speaker adoption, how the adoption changes consumer behaviors, and why. Our results present voice technology providers and developers with managerial insights regarding how their services should be designed to effectively translate voice-based interactions into economic benefits. As our findings indicate, online platforms, particularly those that offer digital content, can capitalize on the enhanced interactions engendered by smart speakers, given their effects on search, purchase, and consumption behaviors.

Our results suggest that the adoption of smart speakers positively affects consumers’ VOD purchase propensities largely because of the reduction in search costs. Hence, service providers should offer marketing campaigns to encourage customers to engage with smart speakers, as providing them with this alternative option to search for content products may change their behaviors. Nevertheless, as indicated by the reduction in consumption rates, managers and developers should continue their efforts to enhance the accuracy and reliability of search functions in smart speakers so that users can search for content more effectively. Our results also provide important practical implications in terms of the issues that should be taken into consideration when embracing smart speakers. Although many believe that the core function of a smart speaker is to act as a virtual assistant and provide needed information (e.g., Mari et al., 2020), we show that service providers should not neglect the importance of conversational interactions between users and smart speakers. More emphasis should be placed on how to encourage conversational interactions, as such engagements are positively associated with purchase behaviors.

Notwithstanding these benefits, managers have reason to be concerned about the increase in voice-based commercial paradigms. Online innovations that necessitate a conversion from text to voice may disrupt certain existing business processes, including product searches and purchases (Simms, 2019), which may cause online retailers to substantially revamp many of their current online operations and marketing strategies (e.g., search engine advertisements, recommendation systems). Whether retailers benefit from or are disadvantaged by the advent of voice-first technology through smart speakers, they have begun to penetrate the online marketplace, ushering in voice innovations in the retail and content industries.

We propose several avenues for future research to extend the nascent literature stream based on the results. First, we did not address the reasoning and consequences of the decrease in the completion rate of digital content; thus, future work could explore why users show lower completion rates following smart speaker adoption and how it affects their subsequent behaviors. For instance, if users repeatedly fail to find the right content and thus do not consume the content, their engagement with smart speakers may gradually drop and ultimately lead to churns (Arkenberg et al., 2021). Second, our results provide evidence of changes in search behaviors; thus, future work could focus on the effectiveness of recommender systems during smart speaker usage. Although we could not access detailed information on the usage of recommender systems, researchers could extend our endeavors by considering the changes in search and recommender system usage behaviors while using a smart speaker. Third, future work could categorize the commands at a more granular level to investigate which types of commands are associated with changes in search and purchase behaviors. For instance, future work could build upon anthropomorphism theory to assess how emotional relationships with smart speakers affect users shopping behaviors. Further, to extend our results on the differential impacts of verbal commands on purchase behaviors, future studies could conduct predictive analytics in regard to users’ behaviors, based on the types of commands used. Fourth, despite the low error rate of the focal smart speaker, we did not thoroughly investigate the error rate, which may have affected search behaviors. Future research could take the error rate into consideration when examining the effect of smart speaker usage. For instance, researchers could investigate the effects of errors on users’ subsequent search behaviors (e.g., greater reliance on the recommender system or the “most popular” list). Fifth, our focus was on the purchase of a single product (i.e., VOD), but smart speakers advance the purchase of various product categories. Hence, our results may not apply to other products whose attributes can be evaluated prior to purchase or consumption (e.g., water, paper towels) and do not require searches to identify the quality or fit with users’ preferences. Further, we did not examine the availability of free VOD content in detail, which could have impacted the purchase of paid content. For instance, we did not consider the availability of different categories of free VOD content. Future studies could also consider the purchase and consumption of free VOD content and explore how consumers’ behavioral changes differ between free and paid VOD content. Sixth, visual aids (i.e., TV screens) allowing users to see the search results as they engage in searches via the smart speaker were associated with the voice channel in our context. Hence, future studies could build on the generalizability of our results by investigating settings without visual aids, as different dimensions of search costs (e.g., memory) would likely affect the search process.

It is only a matter of time before voice innovation evolves into a principal avenue for online commercial transactions. Amazon is now selling TVs with Alexa or Echo in one package and their launch of the voice-controlled Fire TV is expected to extend users’ shopping experience to include transactions via TV screens. Voice commerce will likewise play an important role not only in the seamless integration of existing offline and online channels but also in the establishment of hybrid stores in which online and offline venues intersect through voice-enabled bots. As advanced intelligence technologies progressively mature, voice—a communication and interaction conduit with which most humans feel comfortable—will soon emerge as a major component in future commerce.

Coarsened exact matching procedure: The coarsened exact matching (CEM) procedure is classified as a general class of methods, termed monotonic imbalance bounding, which is known to have several advantages over the “equal percent bias reducing” models to which PSM belongs (Iacus et al. 2011). The technique is considered superior when the correct functional model of how users are assigned to the treatment group is not explicitly known. Further, it has advantages, such as not requiring a separate procedure to restrict data to common support, balancing all nonlinearities and interactions, and meeting the congruence principle (Blackwell et al. 2009).
