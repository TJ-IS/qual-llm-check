James B. Pick and Raso l Azari

Ab str act: This exploratory paper presents a conceptual model of the factors of governmental support and openness, business and technology investment, and socioeconomic level that are posited to influence technological utilization. The conceptual model and conjectures are developed inductively based on logic and prior research about the relationship among variables related to the factors. Structural equation modeling (SEM) is applied to operationalize and test the model. The SEM analysis tests five points of investigation on a large sample of country data from the World Bank and the World Economic Forum. Findings indicate a critical pathway of associations between the factors of government support and openness, investment in business and technology, socioeconomic level, and technology utilization. The paper presents two country case examples of the model and suggests policy steps for national governments of developed and developing countries to prioritize information and communications technology, create openness, strengthen research and development and technology investment, and enhance education and information technology training.

Key wor ds and p hr ases: global digital divide, government investment, societal openness, socioeconomic factors, structural equation modeling, technological utilization.

It is imp or tant that we develop a b etter under standing of national and international patterns of technology utilization so that policymakers and government planners can more thoughtfully weigh the choices and so people in countries at varying levels of development can increasingly receive the benefits of technology. Technology has increased rapidly worldwide; for instance, in 2009, the number of Internet users worldwide was estimated at 1.67 billion, or 25 percent penetration of global population. Of world regions, North America has 74 percent penetration, whereas Africa has 7 percent [25]. From 2000 to 2010, the number of global Internet users increased at a yearly rate of 17 percent [27]. Likewise, in 2009, the number of mobile phone connections was estimated at 4.15 billion, and has grown at a 20 percent annual rate since 2000 [32]. Even with such advances, most of the world’s population, at 6.9 billion in December 2010 [47], does not yet have access to the Internet and mobile technologies, so much more development will need to take place in the coming decades to provide services to the world’s populace.

This paper explores the following research question:

RQ1: What factors correlate with technology utilization for countries, and how do the factors cross-link?

Technology is defined for this paper as physical systems that support access to information and means of communications. Technology utilization is defined as use by people of technologies for information and communications. This section turns to a brief review of the streams of past research, with emphasis on areas with gaps or little prior study; the next section provides inductive analysis of literature in support of building a model.

There are studies at the country level that indicate some governments have mediocre or poor results in planning and realizing technology utilization, often not providing the appropriate training or technology to the citizenry [15, 38, 49, 51]. The challenges of achieving technology utilization are even greater for developing countries because they confront more pressing sets of problems than technology, have training and educational gaps to overcome, and are poorer in resources [27, 31, 33, 41]. Countries have many stakeholders in the process of improving technology utilization, including national governments, cities and local governments, private companies, schools and universities, and the users themselves [18, 19, 21, 45, 53], so part of the challenge is to include them.

The literature does not yet fully explain patterns of technology usage at the country level around the globe. It has shed some light conceptually and empirically on determinants that influence technology utilization. Several studies have examined technology utilization for samples of countries worldwide [12, 28, 37, 39, 42]. They incorporate a variety of models and postulate a set of independent variables that together influence technology outcomes. Conceptual models are either drawn from other disciplines such as economics [12, 14, 28] or sociology [22] or formulated as fairly simple exploratory ones.

Modernization theories provide elaborate models of how countries and their citizens advance socially and economically over time. However, they rarely include information technology (IT) factors. An exception is a cross-national study drawing on modernization and diffusion theories with some mention of dependency and world system theories, in which countries are postulated to evolve toward greater modernization and a more information-diffused, open political realm [42].

The “freedom as development” theory of Sen [43] posits that the longitudinal development process of how a “third world” country transforms to a more modern country does not depend mainly on increase in income, but crucially on the expansion of political and economic freedom. This implies that an individual in the impoverished country would gain greater choice and potential to pursue freedoms, including freedom of speech, freedom to obtain life’s basic necessities, and freedom of active political participation. Income then becomes a means of development, while achieving freedoms is the end [43]. Although this elaborate theory does not include IT utilization, it could be added as a means for the individual to learn about choices, understand democracy, and participate politically. Modernization theories of Sen [43] and others can inform digital divide studies at the country level, such as that of Baliomoune-Lutz [3], by including freedom attributes measured for the country.

Studies have used multivariate analysis to estimate determinants of technology attributes and technology change. A study that focused on member countries of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) made use of a simple Gompertz model of diffusion of Internet technologies [28]. Internet diffusion speed was posited to depend on demand-side attributes of income per capita, Internet access cost, and telecommunications competition, while education and English language were also included. In regression testing, income per capita and Internet access costs were influential. When the sample was extended to non-OECD members, education also became significant. An exploratory, empirical study examined the influence of economic, demographic, infrastructure, telecommunication pricing, and regulatory quality determinants of Internet and personal computer (PC) penetration for 1999–2001 for a large sample of 161 countries [12] and found that the global digital divide is predominantly explained by income differences. A study of the effects of 13 attributes on information and communications technology (ICT) usage, expenditure, and infrastructure (ICTUEI) for 71 developed and developing countries, based on a mixture of prior conceptual frameworks, utilized multiple regression analysis to find that for developed countries, ICTUEI determinants were scientific publications, foreign direct investment (FDI), women in the labor force, and education; for developing countries, the determinants were foreign direct investment (FDI), government prioritization of IT, and education [39]. These multivariate statistical studies have yielded prominent determinants but have the weakness of not taking into account cross-flows and complex paths.

Two other research streams describe conceptual frameworks [21, 41, 44, 45] or focus on small samples of regional or developing countries [3]. A conceptual framework, based on ten country case studies of e-commerce diffusion, proposed global environmental factors that influenced in turn national environmental factors, national policy factors, and e-commerce adoption [21]. E-commerce drivers, enablers, barriers, and inhibitors of the process were identified. A related conceptual study [44], based mostly on India, constructed a somewhat similar framework that formulated a literature-driven set of critical success factors for electronic services. Raven et al. [41] performed a qualitative comparison of e-commerce growth in India and China based on the dimensions of economic development, technology, regulatory environment, ICT infrastructure, national policy, and culture. For understanding worldwide technology utilization, these studies offer concepts and findings for limited sets of countries but have the weakness of small samples, which are not globally representative.

Although important findings come from the body of literature, a number of questions are left unanswered. Studies have not integrated into a model the broad range of determinants identified throughout this line of research. Although some studies describe how complex the process of technology utilization is [21, 28, 42, 45, 56], they leave unanswered how to formulate a quantitative model to account for the complexity. These empirical studies do not answer what cross-flows might occur between the sets of independent attributes; instead, the independent variables are regarded as together influencing an outcome variable, but not each other. The literature rarely groups similar variables together as factors [12, 42]. It sometimes compares results for developed versus developing countries, but has not applied a complex, multifactor model of technology utilization to reveal differences between developed and developing countries.

To close this gap, the present study uses inductive logic based on prior reported findings to build a descriptive model of factors that correlate with each other and with technology utilization at the country unit of analysis. The study is exploratory [46], inducing a model and conducting empirical research to test the correlations of factors in the model.

The answer to this study’s research question goes beyond prior studies in analyzing a group of latent factors with cross-linkages together in their effect on technology utilization for the country unit of analysis. Prior studies have been more limited, examining the effects of a group of variables on a dependent variable. This paper’s contributions are to induce and offer an enlarged theoretical model for the digital divide among countries, to do exploratory testing of the model, and to offer suggestions for national government policies in developed and developing countries. The next section presents an inductive analysis of literature findings and logical thought to build a descriptive theory of factors that correlate with each other and with national technology utilization. The third section presents research methods for an exploratory study to demonstrate findings consistent with the theory. The fourth section gives the analysis of results. The fifth section discusses implications for research and practice, including two examples, limitations, and future research directions, and the sixth section concludes.

Inductive Analysis to the Literature to Support the

Building of a Model

This section of the p ap er inductively b uilds a concep tual model of technology utilization [46]. Four constructs are described, defined, and justified, based on prior literature: technology utilization; business and technology investment; government support of IT, legal framework, and social openness; and socioeconomic level.

## Technology Utilization

Technology utilization has been characterized in prior studies as measuring directly and indirectly uses by people of computing, Internet, and telecommunications. Authors have referred to it as technology capacity [42], technology usage [35, 39], technology use [50], and technology penetration [12]. Although the terminology differs, the measures are similar. Robison and Crenshaw [42] examined the number of Internet hosts per 10,000 population. An Internet host is a computer connected to the Internet. The measure indirectly measures use of Internet resources by people. Chinn and Fairlie [12] defined computer penetration as number of PCs per 100 people and Internet penetration as number of Internet users per 100. Pick and Azari [39] included three dependent variables similar to the above studies in indirectly measuring technology usage: PCs per 1,000 people, Internet hosts per 1,000, and mobile phones per 1,000.

Some studies are able to measure technology at two time points, yielding a diffusion rate, which indicates the rate of change [3, 28, 56]. Kiiski and Pohjola [28] defined Internet diffusion as the logarithmic difference in Internet hosts per capita during a five-year period. Baliamoune-Lutz [3] calculated two-year diffusion per capita of cell phones, Internet hosts, Internet users, and PCs. Zhao et al. [56] utilized nine years of data to calculate “rate of Internet diffusion” based on the logarithmic difference in Internet users per 1,000. Ono and Zavodny [35] analyzed the diffusion of home computer ownership and number of Internet users per 100 people. Because the present study utilizes cross-sectional data, the definition of technology utilization resembles those in the nondiffusion studies [12, 39, 42]. However, it also resembles single time point constructs from the studies with diffusion indicators [3, 28, 35, 56].

Our Technology Utilization construct can be induced by referring to the widely used dependent indicators in the literature. We build this construct based on per capita annual indicators commonly found in prior studies; in particular, number of Internet users, Internet host severs, PCs, and mobile phone subscriptions. The latter indirectly represents the number of mobile phone users. Technology utilization is defined as the overall extent to which people in a country make use of Internet, computing, and mobile phone technologies.

## Business and Technology Investment

To explain the dependent construct, a business and technology investment factor reflects technology determinants, technology infrastructure, research and development (R&D), and the business/investment factors of financial markets, financial investment, and FDI. Baliamoune-Lutz [3] included FDI per capita as an independent variable, reasoning that it is important in influencing diffusion of technologies in developing countries, but found it to be insignificant. Pick and Azari [39] included FDI per capita as an independent variable for similar reasons, as well as the variable of science and technical journal articles per capita. The latter was posited to generally affect the level of business and technology in a country; for example, the United States and Finland have high R&D per capita and are known for their advanced global technology industries. Findings showed the R&D determinant to be dominant for a worldwide sample of 71 countries and for its developed-country subsample. For developing countries, FDI had the strongest relationship with technology use.

In a largely conceptual study with cases for China and India [41], technology infrastructure was formulated to be a major factor for e-commerce adoption and was seen to be more emphasized by China than India. Sharma and Gupta [44] posited that one of the six major factors associated with e-commerce adoption in India is “national environment,” comprising industry structure, information infrastructure, and financial systems, which are relevant to the present Business and Technology Investment construct. Deficiencies were identified for India’s telecommunication infrastructure and technological readiness that reduced the country’s e-commerce adoption. In a broad conceptual model for e-commerce adoption, Gibbs et al. [21] included a construct that comprised financial resources, information infrastructure, and industry structure, but this study lacked quantitative testing.

The Business and Technology Investment construct is defined as the financial, investment, R&D, and infrastructure environment conducive to developing and implementing technology for users in a country. The construct differs from prior constructs in the literature, although it would have some overlap with “national environment” from Gibbs et al. [21]. Other studies offer either one or two individual variables related to the construct. The present construct goes beyond prior studies in broadly defining the national business, R&D, and technology setting.

## Government Support of IT, Legal Framework, and Social Openness

Some prior research has included aspects of government support of IT, legal and regulatory framework, and sociopolitical openness. They are reflected in a factor we term GSLO. This factor is expected to be at a high level in European members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), the United States, Canada, Australia, and advanced, democratic countries. In poor, developing countries that have autocratic government, GSLO is expected to be weaker. For a developing country, government support for technology utilization would be reduced relative to the more basic needs of food, energy, sanitation, and basic education. Robison and

Crenshaw [42] suggested that sociopolitical openness increases technology utilization. They reasoned that a government open to political and social feedback from its citizens would be more likely to adopt “information diffusing” technologies such as the Internet for its citizens. In predicting Internet hosts per capita, Robison and Crenshaw found that openness was tied with secondary education as most significant. Zhao et al. [56] included rule of law, defined as “extent to which agents have confidence in and abide by the rules of society” [56, p. 41]. It was found to be among the six most significant factors associated with Internet diffusion. Chinn and Fairlie [12] included regulation quality, which they found determined gaps in computer and Internet use. In a sample study that included several GSLO variables, findings indicated that government restrictions on Internet content was not important for IT usage, expenditure, and infrastructure, whereas government prioritization of IT was a determinant for developing countries [39]. Raven et al. [41] identified government support of IT development as crucial for China and important for India.

For developing countries, Baliamoune-Lutz [3] posited that social openness and democracy would influence diffusion of per capita mobile phones, Internet hosts, Internet users, and PCs. However, results showed that indices of civil liberties and political rights had only limited effects, while income and international trade dominated.

In our study, the construct of Government Support of IT, Legal Framework, and Social Openness is defined as the presence of legal and regulatory mechanisms reinforced by social openness and national government support of technology. The legal framework and social openness are similar to Baliamoune-Lutz’s [3] two democracy attributes, while government support of IT and government regulation resemble the attributes in the study of Pick and Azari [39]. Several other studies have included portions of this construct [12, 42, 56].

## Socioeconomic Level

Indicators representing socioeconomic level have been commonly included in studies of technology utilization and the digital divide [3, 21, 28, 39, 42, 44]. Robison and Crenshaw [42] included secondary education as a determinant of Internet hosts per capita and found it to be significant. In a study of Internet diffusion, income and education were highly important for the full sample, but only income was significant for the subsample of developed countries [28]. English proficiency was of little importance. The role of education was explained as providing critical skills for Internet use. Another study found that education was influential on technology use, while females in the labor force was of little importance [39]. In two studies with well-developed conceptual models [21, 44], education and income were posited as key factors to promote a national environment conducive to e-commerce utilization.

The Socioeconomic Level construct is defined as the presence in countries of citizenry that is educated, prosperous, and of working age. The construct builds on individual variables in the literature. Prior studies [3, 21, 28, 39, 42, 44] have emphasized income, literacy, and education. Labor force [21, 39] and age-structural attributes have received little attention but are important in stimulating greater technology use because nonworkers and the elderly are less likely to use technologies. Our construct offers a blend of appropriate socioeconomic characteristics conducive to technology use that is broader than in prior studies.

For the three independent constructs, investment in business and technology (INVBT), government support of IT, legal framework, and societal openness (GSLO), and socioeconomic level (SOCL EV), and one dependent construct, technology utilization (TECUTIL), we make the following arguments for the directionality of causal relationships. We posit that INVBT and GSLO are positively linked in both directions with each other—that is, they co-vary. We reason that a country with a well-functioning legal and regulatory system is more likely to have strong financial markets and investments. If government gives higher priority to ICT, then technological readiness is likely to be greater. Likewise, a country with a high level of business and technology investment and R&D is likely to have developed rights and laws related to real property and intellectual property in order to protect new products. By contrast, a country with little governmental interest in ICT and weak intellectual property laws and regulations is likely to discourage internal or external investment, have lower levels of R&D, and be less technologically ready. Simon [45] conceptualized that technical critical success factors (CSFs), similar to the GSLO construct, and societal CSFs, similar to the INVBT construct, are synergistic with each other, contributing to the success of e-commerce.

We posit that higher GSLO will lead to higher SOCL EV. Case studies of Estonia [16] and India [44] support this relationship for midlevel and developing countries. As Estonia shifted in the 1990s from Soviet authoritarianism to a modernized government and an open, democratic society under the rule of law, the federal government consistently supported the development and use of ICT. India’s democratic modernization in the twentieth century led to stronger education and higher income, which in turn favored IT use. Baliamoune-Lutz [3] included freedom indicators with the justification that social and economic freedoms in a country are related to higher income, education, and literacy, and in turn IT use. We found no literature to support the opposite path, that SOCL EV influenced GSLO.

We posit that SOCL EV is related to TECUTIL. This relationship is strongly supported for the SOCL EV dimensions of education and income [3, 13, 14, 21, 28, 40, 42, 44] and is moderately supported for labor force [39]. We reason that an educated, prosperous, and working population will have greater capability to learn about, undergo training for, and be motivated toward technology uses and applications. We found only one study supporting that TECUTIL influenced SOCL EV, a longitudinal study of German households [30]. The household unit of analysis and single-country setting makes it difficult to justify this reverse path for samples of countries.

Last, we posit a direct relationship between INVBT and TECUTIL. One investigation across countries found that scientific R&D was the foremost determinant of ICT usage, expenditure, and infrastructure, while FDI was tied for second [39]. Technology readiness was shown to directly influence IT usage in case analyses of India and China [21, 44]. For Egypt, IT investment for immediate payoff, the equivalent of the INVBT to TECUTIL link, was the goal in a nationwide technology training program, but it was not realized because of poor planning of the program [51]. This link presumes that INVBT, co-varying with GSLO, directly affects utilization by making technology available, without the necessity of an educated, prosperous, and working citizenry. We found no literature to support the reverse, that TECUTIL caused INVBT. The conceptual model and its relationships are shown in Figure 1.

The paths in this model are represented by the following conjectures:

Conjecture 1: The constructs INVBT and GSLO significantly co-vary with each other.

Conjecture 2: The construct GSLO significantly correlates with SOCLEV.

Conjecture 3: The construct SOCLEV significantly correlates with TECUTIL.

Conjecture 4: The construct INVBT significantly correlates with TECUTIL.

## Points of Investigation

Based on the four conjectures, we will test the following points of investigation:

•	 Point 1: Do countries that have higher levels of business and technology investment have higher levels of government support, legal framework, and social openness?

•	 Point 2: Do countries that have higher levels of government support, legal framework, and social openness have higher socioeconomic levels?

•	 Point 3: Do countries that have higher socioeconomic level have higher technology utilization?

•	 Point 4: Do countries that make larger business and technology investment have higher technology utilization?

•	 Point 5: Do the country data conform to or differ from the overall conceptual model, which is described by the conjectures?

## Indicators for Technology Utilization (TECUTIL)

For the construct of Technology Utilization, there are four indicators given in Table 1. For a construct, this table, as well as Tables 2, 3, and 4, gives each indicator’s definition, abbreviation, status as reflective or formative, year of data collection, data source, measurement, data transformation if present, and number of data points. The indicator INTUSERS (Internet Users per 100 population) directly measures utilization. It is assumed in this study that a mobile phone subscriber represents one user, so MOBPHSUB (Mobile Phone Subscribers per 1,000 population) also measures utilization. Because server utilization per capita is difficult to measure and is not available on a worldwide basis from international data sources, we use servers per capita as a proxy for server utilization. We assume that number of servers is proportionate to server utilization. Because a PC is intended and marketed to have a single user, we also assume that number of PCs per capita is proportionate to PC users per capita. Exceptions would be public PCs such as in labs or kiosks, but no attribute is available from international data sources for countries to measure the number or proportion of public PCs and their users.

The two data sources, the World Economic Forum 2006–2007 [17] and the World Development Indicators 2007 [55] have comprehensive, systematic, and accurate data at the country level. Variables drawn from them for this research originated within a two-year time period. A difference is that the World Economic Forum source includes some attributes from a large, worldwide executive survey, whereas the World Development Indicators data are based on national censuses and government surveys. Data transformations were limited to per capita calculations and two natural log conversions.

Indicators for all the constructs are reflective. The latent construct is always posited to cause the indicator variables, rather than the indicator variables being calculated together to cause the latent construct [23].

## Indicators for Government Support of IT, Legal Framework, and Social Openness (GSLO)

The five indicators representing the GSLO construct are from the World Economic Forum’s Executive Opinion Survey and are explained in Table 3.

## Indicators for Socioeconomic Level (SOCL EV)

Four indicators represent the SOCL EV construct and are from World Development Indicators 2007 and the World Economic Forum’s Executive Opinion Survey. They are explained in Table 4.

The Log of Gross National Income per Capita (LGNIPC) indicator represents the natural log of gross national income per capita. There are two transformations: (1) calculation of per capita and (2) natural log. The second transformation was done for this indicator because of the wide range of values after the per capita calculation. Hence, it can more accurately measure differences across a wide range of developed and developing countries. Likewise, the indicator, old dependency ratio, has a wide range, so it has undergone a natural log transformation.

This research is based on structural equation modeling (SEM), an integrated modeling approach that utilizes factor-analytic methods to form factors based on observed variables [10, 23, 29]. SEM analyzes the group of observed variables and the relationships of their latent factors to estimate model fit. The SEM fit is primarily measured by minimizing the discrepancy between the covariance matrix of the sample and the population’s covariance matrix, which is implied by the model [10, 23]. In this study, confirmatory SEM is utilized, in which prior knowledge is applied to formulate a conceptual model of constructs (factors) and the structural model, that is, their hypothesized causal connections [6, 23, 29]. The latent factors must be defined operationally, that is, reflective indicator variables are assigned to the factors [6, 10, 29]. The measurement model describes relations between the observed indicator variables and latent factors. SEM computes the latent factors and fits the model to data for the observed variables by adjusting the specified relationships between the factors in order to estimate how closely the co-variances of the observed variables fit those of the theoretical model [5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 23]. For SEM, the present study utilizes Amos software [1].

SEM methodology is applied to test the five points of investigation that the level of IT in a country can be modeled by the latent factors Investment in Business and Technology (INVBT); Government Support of IT, Legal Framework, and Societal Openness (GSLO); Socioeconomic Level (SOCL EV); and Technology Utilization (TECUTIL). Each latent construct (factor) reflects three to five observed variables. The factors and variables are shown in Table 5. The overall model fit can be evaluated through goodness-of-fit statistics [23, 29].

The SEM of Technology Utilization is shown in Figure 2. One-way arrows between factors represent structural regression coefficients, that is, the influence of one factor on another [6, 10]. A two-way arrow represents co-variances between pairs of variables [6, 10]. The arrows from a latent factor to observed variables represent that those observed indicator variables are influenced by the underlying latent factor [6, 10]. An error term is associated with each observed variable but is not shown on the diagram in Figures 2.

Because Amos does not have a robust missing value feature, a limited number of variable values (less than 15) were imputed by substituting the average values for a group of similar countries in the sample. In the case of the huge FDI for lightly populated Luxembourg, its FDI per capita was replaced with the average per capita value based on a subsample of similar European countries. This outlier occurred because that country records FDI for many other major countries that have funds flowing through it.

The 16 variables were all measured during the period 2004–6. This assures crosssectional consistency for them.

Potential differences of developed and developing country subsamples are tested by applying the conceptual model to the developed and developing country subsamples. The purpose is to determine whether the SEM model is supported for the full sample and for the subsamples, which differ considerably economically, socially, and technologically. Prior studies of developing countries [3, 14, 44, 50], developed countries [35], and both types [39, 56] noted a number of differences by the major development categories, including greater importance of R&D as a correlate of technology in developed countries [39] and in their counties [2], and uncertainty avoidance as an inhibitor of technology use in developing countries [56]. The implications of differences in findings between developed and developing countries will inform policymakers in these two development categories on approaches that might yield greater success in encouraging technology use.

Prior studies have determined that there were different findings for developed and developing countries [28, 39, 42]. Regression comparisons have been accomplished by subsamples or by a dummy variable. In one study, education was not significant for a subsample of OECD members, but was for the full sample, two-thirds of which were developing countries [28]. Robison and Crenshaw [42] tested the developed/ developing country distinctions through regression using a dummy variable as well as subsamples, but did not find differences. Another study found that developed and developing country subsamples had significant differences [39]. A theoretical study conceptualized that developing countries would experience reduced technology diffusion due to lower technology literacy, lower prevalence of trained IS professionals, and greater need for a government support role [45]. Van Dijk [48] pointed to very large differences in average prevalence per capita of PCs and Internet users between high-income and low-income countries. He stressed that although there are many similarities in approach to technology development between the third world and other world regions, there are also major differences “that reflect the political, economic, social, and cultural distinctions between countries” [48, p. 191].

For these reasons, in the present study, in addition to the overall sample of 110 countries, the sample is evenly divided into two subsamples: (1) developed countries, consisting of 55 countries, defined as those with gross national income per capita (GNIPC) equal to or greater than \$3,400, and (2) developing countries, consisting of 55 countries with GNIPC of less than \$3,400. The dividing point of \$3,400 was determined by a 60:40 weighted average of the means for lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries in the data set of the World Bank for 2005 [55]. The objectives are to explore, using the present model, differences that have been reported in the literature, to identify model findings that are similar or different, and consequently, to better inform policymakers in developed and developing countries.

Composite reliability exceeds the commonly accepted threshold of 0.7 for factors in all the samples, and above 0.8 for all except GSLO for developing countries. The average variance extracted (AVE) is above the commonly accepted threshold of 0.5 for three-quarters of the factors. It falls under for GSLO and SOCL EV in the developing country subsample.

Discriminant validity outputs are not calculated by the Amos software. Instead, the identical data and factors were entered in the SmartPLS 2.0 software. A recommended discriminant validity test is to compare AVE values for any two factors with the squared correlation estimates between those two factors [23]. For each comparison, an AVE value higher than the squared correlation indicates discriminant validity. For the samples all countries, developed countries, and developing countries, the test was met for 83 percent of AVE values. This is a strong but not perfect discriminant validity. However, it suffices for an exploratory analysis.

SEM is used in this paper for exploration. The goal is not to test the model given, but to describe the model phenomena and their associations. Because this study is exploratory, the SEM analysis examines relationships and correlations but does not confirm causality.

Overall, the validity testing demonstrates convergent validity with internally consistent factors with highly correlated indicators and predominantly significant loadings of observed variables. The developing country subsample is weaker, especially for the GSLO factor. The discriminant validity test indicated generally strong validity between factors.

Descriptive statistics for the indicator variables comprising the factor TECUTIL are given in Table 6. Wide divergences in technological utilization and corresponding coefficients of variation are apparent between developed and developing countries, as well as differences in coefficients of variation. Although the means for Internet users per 100 population are nearly five times higher in developed versus developing countries, the mobile phone ratio is only 3.4 times higher, which represents “leapfrogging” that has occurred for this less-expensive technology in many less developed countries. The ratio of PCs per 100 is six times higher, which reflects higher cost relative to living standards in developing countries. By contrast, mean values for secure Internet servers per million population are 55 times higher in developed countries. There are greater economic, technological, and training barriers for poorer countries to implement such servers. The difference in ranges between developed and developing countries reflects a balanced factor that is sensitive to technology utilization differentials across a wide spectrum of national environments. It is not surprising that developing countries reveal more variation than developed countries because they are earlier in the adoptiondiffusion stages of technology growth.

•	 Point of investigation 1: Do countries that have higher levels of business and technology investment have higher levels of government support, legal framework, and social openness?

Covariance of GSLO and INVBT is supported for all the samples.

An explanation of the co-variance between INVBT and GSLO is that a country that has a strong and fair legal framework, openness, clear property rights, and governmental interest in furthering ICT is also likely to have healthy internal and foreign investment, R&D, and government–university research collaboration, and vice versa. For an economically disadvantaged, struggling country, progress on GSLO can be difficult. The importance of these openness factors in fostering technology investment and growth was supported for developing countries [3]. Another study [22] found that democratic regimes were associated with growth of Internet technologies and usage.

•	 Point of investigation 2: Do countries that have high levels of government support, legal framework, and social openness have higher socioeconomic levels?

The correlation of GSLO with SOCL EV is supported for all samples except developing countries. This corresponds to findings in prior studies [3, 16, 44].

•	 Point of investigation 3: Do countries that have higher socioeconomic level have higher technology utilization?

The correlation of SOCL EV with TECUTIL is supported for all samples.

The strong impact of socioeconomic factors on technology utilization has been confirmed in many studies at the country level [13, 14, 35, 42], although it is not found in others [3, 41]. However, none of the cited studies utilized SEM methodology, which can be employed to test more complex conceptual models.

•	 Point of investigation 4: Do countries that make larger business and technology investment have higher technology utilization?

The correlation of INVBT with TECUTIL is not supported for the all-countries and developed country samples. It is supported for the developing country sample.

For developed countries, the present broader theoretical model includes the factors of GSLO co-varying with INVBT and SOCL EV. This two-factor indirect path is demonstrated with significant empirical results that may weaken the direct INVBT to TECUTIL link. The path is supported for developing countries in which the two-factor path is not significant. The direct link is supported by several prior qualitative studies showing that scientific research and creative activities are associated with technology adoption and use in developed countries [19, 41].

•	 Point of investigation 5: Do the country data conform to or differ from the overall conceptual model, which is described by the conjectures?

The empirical data fit the conceptual model for the all-countries sample and developed country samples. There is partial support on the data fit for the developing country sample.

A complex multifactor, multipath theoretical digital divide model at the country level has not previously been empirically tested. Several qualitative studies have used examples or cases to illustrate a more complex theoretical model. The reduced goodness of fit for developing countries can be mostly ascribed to lack of significance in the GSLO–SOCL EV path. For developing countries, the GSLO factor has high variation. As seen by GSLO’s Cronbach’s alpha in Table 5, GSLO’s indicator variables are less correlated with each other. This might be due to a greater amount of change in the legal, democratic, and government policy areas in developing countries. For instance, a developing country might form a democratic regime that has property rights and the rule of law only to be soon overthrown by a dictatorial regime that limits these rights and diminishes the role of the judiciary. This type of inconsistency in GSLO would tend to weaken its relationship with SOCL EV.

## Two Illustrative Case Examples

The findings of our study provide a model of factors that together influence technology utilization in countries. This part of the discussion section briefly examines case examples of two case countries, based on literature and government sources, in light of the present model. Each country example includes a recap of the history of ICT development, the role of the government and private sectors, and the socioeconomic level. The county’s experience is related to the present findings. The countries are Singapore, a wealthy country, and Mexico, which is mid-range. On technology utilization, Singapore’s 2005 Internet utilization rate was 57.9, whereas Mexico’s was 17.4 Internet users per 100 population.

Singapore, a country with the world’s highest economic growth rate from its founding in 1965 through the 1990s, commenced strong government support for ICT development in the early 1980s [54]. ICT was a key ingredient in Singapore’s achieving status as a regional center in Southeast Asia for manufacturing, transportation, and multinational corporations. The government invested heavily in IT and Internet infrastructure, which has transformed it to world leadership. Because the federal government is centralized and authoritarian, national IT has been well planned and managed. IT training permeates the public education system, and free government training is available for working adults [51, 54]. A government initiative, “Singapore, Intelligent Nation, a Global City,” seeks by 2015 to foster innovation, integration of entities in the country, and internationalization to become more a part of the global economy [24].

The other side of Singapore’s governmental authoritarianism is constraint on the media, attempt to control Internet access, and restriction of political freedoms [52]. Although in many respects the country’s education system is a world leader, strong control of education over many years has tended to reduce the creativity and entrepreneurial initiative of its citizens. Consequently, the country has been low on start-ups, venture capital formation, and technology innovation [52].

This case exemplifies the research model as demonstrated by the SEM testing. There is a high level of INVBT that interacts with elevated GSLO, which is strongly associated with high SOCL EV, which in turn is strongly associated with elevated TECUTIL. Singapore’s government did not even attempt the direct route of investing to try for immediate payoff in technology utilization. Instead, it staged the process, as our model results reflect. This optimistic example must, however, be tempered because, for Singapore, the GSLO component of openness is reduced and the INVBT creative components of venture capital and scientific research are relatively low. However, overall, Singapore constitutes a success example.

Mexico’s ICT growth has been stimulated more by its private sector. In particular, Mexico has had substantial electronics and computer manufacturing for export since the early 1990s [48]. However, the federal government in the 1990s placed slight emphasis on IT [15]. Consequently, there has been little internal development of domestic hardware or software companies. Even though it is close to the United States, the Mexican IT outsourcing industry is tiny. On the plus side, economic liberalization in the 1990s allowed more of Mexico’s middle-class citizenry to afford and have access to computing and the Internet. The regulatory framework for telecommunications and e-commerce has gradually become less restrictive [38, 50]. There are, however, some cultural constraints even today on using credit on the Internet.

The E-Mexico National System during the administration of Vicente Fox, from 2000 to 2006, sought to provide Internet access to citizens throughout Mexico’s municipios (i.e., counties), including poor ones. This bold program met with mixed success, and faltered during the succeeding administration of Felipe Calderon, as the leadership was diverted with crime and other more pressing problems.

Relative to the structural equation model, Mexico is a country that cannot yet succeed in the multistep pathway emphasized in our model testing. In particular, Mexico has only moderate GSLO, as its legal institutions are weak. Its property rights (PROPRGHT) are below the developed countries subsample mean. Its INVBT is low, since Mexico has deficits in FDI per capita, venture capital, scientific research, and university–industry collaboration. Its technological readiness is low, partly because the government has not given strong support to training and resources [15]. The four indicators range between 14 and 82 percent below the developing country subsample means. What if Mexico could increase both these four weak INVBT underlying variables and the legal underlying dimension? In that case, INVBT and GSLO would have a more positive co-variance. The substantially higher GSLO would correlate positively with SOCL EV, and in turn would be positively associated with TECUTIL. In model terms, the E-Mexico National System can be regarded as an attempt by Mexico’s federal government to short-circuit with a direct INVBT to TECUTIL path. However, this path failed eventually. Based on the what-if analysis, E-Mexico would have been more sustainable if the foundation steps had been put in place, represented by GSLO and INVBT, that is, based on stronger laws especially for property, greater foreign and venture capital investment, technology readiness, scientific research, and industry–academic collaboration.

## Implications for Research

The concep tual theor y is mostly emp ir ically sup or ted. The strength of factor relationships vary between developed and developing countries. The co-variance of INVBT with GSLO is fully supported. It makes sense that freedom and legal rights stimulate business and technology investment. Countries with the greatest INVBT include the United States, the United Kingdom, Scandinavian countries, and Japan. Businesses and technology ventures have benefited from the openness and laws. In the opposite direction, the presence of large technology and business investments stimulates the need for property rights and stimulates governments to give higher priority to IT. The two factors can be in a positive reinforcing loop, or in different circumstances, in a downward reinforcing spiral.

The GSLO–SOCL EV–TECUTIL path received strong support for the all-countries and developed country samples. It demonstrates for them that the indirect and more complex path is more effective than the direct INVBT–TECUTIL path. For developing countries, however, the INVBT–TECUTIL path dominates. It is supported in several non-SEM studies of developing countries [3, 21, 42].

The major differences from hypothesized outcomes are (1) that the indirect path does not hold for all samples, and (2) that the direct path does not hold for all samples. Instead, the results show that one or the other is present: for the all-countries and developed country samples, the indirect path is dominant, whereas for the developing country sample, only the direct path dominates. This unexpected difference might have occurred because, depending on level of development, government policies and business and government practices are set to favor one or the other path, but not both.

## Limitations of the Study

This exp lor ator y study calls for futur e br oader investigation to address the links between technological change and socioeconomic factors, available resources, the role of government, multicultural content, educational effectiveness and motivation, corporate social responsibility, leadership, and the building of infrastructure and communities. Because an expanded conceptual theory would have more factors and more observable indicator variables, it calls for a larger sample size of countries, as many as 140 or more, which is likely to become feasible as international data agencies such as the World Bank and the International Telecommunications Union expand their coverage.

Studies are needed to develop causal theories and to statistically test and confirm those theories based on hypotheses regarding the causality and directionality of the associations and paths discovered in the present study and other studies. Theories developed need to be applicable to both advanced and developing countries, which have had less prior conceptual study. We discovered in this study that findings were weaker for developing countries. Future theoretical studies could benefit by building on existing modernization theories of development, such as that of Sen [43], and modifying them to include technological factors.

One limitation of the present research is that some inconsistencies and inaccuracies in the data, especially for developing countries, reduce sample size. The present sample sizes are small for SEM analysis, although the goodness-of-fit tests remain largely significant. Future studies with larger sample sizes would foster greater stability in SEM testing.

Another limitation is the lack of understanding of the complexity of patterns of interactions for modeling global technological utilization. This calls for in-depth study using a variety of methods to better understand the detailed human and organizational processes leading to decisions of government programs, businesses, educational institutions, legal and regulatory agencies, and technology enterprises in advancing the use of technology.

Because the present research is cross-sectional, it prevents inclusion of timedependent processes, such as educational and economic trends. Future research is called for to formulate longitudinal theory, gather systematic data at multiple time points, and conduct analyses.

## Implications for Practice

This study’s indir ect two-step p athway has the important policy implication that a national government could improve technology utilization by encouraging openness, democracy, freedom of the press, and quality of the legal system, as well as prioritizing ICT as a goal [3, 19]. These policy steps are supported by prior findings on openness and advances in the legal system [17] and on the positive effect of governmental prioritization of IT [39]. Mid-level national governments could take proactive steps to stimulate a social and legal environment conducive for enhanced ICT [17, 41].

In developed countries, government strategic planners and leaders should consider raising the priority of IT, protecting property rights, and adding freedom of the press and openness, which in turn help to stimulate educational advances, greater labor force participation (including by women), and higher incomes, all of which contribute to enhanced technology utilization. Such policies have been recommended in prior conceptual and empirical studies [18, 39, 48].

For governments in developing countries, the research suggests longer-term, anticipatory steps to stimulate government prioritization of ICT, openness of society, democracy, and a stable legal structure. A combination of such steps was recommended for digital technology advances for African developing countries [36]. There are perhaps higher-level reasons to take these steps, such as to better the human condition, but technology is a beneficiary. This research points to a pathway through the intermediate factor of improving social and economic conditions. China in recent years exemplifies this pathway, although it is clearly an uncompleted process there. We can point to the loosening of historical tight autocratic control and the cracking open of some freedoms as a stimulus to economic growth, with technological advancement as a beneficiary. Government leaders in both developed and developing countries need to consider that investment alone in business and technology will not result in high national levels of technology utilization. Rather, long-term and strong policies that improve educational openness, quality of instruction, number of women in the labor force, per capita income, and a higher proportion of the population working will enable society to succeed with technology utilization.

## Conclusion

This p ap er analyzes the r elationship s of government support and openness, business and technology investment, and socioeconomic factors on technological usage for a large sample of countries. Results show a strong pathway of associations from the government framework to socioeconomic level and in turn to technological utilization for samples of all countries and developed countries. The direct pathway of association of business and technology investment with technology utilization is consistently weak. While the findings reinforce prior literature studies, SEM analysis enlarges the model scope and findings. SEM has rarely been applied to the study of the “global digital divide,” largely because large country samples have been unavailable until recently.

This exploratory study’s contributions are a new conceptual model to understand the determinants of technology utilization for countries. It consists of four constructs and an exploratory empirical structural equation model to test the model with a sample of data from 110 countries. The research identifies key significant correlations between factors, namely, for developed countries a two-step indirect path that is correlated with technology utilization, and for developing countries, a direct path in which business and technological investment and R&D are directly associated with technology utilization. The contributions can form the basis for further conceptualization, confirmatory testing, and elaboration.

The study also implies that the developed countries benefit by government support, quality legal framework, and societal openness, leading to betterment in socioeconomic level and to technology utilization. Economically advanced countries can improve their technology utilization through greater policy prioritization of ICT, improved privacy and intellectual property laws, efficient and open business procedures, and greater societal openness. Examples of countries that exemplify and have realized these improvements are Australia, Ireland, the United Kingdom, and Scandinavian countries. Although their governance and socioeconomic levels are already high, further advances can occur through the pathway delineated. At the least, this study implies that business and technology investments need to be coupled and coordinated with governmental priorities and societal initiatives in order to advance a country’s digital utilization level. Digital divides exist in both developing and developed countries and can be reduced by a similar staged approach, although the starting points are quite different. The study findings indicate that governments seeking societal use of technology should foster investment, financial markets, and R&D; open up the society and grow the rule of law; give policy priority to building IT utilization; and foster education, training, and participation in technology training by all societal groups.
