## Abstract

The Bank of America literally changed the banking industry during the 1950s by means of its ERMA and IBM 702 computer systems. These innovations in information technology resulted in a dominate design that helped keep the Bank of America in the lead for over a decade and a half. They were the collective work of a leader, Clark Beise, a maestro, AI

Zipf, and a group of supertechs, all of whom became the prototypes for these crucial roles. Bank of America was the first organization, among a selected few, to successfully negotiate the innovation cascade leading from crisis to a dominant IT design. Due in large part to IBM's failure to deliver a fully operational operating system for its 360/65, however, coinciding with the leadership's attention toward international markets, in the late 1960s the Bank of America lost its lead. After several decades "in the trough," as a result of aggressive investment and leadership, the bank re-emerged as a strong competitor. This story of achieving alignment in strategy and structure by means of technological innovation, of the almost tragic breaking of that alignment, and of fervent efforts made to gain realignment illuminates some of the most important lessons of IT management that can be learned from the field's relatively recent, but dramatic, history.

## The "Little Fellows" Bank

A. P. Giannini founded a bank to serve "the little fellow," many of whom had never used a bank before. The Bank of Italy, soon to become the Bank of America, opened its offices in 1904 to accommodate the needs of small farmers, small businessmen, and average working people in the expanding Northern California economy centered around San Francisco. Giannini's bank obviously satisfied an unmet need and it prospered, well beyond his expectations. A single branch, opened in 1909, grew to 24 branches by the end of 1918 and then to 292 branches by the bank's twenty-fifth anniversary in 1929, at which point Bank of America employed more than 7,000 people and had more than \$1 billion in assets. L. M. (Mario) Giannini, his son, who succeeded A. P. as president, saw the bank reach 495 branches and \$2.1 billion in assets by the end of 1941. During the second World War, California's population mushroomed as did its economy, boosting the bank's resources to more than \$5 billion. Following the war, the Bank opened nine new offices overseas. Having enjoyed over 35 years of continuous growth, at the end of October, 1945, Senior Vice President Francis Baer was proud to report that the bank's assets had reached \$5,0376,500,000, surpassing Chase National of New York's \$4,965,394,000, making Bank of America the largest bank in the world.

A. P. Giannini was, arguably, the greatest innovator in modern banking. Only J. P. Morgan, the elder, is a likely contender. Giannini created the branch banking system and ushered in the era of retail banking, all with an emphasis on the "human touch." He brought, as one observer put it, "pushcart marketing principles" to banking. When the "Giant of the West" died on June 3, 1949, he left a flourishing institution, an enduring legacy of banking strategy, and, as it turned out, a considerable strategic problem to be solved down the line by his hand picked successor, S. Clark Beise. $^{1}$

## A Crisis of Control

Post war economic growth proved to be a dual-edged sword. The increased revenues were, of course, desirable but the costs associated with booking them increasingly became a considerable burden. By the early 1950s, the banking industry hovered on the brink of a disaster. Check volume in the United States had doubled from four billion checks per year in 1943 to eight billion checks in 1952. Furthermore, bankers projected a continuing increased of one billion checks per year until 1955. Awash in paper, banks were almost paralyzed, powerless to expand, and, in some cases, unable even to keep pace with the increasing volumes of transactions they had to process. The Bank of America was especially hard hit, given its strategic commitment to retail banking and its attendant large numbers of accounts with high levels of daily activity.

Major bottlenecks occurred in the check clearing process and in backoffice bookkeeping for checking and savings accounts. Each of the 28 million checks that entered the banking system every business day passed through an average of two and one third banks, taking more than two days to be processed. In some cases, it might take weeks before a check found its way to the drawer's bank and was finally cleared. The result was a staggering 69 million checks in process throughout the United States on an average day and an enormous amount of financial float in the form of uncollected funds. Unless deposited at the bank upon which it was drawn, a check had to be sorted by hand and individually tallied on an adding or proofing machine at least six times during the clearing process.

Beise, who had come to Bank of America at A. P.'s behest, had risen to senior vice president by 1950 and became chairman of the powerful management committee which A. P. had set up in 1944. (He was later president from 1954 to 1964, after Mario Giannini died from a life-long fight with hemophilia.) He was acutely aware of the serious problems that faced the nation's banks in general, and the Bank of America in particular. Bank of America was managing more than 4.6 million checking, savings, and Timeplan loan accounts, with checking accounts growing at a rate of 23,000 per month. Realizing that growth would be limited, not by the bank's ability to generate new business but by its inability to adequately service its accounts, Beise began the search for a solution to automate check handling and demand deposit bookkeeping.

## Search for a Technological Solution: 1950–1953

## The technical nature of the problem

The crisis Bank of America faced in the early 1950s is best understood in terms of the core technological system typically employed by the industry at the time. It was a system that evolved when banks were small, many people did not have bank accounts, and those who did have accounts did not write many checks against them. Banks received checks and deposits from two sources: over the teller's counter and through cash letters from other institutions. Upon their receipt, each collection of the debits and credits were balanced in an operation know as "proof." The items were next sorted into two general categories: "transit" items drawn on other banks that must be sent out for collection and "on us" items drawn on the receiving bank.

The transit items were sorted into piles according to the "end points" to which they would be sent for collection. Large banks would exchange items with several hundred other institutions including the Federal Reserve system, local clearing houses, and other banks. Even small banks might send items to 10 or more end points. The typical bank used one or more multiple pocket "proof and transit" electromechanical machines to complete the task. These machines, which were manufactured by NCR, IBM, and Burroughs, had from two to 32 pockets for sorting items. Each pocket was, in effect, tied to an adding machine that kept a running total and produced an itemized tape which became backup for the transit cash letter that was sent out with the items. Due to the machines' limited sorting capacity, most banks had to "pass" the items several times in order to exhaust all of the end point possibilities. An essential skill for becoming an effective operations officer, consequently, was being creative in developing efficient sorting patterns. Item processing productivity varied widely among banks but most proof and transit machine operators seldom exceeded sorting more than 9,000 items per operator per shift. $^{2}$ To finish proofing early enough to catch stop payments or overdrafts and meet transportation schedules, most banks were forced to shut their doors to business by 2:00 p.m. $^{3}$

"On us" checks were sorted into alphabetic categories called "books" and sent to the bank's bookkeeping department for posting to the depositors' accounts. A typical book consisted of up to 1,000 accounts, arranged in alphabetical order. During an average working day, an operator posted about 1,200 checks and deposits to the accounts in the book. The process was tedious. Each bookkeeping operator took the items routed to her book—they were almost invariably women—and first sorted them alphabetically by name. Frequently, a multiple leather strap device was used to facilitate the sorting. A tub file of account ledger cards, filed in alphabetical order—the "book"—was placed adjacent to an electromechanical bookkeeping machine. The operator processed the stack of items by (1) looking at the name on the item at the top of the stack, (2) thumbing through the tub file to locate the matching ledger card, (3) verifying the signature, (4) positioning the ledger card in the throat of the machine, carefully aligning it to the next line for printing, (5) reading the previous account balance from the ledger card and keying it into the machine (called the "old balance pickup"), (6) reading the amounts of the debit and credit items for the account and keying them into the machine, (7) turning over the processed items, (8) after all of the items for an account were entered, depressing the total key to print the new, updated balance on the ledger card, (9) pulling the ledger card out of the machine and returning it to the tub file, and (10) looking at the next item in the stack and repeating the process. In many banks, as a check on accuracy, the operator also picked up the old balance a second time so that a "zero balance" comparison could be made. At the end of the day, the operator balanced the work and filed the items away to be sent to customers along with a statement at the end of the month.

As volumes grew, banks responded, for the most part, by adding additional proof and transit machines and bookkeeping machines and hiring and training additional operators. Beise realized that, with the enormous volumes the Bank of America anticipated receiving as a result of the burgeoning post war economy, this technological system would eventually break down. It required too many people, too much space, and too much capital investment and it was slow and inefficient. It would materially constrain the bank's ability to grow.

## A leader responds

Beise approached several business equipment manufacturers about creating an automated bookkeeping system. Although they were willing to improve their basic banking machines, no manufacturer expressed interest in investing the time or capital necessary to create an entirely new specialized banking system. He remained frustrated until early in 1950, when a chance event opened up new possibilities.

After a promotional meeting at the Emporium department store, near Bank of America headquarters, ended earlier than expected, Dr. W. B. "Hoot" Gibson, of the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) of Menlo Park, decided to call on Beise. Located in Menlo Park, SRI was an independent technology research institute probing the leading edge of electronics and was looking for opportunities to do automation research. "I told Beise that this was just a 'shot in the dark'," Gibson later recalled, "but that I thought Bank of America should be thinking about electronic applications."4

Given a history of unrequited proposals, Beise's interest was piqued and he acted quickly on Gibson's overture. He assigned Vice President of Operations Frank M. Dana to follow up and to develop a proposal for studying the possibilities. Meanwhile, he contacted SRI's director of engineering research, Thomas H. Morrin, and immediately commenced a series of joint meetings to explore the automation of check processing, account numbering, and paper handling. SRI seemed to Beise to be the perfect partner. As a prominent research and development organization with a strong track record in electronics research, he believed that SRI could develop a state-of-the-art approach to automated check handling. The resulting design could then be sold to a manufacturer. Beise soon formed a close working relationship with Morrin, vice president of engineering and research and development at SRI. Working under a charter from Mario Giannini to solve their banking crisis, he signed a contract with SRI, with the understanding that no publicity would be given to the project. Morrin, in effect, became his research and development manager, perhaps one of the first such relationships in banking history. Subsequently, a highly secret feasibility study was initiated. To carry out this important assignment, Beise assigned a highly respected and seasoned bank officer named Charles Conroy as the lead systems analyst to work with the SRI team.

## SRI undertakes systems research

In late September 1950, Morrin informed Beise that SRI's feasibility study "indicated it was technically possible to build an automatic bookkeeping system for ledger posting and processing of commercial checking accounts" and suggested a three-phase project approach entailing (1) a study of banking procedures external to the machine, (2) logical design, and (3) development, construction, and testing. Because manufacturing ran counter to SRI's mission statement, the last phase was to be carried out by an equipment manufacturer. Beise approved the project using CEO discretionary funds and, in the process, made the first investment toward electronic banking. The \$15,000 contract (later augmented by \$5,000) for SRI's part in the project referred to the system as an electronic recording machine (E.R.M.).

Although the Bank of America was interested in automating proof and transit as well as bookkeeping activities, an April 30, 1951, interim report described the machine as fundamentally a bookkeeping device. Completion of the first two phases of the project found the bank and SRI in agreement that the system would need to perform five basic bookkeeping functions: credit and debit all accounts; maintain a record of all transactions; retain a record of current customer balances to be printed as needed; respond to stop-payment and hold orders on checks; and notify operators of checks that caused accounts to be overdrawn.

SRI subsequently began what was supposed to be its final project for the bank: preparing an estimate of the optimal account capacity, at the time, and of the cost of constructing the E.R.M. An initial rough estimate of \$750,000, with an additional \$15,000 for development of a check reader, was arrived at by comparing estimated construction costs incurred by other companies for such large-scale computer projects as the Mark III, UNIVAC, EDVAC, and Whirlwind I. When E.R.M. development was broken down into all of the specific tasks involved, the final estimate reached \$949,000, a figure deemed high by Morrin, who then suggested making estimates for minimum and complete systems. The readjusted figures were \$530,000 and \$830,000, respectively.

Among the manufacturers, only Burroughs expressed any interest in building an E.R.M. for the bank. Its proposal, however, would deliver less functionality at twice SRI's cost estimate and was rejected. Consequently, Beise asked SRI to construct an engineering prototype of the E.R.M., a decision motivated as much by his concern to maintain secrecy and confidentiality as by his need to move ahead. This project required building one of the largest and most complex computer systems yet designed and, although SRI had no manufacturing experience, Morrin, on January 28, 1952, contracted with the institute to develop, construct, and test a pilot version of E.R.M. Beise, realizing SRI's lack of major manufacturing capability, still planned to sell the system design to a manufacturing company once the research firm had demonstrated a working prototype.

The final phase of SRI's work on the E.R.M. was to involve four phases: completing the logical design of remaining operations; constructing a pilot model; testing the model at one of Bank of America's branches; and finishing and installing the machine at a local branch. The bank was to pay SRI no more than \$850,000 over four years, plus an additional \$25,000 to cover subcontracts (although expenses were never released, most engineers estimate that the final cost was in excess of \$5 million).

## Redesigning the check: a key innovation

From the beginning, the "check" and its flow throughout the banking system was the primary focus of SRI's work. For the Bank of America, a check was more than a piece of paper authorizing a transfer of funds. It embodied an important emotional link with its customers. Consequently, the bank wanted few, if any, changes made to its check. One of the first crucial choices to be made was between paying homage to well entrenched customer habits in check writing versus satisfying the systems needs with respect to the account filing requirements. Ultimately, the systems needs won out. Standard banking practice, at the time, was to organize customer files alphabetically and to identify and verify checks against customers' signatures. This became a costly procedure when the volumes grew large. In addition to the cumbersome sorting tasks involved, the alphabetic filing and the signature identification processes also required changing the structure of the books every time a new account was added. In order to avoid these problems, SRI engineers suggested that a numerical accounting system—assigning a unique number to every account—would make check processing easier to automate and provide a more reliable means of identifying checks. Conroy worked with them to develop a revised paper workflow and he estimated that the potential savings were sizable. The proposed change would entail distributing new checkbooks with customer name and account numbers printed on checks, complete with a leaflet warning customers against loaning blank checks to friends, a common practice at the time.

In 1952, SRI proposed a system for encoding electronically readable customer, bank, and account information on checks to support automatic proofing. A magnetic-ink bar printed on the backs of checks was selected as the best solution over approaches that employed punched cards. Encoding project manager Kenneth Eldredge developed a system to form the magnetic ink into Arabic characters that could be read by humans as well as computers. This magnetic ink character recognition (MICR) system—eventually designated Type Font E-13B—was successfully developed and tested in the summer of 1954 on Bank of America's travelers' check program and proved to be a superb showcase for advances in machine reading and paper handling. By June 1955, more than 300,000 travelers' checks had been scanned and processed through the system.

Once SRI had the mandate to develop the system, Morrin and colleagues Jerre Noe and Oliver Whitby ("supertechs" who were referred to by the few bank employees who knew about the project as the "whiz kids") began working as technical advisors on the creation of a prototype. Conroy moved his office to Palo Alto so that he could supervise the project and work more closely with the team. Monthly review meetings were held with Beise at bank headquarters. Ranaulf Beames, Bank of America's most talented operations officer at the time, was appointed vice president in charge of ERMA. Almost immediately he created what some believe to be the first complete flow charts of major banking processes. Beames provided remarkable systems thinking leadership during the early days of the project. Design and operation of the E.R.M. were fairly well-defined by the fall of 1952. The primary system was to consist of four centrally located 30,000-account capacity E.R.M.s linked by automotive (in some cases, flying) messenger services to approximately 12 branches. Each E.R.M. was to be run by 10 to 12 operators and handle both bookkeeping and proofing functions. In the spring of 1953, Beames unfortunately died and Howard Lief became controller and assumed responsibility for the ERMA project.

SRI launched a wide-ranging technology search for components, visiting companies in the United States and Europe to observe and assess willingness to develop products for the E.R.M., making it an early outsourcer. A major design choice was to develop electronic logic using tubes and wired programs, leaving open the possibility of transistorizing the final E.R.M.

## Finding the maestro and creating an IT department

On December 23, 1950, just as SRI was beginning work on phase one of the E.R.M. design, A. R. Zipf, the operations manager in the San Fernando, California, branch, received a congratulatory letter from Bank of America president Mario Giannini marking his 15 years of service. Zipf, a high-school graduate who had joined the bank as a night clerk and worked his way up to assistant branch supervisor—along the way securing four patents for inventions and improvements in banking machinery and systems—responded with a detailed letter encouraging the president to automate bank activities. "If we are willing to take an active part in the engineering development of new machines," he wrote, "I am confident that it is possible and economically practical to accomplish the virtually automatic performance of such tasks as the sorting, listing, and commercial ledger posting of near-standard paper checks." Referring to the bank's objectives for the year, he closed the letter with "my best wishes for Seven Million Dollars, less staff, and more machines."⁶ Giannini gave the letter to Beise, who promptly invited Zipf to San Francisco for an interview. Impressed by Zipf's intellect, energy and astute recommendations, Beise asked him to join the innovation team, at first on a confidential basis, and to start exploring alternatives to the SRI approach. He also arranged for Zipf to receive Dana's copies of SRI's reports and minutes of pertinent meetings.

With the encouragement of both Beise and Dana, Zipf set out on his own quest to evaluate the applicability of current technology to banking. During the spring of 1952, he went on leave to UCLA, where he came under the tutelage of pioneering computer designer George W. Brown, who added breath to his ideas and saw to it that he was exposed to a wide range of equipment. Zipf visited computer-using manufacturers and firms to observe management and service problems first hand and, in the process, evaluated the banking potential of more than a dozen suppliers' systems. In a letter to Dana, he observed that there "were enormous opportunities in electronics" and suggested that the bank form a systems and equipment research group. In late 1952, and over Morrin's strong protestations that the E.R.M. could be expanded to support all of the bank's data processing needs, Zipf concluded that it was important for the bank to gain experience beyond the SRI project and persuaded Beise and Dana to agree with him. As a result, another computer acquisition program, one directed at improving bank operations in areas other than the automation of check handling, was initiated with Zipf in the lead.

Zipf's primary target was the bank's equally burdensome lending and credit businesses. To help pull California out of the depression in 1932, A. P. had started a "Back to Good Times" campaign issuing bonds to help finance the Golden Gate Bridge, build schools, highways, dams, and other public projects. He followed this up in 1936 by introducing the "Timeplan" program, which offered short term loans for household appliances and automobiles. Within four months of the introduction of Timeplan, Bank of America became the third largest automobile financing agency in the state. The bank's loan portfolio was organized into three categories: commercial loans to industries, Timeplan loans to individual consumers, and a large real estate loan portfolio, consisting mostly of loans on family dwellings financed under the Federal Housing Act of the New Deal days and the Servicemen's Readjustment Act (G.I. Bill of Rights) after World War II. Bank of America also managed trust funds. These services were also growing at a staggering rates. Between 1947 and 1957, for example, Bank of America's dollar volume on all types of loans more than doubled. Trust activities tripled. Meanwhile operating expenses soared from $47\%$ of total earnings to $63\%$ . While the problem of processing checking and saving account transactions was more severe,

Zipf concluded that the lending and money management businesses, too, needed help.

Zipf returned to Bank of America in the summer of 1953 and, in October, was promoted to assistant vice president with responsibility for managing the newly created Systems and Equipment Research (S&ER) department. He opened his office with only one other employee, a secretary, but with an imposing mandate: to establish internal systems capability, evaluate suppliers, and pursue potential economic applications of general-purpose computers. Zipf, on the basis of an analysis of two general-purpose computers, the UNIVAC I and IBM 702, decided that because IBM could deliver better support the bank should begin with a 702. Beise authorized a tentative order in October, 1953, for delivery of one IBM 702 to San Francisco in September, 1955, and for a second one to be delivered to Los Angeles in June, 1956, the order being contingent on Zipf's providing the management committee by the fall of 1954 with a detailed analysis of the project's costs and benefits and profit objectives. $^{7}$ The implementation effort was planned to parallel the E.R.M. project.

## Testing Initial Solutions: 1954–1956

## Building IT competence

As he assumed the maestro role, Zipf knew that he had to build his organization's ability to solve the unique problems created by the newly developing IT field. He was faced with three immediate challenges: deciding which tasks to automate, designing and developing programs to automate these tasks, and selecting and training a team capable of designing, operating, maintaining, and supervising the new technological program. In response, he immediately set about recruiting a group of knowledgeable systems analysts and programmers. An internal personnel announcement of an opportunity for training in computer programming drew 34 responses from experienced bank officers. After careful screening, four senior managers were selected to attend (with Zipf) IBM's 702 programming school in Poughkeepsie, New York. There Zipf met and hired the bank's first data processing "professional," Harry Kahramanian, a graduate of Grace Hopper's UNIVAC program. Hopper, a computer pioneer, had developed the first production control system for a computer and the elements of a systematic approach to programming computers. Kahramanian had gained considerable IT understanding under Hopper's tutelage and quickly became the bank's first "supertech." When the bankers returned from programming school, two teams were organized—one in Los Angeles and one in San Francisco—to analyze existing operations and determine where computer automation would be most profitable.

Zipf believed the computer's greatest potential was for improved management reporting but his years working in the branch system also persuaded him that, within the Bank of America, the cost of large-scale computing could not be justified on that basis alone. So, while keeping management reporting as his ultimate objective, Zipf and his team of newly trained programmers began to search for areas of the bank's credit operations that were characterized by high volume, repetitive, clerical activities. They found them in the Timeplan and real estate accounts. A group in San Francisco was assigned responsibility to analyze installment loans, one in Los Angeles to look at real estate and other loan programs. By November 1954, programs for real estate and installment loan accounting were sufficiently well defined to conduct an evaluation of the functionality and economics of the IBM 702. The study showed that there would be a loss during the first year due to conversion costs, but that a stream of profits would emerge beginning in the second year and leveling off at \$189,364 in the third year as a result of staff reductions. Beise, Dana, and Leif reviewed the analyses with the management committee and, in early December 1954, Zipf was authorized to confirm the delivery of the first IBM 702 in San Francisco. The first 702 programs were completed by the two groups working together during the next few months and tested at IBM's facility at Poughkeepsie.

Subroutines and revisions in basic programs were completed and tested the following April. Each group then proceeded with full-time IBM 702 programming of the system it had analyzed. With this, the Bank of America had become the first bank to automate its credit operations.

By June 1955, and even before the first computer was installed, S&ER had grown from two people to 25. The two major projects it was responsible for, applications for the SRI-E.R.M. and IBM 702, were at the forefront of computer technology. In the fall of 1955, the first operational large-scale computer system for check processing—the E.R.M.—was scheduled to begin servicing the San Jose branch while, at almost the same time, the 702 was to be delivered to San Francisco. Zipf, having learned about the anxieties that the introduction of a computer could generate during his trips to other companies, prepared and launched a training program in the spring of 1955 designed to improve bank employees' understanding of computer systems. Its not so secret other mission was to mitigate their natural apprehensions by emphasizing the joint man-machine nature of data processing. The message promulgated to bank employees was that the IBM 702 (nicknamed the "BEAST" for Bankamerica Electronic Accounting Service Tool) was to take over all of the dull work but it would also rely on them to provide the strong personnel support that the technology needed.

## On the national scene: establishing standards

Meanwhile, it was apparent that a set of national standards must be implemented if the entire banking system was to take advantage of automation. Setting standards is one way to reduce complexity and to facilitate automated solutions and the lack of standards in checks and the way they displayed information was proving to be a major barrier to innovation. The banking community was alarmed by the explosive growth of float that was mounting due to the cumbersome check handling and sorting processes still in use. This led Harold A.

Randall, CEO of The First National Bank of Boston, as chairman of the American Bankers Association (ABA), to appoint a committee on the mechanization of check handling in 1954. In early 1955, Randall also appointed a technical subcommittee of operating managers who were the leaders in using electronic processes in their banks. At the time, 11 banks, ranging from the suburban bank in White Plains, New York, to the Mellon Bank of Pittsburgh, were actively pursuing the use of computers. $^{8}$ Most were automating loan processing and two were testing punched card checks.

The committee was chaired by John Kley, leader of the White Plains' effort who selected his committee members from among the leading IT managers at innovative banks. $^{9}$ Zipf, of course, was one of the first to be invited and, thereby, gained access to the leading users of IT in the country, while at the same time increasing his exposure to the manufacturers' new solutions as they emerged. The committee met regularly, logging 22 sessions over a three-year period, to define a standard that would allow automatic sorting and handling of checks. At these meetings, they heard proposals for establishing a standard from all of the major manufacturers including IBM, NCR, Burroughs, and Addressograph-Multigraph, each of which was lobbying to get its particular character recognition method adopted, thereby giving it a competitive edge. Of major concern was the MICR character reading technique. Invented specifically for this purpose by SRI, each character printed in magnetic ink could be recognized by analysis of its unique variation in voltage which was induced by the winding of a single magnetic read head on a sorter-reader machine as it passed by. In July 1956, the subcommittee recommended, and the ABA approved, that the Bank of America-SRI MICR data transcription concept become the standard for U.S. check processing. This decision, however, did not include a final selection of the precise character shapes or dimensions. These matters were worked out subsequently by a committee of representatives of the manufacturers. In December 1958, agreement on the E-13B font was reached after a cooperative—but during meetings sometimes quite combative—effort involving not only the ABA and the bank equipment manufacturers but also ink manufacturers and suppliers of bank checks. During the meetings, a log-jam emanated among the contending manufacturers which Zipf played a major role in breaking by proposing a kind of lottery system in which each lead manufacturer was permitted to choose the precise character definition of its choice. This way, no one competitor ended up with a special advantage. At the December meeting, the ABA also approved the present location of numerals on the check, one which was also consistent with the ERMA design. When the American National Standards Institute was established in 1960, the E-13B font (labeled ANSC-X3A7 on their docket) was one of the first two standards to be approved. The Bank of America, as they had intended, had developed the standard check processing system, not only for their customers, but for the entire U. S. banking system.

## Going public: from ideas to reality

It is difficult to know when to stop innovating, especially in a technological field in which new improvements are being discovered every day. The costs of the bank's experiments continued to rise, however, and Beise, Leif, and Zipf were growing restless. There is a point at which experimentation must be turned into action. Early in 1955, they decided that that point had arrived. The design of E.R.M. was "frozen" and decreed to be completed by the spring. This design provided less than they had originally hoped. Over time, their goal had shifted from the lofty one of building a complete and total banking system to the more modest one of building a prototype that was to be constructed "as is." September 1955 was set as the date for public demonstration. $^{10}$

Beise, now president and as ever an adroit publicist, shifted his team's attention from constructing an automated system to orchestrating a grand public announcement. The bank's public relations office requested a name change, deeming "E.R.M." too technical-sounding and potentially intimidating to customers. To the considerable dismay of the members of S&ER, who favored the more scientific-sounding FINAC (for financial accounting), the machine was rechristened by the marketing department to the more appealing "ERMA" (for Electronic Recording Machine Accounting). It was as "ERMA"—or "My Friend ERMA" according to advertisements—that the Bank of America's innovations made banking history.

Beise designated September 22, 1955, as "ERMA Day." To avoid leaks, the press was bussed from San Francisco to SRI's Menlo Park headquarters, the site of the announcement. Beise and Morrin conducted the presentation. Beise spoke of the great contribution the machine would make to Bank of America and to the banking community in general. Morrin emphasized the magnitude of the engineering accomplishment and then, with a flourish, demonstrated the system. Neither felt that it was necessary to name the other firms that had collaborated on the project, nor did they disclose the costs incurred. Bank of America had invited an impressive list of journalists—including the financial editors of newspapers and wire services, California business syndicate writers, and writers from The New York Times, Life, Fortune, Newsweek, and Business Week—a tactic which soon paid off in a barrage of articles lauding the Bank of America's "Friend ERMA."

Meanwhile, quietly behind the scenes, Zipf was bringing an equally significant innovation on stream. The same month that ERMA was announced, the bank's first IBM 702 was installed in a new computer room in San Francisco. The installment loan system was brought up on the San Francisco computer within three weeks, followed, in November, by the Los Angeles-developed real estate loan program, to which 90,000 loans had been converted. An S&ER implementation audit of the installment and real estate loan operations compared estimated costs with actual costs to verify the savings and justify to the management committee confirmation of a second 702 for Los Angeles. Increased machine costs were attributed to necessary ongoing system modifications and high labor costs due to variable run times. Scheduled five-hour jobs could be stretched to eight hours or more by systems errors and to as much as 20 hours when unplanned maintenance occurred at busy times, yet overall potential savings still produced a planned positive cash flow in the fourth year and a net profit from labor savings in the fifth. Although the project's actual machine and labor costs exceeded expectations by \$6,373, or 23%, Beise, on the basis of S&ER's analyses projecting that the loan system would eliminate 200 man years of clerical activities, approved the second IBM 702 for delivery to Los Angeles in June 1957.

## Developing a new management approach

In 1956, Zipf appointed the bank's first automation advisory council. It was composed of a number of bank managers whose departments relied on the IBM 702 or who were interested in automation. The committee's primary responsibility was to appraise all of the bank procedures and to suggest automated processing improvements. Being one of the first automation user's group councils on record, this group reviewed potential plans and also suggested new applications to Zipf. Its Annual Progress Summary report for 1957 noted that the committee had made 43 suggestions, most of which were incorporated into 1957 activities or the 1958 plan. Among the applications recommended were five new services for Timeplan loans and automated automobile insurance renewal and, since the new collection system would eliminate the need for delinquent loan notices, they recommended discontinuance of the delinquency application.

Beise and Zipf put in place three managerial innovations that helped the Bank achieve technological leadership. For one, they initiated processes of technology scanning to learn about competitors' and suppliers' products and plans, an approach that the S&ER department and others perfected with experience. They invested early in the technological cycles in research and development in systems, often undertaking projects in parallel, to assure that their approach stayed at the leading edge and that they understood better its strengths and weaknesses. And, third, as a matter of course, they brought the results of these activities to the management committee for discussion. Quarterly reviews, including full scale analyses of new technology, were made a standard operating procedure for running the bank. By following these policies, the bank improved its competency for managing the complexities of computer systems, reduced its costs and was also able to provide more differentiated services than their competitors. These policies made the Bank of America a very unique bank, given the prevailing approaches to management in 1955.

The bank's senior management were deeply instilled with the belief that it was important to mold computer systems to their own banking needs and that existing "off-the-shelf" technology could not completely satisfy those needs. By the fall of 1955, they had become relatively sophisticated managers of technological systems. With appropriate managerial attention, innovation could be achieved economically. They now understood that significant cost savings were available from automating paper processing with computers and they were fully cognizant of the need for ongoing change. The costs of processing loans, for example, had been reduced by the 702 system and, simultaneously, accuracy was improved. This established a base of operation from which volume could be doubled with only marginal increases in cost. The scale economics of automation were evident and the management approach necessary to secure them clear: It was essential to set firm (1) objectives, (2) deadlines, and (3) cost guidelines and to make trade-offs between the three. While all three targets were officially given equal weight, Zipf soon learned that the deadline received top priority and he became a master at making this tradeoff. In the early days, the programs he headed met their deadlines even if the system's functionality had to be curtailed and, although there were minor cost differences in various projects, the total program was keep within the cost guidelines. In this way, Zipf earned the reputation within the bank as an effective and tight-fisted manager; thereby, he avoided the pitfall of technological optimism which besets so many pioneers.

The results of SRI experiments also tempered the bank's expectations about the reliability of electronic systems: systems, they learned, can fail unexpectedly. Daily computer operations in a production shop were very dependent on quick, responsive actions by alert individuals. Repairing breakdowns took time and typically required restarting the entire procedure. Because the bank relied on overnight processing to insure that they begin the next day knowing their cash positions, it was essential to finish the processing runs every night. Development of back-up procedures and emergency actions, consequently, became an early concern of the S&ER team.

The Bank of America was not the only bank that embarked on an automation program around this time. Several other banks had automated their loan processing and two large banks were experimenting with alternative check processing systems. The Chase Manhattan Bank was working with Laboratory for Electronics at MIT to design and build a system. The computer was referred to as Diana, the "Goddess of the Chase." The First National City Bank was collaborating with an International Telephone and Telegraph subsidiary in Antwerp, Belgium. Both projects relied on a technology called "slave systems," in which checks were placed into transparent pockets for sorting after being encoded by operators at the input stage. The subsequent proof process was electronic. A few banks, including The County Trust of White Plains, were experimenting with books of punch-card checks. None of these approaches ever came close to being the dominant design. Following the announcement of ERMA, all shifted to planning for MICR type processes. $^{11}$

Finally, Beise and Zipf initiated management procedures for dealing with system issues within a small cadre of selected, experienced bank managers. This group knew how to deal with the grist of technology in terms of its costs, risks and rewards. The E.R.M. experience had shown the value of carefully tracking the system development process and posing technological questions in bank processing terms. By now, these executives appreciated the trade-offs that must be made between functionality, access, speed, and cost, realizing also that the use of computer technology was in constant, rapid change. The 702 implementation had demonstrated that investing in education and learning was required to achieve technical competence in developing and operating systems. The travelers' check project confirmed the value of preprinted information and taking a system point of view. The bank's management had learned these lessons and this gave them the confidence to expand.

## Implementing the First IT Solution: 1956–1958

## Augmenting competencies

By the fall of 1956, data processing at Bank of America had grown from one IBM 702 in an air-conditioned room in San Francisco to two full-fledged data centers, one in San Francisco and one in Los Angeles, each with an IBM 702 and a 30 person support staff, with 30 extra temporary staff to handle data conversion. The bank's seasoned systems development and programming staffs had established proven track records. S&ER was growing at 30% per year.

The close ties to senior management were drawn even tighter when Zipf was promoted to vice president reporting to Dana and this enabled S&ER to develop a broad research and development program. Its charter was to "function as an internal consulting group [with] authority to initiate studies and projects in instances where a preliminary evaluation of an application suggests it is justified."12 S&ER developed and maintained a list of potential applications that in their estimation would provide significant payback and Zipf prioritized and assigned these so as to keep expensive infrastructure development costs to a minimum. Budget balancing tricks were also applied. A complete rewrite of tape files, for example, was embedded in a project designed to convert all credit applications to the 702.

Managerial communications among the key parties were effective but low key. Zipf met with Dana in an ongoing manner to review progress and they met together with Beise periodically to keep him informed. Quarterly, Zipf discussed a portfolio of projects with the operating committee of senior managers; annually, he submitted a proposed budget and longer-term program plan to the bank's management committee for approval. At these sessions he typically submitted a two-year program of work, which always included two or three research projects. One such project, for example, was to study the use of remote input devices and the necessary modifications to standard machines. While these sessions were vital to the program's success, the key was that Zipf, Dana and Beise shared a common view of what had to be done and they simply went about it. Consequently, Zipf met with Beise on a one-on-one basis only infrequently; nevertheless, he could "see him anytime on a few minutes notice if he needed a decision made."

## Manufacturing ERMA

## Identifying Vendors

Following the announcement of ERMA, Bank of America faced the task of selecting a qualified manufacturer to build 36 of these machines for use throughout California. A bank team outlined four criteria by which to judge prospective manufacturers—technological ability, financial stability, reputation and size, and cost of the proposed system—and issued a request for proposals.

Approximately 30 companies visited SRI to observe the prototype and submitted some form of proposal. The contenders ranged from such predictable companies as IBM and Remington Rand to companies as remote from electronics as General Mills and United Shoe Machines. By late November, the bank had arrived at a short list of four manufacturers, each of which was invited to make a presentation to the bank's committee.

Three of the four finalists, IBM, Radio Corporation of America (RCA), and Texas Instruments (TI), were expected to propose. General Electric (GE) was a surprise entrant with experience only in military computer systems. The companies' final proposals were submitted to the bank in February 1956. IBM, although a logical choice, was suspect relative to its intentions (a 1971 report, retrieved years later, suggested that IBM might have been planning to shelve SRI's technology). RCA, although included in the final list, also was not considered seriously. This left TI and GE as the final contestants.

Texas Instruments' plan was to provide the requisite functionality through staged development of a transistorized computer. Their proposed payment to Bank of America for patents and rights ranged from \$5 million to \$17 million over six to eight years, in addition to which it was anticipated that the bank would realize \$135,000 in annual savings. TI strongly recommended that the automatic input system be developed in conjunction with SRI.

General Electric, the dark horse in the competition, had considerable applications experience in its business units but it had no organizational unit for developing, manufacturing, installing, or maintaining computer systems. GE's proposal encouraged advancing SRI's system and using transistor circuitry wherever possible. Their proposal contained sections showing how new technology could increase reliability and speed while reducing processing costs. Its work program called for GE engineers to collaborate with SRI engineers on the initial design stage.

SRI's engineers overwhelmingly favored Texas Instruments due to its demonstrated technological know-how and interest in extending a number of SRI innovations. The bankers, however, favored General Electric, whose \$30 million proposal was considerably lower than any of the others and specified a \$1 million default to be paid to Bank of America if they failed to produce the machine. TI's proposal required a down payment, was more costly, and the bank was concerned by what it perceived to be the company's shaky financial condition. The debate was heated but, ultimately, the bankers prevailed and GE—a company with no computer manufacturing facilities and embarking on its very first computer venture—was awarded a \$30 million contract to build the 36 ERMA computer systems. Beise, however, was convinced that GE would deliver a banking machine tailored to Bank of America's needs rather than a trumped up version of a general purpose computer. The contract called for a detailed design proposal by December 31, 1957.

## Crystallizing a Systems Design

Once GE recovered from its surprise at being awarded the prestigious contract, Bob Johnson was recruited from their Schenectady electronics laboratory to form and lead the development team. Johnson and his team decided, due to severe time constraints, to develop the hardware logic, assembler language, and control program in three parallel efforts—incredible goals at the time that were nevertheless realized. The team set three design objectives: design a reliable computer that could perform necessary functions without strain given existing capabilities; write assembler programs to perform the necessary banking functions; and develop peripheral hardware to create sorted input tapes. The bank was to design the latter jointly with NCR.

In parallel with the GE effort, Zipf and his team, under Assistant Vice President of Systems Reg Carlson, made a detailed analysis of check processing in the Los Angeles area, the fastest growing customer base. Believing that GE needed more systems guidance, the bank, in September 1956, submitted a counter proposal to GE in which they detailed check processing activities at the branches and added proofing to the system. Carlson and his team concluded that paper handling was the least efficient aspect of check proofing operations and they proposed the capturing of the data for the checks on magnetic tape during the first pass. By proofing and sorting checks in account number sequence by computer and generating a dollar amount control tape, paper processing would be reduced to a minimum because the checks would be captured to tape on the first pass, after which all subsequent data processing would be done from the magnetic tape. The sorter readers could then be used later "off-line" to sort the checks and deposit slips to be returned at the end of the month to the customers.

Performing proof and transit had been in the original proposal, but was lost in the final SRI system design. S&ER argued strongly to put it back in, engendering a flurry of meetings among the bank, GE, NCR, and SRI. Although GE and NCR were concerned about the added cost and complexity of adding proofing, all of them recognized the market value of doing so and their discussions soon became focused on how best, not whether, to implement the function.

The interest and demonstrated flexibility of the vendors led Beise and his team to conclude that GE would likely fulfill its contract and Zipf was asked to develop a proposal for the bank's board of directors identifying the costs and benefits of the proposed system. $^{13}$ The overall economic analysis was favorable due, in part, to recovery of cash from systems depreciation, expected growth in processing activities at relatively constant cost, and projected labor savings from the new system of 51.4% direct clerical labor hours, or 46,566 per month, for the Los Angeles branches. The proposal was subsequently approved.

## Preparing for a massive roll out

Planning for the implementation of ERMA began in late 1956 and continued throughout 1957. An ERMA system was to be installed every other month during the first year, accelerating to one installation every month during the second year. The plan was aggressive. The first system was to be installed in San

Jose by December 31, 1959, the thirty-sixth in San Diego County on February 28, 1961. Each would require a trained staff of 17 and total training time of 221 man-months. With ABA specifications finalized in March 1958, following more than two years of discussion with manufacturers, the bank continued rollout of its now-standard MICR checks for nearly two million accounts. Over the following five years, Bank of America spent more than \$3 million teaching other banks and printers how to print MICR checks and test the quality of printing for character definition and signal strength.

In the fall of 1958, the GE-Bank of America team hunkered down to develop the final working model of ERMA. All components were in the process of being debugged individually, with final connection planned by year end. That target was met in late December when a pilot system handling 100 accounts proved that the integrated system could process checks. Zipf accepted the ERMA system from GE on December 30, 1958. But the terms of the contract were still loose. They spelled out no functions, only the processing of a specified number of checks at a set price and, hence, were open to varying interpretations. Zipf and Mel Gienapp, manager of the bank's data operations, recruited a team of 30 seasoned operations managers to be trained as ERMA programmers to serve as backup. Programmers were trained initially on the 702 and then transferred to the ERMA language as it emerged. An off-contract compromise on proofing needs, reached in late February, called for GE to expand the core memory of the main computer hardware in order to allow the bank to move more quickly to automating the proof operation as the systems were delivered. Drawing on its core of newly trained programmers, the agreement called for the Bank of America to provide most of the programming support needed to code the applications.

# Reaching the Crest Mounting the Dominant Design in Banking: 1958–1964

## Launching the systems

In April 1959, 20 newly-trained programmers and three seasoned IBM 702 programmer-managers began working with the GE team to test the production ERMA system. With an August completion date set for bringing up the San Jose system and a parallel installation of a second ERMA system underway in Los Angeles, activity in the bank became intense. A bank team headed by Tony Russo, a systems manager, had developed a check handling system and set of procedures for implementing the bank's proposed operations which were tested and implemented in the San Jose branch during the fall. Against all odds, in the face of SRI's initial skepticism, and overcoming the stormy beginnings of the Bank of America-GE relationship, GE delivered ERMA on time and within budget, a matter of great pride to those who worked on the project.

The ERMA GE delivered, it turned out, was a far different machine than the computer constructed by SRI just three years earlier. It was a state-of-the-art, programmed computer with automatic check sorting and magnetic character recognition input that took advantage of technological progress made in many areas, including transistorization and the latest data processing techniques. In addition, the central processor now had an instruction set, a command structure, and peripheral equipment that had been especially designed to meet Bank of America's needs.

ERMA almost immediately was an economic success. A 1959 analysis of its economic impact showed that it produced savings at a rate faster than its costs due, in part, to the greater than expected volume of processing which result from higher than projected numbers of customers and concomitant increases in check usage. Original estimates were for 1.98 million accounts. By the time the system was implemented, there were over 2.3 million. Much faster and more accurate check processing reduced the bank's float. Thus, coupled with the elimination of 2,332 proof and transit operators and bookkeepers, the ERMA program contributed directly to the bank's profits while it expanded the customer service capacity of its branches which were now offering a variety of new, computer supported services.

It was time to go public. And Beise was ready. Opening ceremonies for ERMA, held in 1960 at three different locations connected by closed-circuit television, were conducted with great fanfare by the master of ceremonies, Ronald Reagan, host of the popular General Electric Masterpiece Theater program. With the installation of the last ERMA in June 1961, the initial program was complete. All in all, 13 ERMA centers, employing 32 computers, were now servicing over 2.3 million checking accounts at 238 branches. Conversion of the bank's 2,382,230 savings accounts was begun on January 11, 1962, and completed on February 23, 1962.

## Innovating to "informate"

In late 1958, S&ER added exception reporting, trend analysis, and notice of time dependent actions to their application set. Thereby, the Bank of America was among the very first to "informate," to use Shoshana Zuboff's more recent term, rather than just automate. $^{14}$ Use of the IBM 702 was expanded to increase the level of management support for loans and mutual funds and to add such new functions as bond investment, branch clearings reconciliation, accounts receivable, and corporate trust. The trust system was expanded to provide timely information on due dates, coupon requirements, and other operational activities of portfolio management, including inventory management and accounting and analytical investment portfolio evaluation. A program to develop standards, due dates, and cutoff points for exception reporting on overdue loans, initiated by S&ER early in 1958 began to payoff and helped managers identify out of control situations by tracking actual results and to identify exceptions for management analysis. By 1959, almost all loans were being tracked. S&ER was operating in strategic mode. It was developing applications to improve customer service and to deliver new services to the branches and credit offices. In all of these areas, the bank was a pioneer.

Meanwhile, an S&ER team, working with the bank's credit manager for retail businesses, developed an application for a timely exception reporting system that would extend the capacity of the new systems. By ERMA's launch date, the bank had applied exception reporting to all loans, balances, and payment procedures, and a number of analytic packages supported trust and loan officers in portfolio management. The areas remaining untouched were management of large commercial accounts and automation of the general ledger. Commercial banking did not seem appropriate, because, due to the low volume of transactions, it would yield little in the way of cost savings, especially given the fact that managers continuously reviewed most of their credit accounts anyway to provide personal service to their clients.

## Exploiting the new all-electronic base

Following the successful installation of the ERMAs, the bank's strategic orientation was revved up a notch. By shifting data processing to an all-electronic base, they had opened up a new set of opportunities. They were no longer faced with solving a major crisis. Rather, the bank now had formidable economies of scale and experience in providing systems to draw upon. Over the next seven years, Bank of America, under the leadership of Beise and Zipf, expanded the domain of IT support substantially. IT was used to link directly to its customers, to support the introduction of new products and services, and to improve management control. The new technology became a means to market expansion, not just a way to achieve cost savings. By making up-to-date information more readily available, the computer systems promoted more effective decision making and control throughout the bank. During the early 1960s, ERMA and 702 projects changed, fundamentally and forever, the way the Bank of America—and subsequently every other bank—was managed.

The bank's IT successes encouraged an expansion of the branch bank system. Beise, convinced that with ERMA and 702 he could economically put branches in smaller communities than had previously been feasible, aggressively pushed branch growth and began acquiring and converting small banks to branches. Bank of America's branch bank system grew from 617 in 1957 to 871 in 1964, with a peak addition of 81 branches in 1961. Eventually it owned 40% of the branches in California, which accounted for a 44% market share (because its branches could serve more customers). At a Board of Directors review of ERMA on July 19, 1960, Zipf gave a detailed cost analysis of his 1958 proposal estimates compared with a 1960 analysis based upon two years of actual experience. With the former manual system, the cost would have been \$15,880,000 versus an expected total of \$9,775,000 with ERMA, or a \$6 million plus savings. $^{15}$

Rapid growth, however, takes its toll. Its key requirement, Zipf and his colleagues determined, was not the electronic boxes, but capable people who could use the boxes effectively. The internal supply of available and interested bankers to be trained for systems jobs, however, could not keep up with demand and, consequently, a policy that was quite new to the bank was adopted: recruiting from engineering schools and training these beginners for careers in systems. A crucial implication of this policy was that the bank's traditional personnel system would have to be changed, forcing Zipf, Mel Gienapp, Herb Swenson and other S&ER personnel to work carefully with the Bank of America's rather stodgy and bureaucratic personnel department, encouraging them to adopt job classifications and salary scales for systems personnel that were approximately equal to middle-level loan officers or branch managers. Otherwise, Zipf knew, they would be unable to maintain salary levels that were externally competitive.

Gienapp, the human resource leader of the group, creatively documented the functions of the new jobs in order to substantiate the higher salary levels that were competitive with outside employers and proposed a new set of job titles. Career paths were developed leading from operations to programming systems to analyst and, eventually, manager. While the bank's system development organization was growing rapidly, the operations organization provided a faster route to promotion for IT professionals than was enjoyed by other career avenues available in the bank. The creation of a two culture environment—fast track techies versus traditional bankers—did, however, have its costs. Inevitably, the systems personnel became isolated from other bank employees and some, relishing in their successes, adopted a rather haughty attitude, one that may have contributed to the hostility S&ER experienced in later years.

## Creating credit cards

Superior IT competency can serve as the launching pad for competitive preemptive strikes, as the Bank of America's experience with credit cards shows, although an early misfire almost resulted in a disaster. Because S&ER was overloaded during the ERMA and IBM 702 projects, Beise asked Leif to identify other services that might take advantage of the processing potential of the new computer systems. He, in turn, contracted with Arthur Andersen for an analysis of alternatives. Among the projects they identified was to revisit the possibility of offering credit cards. Credit cards had been introduced by Western Union as early as 1914, but they had never been very successful economically due primarily to their expensive operating costs and the difficulty of controlling credit losses. Determining that the computer could potentially reduce costs and provide tight credit control, Leif recommended that the bank introduce a credit card. A program was initiated in 1958 under the direction of the assistant controller. The system's design, however, was subcontracted to an outside supplier who created a system based on a debossed card (depressed character rather than raised). The system was to be tested in Fresno in 1959 and, if successful, gradually rolled out to other branches. The plan entailed a city by city roll out, issuing credit cards to the bank's better customers, beginning with the central valley cities of Sacramento and Redding and being refined as it was moved into the large metropolises.

As Bank of America prepared to enter Sacramento in early 1960, however, the First Western Bank of San Francisco announced its intention to introduce a credit card statewide. Beise was overseas at the time. The situation called for a rapid response and Leif and the management committee, believing that they had Beise's blessings and realizing that their pilot program was in its infancy, decided to preempt the competition by immediately rolling out Bank of America's card statewide, beginning in Los Angeles and San Francisco. Aggressive expansion continued throughout 1960 and into 1961 until an audit of the program's previous year's results revealed delinquent account losses exceeded \$10 million. Leif was forced to halt the expansion and change the program's management. Jack Dillon, an ace trouble shooter, was assigned to pick up the pieces. Upon taking over the credit card program, Dillon initiated a crash new development effort, this time with the help of S&ER. The new system featured an embossed card and incorporated a sophisticated credit tracking system. Dillon subsequently recruited Ken Larkin from corporate lending to manage the revised system, which was quickly introduced through the branches to replace the existing credit cards. The full statewide implementation was completed in late 1961. A follow-up project added new programs on the IBM 702 system to create invoices and account for payments. Now resting on a firmer foundation of management information, the new credit card program became a solid product and a source of substantial earnings for the bank for a number of years.

The market popularity of Bank of America's credit card attracted the attention of banks throughout the country; indeed, the credit card program became a form of currency in itself. The bank exchanged its credit card system with Mellon Bank of Pittsburgh for a loan portfolio management system. After Marine Midland of upstate New York bought the system and three other banks expressed strong interest in it, Bank of America decided to franchise the product. Larkin actively marketed the credit card service nationally and internationally. When federal banking authorities threatened to sue for illegal interstate banking, however, Bank of America decided to divest itself of its out-of-state credit card operations. It subsequently sold its non-California franchises to the founders of Visa and shifted its emphasis to overseas expansion. In the end, however, the Bank of America left an important legacy on banking in the United States. Credit card services are part of the offerings of almost every bank today and the information systems design that dominates owes its roots to the early Bank of America solutions.

## Adapting the organization

The introduction of the IBM 702 and ERMA systems occasioned fundamental changes in Bank of America's operations. Traditionally, each branch manager had reported directly to the president, being considered the personal representative of Mr. Giannini to the branch's community. Granting credit and various financing instruments were subject to a strong functional control system, one that had grown up with the bank. Central credit staffs in Los Angeles and San Francisco had line responsibility for large statewide and national accounts and functional control of branch credit products such as home and consumer loans and Timeplans. Each branch had a manager, an operations officer, a loan/credit officer, and, depending on its size, a range of positions focused on customer areas such as retail, small business, and home loans. Branch managers operated fairly independently, occasionally visiting the central office and being visited by specialists. They tended to focus on the banking needs of consumers and small retailers and worked consciously to be identified with the community. Branch managers whose performance routinely exceeded their projected objectives or who expanded their branch's market share were given greater autonomy or were rewarded with promotions to larger branches.

Beise realized when he launched the automation project with S&ER that some of this traditional structure would have to be changed. The bank would have to be reorganized and managed in a different way. As the automation program proceeded, his first step was to regionalize and to create four functional field staffs—in credit, personnel, operations, and business development—that would exert functional control over the branches from the regional center. Field representatives of these staffs served groups of geographically adjacent branches.

The automation effort also redefined job roles at the branches. Most affected was the operations officer, the position traditionally designated second-in-command with the greatest number of direct reports and bearing prime responsibility for maintaining fiscal integrity. With the expansion of its services into operations and loan procedures, S&ER met often with branch operations officers to assist with developmental activities and training. As automation transformed the branch, many of its clerical duties were shifted to the computer centers while the branch operations offices gained increasing responsibilities related to new systems, staffing requirements, training in standard procedures, and managing support for new products. The latter responsibility drew operations officers more deeply into business development and marketing activities, and their strong link to S&ER increased the importance of the group's presence in the branch. The result of these changes, over time, was a shift from a strategy in which each branch offered a constant product line with functional staff guidance under laissez-faire, independent branch management supervision, to one based on an on-going marketing program of new products orchestration by S&ER. The objective was to make every branch officer a business development officer with a focus on customer service. Although it appeared to be a dramatic change at the time, the bank had actually been moving in this direction since the introduction of the IBM 702. Thus, by means of its system development studies and its continual search for new applications that resulted in new products and marketing approaches—all of which served to improve a branch manager's and branch operating officer's performance—S&ER became the dominant factor in the branch.

As S&ER grew, so did Zipf's power and he assumed more control over field operations, first by managing the development and implementation of IBM 702 applications and later as the architect of the ERMA program. Official recognition had come in 1959, when he assumed responsibility for all field operations. Further consolidation came in 1960, when Zipf was given responsibility for personnel and bank operations. Branch managers now had two bosses, Zipf with his area managers and Beise with his regional credit managers.

## Upgrading the hardware

Managers of technology must constantly be matching their capabilities and capacity to their customers' demand. At the Bank of America, customer and application growth created a capacity problem sooner than anticipated, although, fortunately, rapidly changing technology actually made it less expensive to upgrade than to continue running the existing technology. The bank's experience with the IBM 702 installation and observation of other banks' experiences suggested at least a two year lead time was needed for planning and installing new equipment. Russell Fenwick, one of the bank's first programmers, became S&ER's system planner responsible for preparing an annual forecast of capacity demand for four years out. At the time he estimated demand increases in excess of 15% per year. By 1958, demand was growing due not only to the opening of new branches, but also to accelerated growth of applications and management reports. Among other things, Fenwick's projection showed that the bank could exceed the capacity of its IBM 702s by 1960 rather than the originally planned for 1962–1963 time frame.

IBM had already announced its 7000 family of computers in the summer of 1958. Its initial offering was the 7070, a fully transistorized, core memory machine that was less expensive, significantly more powerful and reliable, took up less floor space, and required less air conditioning than the IBM 702. Moreover, the 7070 could easily be upgraded to take advantage of rapidly changing transistor technology. Based on Fenwich's recommendations, S&ER ordered two 7070s in the spring of 1959, to be delivered in early 1961. Analysis of costs showed that converting to the new system would result in substantial savings. Moreover, 7070 assembly language promised to offer a significant improvement in programmer productivity over the more tedious and confining machine language and standard programs IBM provided for the 702. The proposal called for buying, rather than leasing, the systems at a savings of more than \$5 million before taxes (assuming a five year life expectancy) and the management committee approved it.

New accounting systems for branches, credit card, and trust were designed in late 1960 for the 7070s based on the, by now commonplace, features of exception-based management reporting. The San Francisco-based systems group moved into a new building in February, 1961, and the two IBM 7070s were installed and tested with new applications in March. The major production applications were up and running in fewer than ten days. Soon thereafter, most of the other programs were brought up and the remaining old programs were more leisurely converted from the 702, a complex task that required subtle system modifications to enable the bank to take full advantage of the new features on the 7070s. Conversion was completed by the end of 1961. The conversion was rather painless and S&ER once again proved its vaunted abilities to perform miracles with new technology.

## Reaching out for new markets

The much expanded capabilities of the IBM 7070 provided excess computer capacity, freeing up resources that Zipf and his team believed could be used to expand services to customers outside the bank and to create a new source of external revenue flow. S&ER formed a business services group under Hugh Dougherty, a founding member of the department, to provide computer-based services to retail stores and professionals. Already linked to retail outlets through its credit card, the Bank of America had an established customer list and it drew on this to offer its first business services: payroll for stores, doctors, and other professionals. By pricing its services competitively, the team was able to gain market share quickly and to break even toward the end of its first year of operations, achieving a positive cash flow in the second year. With this success under its belt, S&ER's next strategic initiative was to expand the product line to include professional billing and freight payments services.

## Another upgrade

By offering these new services, of course, the bank began to reach the capacity of its new systems. In the late spring of 1962, Zipf discussed with the management committee the need to expand the bank's computing capacity once again, noting that the 7070s, which had been installed early in 1961, could process the existing workload perhaps until 1968, but that new applications in planning and development, including personal trust, accrual loans, and business services, would require greater capacity. Newly available technology in the form of the IBM 7074, he pointed out, would result in significantly lower per unit costs while adding capacity, with essentially no conversion cost involved, as the new computer was compatible with the 7070. In December 1962, Zipf recommended, and the management committee approved, the conversion to IBM 7074s. As before, the conversion went fairly smoothly.

## Expanding the communications infrastructure

The bank also became a pioneer in telecommunications. An early awareness of the importance of communications as an aspect of information processing had led Zipf and S&ER to assimilate the communications functions within the department and they began to manage its cost and form of service actively. Spurred, in part, by the need to service a broadly distributed branch banking system, the move quickly led to a more strategic consideration of how communications might be employed to improve the delivery of service to customers.

By 1959, the Bank of America boasted a complete internal communications service, with a new switchboard in Oakland and an expanded service switchboard in San Francisco. Two years later, it had the equivalent of an 800 number dial-in service for customers in the San Francisco area. Expansion of direct dial switchboards continued throughout the state and a program was initiated to purchase standard telephone equipment for all branches at quantity discounts. A later study of the feasibility of using private point-to-point microwave links between locations in Fresno led the bank to file for a license, thereby becoming one of the earliest private microwave operators in the nation. By 1963, the bank was operating its own private telephone service throughout California.

Linking computers over telephone lines reduced transportation costs and permitted information to be processed in a more timely fashion. S&ER continued its program of centralizing processing and developing electronic means for collecting and distributing information. A crucial project involved establishing an ability to communicate between ERMA and the IBM 7070s. S&ER developed a magnetic tape conversion method for transferring information from one system to the other. Building on the competence it gained from working on the 7070-ERMA link, the department was later able to select a cost effective input or remote processing system and merely plug it into its existing communication systems.

## Strategically leveraging the dominant design

Confidence gained in the expansion period fueled Beise's appetite for new IT products and, as his team expanded into the new technology, the bank benefited from a real leap in productivity due to the faster and more reliable solid-state systems. The cost savings were enormous on the operations side and new storage capacity opened up the possibility of an entire new set of products. The team shifted to product expansion and new services in addition to automating and informing old systems. Their visions now included providing services well beyond those based on their existing competencies and customer base. Enjoying the fruits of scale economies, the payroll and payable services offered to external customers were still being expanded even as ERMA and the bank's more traditional service offerings were growing in volume. An entire new market opened up for them based on existing business relationships. The S&ER organization, strongly supported by Beise, also expanded rapidly but the cash flow they generated paid for this expansion.

Strategically, the most important lessons the bank had learned centered on using systems competitively. In a 1962 letter to Beise, in which he recommends that a new strategic focus be placed on computer-based services, Zipf reveals his philosophy:

Bank of America's leadership in pioneering the development and application of electronic processing techniques to internal bank accounting activities today yields a competitive advantage from a standpoint of quality and accuracy in existing deposit account services. However, today's advantage is largely, if not entirely, a question of lead time over competition, for with the passage of time, our competition will achieve the same benefits from the plans they now have underway. It is therefore clear that if we are to maintain our position of leadership and the attendant competitive advantages, we must set out now to establish new variations in service that will enable us to preserve our present margin. $^{16}$

## The euphoria of surfing on the top during the early 1960s

From the beginning, the bank's approach to computers was business oriented. The group was motivated to stay ahead in systems development in order to excel in the banking services they delivered, not as an end in itself. During this era, they grew in overall IT and banking competence, especially in their understanding of the intricacies of operating complex systems that were constantly being upgraded. They mastered the growth in volume. On average, two million checks were processed a day while more than one million additional customer transactions were being handled as well. In addition, new services were being offered and linked to the old to provide better customer service, making the necessary backoffice processing more interrelated. By 1962, due to their effective use of IT, Bank of America had the lowest cost in the industry while at the same time offering the broadest product line.

Executive management was dedicated to staying ahead of the competition in systems. Beise, as president, led the management team and was supported by Dana, his operations chief. Together with Zipf, they were the overall designers of several impressive IT systems and, yet, they were still continually scanning for new technologies. A common tactic was to invent a new service system and then find someone to develop it for them, sharing the risks, as they did with the second, and hugely successful, credit card venture. New product services and processes were designed as extensions to existing systems. System support was expanded throughout the value chain to include retail store customers, other banks, and the Federal Reserve. At the time, Buck Rodgers, IBM's leading banking executive, believed that the Bank of America was at least two years ahead of all other banks and it was even further ahead of most financial service companies.

A new style of management emerged in which Bank of America's managers could comfortably assume that computers were reliable and could be counted on to deliver on time. Maintaining the bank's lead required this kind of confidence in the systems. A resilient and responsive support system with a well-trained support staff had been developed during the implementation of ERMA. These managers also became accustomed to making continual reviews of system progress, considering alternatives, and seizing on new market opportunities for system use. Considering complex investments in research and development became routine. Importantly, management had learned to appraise and evaluate the risks of a research and development decision, as they had done, for example, with the SRI overrun and the occasional mishaps with GE and IBM. A tradition had evolved of annually reviewing the three year technological plan which identified major applications and hardware system changes. Beise and Dana reviewed the progress of projects underway and approved new proposals quarterly. For large project proposals, full discussions were held with the management committee. The multiple year plan was prepared annually. Special emphasis was placed on cumulative savings to date and market progress of new services or products, as well as providing information on significant research projects underway such as credit card or microwave communication. A division of responsibilities emerged in which Beise presented the business case and Zipf handled the technology costs and risks.

So, by 1964, Bank of America had developed an extraordinary competence to create new banking products and services and to operate mainframe computer systems in support of its banking activities. The largest bank in the world had rolled out the largest distributed, private sector system ever installed to date, economically and on time, and it could now boast of the lowest costs and largest product line in the industry. The bank was riding on top of a momentous wave, confident and aggressive in their intentions. And Zipf had become a "Teflon man" within the bank. All of his ambitious, innovative projects had succeeded, being delivered on time and most of them under budget. So far, no damaging political debris had stuck to him.

## Losing Momentum · Sliding Into the Trough: 1964–1968

## Changes in leadership

As Goethe's Faust learns, the history of any organization unfolds in an undulatory course.

Early in 1964, Beise was elevated to chairman of the board and Rudolf A. Peterson, a former bank employee who had most recently been CEO of a Hawaiian bank and who had extensive experience in international banking, became the president of Bank of America. $^{17}$ Because California banking opportunities were leveling out and further expansion within the United States was thwarted (Giannini's dream of U.S. expansion had been denied by the Congress and the Federal Reserve), Peterson's new mandate from the bank's board was to expand overseas.

That summer, Zipf sent Peterson his new plan which outlined the personnel expansion he thought was necessary to stay competitive. The thoroughly prepared document also served as an introduction to systems planning. Its summary, highlighting recent growth in demand on central office systems called for increasing administrative staff by 195, with 90 additional people in business services, 74 in the trust department, and 31 in systems and equipment research. Growth was finally catching up. The current system, which in 1964, with 114 fewer people than in 1962, was now handling 51 million items—an increase of seven million. The rationale was to build up programming support to expand the business service product line by introducing a professional billing service. Zipf expected to be called in to explain his proposal but things had changed. This time, in lieu of an in-person discussion with the CEO, Zipf received a rather impersonal note from Peterson, who, as it happened, was traveling abroad at the time. The note simply said to proceed. Peterson, focusing upon overseas expansion, in effect had delegated all IT responsibility to Zipf. This was what Zipf wanted, of course, but not the way he wanted it. Peterson's note proved to be a bad omen.

## The new technological promise

The spring 1964 planning review had convinced S&ER management that business service expansions and increased volume would soon exhaust the capacity of the IBM 7074s.

IBM had just, on April 7, announced its 360 series, which was intended to replace all prior IBM computer series, including the 1401, 7074, and 7094 computers. Meanwhile, Burroughs, Remington Rand, and General Electric had recently announced new computer models as the race to win the competition for the third generation of computers began. Analysis of eight different computer systems convinced S&ER that the IBM 360, with its new operating system, COBOL support, and massive disk storage capacity, was the only true third generation system. An IBM 360/50, they calculated, could replace a 7074 while increasing capacity and reducing costs. S&ER subsequently ordered one and initiated a more in-depth cost benefit analysis.

Zipf and his team were anxious to convert to a higher-level language such as COBOL. Heralded as the programming language of the future, COBOL held the promise of standardized, machine-independent programming, which would minimize the amount of recoding required in shifts to subsequent generations of hardware. S&ER was maintaining more than 400,000 lines of assembly code and adding approximately 80,000 lines of new code per year. S&ER personnel would have to convert all these programs, reformat hundreds of millions of tape records into disk format, and learn new operating techniques. The longer the bank waited, the greater would be the cost to change, and this made moving to COBOL a prime objective.

The first step was to translate the IBM 7074 data processing programs into COBOL. Concurrently, planning could begin for converting the ERMA system, which would be more complex because its master account file had now grown to 22 tape reels and was becoming an operating nightmare. Tape failures during the evening runs were causing delays and the branches were not receiving their necessary balance information before opening the next morning. Fenwick and his colleagues believed that by using the 360's mass disk storage they could eliminate most of the existing cumbersome procedures and improve reliability.

To provide management with background for the IBM 360 decision, S&ER developed an economic analysis called “Impact of Automation.” Dated November 8, 1965, the report was discussed at length at a management committee meeting, called especially to review the progress of automation. Its story was compelling. The analysis, which was based on the standard manual times for the previous system’s processing tasks, concluded that an additional 1,616 employees would have had to be hired to support its current level of business, catapulting its existing level of 2,862 system employees to 4,478. Adjusting for differences in higher system salaries, the present annual savings for ERMA were placed at \$4.6 million. Furthermore, at the time of the report, the head count in check processing was actually being decreased as the volume of checks processed was increasing. Processing costs per transaction had fallen by more than 7% per year for the past four years. As a result of these efficiency enhancements, the Bank of America’s profitability continued to rise while its services were being expanded. But the bank was rapidly approaching the limit of its growth within the existing ERMA system. The new technology promised an opportunity to continue striding forward.

Based on S&ER's analysis, on November 30, 1964, the management committee approved \$3,643,100 for the acquisition of two IBM 360/50s to replace the IBM 7074/1401s. Existing IBM 7074 programs would be converted from assembler to COBOL and IBM 7074 tapes to 360 tape formats. On the basis of its IBM 702 to 7070 conversion experience, the bank decided to subcontract a large part of the effort to reduce the hiring hump and allow development to continue.

In the spring of 1965, IBM announced the details of its operating system for the 360/65, which, with greater disk capacity and more upward growth potential than other 360s, promised "more bang for the buck." Existing ERMA operations were tape and printing bound and processing growth was pushing the 12-hour turnaround window for delivering accurate, up-to-date balances to branches each morning. Zipf's group estimated that four

IBM 360/65s could replace all the ERMA processors and still provide the same overnight service and they could accommodate the 7074 workload and its planned expansions during day-time processing run, thus providing a consistent computer architecture and the potential for additional economy of scale operations. Moreover, the IBM 360/65s promised multitasking (i.e., simultaneous input, printing, and computing), which would reduce elapsed processing time, and supported random access secondary disk storage, which was much faster than serial access magnetic tape. The primary operating appeal of the IBM 360/65 was that it would allow consolidation of all operations into two centers with remote inputs and concentrated operations. This would permit the bank to reduce its operations personnel even further, to concentrate support at two sites, and to operate up to a seven-day work week.

A careful cost analysis of alternative systems of comparable capacity, based on a comparison of continuing to utilize ERMA for its full operating life versus shifting to the IBM 360/65, estimated operating expense savings of \$12 million if both the ERMA systems and the 7074s were converted to 360/65s. Growth and dramatic improvements in technology had led Zipf to make this request four years earlier than he had originally anticipated. The management committee, after some discussion, approved a \$14.22 million proposal (considerably less than the \$32 million it took to build the GE ERMAs) to replace ERMA with two IBM 360/65s and substitute two more 360/65s for the 360/50s on order.

## The conversion plan

Zipf and his team planned to begin converting ERMA and IBM 7074 programs to the 360/65s after scheduled delivery in June or July 1966. Check processing was to be done at night, most normal data processing during the day. Existing 7074 procedures were to be tested on the 360/50 over the winter and spring of 1966 and converted to the 360/65s upon their arrival in the summer. The conversion was scheduled to be completed by December.

The ERMA conversion was recognized to be a massive task. Fenwick estimated that approximately 500 man years of programming, data conversion, and testing would be required to convert all bank systems to a COBOL-based disk system. Existing systems would not only have to be converted, but also rewritten to eliminate the many patches accumulated over the years and to take advantage of disk access. The S&ER staff was to be augmented with IBM system developers and a number of consultant programming groups. In fact, there were to be as many outside as inside staff reprogramming and building new systems during the transition period. The IBM 360/50s were to be installed upon delivery and replaced by the 360/65s as soon as the latter became available.

## Zero hour

The first IBM 360/50 arrived in San Francisco in September 1965, the second in Los Angeles in October. By mid-November, however, it had become apparent that IBM's operating system was not working as promised. In fact, it was very unstable except when running jobs one at a time. Gloom spread through the S&ER group but, nevertheless, the programming continued as one job at a time was debugged using sample data. The conversion schedule had to be revised considerably, due primarily to the instability of the IBM software. On the basis of conversations with senior IBM executives, S&ER members were convinced that they would be able to begin heavy-duty conversion efforts by early summer, 1966. Consequently, a personnel plan was implemented for training and placement of ERMA workers to be transferred to other jobs later that year. But this plan, too, turned out to be premature.

In January 1966, S&ER informed the management committee of a revised plan that postponed the start of the conversion until August 1966, with the possibility of further delays, citing as the reason for this delay an inoperable operating system and unstable tape and disk drives. S&ER estimated the cost of system failure and lost work at between \$70,000 and \$100,000. Capacity restrictions on the old systems soon began to affect operations. Business services, which had previously been growing at 15% to 20% per year, now was convinced that the new computer system could not deliver and it stopped soliciting new business and terminated several of the projects it had underway, such as loan flooring (which promised a 200% return on investment). Worse, conflict surfaced in job scheduling as frustrated users contended for resources and demanded on-time delivery. Growing customer demand for new market services and an unrelenting expansion in volume exacerbated the problem as the delays persisted.

On December 9, 1966, S&ER informed the management committee that the conversion of IBM 360 programs must be deferred yet again, this time as a result of IBM's continued delay in providing a multitasking operating system. IBM informed the bank that "no other large scale business user . . . approaches [the bank's] level of complexity of multiprogrammed systems." The additional expense of delay, estimated at \$364,000, was due to the cost of maintaining parallel systems and a loss of programming effectiveness stemming from the operating system's failures. Meanwhile, the data processing and ERMA programs were converted to IBM 360 assembler language, with some COBOL routines, using IBM service center 360s and the bank's IBM 360/50s.

By late December 1966, the operating system appeared to be stabilized and it was decided to try to convert the mutual fund package, which resided on two files, a massive customer tape file located in Los Angeles and a smaller one in San Francisco. If the mutual fund package conversion went smoothly, the bank could turn its attention to its higher volume applications. The conversion, scheduled to occur over the New Year's holiday, went as planned in San Francisco. But in Los Angeles, where the entire 20-reel customer master file had to be updated to complete the transfer, IBM's new high-speed tape systems crashed. Fenwick was called away from a New Year's

Eve party at Zipf's home to try to come up with a solution. After three hours, he called IBM's "Buck" Rodgers, the company's "banking industry representative," who flew a team of IBM tape specialists and system engineers in from Denver to mount a massive second effort at the conversion. This too failed. At this point, everyone was painfully aware of the scope of the problem: the size and sophistication of the application was pushing the IBM 360/65 to the limits of its capacity. IBM's hardware support approached being adequate but the failure of its 360 operating system severely hurt the effort.

Years later it would be learned that IBM, in its competitive zeal, had rushed too quickly to market. In December 1963, a product test report stated that the 360 was “very risky” due to its hardware and, in particular, its software. It cautioned a substantial delay. When IBM’s Corporate Management Committee met on March 16, 1964, to make its decision to announce the system, it was confronted with an official recommendation of “non-support” from the Product Test Department. The 360 system was so new and different that IBM didn’t know how to manage it. As one of its software experts observed, “Product Test hadn’t done programs that much in the past, and nobody knew much about testing them. But it is certainly true that in any human organization that’s under pressure the first thing that’s shortchanged is quality control.” $^{18}$ Reflecting on his experiences as IBM’s manager of Operating System/360 during this period, Frederick Brooks summed up the problem that the Bank of America, as a sophisticated user intent on taking maximum advantage of the new computer’s advertised capabilities, faced: “the product was late, it took more memory than planned, the costs were several times the estimate, and it did not perform very well until several releases after the first.” $^{19}$

## Recovering from the immediate conversion crisis

The situation had not improved much by the spring of 1967. Frustration at the lack of conversion progress and delays in bringing up new systems generated a storm of criticism from members of the management committee. Zipf, however, felt compelled to defend his decision to proceed with the installation of the IBM 360 and, on May 10, 1967, dispatched a letter of explanation to Peterson in which he noted:

... 1966 was the first disappointing year since this group was created in 1954. Disappointing because the delays in converting to the 360 systems did not permit us to achieve the operating economies that had been forecast. Having acknowledged responsibility for what had proven to be unrealistic conversion schedules, I should not like to think my error in judgment will reflect unfavorably upon a competent, dedicated staff who worked ceaselessly in an attempt to achieve the impossible. $^{20}$

On May 25, Zipf reviewed with the management committee a plan that had been prepared in late March, but placed on hold due to the lack of improvements in systems stability and the need to add more core memory. The plan, written by Fenwick, suggested that S&ER was finally seeing light at the end of the tunnel. The department had regrouped, summarized its progress, and generated a more positive plan with demonstrable results. Morale, which had been terrible, was picking up somewhat and the group's "can do" spirit was returning.

Although only 35,000 of the 400,000 BankAmericard and 504,000 of the 2,000,000 Timeplan accounts had actually been converted by March, the group was gaining momentum. A stable and productive April was followed by an equally productive May which saw conversion of BankAmericard, Timeplan, and one of the largest ERMA installations completed. The group had developed new fail-safe procedures and, with the help of strenuous efforts from IBM, had stabilized the tape subsystems. An additional ray of hope came from the successful start of the demand deposits conversion, the bank's largest ERMA application. With the conversion secure in the spring, Zipf emphasized in a presentation to the management committee that not only had other banks followed their leadership and adopted the IBM 360 systems rather than going with Burroughs or Univac, but the bank's current performance figures vividly demonstrated that it was once again enjoying economies of scale. The results were impressive. In total, the Bank of America was processing the daily transaction volume on 7,121,000 checking, saving, installment loan, and real estate loan accounts using four 360/65s, for an average of 1,780,250 accounts per machine. By contrast, Security Pacific was processing 1,131,500 per machine (two machines), UCB 1,113,000 per machine (one), Crocker Citizens 887,500 per machine (two), and Wells Fargo just 725,000 accounts per machine on their two 360/65s. By this rough estimate, Bank of America was about 60% more efficient than its next closest competitor. Moreover, Bank of America was reputed to have more transactions per account. $^{21}$

But the major damage had been done, politically at least. The delays had resulted in serious costs to the bank. In a widely circulated report, a staff analyst estimated that conversion delays experienced by some services, such as the flooring program, had a direct economic impact on the bank's profits amounting to \$1,471,000. In addition, delays in developing new products for BankAmericard and in providing more automated support for mutual funds and trust had significant negative impacts on the bank's earnings. Total impact was estimated to be on the order of millions of dollars, offset partially, according to the report, by IBM's contributions in the form of paying all equipment costs, providing professional help valued at \$2,700,160, and due to the fact that during the entire period IBM maintained an account balance of more than \$14 million in the bank. $^{22}$

The inescapable conclusion was that the massive conversion effort had failed because of software problems. The report reviewed all the decisions to date, including the new plan and the cost to the bank of programming and outside help. By May, the total direct reprogramming effort was estimated at 167 man years at a cost of \$1.65 million, of which at least 37.4 man years, valued at \$363,834, had been irretrievably lost, a costly effort.

## Trying, but Failing, to Catch the Next Wave: 1969–1977

## Slipping down in the hole

Events returned, somewhat, to normal during 1968. The IBM 360 program, despite an enormous remaining backlog of work, was now under control, although many urgent, high-priority projects, most arising from flaws in outmoded systems, continued to keep considerable pressure on S&ER. The conversion of the general ledger, for example, had to be aborted so that the group could deal with a number of pressing operating systems crises. Fulfilling these demands delayed almost all other requests for support and new products.

Once the total conversion was completed and a modern, stable platform had been put in place, the S&ER leadership believed that it had regained its right to proceed with other innovations. Senior management, however, was still skeptical, if not antagonistic. A. W. "Tom" Clausen, who replaced Peterson as CEO in late 1969, continued to emphasize a strategy focusing on creative credit lending programs and overseas expansion. In the spring of 1970, Zipf, the only management committee member with any operations or computer experience, was promoted to executive vice president and placed in charge of the California bank. He was now charged with reorganizing retail banking operations from a functional to an area structure and given responsibility for marketing, credit, operations, personnel, and, ultimately, the profitability of the retail bank. Fenwick became the operational leader of S&ER, reporting to Samuel Stewart, a lawyer with no IT background who was put in charge of the bank's administrative functions. This had the unfortunate effect of increasing the organizational gap between Zipf and Fenwick, leaving Zipf without a power base and Fenwick without a direct line to the executive suite. Consequently, with a large backlog of system work, Fenwick and the S&ER team focused their attention on it and ignored opportunities to lead the bank into the newly emerging information-driven markets.

To make matters worse, the planning process that S&ER had pioneered was scuttled. The management committee returned to conducting an annual budgeting session that was oriented toward short-term payoffs. Dropped from the bank's management procedures, for example, were the annual three-year IT planning horizon discussion, the follow-up annual budgeting sessions, and the quarterly IT program review. Despite a severe backlog and more work than people, a much needed expansion of S&ER was never considered. The group was also cut off from its flow of research and development funds. Senior management was now preoccupied with expanding overseas and building up its California retail operations, mainly to improve cash flow. Furthermore, it did not perceive any overseas opportunities for IT. The recent conversion experience had shaken its confidence in technology in general and in S&ER in particular.

S&ER was barely able to keep apace with overall volume expansion and it had clearly lost its leadership role within the bank. The early 1970s were devoted to catching up with systems work and improving existing services. The group's experience was firmly grounded in large mainframe systems and batch processing. Its personnel gained little experience with new technology as it emerged. Even though it had been a pioneer in linking telecommunications with computers, the group now had no means to test, for example, on-line processing with new database technologies. Consequently, the Bank of America slipped a full generation behind in computer software. Its systems effort was no longer in strategic mode. At best, it was being managed as a production factory. $^{23}$

S&ER also experienced difficulties when at last it attempted to offer its services abroad. By the 1970s, Bank of America had 88 overseas branches in 11 countries. With the credit market overseas growing faster than that in the United States, management focused on expanding credit markets to gain economy of scale. The first overseas system development began in 1968 with S&ER's help but outside of its purview with an accounts receivable project in London. The system was initially successful and was augmented to provide a comprehensive analysis of overall accounting needs.

Development of a new loan reporting and accounting system for foreign branches was led by S&ER's Bruce Foster, an experienced systems developer. His design was selected after an extended proposal process and called for a central site with distributed minicomputers in the branches. The software worked satisfactorily but the hardware proved to be a problem. The vendor's slow response to machine problems had by now become a common, persistent problem and, in August, major consideration was given to solutions for improving it. Concomitantly, more hardware enhancements were installed expanding printing capacity, increasing disk storage, and speeding up response times. Still the systems failed and, in the spring of 1975, Alvin Rice, senior vice president of the International Division, called for a "high-noon" review of the bank's international maintenance support for IT. The result was that international computer support was transferred from the central bank group to Rice's organization. Each foreign department was given a great deal of systems autonomy and the bank's technology programs became even more fragmented throughout Europe, Asia, and South America. Thus, at a time when Citibank and its other competitors were developing coherent international programs, S&ER was retreating into its California base.

## Coping with management changes

In the early 1970s, there were now four systems groups—S&ER, centralized operations, business services, and management science—all of which reported to Stewart. Their activities were coordinated among themselves, their close historical ties to Zipf providing the glue. When Stewart retired in 1973, Zipf again assumed responsibility for managing the computer groups but, being deeply involved in managing the California bank at the time, he delegated the majority of the ongoing activities to Fenwick, a superb technician and highly effective planner but who, due to his youth, and, of course, his role in the conversion debacle, was not now a highly regarded executive within the bank. As a result, Fenwick could not effectively lead and, lacking coordinated leadership, the systems organizations gradually drifted apart. Business services was the first to defect, creating its own development group. Within a year, business services became completely independent of S&ER and forsook any technology sharing with its former teammates. In 1975, after receiving permission from the comptroller of the currency, S&ER finally conducted its first experiments with ATMs. Fenwick proposed a statewide rollout of ATMs, but the head of branch operations vetoed it as being too expensive, too risky, and counter to the bank's "tried and true" tradition of "in person" personal service. Thus, the Bank of America was left at the starting gate for one of the most explosive technological advances in the banking industry.

Then, Zipf suddenly retired in late 1975 due to illness. With his leaving, S&ER lost its only friend in senior management. Clausen appointed Stan Langsdorf, a former comptroller, to head all administrative systems, including S&ER. Langsdorf, a relatively passive, caretaker type of manager, allowed the three remaining systems groups to continue to coordinate all domestic development efforts among themselves and as they saw fit. S&ER continued to flounder.

Given the success of its global expansion program, the Bank of America was now growing faster overseas than in California, where, in fact, it was now losing market share. The bank's share declined even further as Security Pacific and Wells Fargo rolled out ATMs and other competitors aggressively introduced new loan products. By 1978, the Bank of America was being outstripped by all of its major competitors and was losing market share in the state overall and per branch basis in most of the communities it served. A variety of factors accounted for this: the growth of competitors, increased price and product competition, and its lag in systems development. Senior management in retail banking had a strong bottom-line profit orientation but they had no in-depth understanding of the power of technology or the economics of systems development. In order to acquire a computer system for the California division, for example, at least 26 executives had to agree on the detailed plan and the amount of investment. They didn't and, consequently, no major improvements in IT were made for several years.

## Seeking the Right Maestro/Leader Partnership: 1978 to Present

## Mickel and Clausen

Clausen finally recognized the severity of the problem in 1978 when he appointed John Mickel senior vice president in charge of systems, charging him with reversing the bank's loss of market share by reviving the bank's competitiveness in computer systems and installing newer technology in the branches. $^{24}$ A former CEO of Decimus, one of the Bank of America's most profitable subsidiaries, Mickel was noted for his strong product-line marketing focus. With Clausen's sponsorship, Mickel recommended two projects which he felt were essential to give Bank of America a new foundation for electronic banking: statewide rollout of ATMs and, significantly, shifting the demand deposit accounting system to an on-line database system. The irony was that the bank was only now embracing an idea that it had pioneered over two decades earlier with SRI's original E.R.M. specifications. The on-line system would provide easy access to data and support more rapid development of new products and services. $^{25}$ Both projects received full funding and were targeted for a two-year rollout. Mickel hired Peter Hill from IBM to provide guidance in developing a distributed system and recruited teams of experienced systems managers and outside consultants to support him. A 26 unit ATM system was tested by the Santa Clara branch and, when it proved to be successful, the ATM program was rolled out statewide. In 1981, 18 months into both projects, Clausen left to head the World Bank and Samuel H. Armacost replaced him as CEO.

## Hopper and Armacost

Armacost was forced to conduct the bank's strategic battle by fighting on two fronts. Worldwide banking was in a credit crisis and U. S. banking, after years of political battles, was finally in the process of deregulation. These events revealed the fact that the Bank of America's loan and credit portfolios were mismatched and its decentralized organization was ill prepared to meet demands for rapid response. $^{26}$ And so, Armacost had to realign the bank's global portfolio while, at the same time, reestablish its position in information technology. This task was made even more complicated when Mickel left to start a telecommunications business, forcing Armacost to reevaluate the bank's entire systems strategy. Max Hopper was recruited from American Airlines to restore momentum in retail banking and, drawing on lessons learned in a distant past, a senior retail banking advisory committee was established. $^{27}$ Armacost and Hopper met almost daily to discuss the bank's competitive position in systems and within a few months a vision for the future emerged. Hopper recruited two key new systems executives: Bruce Fadem from IBM Systems to run retail banking and Joe Ervin to head operations and technology. Hopper, Fadem, and Ervin joined with Peter Hill to develop an overall architecture, which for the first time in over a decade was discussed, frequently and openly, with an expanded advisory committee.

Armed with the results of the architecture studies, Hopper initiated the development of a high capacity, bank-wide communications system and centralized policy designed to create an overall bank view. Recognizing that enormous investments would now be required to bring the bank's structure up to competitive standards, Hopper initiated two major system development projects: BankAmerica Systems Engineering (BASE) and BankAmerica Payments Systems (BAPS). These were to be the essential architecture elements for the bank's future. As a member of the bank's management committee, Hopper was also involved in discussions that detailed how these systems were essential for establishing bank-wide standards and for supporting the eventual integration of information and payments. Armacost and Hopper recruited Tom Cooper from Mellon Bank to head up the worldwide rollout of BAPS for the International Division. An early proponent of ATMs and an experienced computer user, Cooper had also established a reputation as a cost-oriented banker. Together they developed a program to integrate the bank overseas and to expand its product line using BAPS.

During 1983, however, the Bank of America experienced a series of earnings declines and technological expansion again became unpopular as demands for stringent cost cutting returned. Cooper, pressed to reduce system costs, felt compelled to slow down the transition to BAPS. Hopper believed that timing was crucial and continued to push for the rollout. When Armacost sided with Cooper, Hopper returned to American Airlines and was replaced by Lou Mertes of SeaFirst of Seattle, a Bank of America subsidiary.

## Mertes and Armacost

Armacost and Cooper immediately laid out Mertes' charge: control systems costs, establish a business-oriented planning process, and develop a bottom-up planning and budgeting process to provide an economically sound basis upon which the management committee could evaluate systems investments. Mertes streamlined and reduced the size of the systems organization while emphasizing a detailed, analytical economic planning discipline as the core of systems planning. But his program was barely taking shape when he left a year later, due to yet another series of systems failures. Hill and Fadem became acting managers of IT.

## Simmons and Clausen

Clausen returned to the bank in 1986 and again assumed personal responsibility for restoring the bank's leadership position in IT and he moved quickly. His search for a strong IT manager produced Mike Simmons from Fidelity, who then launched an aggressive worldwide program to integrate electronic banking in credit and retail. To manage wholesale banking, he hired Dick Griffith, from the Federal Reserve, who brought Bill Ott, the architect of the Federal Reserve system, with him. With this new team, which possessed more than 100 years of systems development experience, and the competent technical managers Mickel and Hopper had hired previously, the systems group regained the base of talent and skills it needed to again make a run for the lead.

Clausen and Simmons worked as a team. Simmons was charged with demystifying the technology and encouraging users to support expansion and he worked in tandem with Clausen to understand users' requirements better. Working together, they finally brought Hopper's vision to fruition and fully implemented BASE and BAPS. At the same time, the bank also became an early marketer of a personal computer-based treasurer's system. Simmons artfully described the impact of IT programs in customer terms and organized a user committee of senior managers that met regularly to review a portfolio of proposed projects and to establish priorities. $^{28}$ Importantly, he rejuvenated Hopper's experiments in future technologies and created a lab to test, among other products, a new smart card. Most significantly, his team produced reliable, money-making services and products on time and within budget.

The communications network that Hopper originally designed was finally completed. Additional economies of scale were sought by consolidating all of the diverse systems then in use at the four disparate sites into three specialized centers: all overseas systems were located in London (for Asia and Europe), national domestic accounts were housed in San Francisco, and the South American accounts domiciled in Los Angeles. This network was implemented on a hardware platform consolidated around a few vendors and run on software systems with consistent operating systems. The Bank of America now had, once again, a modern bank architecture.

## Stein and Rosenberg

Upon Clausen's retirement in 1992, Richard Rosenberg was named CEO. When Simmons subsequently moved on to First Boston, Rosenberg hired, as his new maestro, Marty Stein, from Paine Weber. Stein assumed responsibility for all of the bank's technological programs and was recently promoted to vice chairman. Stein and Rosenberg are now working feverishly to regain the lead in an industry that over 40 years ago their bank virtually created.
