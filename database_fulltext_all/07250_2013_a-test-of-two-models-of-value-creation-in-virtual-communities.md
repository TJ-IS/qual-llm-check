Abstrac t: Does a firm get any extra value from investing resources in sponsoring its own virtual community above and beyond the value that could be created for the firm, indirectly, via customer-initiated communities? If so, what explains the extra value derived from a firm-sponsored virtual community and how might this understanding inform managers about appropriate strategies for leveraging virtual communities as part of a value-creating strategy for the firm? We test two models of virtual community to help shed light on the answers to these questions. We hypothesize that in customer-initiated virtual communities, three attributes of member-generated information (MGI) drive value, while in firm-sponsored virtual communities, a sponsoring firm’s efforts, as well as MGI, drive value. Drawing on information search and processing theories, and developing new measures of three attributes of MGI (consensus, consistency, and distinctiveness), we surveyed 465 consumers across numerous communities. We find that value can emerge via both models, but that in a firm-sponsored model, a sponsor’s efforts are more powerful than MGI and have a positive, direct effect on the trust-building process. Our results suggest a continuum of value creation whereby firms extract greater value as they migrate toward the firm-sponsored model.

Key word s and p hrases: attribution theory, co-creation, online communities, online trust, user-generated content, virtual communities.

Virtual c ommunities “p rovid e unp rec ed ented op ortunities for individuals to interact and share information” [29, p. 68], which can be valuable for customers and the firms with whom they engage. Customers can learn about companies and their products, while firms can build brands, foster customer loyalty, gain marketplace insights, and enhance profitability [1, 3, 13, 18, 21, 44]. More specifically, favorable customer endorsements posted in a community could help existing and prospective customers understand whether a particular firm is trustworthy based on experiences shared by members. Such trust could motivate customers to share useful information with a firm (e.g., product needs, preferences) and, ultimately, with other individuals via positive word of mouth (WOM).

Although the influence of customer endorsements on customer trust has been embraced by scholars (e.g., [78]), it remains unclear whether firms are better off sponsoring their own customer communities to motivate trust-building endorsements and activities or whether customer communities should be viewed as sacred ground to be created and managed by customers themselves [67]. In other words, does a firm get any extra value from making direct investments in a firm-sponsored virtual community beyond the value that could emerge indirectly via customer-initiated communities? If so, what explains the extra value derived from a firm-sponsored virtual community, and how might this compel managers to operate differently as they attempt to strategically manage their virtual community? These are key questions to answer, given that firms could spend over \$1 million during the first three years following the launch of a community, including start-up and ongoing costs associated with technology and management of the community [70].

Customer endorsements have been conceptualized as member-generated information (MGI) [41] and, hereafter, we use MGI to refer to such endorsements. Customers often get recommendations or opinions about firms from others in order to “reduce the potential risk associated with a purchase decision” [43, p. 26]. These opinions or recommendations offer experiences and evaluations that can directly affect the consumer’s trust in a particular firm.

In this study, we explore trust as a critical mediator of value creation in virtual communities and find evidence that supports an evolutionary strategy where firms could garner sufficient levels of trust initially via customer-initiated virtual communities and then migrate toward their own firm-sponsored virtual communities where they could garner significantly greater levels of customer trust as a mediator of value. Specifically, in customer-initiated virtual communities, we explore the influence of MGI on trust by surveying individuals who are visitors to the community who often seek, assess, and rely on such endorsements when they are in the midst of deciding whether to make a future purchase from the firm (see Model 1, Figure 1).

Alternatively, in firm-sponsored virtual communities, we explore the influence of MGI on trust by surveying individuals who are members of the community—not visitors. Members are presumed to read MGI while they continue to learn about the sponsor and extend their relationship with the sponsor, via a future transaction, for example. However, over time, they also learn about the sponsoring firm from their own interpretation of a sponsor’s efforts to manage the community. Thus, in addition to the role of MGI, in firm-sponsored virtual communities, we also explore the influence of a sponsor’s efforts on members who learn about a community sponsor via direct contact with the firm (see Model 2, Figure 2).

Finally, in both types of community, we measure value as a customer’s willingness to share personal information and spread positive WOM. Our outcome variables represent trust-based behaviors that could take place inside or outside of either type of virtual community and, therefore, are not reflective only of “in-community” behaviors. In this way, we cast virtual communities as entities that could become part of a larger multichannel marketing strategy.

Through the design and execution of our study, we find that while customer endorsements generate firm value, via trust, in both types of virtual community, the efforts that a firm makes toward customers in a firm-sponsored community have a greater influence on trust—a critical mediator of information-based value to a firm. Rather than focus on an “either/or” choice about which type of community is optimal, our findings suggest that a “both/and” paradigm is more pragmatic, since our findings suggest that one or the other type of community might be valuable for firms under different resource and marketplace conditions. Indeed, some firms have chosen to use both types of virtual community to facilitate value creation with consumers [87]. As we explain later, our study lays the foundation for future researchers to explore a new paradigm about strategic management of virtual communities. Underpinning this paradigm is the notion that there is a right time and place for firms to leverage customer-initiated and firm-sponsored communities.

To summarize, our findings help resolve the tension felt by managers who have difficulty determining which type of virtual community is best. Indeed, we identify the similarities and differences in both the processes and outcomes that create value in both types of community such that our findings shed light on alternative pathways to value. Specifically, by developing new measures of important attributes of consumer endorsements, our findings not only make significant theoretical contributions but also provide significant managerial insights regarding how managers proactively influence customer perceptions that would create value in either type of community.

## Theoretical Context

In the following sec tions, we p rovid e back ground information on four theoretical concepts that serve as the underpinnings of our conceptual model. We first define customer endorsements and the three main types of virtual communities. Then, we explain relevant processes of trust formation and attribution theory in the context of our study.

## What Are Customer Endorsements?

A customer endorsement is a positive opinionated comment offered by a virtual community member and made accessible to individuals via contact with the community [41, 78]. An endorsement might be viewed with suspicion in the absence of negative comments. However, there is substantial and growing evidence that the customer opinions most likely processed online would be those that are positive about a firm and that negative comments would have a lesser effect on a consumer’s perceptions. Based on recent research [16], this effect would be magnified at later stages of a consumer’s information search and decision process, which is the focal context for our examination of the role of MGI in the trust formation process. While MGI could possibly influence a consumer at any stage of the information search process, in online environments, recent evidence suggests that MGI is most influential at later stages when information gathering and active evaluation of information lead to a decision (e.g., [17]).

Kuan and Bock [45] measured the influence of both positive and negative comments and found that positive comments have the stronger effect. This effect occurs because consumers often search online for customer comments in the final stages of their search for information, after forming a positive perception of the firm. At that point, negative comments would be less persuasive [47]. In fact, when consumers encounter negative comments at the final stage of search, more often than not they extend their search for additional expert information rather than immediately alter their positive perception [63].

Furthermore, the predominance of positive comments shared online coupled with a consumer’s already positive perception of a firm’s trustworthiness in the final stage of search suggests that a consumer would selectively expose him- or herself to information that reinforces his or her favorable perception, focusing on positive information and ignoring negative information about the firm [2, 31, 38]. Indeed, in the final stage of search, a consumer seeks information that will help him or her build confidence in a preference established earlier in the search process [32]. Many theories of consumer psychology support this view. For example, Tversky and Kahneman [83] established that a consumer’s judgment follows an anchoring and adjustment process, where initial preferences serve as psychological anchors that are weighted disproportionately in a consumer’s final judgment. Likewise, Kunda [46] suggested that a consumer engages in so-called motivated reasoning, where the consumer processes new information in a way that supports his or her current thinking. Such hope-driven reasoning leads a consumer not only to selectively expose him- or herself to sources that provide confirmative information but also to perceive such information as highly credible [19, 20].

## A Practical Classification of Virtual Communities

While there is no consensus regarding the definition or appropriate typology to classify virtual communities, we draw on the literature to define a virtual community as an aggregation of individuals who interact around a shared interest, where the interaction is at least partially supported by technology and guided by norms [41, 73]. Consistent with Kannan et al.’s [41] classification, there are three main types of virtual community: (1) third-party managed, (2) firm sponsored, and (3) customer initiated. Below, we offer basic profiles of each type of virtual community in order to clarify the domain of the research phenomenon.

Third-party-managed virtual communities are owned and managed by firms that connect members for the exchange of information (e.g., Epinions; www.epinions. com) or products (e.g., eBay; www.ebay.com) [14, 18, 69]. Customer-initiated virtual communities are organized by individual members in order to interact around a shared product/brand interest such as coffee consumption [44] or a particular brand of automobile [53]. Members typically establish an independent Web site or establish the community on a hosting service (e.g., Yahoo! Groups) because customer-initiated communities are not affiliated formally with the firms that are the focus of community interest. Finally, a firm-sponsored virtual community is organized by a single firm, and often embedded on a firm-owned Web site, in order to foster relationships with customers [74] or facilitate peer support and service [55].

While many information systems (IS) researchers have explored third-party-managed virtual communities (typically hosted by a neutral organizational entity), none have explored both customer-initiated and firm-sponsored communities comparatively, despite the number of consumers participating in both types and the financial investment that many firms have made in sponsoring their own communities. This gap in the current research is noteworthy given that these two types of communities are of equal, if not greater, interest to managers today as compared to third-party-managed communities. Indeed, customer-initiated communities signify organic interest in a firm that could be important to leverage strategically because information sourced from other customers is often perceived to be more credible than information produced by the firm itself [28]. Alternatively, firm-sponsored communities, albeit more resource intensive, offer firms the opportunity to create direct relational bonds with customers. Understanding how trust forms and value is created in both types of community and whether either type of community fosters greater levels of trust remains elusive to managers and researchers. Therefore, the findings of our study have greater generalizability than prior research and offer useful managerial insights to a greater number of managers who operate in different customer, product, or industry settings. Indeed, Zwass [87] suggested that distinguishing customer-initiated virtual communities from those that are firm sponsored in empirical studies is important because it helps to contextualize research findings appropriately.

## The Bases of Trust: Trust Processes

The decision to trust a firm and the subsequent willingness to act based on trust are risky behaviors. Thus, individuals engage in significant cognitive processes in order to reduce risk by understanding the basis on which they should trust a firm [36, 56, 62]. A risk avoider who believes that a firm’s behavior is motivated by rational goals would want to process information about the firm in question and assess trust based on one or more trust processes (see [43]).

The transference process of trust formation operates when an individual has insufficient direct experience on which to base a decision to trust a firm [23, 43, 78]. The process is signified by an individual who relies on information provided by individuals who are knowledgeable about the firm and perceived to share similar circumstances in order to assess the firm’s trustworthiness. In the context of individuals seeking information in a virtual community, trust would transfer from members to information seekers, based on their shared identity as consumers who seek risk-reducing information in the form of customer endorsements [78].

Essentially, trust transfers from those who are knowledgeable about and trust the firm to the individual who needs more information on which to make his or her own decision about whether to trust the firm [78, 81]. The trust transference process requires that an individual be able to identify those who are knowledgeable about the firm, often via an established network such as a virtual community of customers who are known to have direct experience in dealing with the firm [22]. Indeed, trust transference works best when faith among people is high, such that the trustworthiness of the individuals providing the information should enhance the trust transfer process [23, 78].

There are three processes of trust formation—prediction, intentionality, and capability—that emerge based on an individual’s ability to assess a firm’s trustworthiness based on his or her own prior experiences with the firm rather than information provided by others. Each trust process enables an individual to assess a critical dimension of trust (i.e., integrity, benevolence, and capability). For example,

•	 the prediction process of trust results from an individual’s belief that he or she can forecast a firm’s future actions based on the integrity reflected by a firm’s prior actions;

•	 the intentionality process of trust results from an individual’s belief that he or she can accurately interpret the benevolence reflected in a firm’s prior actions that are helpful to customers;

•	 the capability process of trust results from an individual’s belief that he or she can assess a firm’s basic competency reflected in the firm’s prior actions that demonstrate the ability to meet obligations to customers.

Because each of the aforementioned processes require an individual to interpret directly a firm’s actions, in our study, these processes are presumed to be dominant in firm-sponsored virtual communities and triggered by the visible, direct efforts that a firm makes when interacting with members of the community.

## Attribution Theory

Attribution theory has been used sp ec ific ally to exp lain how individuals interpret consumer conversations holistically (e.g., [46]). Based on this theory, three attributes of MGI are assessed by information seekers in a virtual community: information consensus, information consistency, and information distinctiveness [42]. Each attribute is conceptualized distinctly, although these three attributes are evaluated holistically by an information seeker.

Information consensus gives the information seeker interpretive information about whether MGI suggests that opinions are widely held across the community membership. We conceptualize information consensus as the extent to which a consumer perceives agreement among member opinions about a particular firm’s behavior. In the context of determining whether to trust a firm, an information seeker would attempt to answer the following: Does the MGI in this community suggest that most customers would endorse the firm as trustworthy? If so, then the individual is likely to formulate a perception that is in harmony with the MGI.

Information consensus gives the information seeker interpretive information about the variety of circumstances that surround a firm’s behavior. We conceptualize information consistency as a measure of the extent to which a consumer perceives that member opinions suggest that a firm’s behaviors are the same, across time and across situations with different consumers. In the context of determining whether to trust a firm, an information seeker would attempt to answer the following: Does the MGI in this community suggest that the firm is trustworthy with different customers in a variety of situations? If so, then an individual would be more likely to think the MGI suggests that the firm would behave similarly when dealing with them in their particular situation.

Finally, information distinctiveness gives the information seeker interpretive information about members themselves, regarding whether members sufficiently differentiate between this firm and other firms when creating MGI. We conceptualize information distinctiveness as the extent to which a consumer perceives that member opinions are descriptive of a particular firm, and not descriptive of all firms, in general. In the context of determining whether to trust a firm, an information seeker would attempt to answer the following: Does the MGI in this community suggest that customers would endorse the firm as trustworthy because of their unique experiences with this firm as compared to other firms, in general? If so, then the individual would be more likely to believe that the MGI reflects this firm’s extraordinary behavior, above that of competing firms.

In a virtual community, a consumer encounters an abundance of MGI and must find a way to holistically interpret the information provided by numerous community members. In this way, we cast the process of interpreting MGI as a sensemaking process [71, p. 260], whereby an individual will assess not only the substance of MGI but also the attributes of the MGI that help them interpret the information holistically. Furthermore, attribution theory suggests that an individual will observe and interpret not only the individual attributes of MGI (consensus, consistency, and distinctiveness) but also the pattern of the MGI holistically.

Stated differently, attribution theory suggests that if an individual perceives MGI to be high on each of the three attributes, then that individual would be able to interpret the MGI as unambiguously descriptive of the firm in question [46, 82]. When all three information attributes are high, an information seeker is most likely to form a perception that MGI reflects the behavior of the firm in question, rather than an idiosyncratic result based on particular circumstances or people. For example, if consensus were high but consistency were low (i.e., member ratings were situation dependent), then an information seeker may not be certain that a firm could be trusted in his or her particular consumption situation. Alternatively, if consensus were high but distinctiveness were low (i.e., member ratings for the firm in question were no different than ratings for other firms mentioned in MGI), then an information seeker may not be certain that the MGI does not simply reflect the opinions of less-discriminating consumers.

Regarding attribution theory, we advance knowledge in the following way. We take a new methodological approach that enables us to test the robustness of attribution theory in the managerially relevant environment on online customer conversations. While previous researchers experimentally (in a lab setting) manipulated three information attributes [42], we developed three new, multimeasures of these attributes and tested our new measures with real consumers. Our approach enabled us to explore the power of attribution theory and reveal the significant role that information attributes play in the trust formation process.

## Model Development

Trust and attribution theory c ollec tively suggest that in order to reduce risk and increase confidence about a pending decision to trust a firm, an individual often seeks and evaluates information provided by a variety of sources [2, 31, 48, 64]. Yet the information search process has different stages that occur over time.

## The Multistage Information Search Process

We contend that the role of MGI in fostering a consumer’s trust in a firm is based on a consumer’s assessment of MGI in the final stage of an information search and decision process. Seeking and evaluating MGI in a virtual community is a multistage, information search and decision process. In each stage of the process, a consumer evaluates information provided by a variety of sources, based on the consumer’s belief that these sources could provide information to help him or her make a final, consumption-related decision, such as whether to trust a firm [37, 64]. While the number of stages and types of information sources used varies for each consumer, most researchers agree that there are at least three fundamental stages—initial, intermediate, and final—and that particular types of information sources are relied upon in each stage [12, 19, 64, 75].

In the initial stage, a consumer uses information provided by marketer-controlled sources (e.g., advertising, sales brochures, salespeople) that provide information that a consumer perceives as valuable [75]. However, marketer-controlled information often is perceived to be potentially biased in favor of the firm [7, 12]. In the intermediate stage, a consumer obtains information from personal sources (e.g., friends, family members) whose information is perceived to be useful as well as trustworthy, relevant, and easy to access [64]. Importantly, information from such personal sources often is perceived to be less biased than the marketer-controlled sources accessed in the initial stage. At the end of the intermediate stage, if a consumer’s perception of a firm’s trustworthiness remains favorable, the consumer enters the final stage, where he or she seeks information from so-called weak-tie sources who are less personal but highly knowledgeable about the firm in question [9, 64].

In the final stage, a consumer can locate many expert sources of information in virtual communities [19, 27, 63]. In fact, in this final stage, online consumer opinions significantly influence a consumer’s decision-making process (e.g., [11, 17]). As discussed earlier, information gathered in the final stage corroborates the consumer’s already favorable perception of a firm. The decision-making process ends with the consumer reaching a final decision, for example, to believe that a firm is worthy of trust. In our study, we explore the effect of MGI on individual consumers in either type of community (i.e., firm sponsored or customer initiated) who are in the final stages of search and considering whether to trust a particular firm, not all potential firms.

## How MGI Influences Customer Trust

Since consumers make psychological decisions to trust others, we contend that individuals cognitively process, or assess, information to help with their decision to trust a firm. Thus, we posit that individuals will assess the consensus, consistency, and distinctiveness of MGI in virtual communities in order to assess the trustworthiness of a particular firm. After reading MGI in a virtual community, an individual would attempt to assess whether the information accurately reflects the trustworthiness of a particular firm. This holistic processing of MGI is consistent with an attributiontheoretic perspective of information processing and sensemaking [71, p. 260]. As stated earlier, attribution theory explains how consumers interpret externally sourced information in order to formulate beliefs about others, including firms [24, 61]. Based on this theory, three attributes of MGI are assessed by information seekers in a virtual community: information consensus, information consistency, and information distinctiveness [42].

In sum, we posit that, in a customer-initiated virtual community, once a consumer has become favorable toward trusting a firm during the earlier stages of information search, that consumer is likely to transition into the final stage of search wherein (1) he or she will find an abundance of positive information online offered by community members, in the form of MGI, and (2) his or her preference toward trusting a firm will reduce the persuasiveness of negative comments and increase his or her confidence about his or her ultimate decision to trust a firm. Likewise, in a firm-sponsored virtual community, a consumer who has some prior experience with the sponsor as a member of the community will not only use his or her prior perception of a sponsor’s efforts to assess the trustworthiness of a sponsor but will also engage in a similar information search and evaluation process that culminates with the assessment of MGI, especially when he or she is in the midst of a decision regarding whether to continue in a trustbased relationship with the sponsor (e.g., repatronage from the sponsor within a new product category). Thus, we posit that in both types of virtual community, a consumer’s evaluation of MGI, conceptualized as positive customer endorsements, will have a positive effect on a customer’s trust in a firm:

Hypothesis 1: The higher the consensus of MGI, the higher an individual’s trust in that firm.

Hypothesis 2: The higher the consistency of MGI, the higher an individual’s trust in that firm.

Hypothesis 3: The higher the distinctiveness of MGI, the higher an individual’s trust in that firm.

We expect MGI in both types of virtual community to increase customer trust in a firm (i.e., firms that are the focus of MGI in customer-initiated virtual communities, Model 1, and community sponsors that are the focus of MGI in firm-sponsored virtual communities, Model 2). However, in the interest of brevity, our hypotheses use the “firm” terminology for both types of community. Also, we omit any reference to “perceptions” in the wording of our hypotheses, but acknowledge that all of our constructs represent perception-based variables.

## The Relative Effect of MGI Versus a Sponsor’s Efforts on Trust Formation (Model 2 Only)

We conceptualize trust in a firm as a belief about the firm’s benevolence, integrity, and judgment, and these beliefs are based on the outcomes of various cognitive-based trust processes [22, 56, 79]. In online environments, customer endorsements facilitate trust in a firm via the transference process, when an individual relies on knowledgeable sources of information to determine whether to trust a firm [23, 51]. In both types of virtual community, we posit that trust emerges via the transference process, when an individual interprets MGI while in the midst of a decision about trusting a firm.

However, in a firm-sponsored community, members have access to not only MGI, which facilitates trust via the transference process, but also their direct and repeated experiences with a sponsor, which facilitates trust via (1) the intentionality process (2) the prediction process, and (3) the capability process [22, 23, 50]. Few studies have tested the relative strength of trust formed via the transference process and other process that entail direct experience. However, in a study relying on the cultural dimensions of individualism and collectivism [34], Kim [43] found that the transference process of trust is more powerful in collective cultures than in individualistic cultures. Our study was conducted with a U.S.-based (individualistic) sample. While Kim’s findings do not suggest that the transference trust process is insignificant in individualistic cultures, they at least indicate that trust formed via the transference process would be weaker than trust formed via perceptions based on direct experience/interactions among our respondents. Further, Kim’s study was set in an online shopping context, with a focus on how consumer trust in a firm is fostered in an online retail environment. Thus, we hypothesize as follows:

Hypothesis 4: In a firm-sponsored virtual community, the relationships between the sponsor effort variables and customer trust in a sponsoring firm are stronger than the relationships between customer endorsement and customer trust (i.e., H1, H2, H3) in a sponsoring firm (Model 2 only).

## The Influence of a Sponsor’s Efforts on MGI (Model 2 Only)

When a community sponsor makes efforts to provide access to quality content, foster member embeddedness, and encourage interaction among members, members benefit because these efforts help them to fulfill intrinsic needs, such as the need for information, belongingness, and social interaction [21]. Such efforts also are a form of relationship-marketing investment that could alter a consumer’s perception of a firm and, in turn, motivate the consumer to reciprocate with his or her own gratitude-based behavior [62, 68, 84] such as making positive comments about a firm (e.g., creating MGI) [80]. Furthermore, the influence of a firm’s extra efforts on a consumer’s behavior is strongest when such efforts are perceived to be both controllable by the firm and beneficial for consumers rather than for the firm [62, 81].

It is expected that a sponsor’s efforts to provide access to quality content, foster member embeddedness, and encourage interaction among members motivate members to provide endorsements about the firm because the sponsor’s effort are perceived to be controllable by the firm and beneficial for members, rather than for the firm. Specifically, we contend that more members would endorse the firm (leading to higher consensus of MGI), customer endorsements would be infused with a variety of personal examples of the sponsor’s efforts (leading to higher consistency of MGI across time and situations), and the specificity of endorsements would be directly related to the sponsoring firm, rather than reflect generally favorable comments about all firms (leading to higher distinctiveness). Thus, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 5: In a firm-sponsored virtual community, a sponsor’s efforts have a positive influence on the consensus, consistency, and distinctiveness of MGI.

## Valuable Outcomes for Firms in Both Types of Virtual Community (Model 1 and Model 2)

Trust motivates risk-taking behavior in relationships [16, 56]. Because trust is imbued with stable expectations regarding the appropriateness of a firm’s future behavior [38, 78], trust gives an individual a rationale reason to believe that taking risk with a firm would not result in detrimental outcomes [22, 50, 56, 62]. Sharing information with a firm (e.g., personal information, information about product needs), for example, is risky in that a consumer cannot control the information once it is given to the firm.

Likewise, sharing WOM information with other consumers is also risky in that, for example, it can jeopardize a consumer’s reputation with personal acquaintances.

Trust also motivates supportive, expressive acts such as citizenship behavior [57], which is defined as extra-role, relational behavior that is not prescribed, but undertaken voluntarily to serve the needs of another [16]. When an individual perceives that a firm cares about customers and shares their values, the individual is willing to make sacrifices to support the firm [38]. For example, a new/prospective customer might choose to share information with the firm (e.g., completing feedback surveys for customer service personnel) as an act of citizenship behavior in the sense that it takes extra time to give such extra information to a firm. Also, sharing WOM information with other consumers about the firm could be an act of citizenship behavior in that voluntarily providing customer endorsements is an expressive, extra effort that assists a firm in its promotional efforts.

Taken together, we contend that trust facilitates information-based value to a firm, where value is measured in the form of two important customer behaviors: willingness to share personal information with the firm and willingness to share WOM information about the firm with other consumers. In this study, willingness to share personal information is conceptualized as a customer’s proclivity for giving personal information to a firm. Furthermore, we conceptualize WOM as behavior in which a customer makes others aware that he or she does business with a firm and recommends the firm to others [10, 28]. We expect trust to motivate such behaviors from consumers since risk taking and citizenship are at the core of such behaviors:

Hypothesis 6: The greater an individual’s trust in a firm, the greater the consumer’s willingness to share personal information with a firm.

Hypothesis 7: The greater an individual’s trust in a firm, the greater a consumer’s willingness to share positive WOM information about the firm with other consumers.

## Sampling Procedures

The design of our survey is consistent with our theoretical models, and was conducted online with the help of a professional research firm. Although we leveraged the professional expertise of the research firm, we controlled every aspect of the design of the survey, sampling procedures, and data collection processes. The research firm has direct access to several panels of consumers who are members of virtual communities sponsored by a variety of firms. The firm was selected to ensure that our sample was reflective of the population to which our study should generalize and to facilitate the ease with which respondents would agree to participate, since these individuals previously agreed to participate in surveys distributed by the professional research firm. The firm delivered the survey and aggregated responses. The respondents for both empirical models were sourced from the same pool of individuals who were asked to reference a single virtual community—not multiple communities—as they completed the survey.

The respondents for the customer-initiated virtual communities (Model 1) are selfidentified as individuals who had visited such a community to seek and assess MGI at a time when they were in the midst of making a decision about whether to trust a firm in a potential, future transaction. Because these respondents had only visited the community in search of information, but had no prior relationship with a firm, they were aligned with our desire to test the effect of the transference process of trust in customer-initiated virtual communities. The transference process suggests that individuals rely on knowledgeable sources to assess whether to trust another party because the potential trustor has little direct experience on which to base the decision.

Alternatively, the respondents for our firm-sponsored virtual communities (Model 2) are self-identified as members of such communities. These respondents would have had direct, observable experience with the community sponsor, which is aligned with our focus on the multiple processes of trust formation in firm-sponsored virtual communities. Indeed, the direct experience that a member has with a sponsor is the foundation for intentionality, prediction, and capability processes of trust formation. Yet, as discussed earlier, we also assess the influence of MGI (from other members) as a test of the transference process of trust formation in firm-sponsored virtual communities. To do so, we designed our survey to prompt the members to recall a time when they were in the midst of a future purchase decision from their sponsor before answering survey questions related to MGI.

Finally, because MGI is conceptualized as customer endorsements or positive comments along the informational attributes of consistency, consensus, and distinctiveness, we employed two screening questions in the survey to assess the valence of consumer assessments of informational attributes. Specifically, we asked consumers to respond to “In general, the comments I read in this community were: positive/negative about the firm.” And we had a follow-up question: “What percentage of the comments that you read were positive/negative about the firm?” Our usable sample represents only customers who were aligned with our conceptualization of MGI. We pretested the survey with 229 respondents (124 for Model 1 and 105 for Model 2). The results of the pretest confirmed the reliability and validity of the measures and helped to identify areas to improve our screening process for respondent selection.

After the pretest, we conducted the main study, which yielded 231 total respondents for customer-initiated virtual communities and 234 total respondents for firm-sponsored virtual communities after a validation procedure to ensure data quality (the response rate was 23 percent). Some examples of firm-sponsored virtual communities in our data include Apple, Best Buy, Cannon, Dell, Ford, General Motors, Hewlett-Packard, IBM, Microsoft, Nikon, Nintendo, Target, Texaco, and Verizon. As suggested earlier, firm-sponsored virtual communities are organized by the individual firm whereas customer-initiated virtual communities are launched and funded by community members, independent of a single firm. For this reason, our sample includes data from some respondents who referred to a particular company’s firm-sponsored virtual community as well as data from others who referred the customer-initiated virtual communities focused on that same firm (e.g., Hewlett-Packard sponsors its own community yet customer-initiated communities that focus on Hewlett-Packard also exist).

All the survey items were sourced from the existing literature, with the exception of the MGI variables. We followed prescribed methods for scale development in order to develop the information consensus, information consistency, and information distinctiveness measures and generated items from a variety of sources in order to identify potential items for inclusion in the domain of each construct [15, 66]. More details on the scale development are available from the authors upon request.

We acknowledge that there might be differences among the various kinds of virtual communities, and employ the following control variables. First, we use a scale from the existing literature for the usability features of virtual communities [58] that captures the organization and ease of use of the virtual community. Second, we use a classification of the type of virtual community as focused on the good only or the service only or both the good and service. We collected demographic data from the respondents. We conducted several analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests to see if there were systematic differences in constructs based on demographics. We did not observe any significant difference (at a significance level of 0.05) among the variables along the dimensions of age, gender, income, and employment. In the interest of brevity, we do not report the results of these tests. Consequently, we did not incorporate any of the demographic variables in our estimation models. Finally, we analyzed the representativeness of our sample compared to established benchmarks. To do so, we compared the profile of the respondents of this study with data collected from the Pew Internet & American Life Project (Pew; www.pewinternet.org). Pew conducts original research that explores the impact of the Internet on various aspect of society, including individuals and business, and is considered a leading authoritative source on information such as the demographics of Internet users. Since our research focuses on consumers who are Internet users, rather than the general population of consumers that includes non-Internet users, we compared our respondents’ demographic profiles with that of the Pew statistics at the time of our data collection efforts. We found that the respondents from our sample were comparable to the typical users of the Internet, based on the Pew data set.

## Validation of Measures

The first step in content validity is to examine whether measurement items reflect the specific domain of the construct [33, 66], based on theory and the literature rather than statistical testing. The use of extant literature and expert opinion to evaluate measures may ensure content validity [15]. Since our measures were (1) based on prior literature and discussions with members of virtual communities as well as interviews with industry experts, or (2) developed based on prescribed procedures [15], and (3) pretested, we believe they satisfy the conditions for adequate content validity following the guidelines of Churchill [15] and Nunnally and Bernstein [66].

## Reliability and Unidimensionality

We present evidence of measurement reliability and unidimensionality. Cronbach’s α is above Nunnally and Bernstein’s [66] recommended threshold of 0.70. The next step of construct validation is establishing unidimensionality. Factor loadings from the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model as well as t-tests of their significance (at the 0.05 level) provide evidence of unidimensionality.

## Discriminant Validity

Within the CFA framework, we assessed discriminant validity by comparing pairs of constructs. For each pair, we compared two models, one with the correlation between constructs constrained to equal 1 and the other with an unconstrained model. A significantly lower chi-square value for the model with the unconstrained correlation, when compared with the constrained model, provides support for discriminant validity [83]. Based on these significance tests, the constructs used in our study demonstrated discriminant validity (at the 0.05 level).

## Confirmatory Model Fit

Consistent with Hu and Bentler [35], we estimated the CFA model based on various widely used measures of fit. The results for the CFA model were as follows: root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.042, normed fit index (NFI) = 0.93, standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) = 0.037, comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.94, goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.91, and adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) = 0.88. These results are indicative, in general, of a CFA measurement model that is a good fit to the data.

## Common Method Variance

Since common method variance can be a potential source of bias in survey research, we examined this issue in several ways. In addition to Harman’s one-factor test [70], we tested for common method variance based on partial correlations [72]. In the first step, we extracted the first factor from a principal factor analysis of all the constructs. In the second step, we included this factor as a control variable in the analysis, since this factor is assumed to be a good approximation of the common method bias. The addition of the factor score as a control variable did not significantly change the variation explained in the outcome variables, suggesting that common method variance is not a serious concern in our study.

## Difference Between Model 1 and Model 2: The Impact of a Sponsor’s Efforts

The central difference between Model 1 and Model 2, based on our hypotheses, is that the efforts of the sponsor play a role in influencing trust in the sponsor (Model 2). This is not the case in Model 1, since Model 1 reflects communities that are customer initiated, where there is no element of firm participation. In the next analysis, we examine the impact of a sponsor’s efforts (i.e., efforts to provide quality content, foster embeddedness, and encourage interaction) over and above the MGI variables.

Baltes et al. [4] provide evidence that examining the standardized beta weights can be a good way to assess the relative importance of predictors. Following their recommendation, in our study, the mean of the beta weights for all the effort variables is greater than the mean beta weights for the information variables, providing evidence in support of the dominant role of efforts of the sponsor over the MGI variables.

Also, from Table 2 we observe that five of the nine information variables are significant. Correspondingly, eight of the nine effort variables are significant, pointing to the dominant effect of the effort variables.

With our second empirical test of the relative impact of the effort variables versus the MGI variables, we examined the statistical difference between the two sets of relationships, since we observed that the parameter estimates for the majority of paths between the effort variables and the trust constructs were larger than the paths between the MGI variables and the trust constructs. We conducted empirical tests to compare significance of the relative size of the parameter estimates of the effort variables to the trust constructs versus the parameter estimates of the MGI variables to the trust constructs. These tests were based on examining the chi-square test of significant differences between models. The first model is one where the coefficients for the effort– trust paths are set equal to the coefficient for the MGI–trust paths. The second model relaxes this constraint. The difference in the chi-square value between these two models is an indication of the fit of one model over the other. Such tests were conducted for all possible combinations of pairs of paths and provided convincing support that the effort–trust paths were significantly stronger than the information–trust paths. The results of these empirical tests suggest that the effort variables have a stronger effect on trust than do the MGI variables, with only a few exceptions.

The combination of the hierarchical regression analysis, standardized beta weights, and the chi-square difference tests provided support for H4, that is, for firm-sponsored virtual communities, the effect of the effort variables on trust is stronger than the effect of the MGI variables.

## Alternate Model Specification

We first examined whether trust plays a significant mediating role in our empirical models. In testing structural equation models, Marsh [54] suggests comparing the research model to alternative models using various model fit criteria. Therefore, we estimated a structural model where we allowed the effort variables to have a direct effect on information sharing, WOM, and brand loyalty, but we also constrained the trust variables to have no effect on these variables. We conducted a chi-square differences test between these two models and observed that the model with trust mediating the relationship between the effort variables and the outcome variables has a significantly (at the 0.01 level) better fit than the unmediated model. This result was also confirmed using other model fit criteria discussed earlier. Thus, we believe that trust has a significant mediating role, even in the presence of a direct effect of the efforts of the sponsor on information sharing, WOM, and brand loyalty. Finally, we conducted tests to assess the robustness of our results to the presence of additional control variables: members’ prior experiences/knowledge of the firm [60] and members’ expertise [5, 65]. We found our main results to be robust to the addition of the additional control variables.

Next, we examined the moderating role of members’ trustworthiness (Model 1 and Model 2). When an individual considers MGI as input to a decision, he or she needs to assess collectively the trustworthiness of the members who provide the information upon which the individual relies [19, 77]. Indeed, the transference process of trust requires that information sources are trustworthy [23]. Trust often emerges via the transference process based on an individual’s perceived similarity to an information source or perceptions of the source’s knowledge base, which enhances the perceived value and influence of that source’s information [30, 59]. For an individual in the midst of a decision-making process, an information source’s breadth of knowledge also enhances the perceived value of that source’s information [85, 87]. Furthermore, empirical data suggests that perceptions of members’ trustworthiness have a significant effect on information processing and use in virtual communities [7, 12, 86]. Thus, we expect members’ trustworthiness to moderate the trust formation process in both types of virtual community. Based on a latent variable interaction modeling [39, 76], we find evidence for a strong moderating effect of members’ trustworthiness. In this case, the results indicate that members’ trustworthiness had a moderating effect on the relationships between the MGI variables and trust (at the 0.05 level). The results for both models, customer initiated (Model 1) and firm sponsored (Model 2), are similar.

## Discussion

Managing c ustomer relationship s online is strategically important for firms, and virtual communities can be instrumental creating firm value. We find that trust-based relationships between firms and customers can emerge via both customer-initiated and firm-sponsored virtual communities and that MGI is a significant driver of trust in both types of community. Yet, in firm-sponsored virtual communities, we find that a sponsor’s efforts are a more powerful driver of trust, influencing trust directly and via MGI. Interestingly, we also find that different dimensions of trust drive different information-based customer behaviors in each type of virtual community.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our findings make several theoretical contributions, including (1) providing empirical support for emergent and established scholarly paradigms about the dimensionality of trust and trust formation processes, (2) advancing scholarly knowledge about the role of attribution theory in explaining changes in consumer perceptions of firms, and (3) offering new, empirically developed and validated measures of information attributes that are generalizable and provide a platform for future researchers seeking to understand how a large and relevant population of consumers process information in online environments. Below we elaborate on our theoretical contributions.

## Trust Dimensions

Our findings provide empirical support for an emergent scholarly paradigm that suggests that different dimensions of trust have different effects on a trustor’s behavior, based on the stage of a relationship, but heretofore, empirical evidence was scant.

Scholars that subscribe to this paradigm suggest, for example, that perceptions of benevolence can have a significant influence on customer behavior at any stage of the firm–customer relationship. Consistent with this notion, findings from our study indicate that the benevolence dimension of trust is significantly related to valuable customer behaviors at the initial relationship stage (via a customer-initiated virtual community) and during the later relationship stages (via firm-sponsored virtual communities).

Interestingly, we find that the judgment and integrity dimensions of trust are influential trust builders at different relationship stages. As discussed earlier, judgment reflects the “can/do” dimension of trust while integrity reflects the “will/do” dimension. As such, judgment should have greater influence at the initial stage of a customer– firm relationship, but integrity should have a greater influence at later stages, when a trustor has more confidence in a trustee, based on the trustee’s past behavior. Indeed, we find that judgment has a significant influence on an individual’s actions toward a firm with which they have no previous relationship before seeking MGI (i.e., in a customer-initiated virtual community) and that integrity exerts no significant influence on the behavior of these individuals. Also, we find that when members have first-hand knowledge of a community sponsor’s characteristics, over the course of an ongoing relationship (i.e., in firm-sponsored virtual communities) the integrity dimension of trust has a significant influence on an individual’s behavior but the influence of the judgment dimension of trust is muted—influencing only one of the two customer behaviors tested in our model.

## Trust Processes

Our findings provide empirical support for a well-established paradigm of the role of various trust formation processes, particularly in online environments. In comparing the trust formation processes across the two types of virtual community, we break ground in understanding the relevance and power transference trust as compared to the trust formed via a more complex set of processes that often requires significant, direct efforts on the part of a trustor. With the exception of one study (see [43]), previous researchers mostly have speculated about the various trust processes, building robust conceptualizations without empirical testing. Although we do not test each process individually, the design of our study enables us to isolate the trust process of greatest interest in a business environment ripe with consumer opinions online (i.e., trust transference) from other processes that require direct and deeper investments from firms as they interact with consumers (e.g., prediction, intentionality, and capability processes). Our findings should inspire future researchers to better understand how various trust processes operate individually and collectively to influence consumer behavior.

## Attribution Theory

Our findings advance scholarly knowledge about the role of attribution theory in explaining changes in consumer perceptions of firms in the emergent and important contextual phenomenon of virtual communities. Traditionally, researchers have focused on using attribution theory to understand how consumers interpret information presented to them via conventional channels of communication that often are controlled by marketers (e.g., traditional mass media advertising). However, since the advent of digital communications technology platforms and the concomitant rise of consumer-controlled communications, scholars have been called to reexamine how established theories of information processing explain consumer information processing in this interactive environment consisting of information generated by and for consumers.

We rely on attribution theory to explore consumer information processing in virtual communities in two dominant forms of virtual communities: customer initiated and firm sponsored. Grounded by attribution theory, however, our findings reveal the importance of measuring each of the three dimensions of information attributes and then exploring how they differentially influence a consumer’s trust formation process, depending on the type of community in which the information is processed. Because we test the role of consumer attributions in two different community environments, we were able to demonstrate empirically how the presence of other consumers (i.e., members’ trustworthiness in both types of community) or the presence of a sponsoring firm (i.e., a sponsor’s efforts in a firm-sponsored community) alters the strength of the effect of MGI on a consumer’s trust differently, based on the type of community in which the information is processed. Previous researchers could not uncover such insights because, for the most part, they tested the role of attribution theory in explaining consumers’ information processing in the absence of other consumers or firm-controlled efforts.

## Measurement Development and Testing

We developed three new multi-item measures of MGI. In doing so, we not only reveal the significant role of MGI in the trust formation process but also lay the groundwork for future research in the relevance of attribution theory to consumer processes of opinionated information in online spaces. Although much of the prior research on attribution theory has been conducted in a laboratory setting, the volume of information produced by consumers online makes our field setting and survey data more appropriate for conducting our research as well as for future studies.

In addition, our new measures allow us to uncover results that help academicians to place the findings of previous researchers in a richer theoretical context, relative to the role of attribution theory. For example, many have found that attributes of an information source significantly influence an individual’s cognitive response (e.g., [25]), which has led academicians and managers to focus on how to generate trust cues in online environments. While we confirm this result via our finding of the importance of members’ trustworthiness in the trust formation process, our newly developed measures allow us to explain how the attributes of the information itself, in addition to the attributes of an information source, can affect an individual’s response (i.e., trust in a firm).

## Generalizability of Results

Our findings are more generalizable than those of previous studies that focus on customer communities. First, our study includes members of many different virtual communities across two dominant types of community. Second, we explicitly test the effect of moderating and control variables that are relevant to the trust formation process in virtual communities and offer new theoretical insights about trust formation in virtual communities. For example, we find that members’ trustworthiness is a significant moderator into the trust formation process, that our model of trust formation is robust across both good and service providers, and that both MGI and the efforts of a firm are significant in building trust above and beyond the trust-building effect of individuals perceptions about the usability of the design of a virtual community. Taken together, these findings suggest that our model is nomologically rich and generalizable, offering meaningful theoretical contributions that serve as a platform for future research.

## Managerial Contributions

The role of information in driving customer trust and valuable, information-based behaviors sits at the core of our managerial contributions. First, we empirically link MGI to important consequences that differ from those explored by other researchers. Rather than explore the link between MGI and financial outcomes such as sales [14, 52], we focus on the important role of trust in linking MGI to valuable, informationbased variables: willingness to share personal information with a firm and willingness to share WOM information with other consumers.

Second, our results guide managers to take specific actions that could influence trust and create value via virtual communities—actions that extend beyond merely focusing on community-design usability. For example, in firm-sponsored virtual communities, because we find that members’ trustworthiness strengthens the effect of MGI on trust, managers should ensure that they include community features that foster trust among community members. Such design features support the overall “health” of the community and include features such as (1) individual or aggregated peer ratings, (2) community-wide behavioral policies, and (3) actively managed registration of new members, which helps to minimize membership turnover [26]. Including such features would be important particularly to service providers (e.g., providers of financial or medical services) whose customers perceive information quality to be essential but who also look to other consumers for trusted informational and social support in virtual communities [49]. Finally, our findings provide managers guidance regarding how to act proactively in ways that might influence MGI and create value via either type of virtual community. Below, we briefly illustrate the value of this guidance by giving attention to our unique findings related to both types of virtual community.

## Firm-Sponsored Virtual Communities

In firm-sponsored virtual communities, our findings help managers understand which elements of MGI they should try to influence if they hope to foster trust and create value via a community. For example, we find that trust is based on a customer’s assessment of the consistency and distinctiveness of MGI, more than the consensus of MGI about the firm. The implication is clear: While many firms have attempted to help consumers understand the degree of consensus among community-based postings (e.g., displaying summary “star” rating statistics), our findings suggest that these efforts are, at best, only moderately helpful in fostering trust among existing community members. Our findings uniquely encourage managers to prioritize efforts that would trigger perceptions about the consistency and distinctiveness dimensions of MGI in customer conversations. For example, to encourage perceptions of consistency, firms could focus on developing reliable customer management policies and business processes that are aligned across the organization (e.g., return policy, warranty management process).

Also, regarding the distinctiveness of MGI, firms could implement policies that would evoke perceptions among individuals that customers are treated in ways that are both unique (i.e., differentiated) and unrivaled by competitors. Such efforts would make the firm’s source of competitive advantage salient to individuals who are exposed to MGI. Effective strategies could include customizing communications, product offerings, and pricing as well as establishing best-in-class service standards. Overall, influencing the consistency and distinctiveness attributes of MGI should in turn motivate customers to reflect the firm’s efforts via MGI in the virtual community.

Finally, our finding that efforts of a sponsor have a greater influence on trust than MGI alone points to an evolutionary strategy for firms that seek to “harness the power of the Internet” to gain strategic advantage [8, p. 494]. Initially, firms with less market power and resources may be unable to draw a sufficient number of members to a sponsored community, but these firms could still benefit from the trust and value created by MGI in customer-initiated virtual communities. Later, a better-resourced firm could drive additional value by sponsoring its own virtual community, reaping greater benefits not only via MGI among its community members but also by appropriately managing content, embeddedness, and interaction within the community. In essence, once a firm matures, expands its base of customers, and establishes strong brand equity, managers should consider evolving toward investing in a firm-sponsored virtual community, where value is created not only via reputation effects but also via sponsor’s efforts to manage customer relationships directly through their own efforts. In this way, our findings reinforce the fact that firm-specific actions in online environments are essential to a firm’s success (e.g., [8]).

## Customer-Initiated Virtual Communities

Notwithstanding our strong findings regarding the value of firm-sponsored virtual communities, attempts to influence MGI indirectly, via customer-initiated communities, remain a worthy goal for some firms. In customer-initiated virtual communities, we find that individuals tend to build trust in a firm based on their assessment of all three dimensions of MGI. The consensus dimension of MGI is a unique and significant trust builder in these communities versus firm-sponsored virtual communities. Consensus information offered by members of a virtual community is a critical socio-informational trust cue that is effective, especially (1) when a consumer might be suspicious of an unknown firm and (2) when used in combination with other trust cues [6]. Our data suggest that high-consensus MGI (e.g., providing star-based ratings), in combination with MGI perceived as high in consistency and distinctiveness, constitutes a powerful combination of trust cues.

## Limitations and Areas for Future Research

Our study is not without limitations, yet these limitations offer interesting avenues for future research. First, while we had full control over the sampling procedure, we were unable to verify whether our respondents were actually active in the two types of communities that we examined. To resolve this issue, future researchers might consider collecting data from participants of the two types of virtual communities by directly approaching them for data collection within their community environment. Second, we do not consider the possibility that a firm’s value could be enhanced in a customer-initiated community by the firm’ efforts to directly interact with customers, notably because we conceptualize such effort to occur only in a firm-sponsored community. At the time of our data collection, such firm participation in a customerinitiated community was primarily covert, rather than overt, but increasingly, firms are becoming more transparent in dealing with customers and many encourage their employees to self-identify as a firm representative via interactions in those communities. Future researchers should explore this complexity within the customer-initiated community.

Despite limitations, our research paves the way for other researchers to explore the possibility that other situational variables could influence trust and value creation in virtual communities. For example, we explored the influence of perceived usability of the community Web site (as a control variable) on an individual’s trust in a firm, and Web site design is known to influence consumer perceptions. We encourage others to explore the trust-building effects of other potentially influential variables such as community or firm characteristics (e.g., community size, firm size, brand strength).

Finally, virtual communities are part of an emerging class of strategic, social informational technologies that sit at the critical interface between firms and customers, and earning consumer trust in such communities helps firms to create a renewable, information-based source of competitive advantage via proprietary information shared willingly by customers. Indeed, social media venues, such as virtual communities, are fast becoming an integral part of how customers choose to maintain relationships with the firms, but researchers are only at the early stages of explaining how managers might leverage an arsenal of such tools to create and sustain value for themselves and for the customers they serve. Notably, “consumers are no longer just passive value-takers, but have emerged as value-makers” [87, p. 12] as they co-create value with firms using tools such as virtual communities. For these reasons, our findings should inspire future researchers to build on our work by exploring the role of MGI in fostering trust-based relationships with customers, in various social media venues, as a pathway toward value creation for firms.
