# The effects of national culture values on consumer acceptance of e-commerce: Online shoppers in China

Cheolho Yoon

Dept. of Business Administration, Mokpo National University, 61 Dorim-ri, Chungkye-myun, Muan-gun, Chonnam 534-729, Republic of Korea

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 15 March 2008 Received in revised form 2 October 2008 Accepted 1 June 2009 Available online 7 June 2009

Keywords: E-commerce National culture TAM Hofstede Trust Cross-culture

## A B S T R A C T

E-commerce is now international, and thus national culture can affect the behavior of customers. But ecommerce studies related to national culture are limited. Our study explored the effect of national culture on consumer acceptance of e-commerce in China.

Our consumer e-commerce acceptance model considered the effect of culture on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, trust, and intention to use. We adopted Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term orientation) as moderators. Our results showed that uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation had moderate effects on the relationship between trust and intention to use and that masculinity also had a moderate effect on the relationship between perceived usefulness and intention to use and the relationship between perceived ease of use and intention to use. Contrary to expectations, power distance and individualism had no significant effect.

## 1. Introduction

E-commerce has rapidly proliferated, even in developing countries. However, though it is now global, e-commerce research has been conducted almost exclusively in advanced countries. Also, in spite of the fact that differences in national culture can affect customer behavior, most e-commerce research has ignored the effect of culture [5].

A few studies have focused on the effects of national culture on customer trust or perceived risk in e-commerce. Accordingly, our purpose was twofold. First to verify the consumer acceptance model of e-commerce developed by advanced countries’ researchers when applying it to a developing country. Second to explore the effects of national culture in the consumer e-commerce acceptance model that include trust and the variables of perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU). To achieve these objectives, we examined consumer acceptance of e-commerce in China with the research model adapted from Gefen et al.’s [7] study and incorporating Hofstede’s cultural dimensions as moderators in the model.

## 2. Conceptual background

## 2.1. National culture values and consumer behavior

Culture is the collective programming of the mind that distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another [8]. Hofstede had suggested that there were five work-related cultural dimensions along which countries differ [2]. These were:

\- Power distance (PDI): the degree to which the less powerful members of organizations accept that power is distributed unequally.

\- Individualism (IDV): the degree to which a society emphasizes the role of the individual.

\- Masculinity (MAS): the degree to which a society emphasizes traditional masculine values (such as competitiveness, achievement, and ambition), as opposed to others (such as nurturing, helping others, and valuing quality of life).

\- Uncertainty avoidance (UAI): the degree to which people feel threatened by uncertain, unstructured situations and ambiguity.

\- Long-term orientation (LTO): fostering of virtues oriented towards future rewards.

Although Hofstede used a work-related context and originally applied his framework to human resource management, it is now used increasingly in business and marketing studies. Suh and

Kwon [18] reported that consumers from different cultures had different attitudes, preferences, and values, and remained reluctant to buy foreign products even after considerable exposure to globalization. Therefore, differences in national culture can affect consumers’ behavior in e-commerce situations.

## 2.2. Customer acceptance of e-commerce

Early e-commerce researchers suggested that anxiety about security of their information, trust of the vendor, and personal privacy were antecedents to e-commerce acceptance. Consequently, many studies focused on trust as a precursor and were designed to find its antecedents and consequences [4,10]. Jarvenpaa and Tractinsky [9] empirically analyzed size and perceived reputation of an Internet store as antecedents of trust. Gefen showed that familiarity and disposition to trust influenced a customer’s trust, which, in turn, influenced purchases and inquiries. McKnight et al. empirically analyzed structural assurance (consumer perceptions of the safety of the web environment), perceived web vendor reputation, and perceived web site quality as antecedents of trust in a trust building model. Thus, most studies regarded trust as a proxy variable of e-commerce acceptance.

Gefen et al. argued that online shopping intentions were the product of both consumer assessments of the IT itself and trust in the online shopping mall. They proposed that PU and PEOU, together with trust were antecedents of e-commerce acceptance; they empirically tested this. Consequently, e-commerce studies proposed PU and PEOU together with trust as key drivers of ecommerce acceptance. Therefore, we employed Gefen et al.’s integrated model of trust as the basic model for our research.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

In order to explore the effects of national culture on consumer e-commerce acceptance, the research model included both the consumer e-commerce acceptance model together with variables of PU and PEOU and incorporated Hofstede’s cultural dimensions of PDI, IDV, MAS, UAI and LTO as moderators in the model. Fig. 1 shows our model.

Gefen et al.’s model integrated trust and PU and PEOU as main influencing variables of acceptance of IT. These variables have an effect on the intention to use, which in turn affects actual usage. Most studies use the intention to use as a dependent variable.

## 3.1. Consumer e-commerce acceptance model

Our model was based on Gefen et al.’s study. We included the following hypotheses, which were proposed and empirically tested there.

## 3.2. Power distance

In high PDI societies, superiors and subordinates consider each other as unequal and subordinates expect to be told what to do, while in low PDI societies, superiors and subordinates consider each other as equal and thus they are interdependent. This is a necessary condition for trust because it is a willingness to be vulnerable under conditions of risk. Therefore, we argue that low power distant societies will exhibit more interpersonal trust.

Customers from high PDI countries believe that a service provider will be more likely to engage in unethical behavior than customers from low PDI countries. Thus, customers from high PDI countries have less trust toward an online shopping mall than do customers from low PDI countries.

Hypothesis 7. The higher the degree of power distance (PDI), the lower the effect of trust on intention to use the online shopping mall.

## 3.3. Individualism

IDV is viewed on a high/low continuum, with collectivistic countries occupying the low end of the spectrum and individualistic nations at the high end. Because collectivistic societies emphasize strong relationships and interdependence, collectivists are sensitive to the in-group and out-group boundary [19]. They regard trust as a necessary condition for in-group membership and therefore, are less likely to trust someone who is outside their in-group. Unlike these societies, individualistic ones are characterized by universalism: members identify themselves with broader groups of society. Also, they are very good at meeting new people [20] and thus are generally more willing to rely on strangers and trust them.

Thus a collectivist may express less trust toward an online shopping mall than an individualist.

Hypothesis 8. The higher degree of individualism (IDV), the higher the effect of trust on intention to use in using the online shopping mall.

## 3.4. Masculinity

Srite and Karahanna [14] argued that because PU is closely related to achievement of work goals and advancement, the higher the degree of MAS, the higher the effect of PU on IT adoption. Conversely, because effort-free use is more concerned with creation of a pleasant and less frustrating work environment, and quality of work life concerns are typically feminine values, the lower the degree of MAS, the higher the effect of PEOU on IT adoption.

The online shopping mall is an IS based on the web and Internet, and online shopping intentions are the product of both consumer assessments of the IT itself and trust in the online shopping mall. Gefen et al. proposed PU and PEOU as the important variables in consumer e-commerce acceptance. Therefore, the online shopping mall would be affected by consumers’ perceptions about IT adoption.

Hypothesis 9. The higher degree of masculinity (MAS), the higher the effect of perceived usefulness on intention to use the online shopping mall.

Hypothesis 10. The higher degree of masculinity (MAS), the lower the effect of perceived ease of use on intention to use the online shopping mall.

## 3.5. Uncertainty avoidance

UAI is expected to be intimately associated with trust. It refers to the extent to which people feel threatened by uncertain, unstructured situations, and ambiguity. In high UAI cultures, people usually feel threatened by uncertain situations [3]. However UAI might increase perceived risk of online shopping; UAI and perceived risk may have similar effects on trust in online shopping.

In a study that examined the effects of trust and perceived risk on consumers’ willingness to purchase online, Stewart [16] considered perceived risk to be a moderating factor on the relation between consumers’ trust and their willingness to purchase products from Internet vendors. Therefore, trust would be likely to have less effect on people’s behavior in a high UAI culture.

Hypothesis 11. The higher the degree of uncertainty avoidance (UAI), the lower the effects of trust on intention to use the online shopping mall.

Uncertainty accepting societies are open to change and innovation, while UAI societies are characterized by conservatism, law, and order [15]. Therefore, people in uncertainty accepting societies are willing to try new products and technologies, whereas people in UAI societies tend to be hesitant toward new products and information, thus they were slower in introducing electronic communication tools [21].

UAI has been cited as influencing IT adoption. Straub et al. [17] suggested that the reason perceived usefulness did not have the expected effect on e-mail usage by Japanese workers was because of Japan’s high UAI culture. Png et al. [12] proposed that higher UAI countries were less likely to adopt an IT infrastructure (frame relay). Therefore, we also posited.

Hypothesis 12. The higher the degree of uncertainty avoidance (UAI), the lower the effect of perceived usefulness on intention to use the online shopping mall.

## 3.6. Long-term orientation

This involves the fostering of virtues oriented towards future rewards—in particular, perseverance and thrift. Future rewards are contingent assets that have not yet been secured. Therefore, an LTO society emphasizes behavior, such as thrift or perseverance, intended to secure future rewards. Generally, trust recognizes risk, such as vulnerability and/or uncertainty, about an outcome. People of a high LTO culture have strong beliefs that allow them to take risk during uncertainty. Also an LTO society encourages trust because the value of short-term gains from untrustworthy actions are reduced. Therefore, we posited.

Hypothesis 13. The higher degree of long-term orientation (LTO), the higher the effects of trust on intention to use the online shopping mall.

## 4. Research methodology

We wished to verify the validity of the consumer acceptance model of e-commerce that was developed for advanced countries’ research for use in a developing country; we selected China as our survey area. We also wished to explore the effect of national culture on the model. For this, we employed the Mark Srite and Elena Karahanna approach, which assessed cultural traits at the individual level of analysis using Hofstede’s cultural dimensions.

## 4.1. Data collection

In order to test the hypotheses, data was collected from university students in China. In total, 270 valid questionnaires were used in our analysis. 141 of the respondents were male and 129 were female. About 80 percent of the respondents reported experience with the Internet for over 3 years, and only 10 percent had experienced Internet shopping by buying more than once a month. Detailed descriptive statistics on the respondents’ characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Although we had some concern about homogeneous background caused by gathering data from university students, we think it decreased the bias causing sample inequivalency in terms of educational experience, organizational position, cultural difference, etc. in addition, the fact that only 10 percent of the respondents had experience with Internet shopping more than once a month suggests that this sample was appropriate in performing a consumer e-commerce acceptance study.

Table 2 shows the mean scores and standard deviations of constructs. All items had been scored using a Likert-style score ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), and showed a relatively high dispersion in their distributions across the ranges, as seen in the standard deviations. These results may include some bias caused by social desirability of responses or high inconsistent scoring across populations.

As shown in Table 2, the mean scores range from 2.41 (for IDV) to 6.28 (for LTO), which is similar distribution to the scores on cultural dimensions of China reported by Hofstede. IDV and LTO scores of China are 20, 118, respectively.

## 4.2. Measurements

The questionnaire used for data collection contained scales to measure the various constructs of the model. As shown in Table 3, the measurements were adapted studies by Gefen, Gefen et al. and Pavlou [11] and the measurements for national culture values were adapted from Srite and Karahanna and Shin et al.’ [13] studies. Some items for national culture values were newly developed based on the Hofstede study. A pilot test of the measures was conducted by university students in Korea. Individuals indicated their agreement or disagreement with the survey items using a seven-point Likert-scale. The wording of items was modified on the basis of results of the pilot test. Appendix A shows all items used in the study.

## 5. Results

SEM was used to validate the research model, while PLS-Graph Version 3.0 was used to perform the analysis. Although the LISREL technique is widely used for the analysis of empirically based studies, PLS is more appropriate in analyzing the moderating effects of our study.

## 5.1. Reliability and validity of measurement items

PLS was used to test the convergent and discriminant validity of the scales. In a CFA by PLS, convergent validity is proved if a measurement loads highly [with a coefficient above 0.60] or very significantly [if its t-values are within the 0.01 level of their assigned construct [6]. Table 4 shows the factor loadings of the measurement items and t-values.

The factor loadings therefore demonstrate convergent validity, and all t-values are also above 1.96.

Discriminant validity is shown when:

(1) measurement items load more strongly on their assigned construct rather than on the other constructs in the CFA, and

(2) the square root of the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) of each construct is larger than its correlations with the other constructs.

As shown in Table 4, all the measurement items loaded considerably more strongly on their respective factor than on the other constructs. ## 6. Discussion and conclusions

We empirically tested a model of consumer acceptance of ecommerce. Data were collected in China and also used to explore the effect of national culture values on consumer acceptance of ecommerce by incorporating values of PDI, IDV, MAS, UAI and LTO as moderators in the model.

The findings of the hypotheses testing of this study were as follows.

First, we confirmed that the consumer acceptance model developed by advanced countries’ researchers could be applied in developing countries. PU, PEOU, and trust must be important factors of consumer e-commerce acceptance in developing as well as advanced countries.

Second, we found that UAI was the most influential national culture value affecting consumer e-commerce acceptance. It not only had a moderate effect on the relationships between PU, trust, and intention to use but also had direct effect on intention to use. In a high UAI culture, people may decrease online shopping. This is consistent with the result of a marketing study in which. Yeniyurt and Townsend found that UAI had a negative effect on the acceptance rates of new products.

Third, LTO has also a huge impact on e-commerce acceptance. LTO increases the impact of trust on intention to use an online shopping mall. In LTO societies, building relationships is a social characteristic. And, trust is a core factor in forming business relationships. Therefore, in a high LTO culture such as China, trust is not only an important factor for social networking but is also a very important factor in acceptance of e-commerce.

Fourth, contrary to expectations, PDI and IDV had no significant effect on trust and intention to use e-commerce. It is surprising that PDI had positive effects on intention to use at the 0.05 levels. Since customers in high PDI societies are less open to new ideas and products, consumer acceptance of e-commerce in high PDI societies was expected to be lower. But the results showed the opposite. One possible explanation for the unexpected result was that customers in high PDI societies may regard online shopping as an authoritarian value. The result that IDV had no significant effect on trust and intention to use was also unexpected.

Finally, we found that MAS had a moderating effect between PU and PEOU and intention to use in e-commerce acceptance. However, the moderate effect of MAS on relationships between PEOU and intention to use was in the opposite direction that hypothesized.

## 6.1. Contributions and implications

Our study provided important results for research and practice. We extended prior research by including national culture values. In addition, we used a new approach in examining the effects of national culture on customer behavior; we employed the Mark Srite and Elena Karahanna approach, which assessed cultural traits at the individual level of analysis using Hofstede’s cultural dimensions.

The results showed that national culture values affect consumer acceptance of e-commerce. In particular, UAI and LTO played important roles. These findings suggest that in high UAI countries, customers are reluctant to engage in online shopping because they usually perceive e-commerce as having uncertain outcomes. Therefore, e-commerce managers should help consumers feel safe in the web environment by introducing institutional mechanisms such as customer feed back, third-party escrow services, and credit guarantees. In high LTO countries such as China, customers feel trust is more important than PU or PEOU in using online shopping.

## 6.2. Limitations

Our study had some limitations. First, our model does not have a strong theoretical background. Although it and the hypotheses were established in the literature, we could not provide a theoretical background for our model. Second, in Table 4, the measurement items for all of the national culture values showed an acceptable level of reliability and convergent validity. However, several items of national culture values (PDI1, UAI4 and LTO3) were dropped due to their low-level factor loading values for convergent validity. Finally, most data collection in this study was conducted with respect to university students in China. This might have affected the external validity of the empirical results.
