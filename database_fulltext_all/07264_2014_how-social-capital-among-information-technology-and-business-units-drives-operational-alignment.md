# How Social Capital Among Information Technology and Business Units Drives Operational Alignment and IT Business Value

Hein z-Theo Wa gne r, Danie l Beimbo rn , and Tim Weit ze l

Heinz-Th eo Wagner is a professor of management and innovation at the German Graduate School of Management and Law (G S), Heilbronn. He received his Ph.D. for his research on IT business alignment from Goethe University in Frankfurt. His research on IT business alignment, IT management, and innovation has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly Executive, International Journal of Innovation Management, International Journal of Information Management, and conference proceedings such as the International Conference on Information Systems.

Daniel Beimb orn is a professor of information systems at the Frankfurt School of Finance and Management, Germany. Previously, he worked at the University of Bamberg, Germany, and graduated in business administration at Goethe University in Frankfurt, where he also received his Ph.D. in information systems. His research on outsourcing, serviceoriented architectures, shared services, standardization, and IT management has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, International Journal of Information Management, and Business and Information Systems Engineering.

Tim Weit zel is a professor and chair of information systems and services at the University of Bamberg, Germany. His research on IT management, standardization, IT business alignment, and eHRM has been published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, MIS Quarterly Executive, European Journal of Information Systems, Journal of Information Technology, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Business and Information Systems Engineering.

Ab st ract : It is widely acknowledged that information technology (IT) and business resources need to be well aligned to achieve organizational goals. Yet, year after year, chief information officers still name business–IT alignment a key challenge for IT executives. While alignment research has matured, we still lack a sound theoretical foundation for alignment. Transcending the predominantly strategic executive-level focus, we develop a model of “operational alignment” and IT business value that combines a social perspective of IT and business linkage with a view of interaction between business and IT at nonstrategic levels, such as in daily business operations involving regular staff. Drawing on social capital theory to explain how alignment affects organizational performance, we examine why common suggestions such as “communicate more” are insufficient to strengthen alignment and disclose how social capital between IT and business units drives alignment and ultimately IT business value. Empirical data from

136 firms confirms the profound impact of operational business–IT alignment, composed of social capital and business understanding of IT, on IT flexibility, IT utilization, and organizational performance. The results show that social capital theory is a useful theoretical foundation for understanding how business IT alignment works. The findings suggest that operational alignment is at least as important as strategic alignment for IT service quality; that managers need to focus on operational aspects of alignment beyond communication by fostering knowledge, trust, and respect; and that IT utilization and flexibility are appropriate intermediate goals for business–IT alignment governance.

Key w ord s and ph rases: business–IT alignment, business value of IT, information systems alignment, operational alignment, social capital.

Alignment b etw een informat ion t ech nology (IT) and b usiness is considered one of the most important conditions for superior IT service quality and is a key source of IT business value creation. Especially strategic alignment, that is, the fit between IT and business mission, objectives, and plans [46], has been researched intensively and helps firms achieve their organizational goals through information systems (IS) [1]. While there are various conceptualizations of alignment [12], it remains somewhat vague what exactly is alignment, how and why it works, and how it can be achieved. The challenge of fully understanding alignment is apparent in IS research and practice. For example, a study on business–IT relationships [60] exhibits an interesting finding. The different branches of a bank showed substantially different internal (e.g., business process) and external (e.g., revenue, profitability) performance despite identical strategies, recruiting policies, formal organizational processes, and IS, and even their competitive environments were similar. The analysis revealed that the better-performing branches showed a more sophisticated use of their IT because of better cross-domain interconnectedness in daily business. Thus, the performance differences could only be explained by combining an operational and a cross-departmental perspective. Tellingly, in IS practice, year after year IT executives name alignment among their top three open challenges [32]. A comprehensive overview of the state of alignment research in IS finds that we lack a theory of alignment and need what Chan and Reich call “new theoretical underpinnings” [12, p. 311]. Encouraged by recent exciting theoretical (e.g., [12, 41]) and practical [59] insights into the social aspects of align ment, such as formal and informal teamwork or strong working relationships, which have been shown to “dramatically influence an organization’s performance” [11, p. 107], we draw on social capital theory (SCT) and develop a model to explain the interrelationships among components of business–IT alignment and how it affects IT business value. We also draw on Henderson and Venkatraman’s strategic alignment model (SAM) [26] that distinguishes between a strategic and an operational level. While the strategic perspective prevalent in the literature focuses primarily on alignment among high-level executives, we focus on the operational level, which is termed “operational integration” in the SAM.<sup>1</sup> Specifically, we focus on social IT and business linkages among business and IT units at an operational organizational level. We define operational (business–IT) alignment as cross-domain interconnectedness comprising social capital between IT and business departments and their common knowledge base (cf. [49, p. 243]). We expect better alignment to be related with higher degrees of IT efficacy. Our research question is

RQ: How does business–IT alignment at an operational level (“operational alignment”) influence organizational performance?

Drawing on both SCT and the findings from the literature on IT business value helps shed light on why operational business–IT alignment acts as a significant driver of organizational performance while IT utilization and IT flexibility are intermediary outcome variables. Introducing operational alignment by referring to SCT significantly alters our understanding of IT business value creation by putting a threefold focus on (1) social capital among business and IT units instead of pure formal interaction, (2) the operational level instead of the strategic level, and (3) business and IT knowledge instead of codified goals, mission, and plans. The results of this study will help practitioners understand how to employ IT to achieve organizational goals such as better business process performance, and suggest that IT management focus on business–IT relationship building beyond the strategic level.

## Gaps in Business–IT Alignment Research

Th e lit erat ure id ent ifies several view s on b usiness–IT alignment , including strategic and intellectual alignment, structural alignment, and social aspects of strategic alignment. Strategic and intellectual alignment refers to the degree of fit between business strategy/plans and IT strategy/plans, whereas structural alignment refers to the structural fit between a firm’s IT and business organization, and includes “IT decision-making rights, reporting relationships, (de)centralization of IT, and the deployment of IT personnel” [12, p. 300]. The third view on alignment addresses mutual understanding of and commitment to IT and business objectives and plans among business and IT executives. More recent research provides insights into these social aspects of alignment, highlighting the importance of informal structures, such as “relationship-based structures that transcend the formal division of labor and coordination of tasks” [11, p. 107].

Research on alignment has matured significantly and there is widespread consent on the importance of alignment for IT service quality and value, or as signal to gain external legitimacy [30]. However, a recent thorough literature review [12] has identified the lack of a consistent theoretical foundation. Consequently, the drivers and outcomes of alignment remain unclear. The reasons for these open challenges are related to how alignment is conceptualized and empirically studied. First, strategic/intellectual and structural alignment stress the importance of formal coordination mechanisms such as meetings and steering committees in achieving explicit outcomes such as aligned IT plans or governance. While these views on alignment are useful and provide a number of insights, they ignore other, often more important, forms of coordination and outcomes. In corporate reality, many key mechanisms and outcomes are informal and intangible. For example, IT plans do not typically describe how IT and business personnel have to interact to put these plans into action, and formal meetings are often only a minor part of all interaction between business and IT [11]. Second, alignment research focuses on alignment at the strategic level and among high-level executives. However, most parts of the strategy implementation, IT projects, and operations are not part of the strategic level and do not involve high-level executives, or involve them only to a minor degree. To render effects such as organizational performance, “[o]rganizations must ensure that they have aligned strategies, but they must also deploy resources appropriately in order to execute their strategies” [3, p. 315]. This is also reflected by models of alignment, such as the SAM, that encompass more than only the strategic level. Alignment is not only paramount among high-level executives but must also be achieved across the remaining levels of the organization. The SAM refers to this as “operational integration” between business and IT.

Chan and Reich’s [12] review of the alignment literature calls for the scope of research to be expanded to include a theoretical underpinning for alignment. Our research takes steps in this direction by focusing on the social business–IT linkage, that is, “informal structures,” and formal interactions to provide a consistent social capital–based research perspective on the social aspects of alignment. Using the SAM as frame of reference, we position our research at the level of “operational integration” between business and IT.

## Model Development

## Model Overview

Th is sect ion int rod uces th e k ey concept s of our suggest ed mod el of alignment and business effects of IT and develops the hypotheses in detail. We argue that social capital among IT and business units consists of formal and informal relationships along different dimensions, such as interaction patterns, mutual trust, and shared language. Social capital provides the relationship infrastructure between IT and business, forming the skeleton for the process of knowledge integration that combines business and IT knowledge [28]. Social capital between business and IT units and its outcome—combined business–IT knowledge—together represent the cross-domain interconnectedness that we refer to as “alignment.” This in turn leads to effective IT services and thus drives IT business value. Emphasizing the relational aspect of value creation is in line with prior IS research and has been put forward in the relationship asset [50], business–IS linkages in IS development [57], and in the SAM [26]. Figure 1 illustrates our theoretical model, and is described in more detail below.

## Social Capital Between Business and IT

To understand the underpinnings of operational business–IT alignment, we look at the relationships among IT and business staff through the lens of SCT. Central to this theory, social capital is described and measured along structural, cognitive, and relational dimensions. The structural dimension of social capital (structural linkage) refers to “the overall pattern of connections between actors” [37, p. 244], including the settings in which IT and non-IT employees communicate and interact, such as in meetings and projects. Formal and informal meetings help staff see beyond the scope of their own work and consider the impact of their work on others, thereby reducing the risk of myopic views of the business and of working and thinking in silos. Accordingly, the frequency of formal meetings can be a predictor of structural linkage quality [46]. However, just communicating does not guarantee, per se, that anything sensible will happen; rather, it enables knowledge exchange, knowledge creation, and improved mutuality. Nahapiet and Ghoshal argue accordingly that structural linkages imply that “the opportunity exists to make the [knowledge] combination or exchange” [37, p. 249]. Consequently, communication is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for “good alignment” or high levels of social capital. Instead, we expect structural linkage to increase cognitive and relational linkage, which is also congruent with a recent study of Karahanna and Preston [27].

The cognitive dimension of social capital (cognitive linkage) captures shared codes, language, and perspectives, and refers to the extent to which IT and business staff know each other’s interpretations of reality [37]. This includes the IT staff’s ability to talk to business colleagues about technical issues in business language rather than technical jargon, but also the extent to which business staff has a general background understanding of what IT projects are about.

The relational dimension of social capital (relational linkage) “focuses on the particular relations people have” [37, p. 244], and includes trust and respect, which are important for the exchange and combination of knowledge. Relational linkage represents the extent to which IT and business staff trust each other and respect each other’s work. One indicator of the strength of such mutual relationships is the degree to which IT and business personnel are “partners,” actually work together, and consult each other [57].

## Business Understanding of IT

Some researchers, such as Kearns and Sabherwal [28], view alignment as a process of knowledge integration that combines business and IT knowledge, leading to a common knowledge base in which explicit outcomes are (at a strategic level) integrated business and IT plans or (in the operational context of our model) a high degree of IT efficacy. In this context, some alignment studies investigate shared knowledge, while others investigate the combination of business and IT knowledge. It is important to distinguish between shared knowledge, which refers to information uniquely held by individuals that is identified and communicated, and combined knowledge (knowledge integration outcome), which results when people interact and “combine their information to create new knowledge” [39, p. 383]. The latter is the focus of our study because the integration of function-specific business and IT knowledge increases the potential to achieve effective and efficient solutions for business challenges by leveraging complementarity among specialized knowledge pools [25]. Taken to the IT context, we thus focus on the business understanding of the IT staff, or more precisely on the business knowledge that is interpreted by IT personnel on the basis of their background knowledge.

## Social Capital Between Business and IT Drives Business Understanding

Following the central arguments of SCT, we posit that social capital facilitates the development of the IT staff’s business understanding by facilitating knowledge exchange through social ties among organizational units [37], by avoiding misunderstandings through a shared vision [58], and by providing channels for information flows [58]. This increases knowledge accessibility and availability [51], enhances collaboration through trusted relationships [58], and provides IT staff with a deeper understanding of business needs [34] through a shared vision and shared vocabulary. Furthermore, increasing knowledge reach and richness through what Sambamurthy et al. [51] call “perspective-sharing” and “sense-making” enhances IT professionals’ understanding of business practices and their perception of IT as a resource. Hence, and in line with SCT, it is through social relationships that knowledge is exchanged and combined. This view is fully supported by Kearns and Sabherwal, who investigate executives and view business–IT strategic alignment as “an outcome of knowledge integration between business and IT” [28, p. 138]. In a similar vein, but focusing at the strategic level, Karahanna and Preston show “that social capital of the relationship between the CIO [chief information officer] and TMT [top management team] . . . facilitates exchange and combination of IS and business knowledge, both of which are necessary for knowledge integration and organizational value creation” [27, pp. 16–17].

Hypothesis 1: Social capital in terms of structural, cognitive, and relational linkage between business and IT positively influences business understanding of IT.

## IT Business Value

Drawing on the rich business valuation literature, we use a multifaceted concept of IT business value and distinguish between the three variables: organizational performance, IT utilization, and IT flexibility. Choosing these three variables reflects the argumentation of Melville et al. [35], who define IT business value as the organizational performance effects of IT at both the intermediate business process and organization-wide levels, and Chung et al. [15], whose study of strategic alignment focuses on application implementation and flexibility. The first IT-related variable, organizational performance, is used rather broadly in the literature and includes, for example, productivity enhancement and cost reduction [35]. In our model, organizational performance describes the performance of a bank’s business processes and includes effectiveness and efficiency dimensions such as effective handling of credit risks. The second variable, IT utilization, describes the extent to which a firm deploys IT to support operational and strategic tasks [8]. In particular, we refer to the utilization of IS rather than the availability of IT in general because the primary impact of IT resources on the business is through its use [18, 42]. The business value accruing from an IS is determined by how well people use it in the business context [42]. IT utilization improves operational effectiveness by unambiguously improving the output and quality of a business process [36] and its operating efficiency [33].

Hypothesis 2: Higher levels of IT utilization positively affect organizational performance.

The third IT-related variable of our model is IT flexibility, defined as the ability to adapt to changing business requirements quickly and economically [29]. Many studies discuss the effects of flexibility on firm performance, mostly in terms of a flexible IT infrastructure (e.g., 19]). Flexible IT enables business processes to be broken down and modularized into individual activities, which can be recombined to create new business processes [51]. Furthermore, IT flexibility facilitates the responsiveness to changing business requirements and influences the cost and value of IT-enabled innovations [19]. Therefore, IT flexibility is a significant source of value [29] and a key factor in the success of firms, particularly in volatile environments. It contributes to organizational performance by allowing a rapid response to changes in business requirements or the environment [19]. Also, IT flexibility improves organizational performance by lowering the cost of adapting to change [57] and by facilitating the integration of heterogeneous business applications, which helps to better match the competitive environment [10].

Hypothesis 3: IT flexibility is positively related to organizational performance.

## Business Understanding of IT Creates Business Value

Actual IT utilization is one of the most important arguments in the IT business value debate. As higher levels of business understanding are related to better interpretation of business knowledge in the IT units, this improves IT’s ability to provide IT support and services that are deemed useful by the business users [34]. Deployment of resources through new ways of exchanging and combining knowledge [58] about effective usage of the IS make the appropriate use of the systems more likely. Similar arguments can be made for developing and changing IS. Higher levels of business understanding of IT facilitate system support and changes (see [43]). At the same time, it increases the likelihood that IS are developed according to business requirements and that these systems will be used in an effective manner. Hence, business understanding raises the likelihood of appropriate IT utilization by influencing the development of the IS [1, 43] through facilitating deployment of IT congruent with business requirements [52].

Hypothesis 4: Business understanding of IT positively influences IT utilization.

Furthermore, we expect business understanding of IT to improve IT flexibility by enabling the IT domain to address changing business environments [7], by enhancing the responsiveness of the IT unit, and by improving readiness for change [19].

Hypothesis 5: Business understanding of IT positively influences IT flexibility.

## Relationship Between Business Understanding, Social Capital, and IT Business Value

Social capital facilitates the creation of business understanding of IT and thus IT effectiveness. For instance, frequent communication between the IT and business units (structural linkage) enables sufficient knowledge exchange to interpret business requirements and thus develop the “right” system or realize change requests, which in turn increases utilization. As discussed above, higher levels of business understanding reflect knowledge of IT personnel about how IT is used by business personnel, how to provide IT services to promote utilization, and how to deploy IT to support the flexibility to respond to changing environments. Accordingly, business understanding “transforms” social capital into IT utilization and IT flexibility. Thus, we expect business understanding to be a mediating (and thus explanatory) argument between social capital and IT business value and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 6: Business understanding of IT mediates the influence of social capital on (a) IT utilization and (b) IT flexibility.

## Method and Data

Empirical Domain and Level of Empirical Analysis

We analyze th e impact of IT on th e performance of a single business process, thereby drawing the analysis closer to the actual operations and the locus of IS impact and allowing the effects to be identified more accurately than in, for example, firm-level studies where only aggregate results could be measured. Since firms will often have superior processes in one area and inferior processes in another, a resulting average firm-level performance score would be useless for our analysis. Instead, by analyzing a particular business process, we can explain the how and why of IT value creation (e.g., [54]).

Our empirical research focuses on an important primary business process in banks: granting and managing credit to small and medium-size enterprises (“SME credit process”). We chose the banking industry because of its high IT reliance and information-processing intensity. Also, changes in regulations, workflows, or product portfolios require permanent adaptation of IT. In particular, the SME credit process was chosen because it is a primary business process that contributes significantly to most banks’ business and performance. In addition, there are some non-automated and non-automatable activities (e.g., compared to payments processing) that require human business competencies and alignment between IT and business units.

By restricting our analysis to one single industry, we followed Chan and Reich’s suggestion that “alignment research that focuses on specific industries . . . has the potential to result in better granularity in findings, and a deeper understanding of the way that IT enables value creation” [12, p. 310]. Establishing industry boundaries reduces potential confusion for the following reasons. First, the value chains within industries are relatively consistent and stable, second, considering the industry context helps create a frame of common reference, and third, industry boundaries determine the social and technical boundaries within which the application of theory is researched [14].

## Data Collection and Analysis

The data for this study were collected in the German banking industry. After having identified the managers responsible for the operations of the SME credit business of the largest 1,000 banks, we mailed the questionnaire to them, and followed up by reminder correspondence and telephone calls if needed. Ultimately, we received 136 usable questionnaires. The data were analyzed regarding normality, nonresponse bias, and missing values. A few items exhibited some values missing, which could be corrected by applying the regression algorithm for missing value treatment in SPSS 18 (except for controls such as firm size or system age). This procedure resulted in a sample of 132 (136) for testing our research model with (without) controls. We chose partial least squares (PLS) to analyze the sample because of the nonnormal distribution of some items, the relatively small sample size, and the exploratory character of parts of our study [22]. We used SmartPLS [48] to carry out the analysis.

## Measurement

Most of our constructs were measured by qualitative multi-item measures. The IT business value variables organizational performance, IT utilization, and IT flexibility were derived mainly from established measurement instruments and adapted to our research context (for the items used and sources of origin, see Appendix Table A1). Furthermore, the questionnaire provided a clear introduction to the business context and asked several focus questions that made sure that the study participants had the operational context clearly in focus when evaluating the survey items.

At the level of a business process, the eventual success variable organizational performance manifests as “business process performance,” which we measured along different performance dimensions covering processing performance, effective handling of credit risks, and profitability and market share increases (e.g., [43]). Since the return and risk dimensions in particular represent a major trade-off in the financial industry, both have to be covered; they are complementary concepts of overall performance, requiring formative measurement. Since multicollinearity problems prohibited the direct formative use of the items in PLS, we aggregated them as a summated scale into a single performance score. In the hope of receiving quantitative data providing more objective performance data, we also asked for profitability, interest margins, and average processing times for handling loan applications. Unfortunately, too many missing values prevented us from using these measures directly in the PLS calculation. However, the available data show strong correlations with the qualitative performance items used.

IT utilization and IT flexibility are the determinants of performance. Every bank uses an IS for processing and administering loans. Therefore, our survey did not focus on the introduction of or a substantial change in the credit system, but rather on how it is adapted and utilized in the operational day-to-day business. Our measure for IT utilization focuses on whether the credit system in place is used appropriately and whether it provides the functionalities required for an effective credit process.

IT flexibility is the second performance determinant. It is a technical as well as a social concept consisting of technical components (modularity, compatibility, connectivity) and IT personnel flexibility [10]. Byrd and Turner [10] show that IT personnel flexibility contributes more strongly to performance than technical attributes. From a measurement point of view, the IT personnel flexibility perceived from outside (we asked the business manager responsible for the credit process) also covers the technical flexibility, since the staff’s ability to solve problems and fulfill change requests depends not only on their skills but also on the flexibility of the technical infrastructure [19]. Consequently, we use items that reflect this human-oriented, overall IT flexibility concept that represents the relevant proportion of the overall flexibility potential, and ask whether and how quickly change requests are fulfilled.

The items for the social capital dimensions were derived from the literature and adapted to our research context. Drawing on the literature, we operationalized the three dimensions of social capital as linkages between the IT and business units as follows.

Structural linkage reflects the degree of interaction between business and IT. Preceding this study, we conducted case studies in the banking sector that showed that structural linkage consists predominantly of formal exchange at or related to certain types of scheduled meetings. Considering that the loan process and the respective IS had typically been established many years ago, we associated structural linkage with specific types of meetings dealing with changing the business process, controlling the change process, and discussing possible improvements. Therefore, we used a formative measure capturing the frequency of such meetings in the loans process and system context. The frequencies of the different types of meetings represent formative indicators of structural linkage that were aggregated to a single item (weighted summated scale).

Cognitive linkage covers shared language and codes as well as the IT staff’s competence to speak the same language and use the technical terms common in the respective business [5]. Cognitive linkage was measured as a reflective three-item construct.

Relational linkage refers to trusted relationships. The literature and our case studies show that relational linkage manifests as trust [37], close cooperation [52, p. 144], and mutual consultancy [6]. Therefore, relational linkage was measured by three reflective items (e.g., adapted from [6, 43]) that ask for the degree of mutual trust or closeness between business and IT.

Finally, the model contains business understanding. In accordance with Tiwana’s [56, p. 16] reasoning, our business understanding of IT employees is asymmetric in a way that “the IT unit has higher business domain knowledge” that is interpreted on the basis of prior IT knowledge that is closer to reality than the assumption that business and IT units “have knowledge of each other’s domains.” In the credit process context, minor system changes are common and IT personnel must interpret change requests effectively, which requires that they have a high level of business understanding [55] regarding the role of the credit system in the business process and the relevance and impact of the requested changes. Consequently, our reflective items asked for the state of this kind of goal-oriented business understanding on the IT side [6, 8, 43] in the context of the loan business. Furthermore, because of the potential effects of business understanding of IT on the business domain, the measure “business understanding of IT” is assessed by the business unit.

## Controls

To rule out rival hypotheses, we added the following controls: Firm size (SIZE), based on total assets; age (AGE) of the IS supporting the SME credit process; competitive environment (CompE): the degree of competition (two reflective items); business competencies (BC): the capabilities of the staff and management involved in the credit process (three reflective items; see Table A1); business-internal relationship (BIR): regulatory requirements in Germany require the credit process in every bank to be divided organizationally into a front-office part (sales department) and a backoffice part (loan department). In both departments, staff accomplishes different tasks and therefore requires differing competencies. Therefore, we not only looked at the interface between IT and business but also at the interface between front-office and back-office as a type of business–business alignment. This BIR construct is operationalized by three reflective items [31]. Finally, to be concordant with the existing strand on alignment research, we include strategic business–IT alignment (stratBITA) in an extra section below. We used the single-item measure from Tallon et al. [54] that asks how well business strategy and IT strategy are aligned.

## Validating Data and Measurement Models

First, we tested the indicator-based data on univariate normality and found slight deviations of kurtosis and skewness from normality (< | 1.0 |). The Shapiro–Wilk test found that some variables do not sufficiently fulfill the normality criterion. Second, we analyzed the data for indications of nonresponse bias. Comparing the answers given by early respondents with those that answered following a reminder telephone call and resending the questionnaire showed no significant differences. Third, the validity of the measurement models had to be verified. As Appendix Table A2 shows, all the loadings are significant (based on a bootstrapping with 500 samples) and most are greater than 0.7 as recommended by Nunnally [38]; only cogL1 slightly misses the 0.7 threshold, but is greater than 0.6 as suggested by Bagozzi and Yi [2]. Furthermore, the results for composite reliability and average variance extracted (AVE) show that all the constructs are consistently represented and explained largely by their reflective set of indicators (i.e., composite reliability > 0.7 and AVE > 0.5) [20]. To ensure that the measurement models of the different constructs are sufficiently distinct, we compared the interconstruct correlations with the square root of the AVE (with the latter required to be higher; see Appendix Table A3) [23]. Also, all the indicators are more strongly correlated with their own construct than any other.

Finally, we tried to detect indications for common method bias. First, we conducted the Harman single-factor test and found that no single component explained the majority of the total variance shared by all the items used (the most substantial component explained 26 percent). Second, we applied the confirmatory factor analysis marker technique suggested by Williams et al. [61] and shown to be the most effective post hoc common method bias detection technique by Richardson et al. [47]: All the items were converted to single-item constructs that are allowed to load both on their own substantive construct and on a theoretically unrelated marker variable. ## Evaluating the Structural Model

Figure 2 depicts the full model, including controls. To account for the mediating character of business understanding (BU) between social capital and IT business value, we also modeled the direct effects between social capital and both IT utilization (ITU) and IT flexibility (ITF) as well as between BU and organizational performance (P). Solid edges represent significant relationships.

Overall, the propositions of SCT can essentially be confirmed for alignment and IT business value contexts. On the left side of the model, the different dimensions of social capital are highly interrelated as expected, and the cognitive and relational dimensions are positively related with BU, confirming H1. The influence of the structural dimension is not apparent, but the cognitive and relational dimensions act as mediators, since the link between the structural dimension and business understanding becomes significantly positive $( \beta = 0 . 2 0 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ when removing the cognitive and relational dimensions [4]. On the right side of the model, and in line with the IT business value literature, IT utilization and IT flexibility are significantly related with the overall performance of the loan business, confirming H2 and H3. Moreover, the cognitive and relational dimensions of social capital are related significantly and directly with IT flexibility; again, the effect of the structural linkage is strongly mediated by the cognitive and relational linkages. Business understanding of IT drives IT flexibility, confirming H5, but only weakly mediates the effect of cognitive and relational linkage on IT flexibility: Removing business understanding from the model leads to even stronger paths between cognitive and relational linkage and IT flexibility [4], while the variance accounted for (VAF) is very low for relational linkage $( \mathrm { V A F } = 0 . 1 3 9 )$ but high for cognitive linkage $( \mathrm { V A F } = 0 . 5 9 7$ , see Table 1). This partially confirms H6b.

While operational alignment is also a significant driver of IT utilization in terms of business understanding (confirming H4), social capital dimensions are hardly directly related with this construct. Instead, business understanding strongly mediates the relationship between cognitive linkage and IT utilization: If business understanding is removed from the model, the path from cognitive linkage to IT utilization will increase from insignificant 0.126 to significant 0.190 $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . By contrast, any effect of the relational dimension on IT utilization remains nonexistent (partially confirming H6a).

## Analysis of Business Competencies

Business competencies (BC) naturally account for at least as much variance as the IT factors (ITU, ITF). A successful credit process requires sophisticated sales capabilities, knowledge about the SME customers, and high experience for the early detection of risks that might lead to bad loans. IT can support sales and risk assessment, but there is little potential to automate these value-adding activities completely (at least in the SME business) and thus make obsolete the business competencies of staff and management. The impact of business-internal relationships (BIR) proves to be significant. On the one hand, social linkage between the different business departments involved in the credit process is highly important, and IT cannot substantially substitute for this by “digitizing” and formalizing the interaction between business units along the workflow. On the other hand, operational alignment increases organizational performance over and above business competencies.<sup>3</sup> In a post hoc analysis, we removed ITU and ITF from the full model and analyzed the direct effect of operational alignment (i.e., business understanding and social capital) on performance while BC, BIR, and the other controls are present. The result is that operational alignment has a weak but significant single effect of $f ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 6 8$ since it increases R<sup>2</sup> from 0.357 to 0.398.

## Limitations

While we have attempted to overcome some limitations of previous research on alignment, our results must be interpreted with regard to their own limitations. First, while all of the tests we applied indicated the absence of a common method bias, collecting data for both the independent and dependent variables from the same sources at the same time always goes along with the possibility of method variance. Second, our construct business understanding of IT is measured by an assessment of business representatives overseeing the credit process who assessed the respective competencies of the IT unit. While this has the advantage of fitting the application domain and its business–IT relationship, it comes at the cost of neglecting the effect of IT understanding of business staff. Third, using a single industry data set and a dedicated business process has the advantage of obtaining a more precise impression of how alignment works, as the environment is comparable. This comes, though, at the cost of limits to the generalizability of the results to other application domains. In particular, we expect the effect and importance of alignment to be weaker in industries in which IT plays a less prominent role. Still, our own case studies and surveys in different industries (including aerospace), on different processes (including secondary processes), and in different countries reveal analogous results. We discuss these and the resulting possibilities for future research in detail in the next section.

## Discussion of the Findings

understanding of IT provides a differentiated picture of how the different facets of operational alignment are interrelated and how they affect organizational performance.

Cognitive, and Relational Linkage

A key result of this study is the theoretical and empirical clarification that the structural dimension of social capital between business and IT is the enabler of the cognitive and relational dimensions and that those, in turn, influence business understanding of IT, which eventually creates better business process performance. Overall, the cognitive dimension exerts the strongest influence.

Cognitive linkage represents a common language between the business and IT domains, which facilitates knowledge exchange and combination, leading to an understanding about the core business subjects, which is combined with IT knowledge and manifested in the IT domain.

Structural linkage refers to frequent interaction and thus influences the development of common language. Similarly, over time, frequent interaction also builds trust between the IT and business domains—which further improves the exchange and integration of knowledge and enhances IT’s business understanding.

Analogous to Karahanna and Preston [27], our results show that the effect of structural capital on business understanding is mediated through cognitive social capital. In essence, this means that meetings per se have no positive impact but that those meetings need to be effective in creating and maintaining a common language. The same argument holds for the relational dimension of social capital: Without trust and openness between business and IT, communication/meetings can hardly contribute to the creation of a mutual business–IT understanding [58]. Future research should further clarify the interrelationship among the dimensions of social capital, for example, by including contingency variables such as management power and leadership [30].

## Social Capital and Business Understanding Improve IT Flexibility

Another important finding of this study is the strong impact of social capital and business understanding on IT flexibility. Business understanding of IT provides a deeper understanding of the functionalities needed in the business process as well as of goals and constraints, and thus further effectuates proper changes. It is necessary for responsive actions and goes beyond simply being fast when it comes to changes in the IS. In addition, a strong cognitive linkage provides a shared system of meaning that is needed to interpret and respond to requests from the business domain, for example, by implementing changes to the system in an accurate and appropriate manner [63, 64].

However, the influence of business understanding on IT flexibility is comparably weak, particularly when compared to relational linkage. Moreover, it mediates the effect of relational linkage on IT flexibility only to a weak degree.

Relational linkage may exert its influence through increasing responsiveness to a changing environment as well as to user requests and requirements as an incentive to preserve the relationship between the business and IT domains [62]. Thus, relational linkage fosters IT flexibility so as not to jeopardize “carefully built reputations for quality and reliability in product and service offerings” [43, p. 245] (see also [55]). In this regard, the strong direct relation between relational linkage and IT flexibility points to an interesting difference between how social capital versus social capital theory explain flexibility: While the expected indirect link, as suggested by SCT, results from better cross-domain knowledge, the direct link from relational linkage to flexibility indicates a more tacit mutuality. One can argue that the business understanding link to flexibility drives IT’s capability to change, while the direct link from social capital relates to IT’s willingness to behave flexibly: The better the joint relationship, the more willing and engaged the IT unit will be to solve IT-related problems of the business. From a managerial perspective, it is an interesting thought to create “IT responsiveness through willingness.” This implies that “scarce management time and resources are better spent on improving the informal organization than on aligning formal structures. Although less visible than the formal structure, it can be more malleable and paradoxically more enduring” [12, p. 301]. Theoretically, this insight is not only an interesting suggestion for extending SCT but also confirms expectations that informal structures might play a key role [11].

## Operational Alignment Drives IT Utilization

The main finding concerning the impact of operational alignment on IT utilization is that the influence of all social capital dimensions on IT utilization is strongly mediated by business understanding. Business understanding provides the basis for IT personnel to understand the context in which the IS is used (e.g., usage situations such as serving a queue of impatient customers, or complex loan requests). This understanding, in turn, creates the opportunity to promote effective support and training for users to ensure both effective and continual use of the system. This effect clearly goes beyond the mere use of a common vocabulary, rendering the cognitive linkage an insignificant (direct) factor.

## Strategic and Operational Alignment

Consistent with existing alignment research, the results show that strategic alignment is significantly related to process performance. This impact is fully mediated by IT flexibility. Looking at the influence of strategic alignment on the other variables reveals a strong impact on relational and cognitive linkages. Thus, the effect of strategic alignment is twofold: First, it drives IT flexibility at the strategic layer by ensuring that the basic IS architecture meets the flexibility needs in the particular business environment (flexibility enablement: IT is able to react to new requirements; see, e.g., [1]). Second, the existence of fitting strategies and plans might create a common frame of reference [55] that supports the creation and maintenance of the social linkages between business and IT people, for example, by a lower level of misunderstandings (cognitive dimension), which may turn into trust (relational dimension). These social linkages are part of operational alignment, which in turn also drives IT flexibility in a more operational sense (flexibility willingness: the IT is willing to react to new requirements; see [62]) as well as IT utilization. We can conclude that, while acknowledging that strategic business–IT alignment is important, operational alignment is a critical factor in closing the causal IT–business value chain. Alignment of strategies leads to appropriate IT investments and implementation, which leads to—mainly driven by operational alignment—appropriate utilization and flexible adaptation of IT, which finally creates the business value of IT.

## Conclusion

Our st ud y cont rib ut es t o th eory in several w ays. First, we suggest and validate a theoretical foundation for alignment by integrating reasoning from SCT and IT business value research. The model explicitly responds to the call that “[g]reater use of well-established theories in alignment research is needed” [12, p. 311]. By developing a social capital–based alignment model as a new theoretical underpinning for alignment and by arguing theoretically how business–IT alignment is composed and how it affects IT business value, this paper addresses a key concern in prior studies and helps us advance a consistent view on different social aspects of alignment. Furthermore, the analysis reveals the extent to which alignment is not merely a strategic or executive-level issue, but that it is probably even more important at an operational level, in particular, when it comes to actual IT utilization and organizational performance in business operations.

Second, looking at the constituents of IT business value, our results show a strong influence of IT utilization and IT flexibility on organizational performance, as expected. These, in turn, are strongly driven by operational alignment encompassing business understanding and social capital. From a practitioner’s perspective, identifying and understanding the drivers of such effects is an elemental governance issue. Theoretically, a unified view of business and IT capabilities can explain phenomena such as the IT productivity paradox, in which even the best IT cannot translate into superior performance when there are deficiencies concerning business skills (see, e.g., [21]).

Third, while the overall expectation that alignment has a positive effect is largely supported, communication between IT and business staff (i.e., structural linkage) has no direct effect on performance. This is contrary to conventional wisdom. Simply increasing the number of meetings and talking more does not do the trick. Rather, as the SCT lens discloses, knowledge (through business understanding) and trust (effect on flexibility) must be improved. Furthermore, while the significant effect of communication on cognitive and relational linkage shows that communication is indeed an important driver, the rather low R<sup>2</sup> for cognitive and relational linkage indicates that they are facilitated, but not necessitated, by communication.

Fourth, the analysis of the role of strategic business–IT alignment extends the core theoretical model and links our arguments to related alignment work. Following Chan and Reich, who state that “[m]ore focused (vs. broad brush) investigations of alignment are needed to go beyond ‘alignment is good’ statements, and to explore subtleties concerning how, when, and where IT alignment really matters” [12, p. 310], we can speculate that the process of alignment enables business–IT knowledge spillovers (a view supported by the relation between strategic alignment and relational linkage) or that the outcome of strategic alignment is manifest in documents that serve as a joint knowledge source among business and IT units. In any respect, the interplay between strategic and operational alignment calls for closer scrutiny.

Overall, the results of this study reveal a profound impact of operational alignment on the business value of IT. The boundary between IT and business employees has structural and social facets and is a line meant to be crossed.
