# The Gender Bias Tug-of-War in a Co-creation Community: Core-Periphery Tension on Wikipedia

Amber G. Young<sup>a</sup>, Ariel D. Wigdor<sup>b</sup>, and Gerald C. Kane <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Information Systems, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, Arkansas, USA; <sup>b</sup>Operations and Information Management, Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Massachusetts, USA; <sup>c</sup>Information Systems, Carroll School of Management, Boston University, Chestnut Hill, Massachusetts, USA

## ABSTRACT

Commitment to a neutral point of view is a pillar of Wikipedia culture. Despite a strong culture, the open nature of the community presents challenges for cultural assimilation. Central contributors who make up the community’s core engage in activism to combat traditional forms of bias. However, peripheral contributors may not adopt or adhere to the community’s cultural values and may reflect the very bias the community attempts to combat. These diferences create tensions between central and peripheral contributors. Peripheral contributors are likely to contribute traditional types of information bias (e.g., bias against women). In response, central contributors may overcorrect in ways that reverse traditional biases. We compare gender bias on female and male CEO profiles and find evidence of bias that advantages women and disadvantages men. While peripheral contributors may dominate early in the life cycle of coreperiphery structured open collaboration communities—due to the volume of contributions—the core dominates in the long term through sustained, systematic activism. Our results suggest that a balance of activity from central and peripheral contributors results in content with the most neutral point of view.

## KEYWORDS

Online collaboration communities; digital co-production; core-periphery structure; digital collaboration; knowledge co-creation; gender bias; Wikipedia; CEO; online engagement; Wikipedia neutrality

## Introduction

Widespread difusion of online open collaboration communities has revolutionized the way individuals and organizations create and consume information [38, 46, 102, 103]. These communities facilitate open collaboration among diverse stakeholders who engage in generating and disseminating information. In so doing, these communities create new challenges for organizations and individuals seeking to manage digital information online. These challenges stem from community design features which facilitate networking among stakeholders with competing goals and perspectives, give a platform for critical voices to speak against organizational leaders, and reduce organizational control over how information is framed [33]. The potential for negative or biased representations in digital information created on open collaboration communities has important implications for organizations seeking legitimacy and consequent resources.

Open collaboration community culture influences how information is framed to some degree, but the open nature of such communities leads to inconsistent application of cultural norms and tension between central and peripheral contributors who adhere to community culture to difering degrees. One notable open collaboration community is Wikipedia, the fifth most visited website in the world [87]. Information on Wikipedia has been hailed as comparable to professionally developed encyclopedias [26]. This may be attributable to unique cultural and technological structures that facilitate emancipatory discourse [31].

Like many online communities, Wikipedia exhibits a core-periphery structure [82]. A central group of community leaders sets the direction and culture of the community. On Wikipedia, a key pillar of this culture is the need to adopt a neutral point of view when developing content, which serves to combat common types of bias such as gender bias. Wikipedia’s socialization tactics are intended to foster a shared vision and promote ideological convergence as core contributors and those at the periphery of the community engage socially [11]. Still, these leaders cannot force assimilation or adherence to neutral point-of-view policies on peripheral members who contribute sporadically and may not even be aware of these standards. This core-periphery structure can also alienate some contributors [53], leading to disparate goals and value systems. This tension creates a problem for Wikipedia in that those who are most enmeshed in the community and actively shape culture do not carry out the brunt of the work; rather, it is contributors at the periphery—those who come and go without necessarily engaging the community culture—who make most of the edits on Wikipedia [71, 75].

Given that barriers to entry are low [51], contributions made by the periphery may be impulsive or even contributed with malice and are thus prone to bias. Tensions arise between the core values of those central to the community who are most invested in the cultural preservation of Wikipedia and those not fully integrated in the community yet critical to community success. Though Wikipedia culture strives for a neutral point of view, neutrality may prove elusive as those with central roles and those at the periphery negotiate inclusion and exclusion of information in perpetuity. This tug-of-war may have one of three outcomes at any given time: balanced at neutrality, leaning toward the periphery and associated biases, or overcorrected for by central contributors, likewise leaning away from neutral balance.

Activism eforts by those central to the community combat bias against groups that are traditionally marginalized or underrepresented [80]. In seeking to correct issues of bias against minority groups, might the core group be overcorrecting such that uncommon types of bias (e.g., bias against the majority) emerge? One salient form of bias, brought to public attention by recent cyberactivism campaigns such as #MeToo and #ILookLikeAnEngineer, is gender bias. Building on the digital discourse bias framework developed by Miranda et al. [64], we investigate the nature of gender bias in information about Fortune 1000 CEOs on Wikipedia. The findings of this study suggest that while eforts of the central contributors to reduce gender bias against women have succeeded, an overcorrection is occurring. This research highlights the fact that neutrality is hard to achieve and that the way for the community to win the gender bias tug-of-war is for neither central nor peripheral contributors to dominate.

## Literature Review

## Digital Discourse Bias Framework for Assessing Bias and Disparities

Decades of systematic study have revealed bias in information difused through traditional media. Such bias often perpetuates the interests of power holders and enforces the status quo [55]. While information may be intentionally infused with bias toward the goal of shaping public consciousness [2], bias often creeps in without conscious intent by content creators [28]. Unintentional forms of media bias stem not only from the subconscious minds of content creators, but also from media features that facilitate or inhibit unbiased content generation. Media shape the structure (how content creators interact) and content (what content creators say) of information in ways that contribute to or reduce bias.

Drawing from the literature on mass communication and media studies, information systems (IS) scholars developed a framework for assessing bias in digital discourse [64]. This framework depicts two sources of unintentional bias: structural constraints and content restrictions imposed by media platforms (see Table 1 for a definition and example of each). Structural constraints are barriers to authorship, sourcing and citing of information, and how much influence is garnered by information relating to minority cases or opinions. Increases in structural constraints are associated with higher levels of selection bias, source bias, and influence disparities. Selection bias occurs when there are systematic diferences in which individuals, stories, or events are selected for media coverage. Omission of information relating to specific groups indicates selection bias. Source bias occurs when there are systematic diferences in the quantity or reliability of sources from which information is constructed and verified. Influence disparities occur when there are systematic diferences in the degree to which information gains and sustains attention from the community.

Content restrictions are barriers to neutrality and truth. Increases in content restrictions are associated with higher levels of content bias. Content bias is complex and can take a variety of forms. Content bias occurs when rhetorical devices are used to promote specific, value-laden interpretations of information. Absence of bias is reflected in parity across groups in terms of content (e.g., neutrality and accuracy). Departures from parity across groups suggest bias, which necessarily advantages certain groups while disadvantaging others. Application of the digital discourse bias framework in the context of public discourse revealed that digital media imposed fewer structural constraints than traditional media, yet greater content restrictions than traditional media.

Lean digital media, such as Wikipedia, impose fewer structural constraints, yet greater content restrictions, than rich digital media [64]. Though information bias on Wikipedia is a concern of management information systems (MIS) scholars, most research in this area is not theory driven, and it focuses on either content (e.g., bias) or the collaborative process (e.g., influence disparities), but not both [36]. The digital discourse bias framework provides a basis for theory-driven investigation of information bias and disparities.

## Wikipedia: A Core-Periphery Structured Open Collaboration Community

An open collaboration community is a “system of innovation or production that relies on goal-oriented yet loosely coordinated participants who interact to create a product (or service) of economic value, which they make available to contributors and noncontributors alike” [46, p. 1416]. Wikipedia, a free online encyclopedia, has been touted by scholars as a rich context for investigating the consequences of open collaboration [36]. Individuals contribute to Wikipedia for a variety of reasons, including for fun, to feel like a part of something, to help their business or career, to gain insights or perspective, to feel needed, or to satisfy personal ideologies or values [68].

Wikipedia is organized according to a core-periphery structure [43, 53], in which central actors from a core group are active contributors, enmeshed in the culture of the community, while those at the periphery engage in a limited way, may not identify as ingroup, and do not benefit from social modeling [100]. Broadly, those most central in the community shape and enforce social norms and Wikipedia’s culture of neutrality, while those who complete most information generation tasks may not be well acquainted with Wikipedia norms and cultural practices (e.g., promoting a neutral point of view through rules like no original research and facts must be verifiable). While some researchers have conceptualized this core-periphery structure in terms of certain administrative roles [e.g., 101], this conceptualization does not necessarily reflect the level of involvement in the development of particular content, since Wikipedia’s explicit policies is that these roles do not grant privileged status in the community [92]. Other researchers have conceptualized this core-periphery structure in terms of participation inequality [38, 42, 43], which is more consistent with the theoretical issues associated with content development that we study here. Therefore, when we refer to core-periphery structure, we refer to participation inequality with respect to content development, not administrative roles.

Despite a culture of neutrality, evidence of bias and ideological segregation on Wikipedia is mounting [29]. As in many open collaboration communities, the demographics of content creators on Wikipedia do not reflect the demographics of society. Rather, most editors on Wikipedia are educated males [13]. For this reason, Wikipedians have taken steps to counter systematic bias and cultural perspective gaps by creating WikiProjects devoted to promoting subjects and points of view that have traditionally been marginalized [78].

The Wikipedia community has developed sophisticated cultural artifacts that communicate and enforce social norms and patterns of behavior among central contributors. The technological platform underlying the community afords networking of information such that links to pages with policies and rules enable eficient communication, rich with explanations of community enthymeme for new contributors and others at the periphery of the community. Policy forums, talk pages, and WikiProjects serve as platforms for coordination and social support [24, 39, 59]. Platform capabilities enable equal participation and optimize the advantages of diverse input by allowing message focus and triggering systematic rather than impulsive, biased contributions. Vast digital trace data created through the collaborative process can be used to identify bias and disparities in texts [94, 95], flag edits likely to reduce bias [85], and develop actionable models that suggest strategies for improving article content [91]. Automated content editing bots contribute to this process in ways that, paradoxically, reduce source, selection, and content bias, while increasing influence disparities [99]. Developed by central contributors, bots are designed based on developers’ values to enforce the core’s culture. More information about Wikipedia’s culture and core-periphery structure can be found in Online Supplemental Appendix A.

Despite eforts and tools to reduce bias, bias still exists in content created on Wikipedia. Only 19.3 percent of Wikipedia profiles featured women, as opposed to 29.9 percent of biographies in an online biographical database [49], indicating selection bias against women on Wikipedia. As a follow-up, researchers systematically examined profiles for 18,495 individuals listed in the Chambers Biographical Dictionary, which focuses on historical figures from the 18th and 19th centuries, and concluded that the odds of being omitted from Wikipedia were slightly higher for women than for men, but that article length did not difer by gender [78]. More recently, a study found no evidence of selection bias in information on Wikipedia about women in high-profile positions; however, this same study found evidence of categorization and content bias (e.g., females profiles were less likely to be cited when the woman was mentioned in another Wikipedia article and were more likely to discuss domestic issues than were males’ profiles) [90].

Looking at this research over time, gender bias against women appears to have decreased in recent years, coinciding with increases in anti-bias activism by central contributors on Wikipedia. Building on the strong foundation of content research on Wikipedia [e.g., 30, 62], more nuanced, theory-driven understanding of information bias on Wikipedia can be developed to explain how bias in open collaboration communities reflects ongoing tensions between central and peripheral contributors.

## Hypotheses Development

Despite a culture of neutrality, commitment to neutral point-of-view ideology difers among central versus peripheral members, resulting in a gender bias tug-of-war on Wikipedia. This research seeks to explain the consequences of central-periphery tension and whether oversimplistic anti-bias activism by those central to the community is causing an overcorrection where reverse bias favors the minority. To understand bias on Wikipedia, we look at selection, source, and content bias as well as influence disparities. We then examine revision histories by core and peripheral contributors to identify which group is responsible for types and patterns of bias over time.

Selection bias against members of minority groups (e.g., women) in information difused through traditional media has been well documented. For example, minorities tend to be underrepresented in information difused through historical records, reference works, and encyclopedias [78]. Selection bias in information difused through traditional media is often attributed to the concentrated ownership of media platforms and to the power media owners have to silence voices they disagree with [64]. Contrary to traditional media, digital media have been found to give voice [23]. More specifically, digital media give voice to underrepresented groups, such as women, that traditional media may silence [15, 98]. In doing so, digital media alter societal power structures such that the narrative of the minority may overpower the narrative of the majority [97]. This efect is exacerbated by central Wikipedia activists with administrative privileges to block peripheral contributors whose contributions tend to favor the majority.

At the same time, lean digital media may quiet the voices of individuals with less formal education, such as peripheral contributors, whose written contributions often lack the refinement and grammatical astuteness to garner attributions of credibility [1, 32], thereby empowering the more educated, central content creators to set the tone. Higher levels of educational attainment are associated with decreases in expressions of prejudice [19], decreases in adherence to traditional gender roles, and increases in feminist consciousness and activism on behalf of minorities [6]. Thus, we expect to find more information relating to the minority group than the majority group on Wikipedia.

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Selection bias in information generated by the open collaboration community will advantage the minority group and disadvantage the majority group.

Source bias occurs when content creators cull information from sources that are unreliable or reflect a narrow range of values and opinions [64]. The most reliable sources (e.g., peer-reviewed journals) are often challenging to digest. Novel sources citing less popular opinions (e.g., niche websites) may feature unreliable information and are susceptible to removal by bots. Consulting a variety of sources in a way that balances the limitations of each requires a great deal of time and efort and may require skills developed through formal education, which peripheral contributors may not have. Learning about new topics is a costly endeavor [88], but individuals will spend time and energy to contribute to open collaboration communities when they are driven by prosocial motivation [45], as is the case for central Wikipedians engaging in WikiProjects to end systematic bias against women and other minorities. Thus, we predict that those in the minority group, as the focus of prosocial agendas of educated contributors, garner more efort, resulting in greater numbers of sources and more reliable sourcing. We expect the opposite for those in the majority group given the lack of precedent from which to base activism around promoting fair, multi-sourced representations of those in the majority.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Source bias in information generated by the open collaboration community will advantage the minority group and disadvantage the majority group.

Within Wikipedia, central contributors are likely to focus sustained attention on profiles representing those in the minority for prosocial and ideological reasons. Alternatively, peripheral contributors are likely to give attention to profiles of those in the majority, who are more common. For instance, antifeminism on Wikipedia may spur attention to profiles of male CEOs by peripheral contributors, though it is unknown whether this attention is sustained as contribution [18].

Though peripheral contributors collectively make the most edits on Wikipedia, their attention is spread across many categories, ranging from science to popular culture. Central contributors, on the other hand, systematically contribute to biographies of individuals who are part of a marginalized group. Guided by WikiProjects, central contributors coordinate eforts to promote consistency and efectiveness of anti-bias activism eforts. Using bots, central contributors categorize information in ways that further increase influence disparities by overcoding information about individuals who are part of a minority group [99]. That is, while an article on a male CEO may be categorized under “CEO” and “Business,” one on a female CEO will likely receive more categories such as “CEO,” “Female CEO,” “Business,” and “Women in Business.” Influence disparities result as information about minorities becomes more visible within the larger body of knowledge and attracts more internal attention from multiple WikiProject groups [99]. Thus, we expect information about those in the minority group to gain influence and attention from Wikipedia contributors to a greater extent than information about those in the majority group.

Hypothesis 3 (H3): Influence disparities in information generated by the open collaboration community will advantage the minority group and disadvantage the majority group.

Traditional media research reveals a long-standing pattern of content bias against minorities, such as women in male-dominated fields [21]. Systematic research comparing content bias across media found that content bias problems associated with traditional media were exasperated on digital media—particularly, on lean digital media [64]. Examples of content bias found in open collaboration communities include stereotypical, domestic portrayals of women [90] and diferences in emphasis on career controversies across professionals of diferent nationalities [10]. Biased content often features expressions of emotions or exaggerations that undermine neutrality. The culture of neutrality and activism on the behalf of women and other minorities counters dominant cultural tendencies toward bias against women on Wikipedia. Systematic eforts by those at the core of the community to not only monitor and remove emotional and prejudicial content, but also add flattering content, may positively skew information about women.

Content problems exist not only in information about women, but also in information about men. This is especially true in content produced by peripheral contributors given that those with less formal education are more likely to write using emotion and opinion than those with formal education [86]. Yet, educated central contributors are not likely to actively remove this bias from profiles about those in the majority. Thus, we expect information about those in the minority group to feature less content bias than information about those in the majority group.

Hypothesis 4 (H4): Content bias in information generated by the open collaboration community will advantage the minority group and disadvantage the majority group.

Research suggests that core and peripheral contributors hold diferent biases and values [90, 100]. Specifically, peripheral contributors may add content spontaneously without critical thought as to whether the content is biased [100]. This makes contributions by the periphery prone to common types of bias, such as bias against minority groups. Core contributors, on the other hand, are more likely to be cognizant of community guidelines and anti-bias initiatives [39, 100, 101]. Core contributors on Wikipedia also tend to be more educated than the general public [13] and therefore less likely to hold anti-minority biases [19] and more likely to engage in activism to counter bias against minorities [64]. Thus, we expect that the peripheral group will contribute content that is biased against minority groups, and that the core group will contribute content that is biased against majority groups.

Hypothesis 5 (H5): Bias against the minority group in information generated by the open collaboration community will be driven by peripheral contributors, while bias against the majority group will be driven by core contributors.

The ofline community literature argues that the core group exists as a by-product of the process of founding of the community [57, 58]. Without an existing community to provide value for participation, the founders must overcome startup costs and exert energy without receiving benefits from participation. Once the community has attracted enough members to sustain itself, then these founders become the core group and the leaders of the community. Early theories of online communities suggested that similar dynamics also explained the presence of a core group in online discussion forums [43] and open-source software projects [69].

More recent research, however, suggests that core groups in peer production communities like Wikipedia may have a somewhat diferent origin as a result of the nature of the information artifact the community is creating [37, 38]. Whereas online discussion forums require a critical mass of participants [43] and open-source software projects need a founding vision [69], information artifacts on Wikipedia can simply emerge over time by preserving the independent contributions of participants [38]. In this situation, the core group is not the founder of the community; the core group emerges over time as the information artifacts develop and gain in value. As content gains in value, new contributors join both to contribute to that content, as well as to adopt, shape, and protect the valuable content that the disparate contributors have created [37, 38]. Thus, the core group emerges to nurture and protect the content that disparate contributors have established, and the role of the core in establishing the content (and its influence on bias) will strengthen over time as this emergent core group grows in authority.

Hypothesis 6 (H6): The relative influence of the core group will strengthen over time, and the relative influence of the periphery group will weaken over time.

## Method

This research investigates gender bias in information about Fortune 1000 CEOs on Wikipedia by operationalizing the four dimensions of the digital discourse bias framework through archival data. We test six hypotheses by comparing group means between CEO genders. Our findings are then validated by using alternative measures as robustness checks and conducting post-hoc analyses. In the next section we review data collection and measurement procedures before discussing findings and additional analyses.

## Sampling and Data Collection

Research has documented gender bias against women in male-dominated fields such as information systems [4], finance [67], and athletics [34]. In business, barriers for women are so pervasive that the phrase “glass ceiling” was coined as a metaphor for the “transparent barrier which prevents women from moving up the corporate ladder past a certain point” [70, p. 321]. In fact, women make up only 5 percent of Fortune 1000 CEOs. Even when women do break through the glass ceiling to become CEO, they often face unique challenges relative to their male peers. These challenges have been illustrated by scholars using a metaphor—“glass clifs”—to describe the phenomenon wherein “women may be being preferentially placed in leadership roles that are associated with an increased risk of negative consequences” as a company board decides to take a chance on a nontraditional CEO, given a precarious and often desperate situation [14, 81, p. 86].

Feminist theory explains that women have traditionally been valued by society based on their contributions to the domestic sphere (e.g., as homemakers), while men have been found notable based on contributions to the public sphere (e.g., as professionals) [56]. Although societal attributions of competence and expectations of gender roles in the public versus domestic sphere have evolved throughout history, some underlying biases remain [56]. Subtle cues embedded in information are interpreted by individuals and used to create impressions of organizations and organizational leaders [93]. Discrimination faced by female entrepreneurs seeking business loans [22], female job applicants [27], and female employees during performance evaluations [8] and compensation negotiations [65] demonstrates the real-life consequences of gender bias.

These consequences extend beyond personal implications for CEOs. Organizations with female CEOs can also be disadvantaged by discrimination because shareholders are often less willing to pay top dollar for shares in female-run corporations [44]. When individuals are presented with information containing gender bias, the bias embedded in the information not only colors the individuals’ perceptions of the focal topic, but also becomes a subconscious frame that is applied in other contexts [17]. In addition to afecting investors’ opinions of the focal CEO, information with gender bias against one female CEO may result in a “guilt by association” phenomenon in which investors become less likely to invest in any firm run by a female CEO [16]. Gender bias against a male CEO would be atypical.

Given the historical prominence of gender bias in the business world, we focus on the chief executives of top U.S. firms on the 2015 Fortune 1000 list. To make comparisons by gender, we gathered data from Wikipedia on the population of 50 female CEOs and four corresponding samples of male CEOs in spring of 2016. Matched pairs are identified as follows: (1) male+1, male CEOs of firms ranked one higher than a female counterpart’s firm; (2) male-1, male CEOs of firms ranked one lower than a female counterpart’s firm; (3) male-i, male CEOs in the same industry as a female counterpart with the closest ranking firm; and (4) male-p, male CEOs who were predecessors to their female counterpart at the same firm. When studying treatment of gender minorities, sampling individuals who are part of a minority group in terms of sexual orientation can muddle findings, as gender bias may be a response to perceptions of femininity or masculinity that are influenced by sexual orientation [60, 84]. Thus, we omit one CEO gender pair.

For each CEO, we captured the text of the corresponding Wikipedia biography (i.e., all information bounded between the article title at the beginning of the page and the references section at the end of the page). In accompaniment to the textual data, we also collected metadata about each article via XTools, a statistical service publicly available for free through the Wikimedia Foundation. XTools is an application program interface (API) for Wikipedia that provides real-time data and has been used by prior research investigating bias [35]. Additionally, we separately collected the edit histories for CEO articles using Wikipedia’s export pages tool [20]. Edit histories enable a more granular analysis using revision-level data. These data include 16,172 revisions by 6,415 editors, over a span of 13 years. Reverted edits are excluded, as they often indicate the correction of vandalism or revisions made in error. Also, the export is limited to the first 1,000 revisions, which afects data availability for a few CEOs. The earliest revision is dated January 22, 2003, and the most recent, March 1, 2016, corresponding with data collection.

## Measurement

Selection bias references systematic diferences in information selected for difusion, as indicated by missing or incomplete information. Prior research has studied selection bias by measuring whether specific perspectives and topics were consistently addressed or ignored in public discourse [64]. We operationalize selection bias by measuring diferences in omitted profiles between male and female groups.

Source bias references systematic diferences in the quantity or reliability of sources from which information is constructed and verified. Information has less source bias if compiled from a wide variety of sources as opposed to few or narrow sources [64]. We operationalize source bias in two ways—number of sources and source reliability. Number of sources is a count of the sources in the reference section for each profile. Source reliability is measured by creating codes to classify source types [76] and manually ranking source types by reliability. Working together, the first two authors sorted sources into similar categories until clear themes emerged, revealing twelve higher-order codes. The twelve source categories are ranked in terms of credibility as follows: tier 1: (most credible, w = 1.00) academic journals, publications backed by academic associations or organizations, government websites, and academic websites; tier 2: (w = 0.75) industry websites, magazines, newspapers, and news agencies or organizations; tier 3: (w = 0.50) corporate websites, information services websites, and documents written by an individual or group without organizational backing; and tier 4: (least credible, w = 0.25) “other” (e.g., blogs). Rankings are used for constructing a score for each profile. Each rank of sources is assigned a reliability weight, denoted w, and is then multiplied by the percent of sources in that tier and summed.

Influence disparities reference systematic diferences in the attention paid to information in a community. Editor contributions reflect the extent of sustained attention that an article receives from the Wikipedia community. Influence disparities are measured by comparing edit frequency (i.e., average number of days between edits) and total edits between male and female profiles.

Content bias references systematic diferences in content (e.g., neutrality of content). We operationalize content bias using semantic measures obtained through the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) 2015 software [72, 73, 74], which has been utilized in similar research [64]. LIWC provides four aggregate semantic measures derived from published research and expressed as standardized scores: analytical thinking, clout, authenticity, and emotional tone [73]. Analytical thinking, clout, and emotional tone can reveal insights into how a subject is portrayed, while authenticity can reveal instances of deceptive or exaggerated information.

## Article-Level Analysis and Findings

Our first four hypotheses involve the article as a whole, which we test using summary profile data. First, we evaluate the pairwise association of omitted profiles between genders using McNemar’s test [52] to determine whether there are systematic diferences in selection bias. Results in Table 2 show statistically significant diferences between female and male CEOs $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , suggesting that gender pairs are on average 22 percent more likely to be missing a male profile versus a female profile. Thus, H1 is supported.

For H2 through H4, we evaluate the practical significance of gender bias using efect size. Appropriate for comparing our male samples to a female population, we calculate Cohen’s d [5, 12] between CEO gender pairs and evaluate results by the recommended thresholds for small, medium, and large efects [12, 79]. ## Revision-Level Analysis

In our final two hypotheses, we examine how gender bias is shaped by diferent types of Wikipedia contributors—core and peripheral—by analyzing pages’ edit histories. To distinguish between the two types of contributors, we quantify Wikipedia users’ involvement in the editorial process by their total edits. Among the overall editor pool in this study, core and peripheral contributors are partitioned by one standard deviation (s = 6.2) above the mean number of edits (x̅ = 1.9), rounded up. In other words, the cutof for any editor to be considered a core contributor is nine or more edits (rounded up from 8.2), which represents 3.7 percent of all Wikipedia users in this study, who are responsible for 34.8 percent of all edits. Peripheral contributors are defined as contributors who are not a part of this core group. As a robustness check, we also tested the efects of defining the core group as two and three standard deviations above the mean number of edits, and the results are qualitatively similar.

Given the focus on specific contributors, our analysis for these hypotheses occurs at the revision level, necessitating slightly diferent measures to assess our various types of bias. First, we measure selection bias as page creation, a binary indicator of who performed the first edit on a particular page. This initial edit eliminates an omitted profile, which is how we operationalized source bias at the article level. Second, we measure source bias in terms of source changes, edits performed that either added or removed a source. This measures who contributed the various sources that were measured at the article level for source bias. Third, we measure influence disparities in terms of total edits by each type of contributor, which is a relatively straightforward conversion of our article-level operationalization.

We did not operationalize content bias at the revision level, however. Our measures of content bias focus on the article holistically, on the overall sentiment of the article, which would have been dificult to capture in individual edits. Previous research has also shown that core contributors often make revisions that refine and protect content once developed [38]. Those types of edits would have played a critical role in developing and maintaining content bias, but they would not have been reflected in revision-level edits. Furthermore, even if it was theoretically justified to capture content bias at the revision level, it would have been computationally prohibitive to calculate and compare changes in sentiment on every article revision in our sample. Thus, we measure only three of four types of bias at the revision level to test H5 and H6.

In H5, we predict that bias against the majority group is driven by core contributors, and bias against the minority group, by peripheral contributors. The total efect size is reported using Cohen’s $d ,$ and partitioned into component efects for core $( d _ { c } )$ and peripheral $( d _ { \mathrm { p } } )$ contributors (Table 4). When we partition efects by core and periphery user designations, core contributors are clearly driving selection bias, source bias, and influence disparities, advantaging females and disadvantaging males. Peripheral contributors show a marginal contribution to bias. For example, the results for source changes attribute small negative efects by peripheral contributors in two male samples. H5 is partially supported.

The final hypothesis (H6) explores the relative influence of core versus peripheral contributors on bias over time. Previous research has shown that articles and authors are interconnected in an ecosystem of content and contributors within particular topics [36]; therefore, we operationalize time over the entire ecosystem of content, not relative to the life cycle of a single article. To test this hypothesis, we first calculated cumulative gender bias efects over the past 10 years (2007–2016) between female CEOs and four male samples. We then split the sample into early (2003–2011) and later (2011–2016) stages using the midpoint year, 2011. The midpoint year also provides a relatively even split between content development, with 57.9 percent of the profiles started before 2011 and 42.1 percent of the profiles started after 2011. Table 5 shows our analysis, reporting weighted average efect sizes [50].

While both groups afect bias in Time 1, we see a relatively stronger influence of the core group on bias in Time 2. In Time 2, the core group is the key driving factor for selection bias, source bias, and influence disparities. We observe significant influence in bias among both core and peripheral groups in T2. These results suggest that it takes time for the core group to emerge and broadly exert its influence over the content. Thus, H6 is supported.

To further explore selection bias, we measure profile lifespan as the number of days between a profile’s creation and the date of data collection. ## Source Bias (H2)

Information bias decreases when a variety of sources are consulted [64], and a greater saturation of inline citations in an article indicates trustworthy sourcing [66]. We construct a measure of citation density using the proportion of inline citations to paragraphs of text in each article. Medium and large efects are observed across groups, suggesting that male profiles are less saturated with inline citations.

## Influence Disparities (H3)

In our additional analysis of influence disparities, we investigate the editing volume of registered and unregistered Wikipedia users. We measure anonymity ratio, which represents the ratio of edits by registered to unregistered users. Though anonymous contributors may provide credible information, registered users are generally considered more trustworthy and accountable [3, 47]. Registered Wikipedia accounts include humans and bots with usernames, while unregistered accounts remain anonymous and are shielded by IP addresses. Large, medium, and small efects suggest that male profiles contain a smaller proportion of edits by registered users compared with female profiles. These findings support H3 and suggest that registered Wikipedia users have attended more to female CEOs and contributed to the current state of disparity with male CEOs.

## Content Bias (H4)

Prior research comparing multiple measures of content bias found that the number of words in an article, though a simple measure, is an accurate predictor of content bias [7]. We use words per sentence to quantify content bias. Medium and small efects show that male profiles contain shorter sentences than female profiles, suggesting that content bias is disadvantageous to male CEOs. This finding provides additional support for H4.

## Contributor Bias (H5) and Bias Over Time (H6)

We scrutinize our classification of central and peripheral contributors by testing H5 and H6 at additional partitions of two and three standard deviations above the mean number of edits (14.4 and 20.6, respectively). Across male samples, contributor efects display similar directional patterns at all partitions, with slight fluctuations in magnitude. Between one and two standard deviations there is a noticeable jump in selection bias efects, but this does not change the bias. This robustness check confirms our findings for H5. Concerning bias over time, average contributor efects follow the same general pattern at all partitions. The only exception is the early life cycle efect (T1) of content bias at three standard deviations, which does not alter results. We consider the findings for H6 robust.

## Discussion

Comparisons of Wikipedia profiles for female versus male Fortune 1000 CEOs revealed that selection, source, and content bias and influence disparities on Wikipedia advantage females and disadvantage males. Though early research found evidence of bias against women on Wikipedia [49], more recent research has found a minimal amount of evidence of gender bias [78, 90], and we find evidence of bias against men.

The evolution of the nature of bias on Wikipedia can be explained using the notion of core-periphery tensions. In communities with a core-periphery structure, tensions between the values and goals of each group are inevitable. On Wikipedia, peripheral contributors tend to contribute traditional types of bias (e.g., bias against women). Central contributors, however, are driven by community culture to combat traditional types of bias. While the peripheral contributors, who do most of the editing on Wikipedia, initially “won” the gender bias tug-of-war, as evidenced by early reports of gender bias on Wikipedia, eforts by the core to return to a state of neutrality pushed the community away from bias against women. Over time, central contributors have overcorrected to the point where bias against men is becoming an issue. Thus, we see that while the culture of the periphery dominates at first, it is sustained contributions and dedicated eforts by the core that shape the community in the long term.

## Theoretical Implications

This study addresses concerns that in seeking to correct issues of bias against minority groups, central contributors on Wikipedia might be overcorrecting such that uncommon types of bias (e.g., bias against the majority) emerge. While the focal study explores this issue in the context of Wikipedia, the implications of this research extend far beyond Wikipedia, and even beyond open collaboration communities. The phenomenon of coreperiphery tensions and tug-of-wars can be observed in many information systems (IS) contexts, including blogs, open source software forums, and micro-blogs [53]. In fact, the core-periphery structure has been studied in by sociologists for some time [9]. Thus, understanding of how this tug-of-war plays out and the potential for overcorrection against cultural biases of the periphery contributes to understanding in multiple fields of study.

Scholars have a robust understanding of how bias is embedded in information difused through traditional media, yet there is much left to be discovered about bias in information difused through digital media. Information systems scholars have called for research into how information digitization is afecting the trustworthiness of information consumed by the public [64]. In this manuscript, we show how the digital discourse bias framework can be used to study information bias and disparities in digital contexts. This study provides a foundation that, along with future research, can be applied to develop theories explaining why, how, and when platform-specific structural constraints and content restrictions lead to bias in digitally co-produced information [64]. By focusing on Wikipedia and its unique culture, we reveal how open-sourced governance of information co-production can shape information in ways very diferent from traditional media processes due to core-periphery cultural tensions.

Notably, bias stemming from structural constraints online may advantage and disadvantage groups in ways opposite of what existing theory predicts. This finding contributes to theories of digital information bias and disparities. Despite Wikipedia contributors’ demographic makeup being largely educated males, the minority group we sampled benefited from structural constraints and content restrictions while the majority group composed of educated males was disadvantaged. This finding is surprising in light of theories of homophily, which suggest that individuals tend to befriend, work with, and share information with individuals with whom they have much in common [61]. It could be inferred that individuals would also write about individuals with whom they have much in common, but the finding to the contrary contributes to understanding of internet culture as a culture that values novelty, uniqueness, and exceptional cases.

Scholars have suggested that the quality of digital information is rooted in platform design [83] and ofered design solutions for assessing and promoting information quality [89]. However, Wikipedia is designed in a way that should reduce or even eliminate bias as information is filtered to fit a template. This process of systematically structuring information should result in formulaic profiles with only minor variations. Yet, this study demonstrates that information found on Wikipedia is neither formulaic nor objective. Rather, information generated by open collaboration communities will be shaped and reshaped over time by contributor values as core-periphery tensions play out, even when platforms are designed for uniformity.

## Practical Implications

Early in the community life cycle, the periphery may tend to dominate due to the sheer volume of contributions and influence, but in the long term, the core’s perseverance and activism are likely to overwhelm the periphery. For Wikipedians and others in communities organized by a core-periphery structure, eforts should be taken to monitor the overall state of the community to audit for balance of core and peripheral values and biases so that the community does not veer too far to any extreme. Platform managers can incentivize balance through platform design. For example, dashboards could provide realtime information to editors about the current state of bias across groups of articles. Bots may be developed to prompt editors to investigate potential patterns of bias and take action where needed.

Given that the core-periphery structure has been observed by sociologists studying society at large, this research has practical implications for civic understanding and government leadership as well. Namely, eforts to prevent ideological domination should consider both peripheral and core social groups. Balance is needed.

Content created by open collaboration communities is being consumed, and often trusted, by internet users from all walks of life, including professionals engaging in work tasks [25, 40, 54]. The findings presented in this manuscript demonstrate that bias embedded in information on Wikipedia may come in nontraditional forms. Thus, apparent lack of bias should not be relied upon as an indicator of neutrality online, whether the source is Wikipedia or other open collaboration communities such as Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, or YouTube. Notably, this study reveals bias opposite of that commonly found in traditional media sources, which suggests that individuals may be able to counter the efects of bias in each realm by consulting both digital information created by open collaboration communities and information created through traditional means and difused through traditional media. Consulting multiple and varied sources across digital and traditional media may reduce the efectiveness of biased frames and help individuals evaluate information more objectively.

Given the severity of consequences of biased portrayals of organizational leaders, organizations should monitor open collaboration communities such as Wikipedia as well as other sources of information stemming from core-periphery structured communities. Many open collaboration communities have codes of conduct to which content creators adhere, allowing individuals and organizations to voice concerns about bias. In particular, Wikipedia has designated pages on which organizational members can leave comments for editors and explain concerns. Bringing attention to errors of commission will allow contributors a chance at redemption.

## Limitations and Future Research

This research highlights the tensions between central and peripheral groups in open collaboration communities and explains that core ideologies are dominant in the sample studied. Future research is needed to understand how tensions between core and peripheral values play out over time and how these tensions are resolved. We found that the core group was able to shape Wikipedia according to its ideology. But, does this tension exist in communities with weaker culture? Under what conditions does each group dominate? How are tensions resolved? How can new member socialization processes be implemented to address ideological discrepancies and promote cultural convergence? Future research addressing these issues in a variety of virtual communities would provide value.

Future research operationalizing each facet of bias in increasingly sophisticated ways is needed. Though all profiles for female Fortune 1000 CEOs were sampled, along with profiles for four matched pair samples of male Fortune 1000 CEOs, this represents only a fraction of over five million English-language articles on Wikipedia. Much of the research examining Wikipedia has taken advantage of the vast of amounts of data Wikipedia provides to conduct large-scale investigations at a macro level [e.g., 63, 77]. This sort of macro-level view was imperative when understanding of open collaboration community phenomena was at a nascent stage. Now that foundational knowledge around open collaboration exists, scholars are recognizing the need for finer-grained examination such as qualitative analyses of single Wikipedia articles [e.g., 38] or groups of Wikipedia articles [e.g., 96]. Future research should also harness the value of big data on Wikipedia to take a closer look at multiple smaller samples with commonalities (e.g., the gender or race of the focal subject) to identify patterns in bias across similar groups of information. More research is needed to test these hypotheses among other groups (e.g., entertainers and political leaders) to see under what conditions, and for which minority groups, the pattern of structural constraints disadvantaging the majority group holds. Regardless, the findings presented in this manuscript demonstrate the inadequacy of traditional media theories to predict bias in information found online and suggest that more nuanced views of bias in open collaboration communities are needed.

## Conclusion

While individuals and organizations are increasingly consuming, trusting, and basing decisions on information generated by open collaboration communities and difused through digital media, little is known about the types of systematic bias contained in such information. The objective of this research is to investigate information bias in one context—the Wikipedia profiles of Fortune 1000 CEOs. The findings reveal an atypical type of bias (i.e., bias disadvantaging the majority group and advantaging the minority group). Specifically, the findings reveal systematic diferences in selection bias, source bias, influence disparities, and content bias in information about CEOs of diferent genders. Profiles of female CEOs are more likely to exist, benefit from editors’ eforts, be appropriately sourced, gain more influence, and have more neutral content than profiles of male CEOs. These findings are explained using the concept of the coreperiphery network structure. Tensions between central contributors at the core of the open collaboration community and peripheral contributors at the margins may result in diferent types of bias at diferent stages of the information generation life cycle. While peripheral contributors dominate early, the core is likely to dominate in the long term through systematic activism unless steps are taken to balance central and peripheral influence. Just as there is an optimal level of engagement [41] and turnover on Wikipedia [77], there is an optimal balance of core-periphery influence. This research demonstrates the potential for unanticipated forms of bias in information found online and calls for future research into antecedents and consequences of core-periphery tensions and information bias.
