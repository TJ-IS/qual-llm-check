# Governance lessons from the experience of telecentres in Kerala

Shirin Madon<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics and Political Science, London, U.K.

Correspondence: Shirin Madon, Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, U.K. E-mail: s.madon@lse.ac.uk

## Abstract

Telecentres are seen as essential components of governance reform in the international development community and great optimism has been expressed in academic and policy literature regarding their developmental potential. The scope of our paper is to move beyond the confines of conventional governance ideology and to propose a broader conceptual lens through which to study the complexity of issues that need to be addressed in order to make telecentres sustainable. Following several years of experience with these projects, evidence shows that their long-term survival depends upon how interactions are managed between a host of players including the government, private entrepreneurs, international donors, telecommunications suppliers, local companies, civil society organisations and individual community members. This paper proposes that the sociology of governance approach is highly relevant for a study of telecentre sustainability. This theoretical approach is used as a lens through which to investigate issues regarding the sustainability of the Akshaya telecentre project in Kerala in terms of interactions between various groups of players. We adopt a practical reflexive-interpretive methodological approach to encourage the development and reshaping of theoretical ideas about governance through our empirical data. We identify five critical issues currently affecting sustainability of the Akshaya project focusing on how relations and exchanges have been managed over time. Our analysis has important theoretical and policy implications for the Akshaya project and more generally for telecentre initiatives launched as part of governance reform in developing countries.

## Introduction

The topic of telecentres has occupied centre-stage in much current policy and academic discourse as a way of aiding communities in rural parts of the developing world. Telecentres appear to have no universally accepted definition beyond the general concept of a physical centre to provide public access to long-distance communication and information services using a variety of techniques including telephone, fax, computers and the Internet (Whyte, 2000). Two main types of telecentre projects can be identified from the literature (Proenza et al., 2001). The first type of initiative includes the micro-enterprise telecentres or small phone shops, which have proliferated in parts of Africa, Asia and Latin America. These centres, typically run by small entrepreneurs, have been quite successful in generating revenue by offering basic telephone services and increasingly fax and Internet services (Proenza et al., 2001; Salvador et al., 2005). However, the majority of these types of telecentres are in the main cities and towns of the developing world and there is usually no explicit commitment to wider developmental goals. For example, Proenza et al. (2001) conducted a survey in 14 telecentres of this type in Peru. The principal finding from the study was that the centres were benefiting only those users who had the money, skills and interest to interact with the technology. Clearly, the leap is far too great to expect this type of telecentre initiative to have any noticeable impact on the mass of low-income people with little formal schooling in rural parts of the developing world.

The second type of telecentre initiative, the topic of this paper, offers greater scope to support socio-economic development by facilitating the provision of a wide variety of public and private information-based goods and services to rural communities. These telecentres (sometimes called multipurpose community telecentres) are designed to offer communication facilities, training on IT- and non-IT-related subjects, and to serve as local hubs for government information and services and for commercial activity. These centres, typically owned by local entrepreneurs, soon began to be scattered across various developing countries set up by international development agencies<sup>1</sup> and country governments as essential components of governance reform. In Africa alone, over 20 projects were implemented over the past few years in Ghana, Mozambique, Uganda, Benin, Tanzania, Zambia Zimbabwe and South Africa (Mayanja, 2003). Sood’s (2003) study found that telecentre installations are increasing at a faster rate within India than in other countries with over 30 such projects currently in existence – most of them in South India. In 1998, the Latin American and Caribbean region had fewer than 50 telecentres, while towards the end of 2002, the number was estimated at more than 6500 in that region (Delgadillo et al., 2002).

There is much optimism expressed in the literature about how telecentres can advance the agenda of governance as part of a development strategy. This agenda has often been propagated through the ‘good governance’ paradigm using such constructs as bridging the digital divide, promoting democracy, increasing transparency and accountability of government, and encouraging greater private sector involvement in civic affairs and in poverty reduction (Okot-Uma, 2000; Heeks, 2001). For example, there is optimism that telecentres can help bridge the digital divide by providing access points to poor communities in rural areas (Delgadillo et al., 2002; Kanungo, 2003). There is also widespread hope that telecentres can provide the public with better access to government information and services thereby improving efficiency of operations and improved systems of accountability among public servants (Roman & Colle, 2002; Bhatnagar, 2004). Telecentres are also promoted in order to increase the input of citizens into public sector decisions and actions thereby strengthening civil society and processes of democratisation (Whyte, 1999). Finally, telecentres are thought to offer new opportunities for small business enterprises leading to a promising source of revenue for local economic development enabling a reduction in transaction costs for farmers and local traders (Young et al., 2001). The launching of numerous telecentre initiatives in the developing world has been paralleled by significant efforts to develop performance criteria and evaluation frameworks to assess their impact, especially through the work of agencies such as the IDRC and ITU (Khumalo, 1998; Whyte, 1998, 2000). These studies have created a template for measuring the performance of telecentres based on contemporary governance measures of digital access, network coverage and traffic, economic costing, government services available online and private sector involvement in implementation.

However, while telecentre initiatives have been implemented for several years now in many parts of the developing world, experience shows that many projects have encountered greater problems than anticipated. Many of the externally funded telecentres have been topdown projects driven by a narrow deterministic view of how information technology can lead to socio-economic development, and very few centres are actually providing relevant information for local usage (Whyte, 2000; IIITB, 2005). The reality of telecentre experience to date suggests that government support for these projects has either been lacking from the start or withdrawn soon after the project has been launched (Harris et al., 2003; Colle, 2005). Moreover, many projects have been launched without making efforts to integrate with the local socio-political infrastructure (Iyer, 2005). This evidence paves the way for an urgent and important area of research regarding how telecentres can be perceived as important conduits for governance reform.

A central argument of this paper is that to understand the reality of telecentre activity in rural communities of the developing world and its impact on systems of governance, we need to move beyond the confines of the good governance paradigm that gives rise to them and that continues to frame the parameters used to study these projects. The growing body of work on telecentre sustainability provides a useful starting point for understanding some of the critical issues regarding the survival of telecentres over the long term. Most of this literature has focused on the issues involved in the achievement of financial sustainability of these centres, although discussion of their economic survival has also been linked to other non-financial issues such as the need for adequate staff capabilities to support the project and for community involvement (Khandker et al., 1995; Whyte, 2000;

Roman & Colle, 2002; Harris et al., 2003, Colle, 2005; IIITB, 2005). Evidence from this literature suggests that the achievement of telecentre sustainability is dependent on the complex exchanges and relations between state and society. For example, not explicitly mentioned but crucial for the attainment of sustainability in many of the projects documented in the literature is the role of the government in sourcing funding, providing other resources to support the initiative, fostering civil society participation and negotiating with private companies to promote commercial activities (Whyte, 2000; IIITB, 2005).

The scope of this paper is to go beyond the ‘telecentre as good governance’ rhetoric and to propose that a broader conceptual understanding of governance permits us to surface how, why and in what ways telecentres can address governance reform agenda. We proceed as follows. In the next section, we review telecentre sustainability literature and propose that ideas from the sociology of governance literature can provide intellectual tools for analysing telecentre sustainability beyond current parameters. The following section describes our methodology and this is followed by our case study of the Akshaya telecentre initiative in Malappuram, Kerala, based on fieldwork carried out by the author. The penultimate section then offers an analysis of the case specifically focusing on sustainability in terms of interactions between the numerous actors involved in the telecentre implementation. The final section proposes some implications drawn from our study for theory and practice in the specific topic area of telecentres, and more broadly for the study of information systems in development.

## Telecentre sustainability: a sociology of governance approach

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the literature on telecentre sustainability. The bulk of this literature focuses on financial sustainability in terms of the extent to which a telecentre project is able to generate funds for recuperating capital outlay and for sustaining operating costs (Khandker et al., 1995; Harris et al., 2003). However, financial sustainability is complicated because community telecentres in developing countries are typically a mix of public good and private service and this mix is usually reflected in the funding structure with most telecentres receiving at least some public funds during the start-up phase in the form of reduced tariffs and special investment in equipment and infrastructure (Whyte, 2000). Private sector players may include local entrepreneurs who invest their capital in the telecentre, small local businesses and larger corporates with whom telecentres need to negotiate for technology purchases and for encouraging commercial activity.

When telecentre revenues (including grants, in-kind support, and cash earnings) are greater than expenditure and assuming that this situation is likely to continue for at least 3 years, the telecentre is generally thought of as economically viable. Various business models reflecting a mix of public/private partnership have evolved to achieve financial sustainability in telecentre projects (Hunt, 2001; Reilly & Gomez, 2001; Roman & Colle, 2002). However, experience reveals that many pilot projects have a hard time achieving sustainability once the subsidies and grants end. This has been the case with a number of pilot centres supported by IDRC’s ACACIA initiative where public funding and international donor support fully or partially met initial costs of housing the telecentre, putting in suitable power and telecommunication supplies and equipping the telecentre. Another example is the Information Village Research Project in Pondicherry, South India, where 8 years on after its initial launch, the project remains completely dependent on external funding for its survival with self-sustainability unlikely to be achieved in the near future (IIITB, 2005).

Experience from telecentres in various parts of the world shows that apart from the costs of updating and maintaining equipment, commonly underestimated costs are those relating to staff training, particularly for activities such as education and business services, which depend on a more qualified staff. Budgeting becomes difficult because uncertainties are often built into costs, especially communication costs which are estimated by telecommunications suppliers and which can change suddenly with a change in government policy. For example, a preliminary study of USASA’s experience in South Africa showed that one of the major problems was in the prices charged for telecentre services, which could vary well below or above actual costs jeopardising the financial sustainability of the centres (Khumalo, 1998). Moreover, these figures take no account of collect calls or of incoming calls at the centre, which is a common use of pay phones in rural and small town Africa and which often results in the telecentre incurring fixed costs to provide a service without necessarily receiving a revenue (Kayani & Drymond, 1997).

On the revenue side, important considerations regarding financial sustainability concern how long the initial grant or public subsidy will continue and whether other sources of revenue will replace this often major initial funding. Whyte (2000) draws a distinction between revenues earned from the core business of a telecentre (which generally relates to connectivity and ICT services), and those earned from ancillary activities. Often the core business is unsustainable by itself and, over time, successful telecentres increase the number and volume of their ancillary activities depending on local demand. Attention is increasingly directed in the literature to ways of stimulating demand for services among the local community with the argument often being made that this demand should be reflected in the community’s willingness to pay for services (Roman & Colle, 2002; Harris et al., 2003). Indeed, one of the controversial ethical issues that has arisen from several telecentre projects is whether the rural population should be asked to pay a fee for services that have very clear business or personal uses (Etta & Thioune, 2002).

The issue of financial sustainability of telecentres is closely linked to social issues of accessibility and relevance. The identification, generation and access to relevant local content are reported to be of great importance for sustaining community interest in the project (Harris, 1999; Hudson, 1999). Some writers argue that it is important to pay particular attention to applications and content that are valued by key institutions and services already in the community like government departments and local private companies. For example, Bhatnagar (2004) argues that the generation of relevant local content necessitates greater linkage between the telecentre and local administration in order to identify applications to support growth in key economic sectors such as health, education and agriculture. Some telecentre projects aim to increase rural productivity, income and employment by establishing tie-ups with local companies. For example, a major focus of the NGO-led Community Information Centre initiative in Kuppam, Andhra Pradesh, is to train unemployed youth from the local community to produce low-cost products for the market such as garments, incense sticks and soap products (Muthukumaraswamy, 2004). For such projects, the ability of telecentre operators to act as community development officers critically affects the social sustainability of the project. The telecentre operator’s familiarity with locally relevant information networks for generating useful content also affects sustainability (Whyte, 2000). Whyte describes the growth of regional and national electronic networks such as HealthNet in Africa linking research with academic institutions, government, businesses and individuals for the dissemination of information on public health, medicine and the environment. Another factor affecting social sustainability relates to the telecentre operator’s ability to present content to the community in an affordable and locally appealing manner. For example, many operators have sought innovative ways of integrating traditional technologies such as radio with new technologies (Dlamini, 2004; Slater & Tacchi, 2004).

Linked to financial and social sustainability is the need to ensure that the telecentre is politically sustainable. The need for community participation in telecentre initiatives has been well documented by the IDRC through its longitudinal studies of participatory community involvement and social inclusion from different projects in the developing world (Whyte, 1999; Stoll et al., 2004). These studies show how civil society organisations such as cooperatives, NGOs, religious bodies and local media play an intermediary role between the government and citizens in mobilising participation from the community. A study of the Information Village Research Project in Pondicherry, South India, bears witness to the process by which the initiative has enabled citizens to have access to information previously controlled by traditional powerful groups (Kanungo, 2004). Along similar lines, an IIITB study of 14 telecentre projects in India concluded that relatively successful projects are those which undertake adequate community consultation in order to muster support for the initiative (IIITB, 2005).

While evidence shows that sustainability of these initiatives depends on the totality of connections between these players, telecentres continue to be implemented and evaluated based on a narrow politicoeconomic paradigm. Official Western aid policy on governance reform favours economic and political strategies, which seek to minimise the role of the state – a view that arises from a concern to overcome stagnation in developing countries caused by post-war, state-led development. International development agencies have urged developing countries seeking aid and loans to correspond to this governance ideology by privatising state corporations, encouraging competition, markets and private enterprises, downsizing the political apparatus, and promoting new management systems and improved accountability procedures (Wade, 1990; Leftwich, 1993; Rose, 1999). Conventional development ideology is also full of explicit reference to the liberal state model of Western democracy, which encourages strong anti-state and pro-civil society tendencies (Putnam, 1993; Hewitt de Alcantara, 1998; Court & Hyden, 2000, 2001). Governments and development agencies are urged to invest resources in building stocks of social capital in the hope that concerted action made possible by civic associations will enable citizens to engage state and market agencies more effectively (UNDP, 2002).

Many writers have challenged this ‘good governance’ perspective as biased and controversial. With regard to economic development policies, critics have argued that the neoclassical praise of newly industrialising countries has downplayed the important role that the institution of the state has played in imparting direction via the market mechanism (Wade, 1990; World Bank, 1997,

2002; Petiteville, 1998; Kaufman et al., 2003; Transparency International, 2003). On a political level, the argument is that the neo-liberal policy perspective does not apply equally well to many parts of the developing world for two reasons (Hewitt de Alcantara, 1998; Narayan et al., 2000; Jackson, 2001; Gronlund, 2003; La Porte et al., 2002). First, such a measure downplays the extent of social capital that prevails in a developing country setting where typically informal rather than formal associations have most value for citizens. Second, the neo-liberal doctrine neglects the important role of government in promoting civil society action in the context of developing countries (Evans, 1996; Varshney, 2001; Krishna, 2002). For example, in their study from 16 developing countries, Hyden et al. (2004) show that democracy and accountability can only exist where governments genuinely exercise authority and are able to enforce law and order required for civil political action. Still on a political level, Harriss (2001) criticizes the stance taken by aid agencies to downplay local political activity in the name of promoting grassroots civil society activity. Harriss’ argument is that civil society activity cannot flourish in a vacuum – it needs to be nurtured within a political process in which different groups of actors are all involved.

In recent years, the work of several governance scholars offers a broader theoretical approach to study processes of reform without subscribing to any particular economic or political ideology. The focus in these studies is on understanding how procedures and structures are constituted and managed between different players involved in governance activity (Stoker, 1998; Jessop, 1998; Hyden et al., 2004). One strand of literature in this area has focused on governance interactions in terms of policy, procedures and structures. For example, in their study, Hyden et al. (2004) show that a major aspect of governance reform is the extent to which the private sector is seen as a legitimate part of policy consultation processes by government. Other writers have focused on governance as interactions between implementers of development projects (World Bank, 1993; Heeks, 1998; Evans & Rauch, 2000). Evidence also shows that appropriate levels of interaction are needed to activate the stock of social capital that exists to make it more productive through mediating agents who have regular contact with state officials and market operators and who are familiar with their procedures and practices (Evans, 1996; Krishna, 2002).

The writers reviewed above have focused on governance as interaction more at a macro-level. Another strand of literature, from which we draw our analytical focus, derives from the recent work of sociology of governance writers who themselves have been inspired by many other areas of social inquiry where the concept of interactions has been used at a micro-level. The sociology of governance writers utilise the notion of interaction to conceptualise governance at the micro-analytical level of social relations (Kooiman, 1993, 2003; Rhodes, 1997;

Rose, 1999; Kjaer, 2004). For these writers, the process of governance constitutes a complex set of relations between social and political actors and entities including individuals, organisations and institutions. The relations are necessarily complex because each actor or entity has different interests and ambitions and as a result, all kinds of tensions and conflicts are articulated in these interactions. One of the most developed attempts to conceptualise governance in this sense can be found in the work of Kooiman (2003). Kooiman, drawing from other social theorists,<sup>2</sup> conceptualises an interaction between two or more entities in terms of action and structure (or context). Action implies an intention in the mind of the acting party and in this body of work a lot of importance is placed on understanding the way in which intentions are formed. The structural level refers to the context within which interaction takes place and can either enable or constrain it.

A basic assumption in Kooiman’s sociology of governance approach is that any individual or entity involved in governance activity forms images about what is being governed as a precursor to acting. An image is seen as a social construction of reality and can be extensive or limited in scope, be well articulated and shared between actors or exist implicitly in the background. Images are formed using many different types of information such as visions, theories, facts, assumptions, wishes, values and personal goals. No single actor has complete knowledge required to deal with governance challenges and it is assumed that interactions are based on mutual interdependence between actors. An important aspect of the sociology of governance approach is therefore to study how images are formed, sustained and changed over time. The degree to which these images, themselves created through interactions with other players, are made explicit affects the overall legitimacy of governance initiatives. Images also constitute the point of departure for the selection of governing instruments for implementing projects (such as legislation or more informal instruments such as speeches), and for eventual action taken by individuals and organisations with respect to the initiative (Kooiman, 2003).

For this paper, we suggest that the sociology of governance approach is highly relevant. Our focus will be on the process of image formation as it provides us with a broad lens within which to study the emergent and fluid nature of governance reform and gives us scope to investigate the less formalised, personalised types of exchanges prevalent in developing countries. Our review of telecentre sustainability literature suggests that the survival of these centres over the long-term depends upon the relations between a host of players including private entrepreneurs, the government, international donors, local banks, telecommunications suppliers, local companies, civil society organisations and individual community members. In the next section, we describe our research methodology that enables us to pose significant questions about the sustainability of telecentres and learning from the field.

## Methodology

Our methodological approach is based on the assumption that ground-level practical experiences to implement telecentres provide the potential for improved understanding of governance reform processes. We adopt a practical, ‘reflexive methodology’ in our study following Alvesson & Skoldberg’s (2000) usage of the term. This approach, which appeals to a broad range of qualitatively minded researchers in the social sciences, has two basic characteristics – interpretation and reflection. Interpretation implies that the multiple perceptions provided by participants involved in the field situation are taken to represent ‘reality’ and that these perceptions are then interpreted by the researcher according to her own theories about the domain of study. Reflexivity is viewed as an essential part of the research process by stimulating critical reflection on taken-for-granted theories through a consideration of the perceptual, theoretical, political and cultural circumstances that influence them. According to this approach, the researcher allows the empirical material to develop and reshape theoretical ideas rather than letting theories control or straitjacket research findings. By taking a sceptical approach to what appears at a superficial level to be an undisputed ideological view, fieldwork provides an important basis for the generation of new knowledge that improves our understanding of the phenomenon under consideration. We consider a reflexive-interpretive approach appropriate for our study. It allows us to take a critical stance against the currently dominant good governance ideology that frames the implementation of development projects like telecentres. At the same time, it allows us to maintain a certain degree of flexibility to accommodate ‘home-grown’ interpretations of governance issues to ‘speak for themselves’ without prejudging their relevance to theoretical knowledge of governance.

Our case study takes place in the South Indian state of Kerala. The choice of this state in India was deliberate as we believe that its long legacy of state–society interactions provides fertile ground for a study of telecentre activity. At the same time, the state provides an interesting opportunity to investigate how good governance policy prescriptions currently influencing the state are blending with ‘home-grown’ ways of governance within the state. Our case study is the Akshaya<sup>3</sup> telecentre project that was launched in 2002 as a pilot in Malappuram District, North Kerala, with the specific aim of stimulating rural productivity, income and employment. The author has been involved with this project from its inception together with other telecentre initiatives in India. In Kerala, she continues to study different aspects of e-governance ranging from back-end administrative reform to front-end e-services projects (Madon & Kiran, 2002; Madon, 2003). Fieldwork has been organised at roughly 6-month intervals over the past 2 years from December 2002. During the course of the research, a total of 98 interviews were conducted in Trivandrum (state capital) and Malappuram (the pilot site) with government officials, politicians, telecentre entrepreneurs, private sector employees and various citizens. Further details of the interviews are provided in Table 1 below. The table shows that in a number of cases, repeat interviews were conducted – that is, the same person was met more than once at different points in time. The overall purpose of the interviews was to understand the perception of the project among different groups of people.

For the interviews, we did not have a fixed schedule of questions, but encouraged the respondents to comment on issues as they related to their experiences, to identify new issues, and to describe how they were dealing with them. Over time, as we met people for the second and third time, there already existed a shared understanding between us and we could start the conversation from where we had left off at the last meeting. All the interviews were conducted face-to-face in the location of the interviewee’s workplace, in the telecentre or at the home of telecentre operators or citizens. None of the interviews were tape-recorded. The duration of the interviews ranged from a few minutes to a few hours and we were invariably interrupted by phone calls and by the entry and exit of various people coming into the office to meet the same person that we were interviewing. Although data collection was done mainly through semistructured interviewing, additional data was obtained through participant observation, attendance in public meetings, study of press reports, study of websites and various other sources.

## Case study

## Background

Over the past 50 years, Kerala has transformed itself from an extremely poor state ridden with caste and class conflicts into a state with high levels of social development indicators, a rich agrarian economy, a high degree of urbanisation and infrastructure development, and high foreign exchange remittances from migrant workers. There is an extensive literature about how the state contradicted the neo-liberal doctrine of rapid economic growth being the sole determinant of improvements in standards of living of ordinary people (Isaac & Tharakan, 1995). Kerala’s successful promotion of poverty alleviation has a history of several decades of sustained public action that began at the turn of the 20th century and spanned several decades in the form of social mobilisation of large sections of the population including the labouring poor (Parayil, 2000). The focus was not only on formal education and literacy but also on increasing awareness among communities about social and political issues, particularly health and lifestyle awareness (Kannan, 2000). The vigour and dynamism of civil society in Kerala are matched only by the size and activism of the state in areas such as land reforms, social welfare and legislation for the formal and informal labour market (Tornquist, 2000; Franke & Chasin, 2000). However, the extent to which Kerala’s unique development model can be sustained in the long run has become a serious question given the poor performance of the commodity-producing sector, high unemployment and low internal income generation, particularly in rural parts of the state (George, 1993; Tharamangalam, 1998).

Since the late 1990s, there has been an increasing expectation that information technology would act as an enabler for development of the state of Kerala and in 1999 the State Government set up the Information Technology Mission Group to selectively induct information technology into sectors of the economy and departments where there could be immediate and tangible benefits to the citizen. In 2002, the Kerala government received funds from the Asian Development Bank under its Modernizing Government & Fiscal Reforms Programme (MGP). The MGP strategy, derived fundamentally from good governance ideology, focused on the development of indicators for measuring outcomes using parameters such as transparency of documents available to the public, service-provision in terms of numbers of services offered to the public, public– private tie-ups in the provision of services, improved management and financial accounting practices in government agencies, and decentralisation of decisionmaking in local government bodies (MGP, 2003). Operating under the MGP strategy, the Kerala government identified a two-pronged strategy for improved governance in the state. A long-term strategy was launched for procedural simplification and administrative reform in local government, particularly following the devolution of powers to these agencies in the late 1990s (ADB, 2001). A medium-term strategy was launched involving high visibility applications for the public. One example of this type of application was the setting up of IT-enabled district payment counters for the collection of bills and government dues from citizens (Madon & Kiran, 2002; Madon, 2003). A second example was the launching of the Akshaya telecentre project at sub-district level, which is the focus of this paper.

## The launching of Akshaya

The seeds of Akshaya were sown in a proposal made by the 100 gram panchayats<sup>4</sup> members of Malappuram District to the Kerala IT Minister in April 2002 to use their budget for imparting district-wide computer literacy. The state government took the idea forward and converted it into a telecentre project using Malappuram as a pilot. Relative to other districts in the state, Malappuram is characterised by high population levels, low levels of overall social development, urbanisation and industrialisation. At the same time, the district accounts for the largest number of emigrants from the state resulting in high purchasing power from external remittances often visible in the form of large houses, gold shops and motor vehicles (KSITM, 2002).

Akshaya was fully launched in November 2002 with the overall aim of generating economic growth and creating direct employment first in Malappuram and then in the rest of the state through collaboration between the public and private sectors. This was to be achieved by addressing issues of ICT access, skills and relevant content in a phased manner. At the genesis of the project in December 2002, a meeting took place in which the 100 gram panchayat presidents, local politicians and religious leaders of Malappuram were invited to discuss the socio-economic relevance of Akshaya for the district. There was overall consensus that the project was conceptually sound although concerns were raised regarding the possible sidelining of the local councils in the wake of increasing entrepreneurial influence in the project. A series of workshops took place at the block level in the months that followed to bring together village and block-level<sup>5</sup> presidents and entrepreneurs to identify local socio-economic priorities.

The state agency KSITM (Kerala State Information Technology Mission) headed by the IT Minister and IT Secretary planned the project and created the initial suite of applications for Akshaya. KSITM also planned the geographical location of the centres in such a way that no house in the district was more than 3 km from a telecentre<sup>6</sup> with each centre servicing approximately 1000 families. Most entrepreneur franchisees for the centres were selected through a government tender announcement for the new project, while about 158 ecentres were created by converting existing cyber cafe´s. Around 1000 entrepreneurs showed interest in the project in December 2002, out of which 400 applications were finally received selection of entrepreneurs was carried out by the state government in conjunction with local village council members based on social commitment and entrepreneurial talent.<sup>7</sup> Each e-centre was created at the cost of about Rs. 200,000 (in \$4570) to the entrepreneur normally obtained through a bank loan facilitated by the government. Right from the start the KSITM field staff gave the signal to entrepreneurs that this was ‘their’ project. In the words of one of the mission coordinators:

we will not spoon feed entrepreneurs – they must be willing to work hard. If they are, then the government will support them.

For example, rent and equipment purchase of the computers for the e-centres became the responsibility of the entrepreneurs for which the government facilitated an IT Expo fair in 2003 where 45 IT companies participated. This provided the entrepreneurs with an opportunity to form pressure groups and to lobby and knock down the price of equipment. In May 2003, KSITM initiated bids for connectivity and around 75 providers proposed a variety of wired and wireless solutions. Given the hilly terrain, it was clear that rolling out a wired infrastructure would be impractical and expensive and eventually a North Indian IT company Tulip was chosen as the wireless systems integrator for the project.

## The first phase of Akshaya: e-literacy

The 630 centres started functioning from early 2003 with between 5 to 10 computers and other peripherals such as printers, scanners and web cam depending on funds of the entrepreneur. The initial phase of the project, which lasted until December 2003, was devoted to e-literacy.

Every household in the district was invited to send one member to its local e-centre to acquire training in the basics of computing through a local language 15-h CD-ROM-based e-literacy module designed by KSITM. Eliteracy training was state-subsidised with the village council paying the largest share of the total training costs (Rs. 120 per head, approximately \$2.8) to the e-centre franchisees towards the training while each trainee paid Rs. 20 (\$0.5). The village council was therefore seen as an important player in the project, although from time to time the KSITM team had to deal with sorting out tensions between the village and district councils as both wanted to be seen politically as ‘owners’ of the project. A critical component of the project from its inception to the end of the e-literacy phase was the grassroots campaigning of the implementing team which successfully mobilised communities to accept Akshaya as a project endorsed by the government even though the owner of the telecentre was a private individual. For many entrepreneurs, Akshaya had completely transformed their lives in terms of providing them with a livelihood and hope for expansion. With pride, one of the entrepreneurs in a remote fishing village told me:

For me, Akshaya is God-given. Everyday I now have an ‘office’ to go to.

The fact that the entrepreneur was a known and trusted figure in the local community attracted a large number of women trainees for e-literacy training, this was particularly unique in a conservative Muslim society such as Malappuram where women were often not allowed out without an escort. Indeed, in the words of one of the female trainees:

Apart from learning about IT, Akshaya has provided me with an opportunity for an outing. Before that, I was not allowed out of the house on my own.

Overall, by the end of the e-literacy phase in December 2003, around 500,000 people had received training and there was tremendous excitement and public enthusiasm for the project, which received coverage as a local television broadcast. The project was deemed a success in terms of achieving 100% family e-literacy meaning that one member of every household in the district had received training and was certified by the government as ‘e-literate’. However, once the e-literacy phase was over, there was a sort of vacuum in terms of how the centres could be used in the future. The message from the IT Secretary of State was clear:

The object of Akshaya was never simply e-literacy, but socioeconomic development.

However, it was not quite obvious how this would be achieved, although there were some signs that the e-centres were being already used for non-IT activities for which there was a clear demand. For example, many e-centres served as a meeting hub for the running of regular women’s clubs, kids’ clubs, tailoring clubs and betel leaf producers’ clubs on a payment basis.

## Moving beyond e-literacy: promoting commercial and government applications

Following on from the e-literacy phase, it was realised that computer access and training alone may not be adequate drivers for sustainability of these e-centres. Although entrepreneurs had recuperated about 50% of their initial investment in the e-literacy phase, new areas of telecentre activity were being sought with enthusiasm. In the words of the KSITM Director,

as soon as people start making money and society generates demand, Akshaya will become sustainable.

However, it was not obvious where this demand would come from. In many cases, entrepreneurs had taken initiatives to provide services according to local demand. For example, in one location where dengue fever was rampant at the time, the entrepreneur had made his ecentre a hub for the generation and distribution of information on the fever, its causes and cures. However, such activities were adhoc and dependent on the existence of a particular demand in the catchment area, and on the initiative of the entrepreneur. It was clear that in terms of its long-term survival, Akshaya was at a crossroads and there was an urgent need to manage expectations about what the project could deliver in the next phase.

In the latter half of 2003, the state government appointed the KSITM Director as the District Collector of Malappuram (the highest government official for the district) to oversee the project’s implementation in its critical second phase. It was decided that all e-centres would provide a set of basic services such as training and computer usage, but that the focus would be on generating a commercial revenue stream for the e-centres. This was targeted particularly at small local businesses rather than at large companies and multinationals. The 630 centres were divided into six groups with each village council having one from each group. One group focused on data digitisation (especially for the local administration under the state’s long-term strategy of administrative reform); a second on servicing activities for the newly constructed hardware park in Malappuram; a third on generating insurance, financial services and tourism-related activities; a fourth on multimedia, animation and design; a fifth on IT-enabled sector interventions (such as health mapping, ayurvedic plant cultivation information and market linkage support) and health kiosk/telemedicine; and the last group on product sales. A second round of specialised training was conducted in early January 2004 bringing together entrepreneurs of a particular group and imparting skills in the specialised commercial activity as well as in enterprise management, information technology and marketing.

The KSITM team had also started to consider local content development in key economic sectors as an important activity for the e-centres. Proposals were invited from content developers in agriculture, health, tourism, education and other sectors and links were made with domain experts from academia for advice on how to proceed in this direction. Recognising that usage of the Akshaya centres for the distribution of local content would require linkages with local administration, the District Collector used his authority to demand improvements in the internal functioning of local government departments. While at this stage it was not clear how these applications would be funded and what sort of synergies were needed with the local administrative apparatus, a first serious attempt at local content generation was made in the health sector with a pilot project undertaken at Cheekode Panchayat to conduct a door-to-door health-mapping exercise using medicinal monitoring devices and questionnaires to collect data. This data was entered in the e-centres and later analysed by health specialists to identify risk groups and to guide individuals with abnormalities to seek further treatment. A medical camp for people diagnosed with abnormalities was scheduled for September 2004, but eventually did not take place. An example of content development in agriculture related at the time to the efforts made by one panchayat to collaborate with an internationally renowned ayurvedic manufacturer to use the Akshaya e-centres to disseminate cultivation and marketing information. An example of content creation in education was the development of a CD-based educational programme for school leaving students with the support of panchayats. Among all the districts in Kerala, Malappuram has a high student ratio with over 22% of the population enrolled in schools and this application has been very popular in the e-centres with numbers of users reaching a peak during the Indian school summer vacation period in April and May.

While high-speed Internet connectivity was supposed to be delivered to all e-centres by January 2004, this was delayed due to a combination of administrative and technical issues. This meant that progress in generating revenue through commercial activities or from the delivery of local content had to be delayed and most e-centres had to rely on training as their biggest income source. About 40% of persons trained under the e-literacy module returned for more training. Most popular have been courses on software packages, particularly through a CD course prepared by the KSITM team in April 2004 called E-Vidya containing lessons on word processing and spreadsheets. This course was popular, especially among youngsters, who were willing to pay Rs. 450 (\$10.32) per person per course. There has also been some demand for online language courses in English, Hindi and Arabic and IT-based training modules in personality development, career planning and accounting. A few e-centres are able to generate some revenue from other activities such as digital photography and photo album making, although this is seasonal.

In August 2004, the Akshaya Enterprise Connect meeting was organised to coincide with the first Akshaya media launch and private enterprises were invited to attend. Large companies including Malayala Manorama, Indian Express, Western Union Money Transfer and other large groups solicited their interest in partnering with the government and selling their products through the Akshaya centres. For example, India’s second largest bank ICICI started negotiating with KSITM to use the e-centres to sell its financial products and policies online to interested community members. However, so far no revenue has been secured for the e-centres as contracts have yet to be signed with companies who have shown an interest in using the centres to sell their products and services. The District Collector Malappuram was disappointed in the slow take-up of these activities when he reported:

Despite the declaration of interest by companies at the B2B Meet in August 2004, promoting corporate interest is proving more difficult than we imagined.

Recently, some new e-governance applications have been launched in the Akshaya e-centres. Most notably, a system called E-Pay has been launched since early 2005 facilitating internet-enabled government payments similar to the current system which exists at district level. The assumption behind E-Pay is that the public at sub-district level would use this service if they perceive that the charge of Rs. 10 (\$0.23) per transaction is lower than the transaction costs they would otherwise incur for paying bills including travel and time. It is envisaged that in the future E-Pay will generate a periodic (monthly) usage pattern and consequently establish a recurring source of income for the centres, rather than occasional or seasonal revenue generated by many other of the current activities.

Although achieving broadband connectivity was delayed considerably in the Akshaya project, this has now been achieved in around 400 of the e-centres with the state continuing to underwrite the cost of usage. ## Analysis

A central feature of the Akshaya telecentre project has been the role of the state in creating and coordinating the network of public sector, private sector and civil society actors. The state provided basic software for the project including e-literacy software, E-Vidya, an e-payment gateway and locally relevant content, as well as providing Internet connectivity and hosting a platform for volume pricing of computers and peripherals. As the project unfolded, it has become increasingly clear that a critical stream of activities was necessarily for Akshaya to be selfsustainable both from a financial and socio-political point of view. Applications like E-Pay are more likely to generate a regular stream of income, but it is unclear that this will help to sustain the whole project. So it seems likely that sustainability of Akshaya will eventually depend on a mix of locally relevant content in application areas such as health, education and agriculture, and on non-government services like training and commercial activities to support, if not subsidise, the provision of e-government services. Our analysis tries to unravel the complexity of the relations and exchanges involved in the generation of these telecentre activities explaining why, at the current point in time, these two activities do not constitute a significant proportion of revenue for the entrepreneurs. Focusing on governance as interactions and on the process of image formation among actors, we identify from our case study five critical issues that currently affect the sustainability of Akshaya: (1) Building Corporate Confidence, (2) Working with Government, (3) Renewing Grassroots Campaigning, (4) Involving the Legislative System, and (5) Continuing support of Political Champions.

(1) Building Corporate Confidence: The building of corporate confidence is a necessary step for commercial activity to take off at the Akshaya e-centres and several players have a stake in its promotion. The government, fuelled by the mass media, has historically resisted private sector involvement in its affairs and strong anticapitalist sentiment continues to brew and periodically spark up in Kerala. The government has been explicit in portraying an image of Akshaya as a social development project targeted at citizens through the local village councils rather than advertising its public–private partnership aspect. This image has been well articulated to citizens and to local village councils from the initial launch of the project to subsequent meetings. But as the project unfolds, it is clear that its sustainability critically depends upon cultivating the interest of the private sector. The private sector has historically been cautious about investing in Kerala given its stringent labour laws and worker militancy. This has been one of the main problems the state has faced in promoting investment in the newly constructed technology parks in Kerala. At the same time, many companies are beginning to realise that a significant opportunity exists to tap the large, potentially lucrative consumer base in Malappuram. Some companies have already started to conduct market research surveys to target consumers for the sale of their products and services.

As the Akshaya project reaches its third year of implementation, new images of public–private partnership have entered the minds of governors based on optimism about the role of information technology in governance reform and based on confidence in Kerala’s entrepreneurial talent to absorb this opportunity. A lot of resources and effort have been directed towards making these images explicit by attracting corporate investment and tie-ups for promoting commercial activity in the Akshaya centres. For example, the Akshaya Enterprise Connect meeting saw a healthy declaration of intent by companies to use the Akshaya centres as agents for selling their products and services. However, evidence so far indicates that the declarations of intent have not yet translated into any concrete contracts with commercial terms of offer yet to be finalised. This may pose to be a serious threat vis-a\`-vis the generation of commercial activity to support the long-term sustainability of the telecentres.

One of the main reasons for this lack of corporate confidence is that companies find themselves having to negotiate contracts with a single entrepreneur. Given the historical mistrust of private companies in Kerala, it seems much more acceptable for a company to deal with a state-supported entity, rather than an individual. For this reason, KSITM has so far played the role of interacting with private agencies and other government agencies implicated in the commercial arrangement on behalf of the entrepreneur. A problem arises for the future because currently no organisation collectively represents the 630 individual entrepreneurs of the project. Although an Entrepreneurs’ Forum of 105 members (one from every village level panchayat) has been formed, it is more an informal organisation than a legal entity and all entrepreneurs do not accept it as ‘legitimate’ since many of them are excluded. Moreover, there is no hierarchy in the forum making it difficult for companies to identify a responsible person to approach. It is also relevant to note that there is great variation in companies that have expressed an interest in partnering with the Akshaya project from huge internationally renowned banks like ICICI to small herbal soap manufacturers with many of the smaller and more companies feeling intimidated about the whole negotiation process.

(2) Working with Government: For the Akshaya e-centres to serve as hubs for generating content to supplement growth in key economic sectors, it is apparent that there needs to be close interaction with government line departments. Although from the outset, an image was projected of Akshaya as a social development initiative and discussions had taken place involving local councils, no explicit channels of communication were established with local government departments. Drawing from our case study, we expand on two applications currently being developed for Akshaya e-centres, both of which depend crucially on collaboration with government departments: health mapping and digitisation of administrative records. In both cases, our analysis focuses on the critical issue of data quality, which needs to be addressed through improved processes of interaction between the e-centres and the traditional vertical orientation of government agencies.

There is recognition that health mapping, by characterising the distribution of various health conditions, can aid better targeting of health care and resources as well as bring about better monitoring of health programmes. Health mapping also encourages local community participation in healthcare decision-making by making freely available knowledge about health conditions and resources in the neighbourhood, and about schemes to which people may be entitled. Based on this image held by the District Collector, Malappuram and the team at KSITM, Akshaya entrepreneurs have been used to carrying out rapid health screening of local populations from two gram panchayats under the health-mapping function in order to refer suspected positives to a second screen, followed by treatment. The panchayat level health authorities are working systematically with and remunerating Akshaya entrepreneurs for data entry work. However, while explicit images of how health mapping can assist social development exist at the level of the project implementing team, this idea has not been discussed and debated with government health departments. Unless this happens, it is difficult to see how the concept of health mapping can realistically be integrated into existing public health planning and implementation systems. Without this integration, the application will never gain the legitimacy required to survive over the long term. An important issue regarding legitimacy of the application relates to data quality. If the data from the initial screening are to be used for mapping, then appropriate data validation activities need to be applied at the time of conducting the data collection exercise. It seems therefore that without establishing and managing interaction and information sharing between the project implementers and the government health department, the health-mapping application cannot survive.

The local panchayat bodies are currently outsourcing data entry work for birth and death registration to the Akshaya e-centres on a payment basis providing good revenue for some centres. Here again, an important issue that requires careful intervention relates to data quality. Registration records found in gram panchayat offices have been kept in manual registers often in multiple formats, notation and standards all dating back several decades (or a few centuries in the case of land records!). For example, the actual registration of the large majority of births in Kerala occurs in hospitals and the data is then transferred from there to the gram panchayat. However, over 90% of the data is reported to be of spurious quality (an issue which is much worse in other parts of the country where births take place at home and may not even be recorded). In the Kerala case, many people are given pet names and other statistical information collected related to the birth is very casually handled. Even more complex is the situation with death registration in cases where an autopsy is required but where the doctor is pressurised to keep that hidden because of the social stigma associated with unnatural death among certain communities. For these reasons, as little as 15–20% of the data is clean exposing a serious concern that before any digitisation of data is carried out, manual records must first be verified with regard to their integrity. However, this has created tensions among the gram panchayat staff who have been used to working with ‘dirty data’ and who resist any form of intervention.

In both these two examples, there lacks an explicit perception or knowledge base about the processes and work flows implicated in government departments. On the contrary, the tendency of actors is to ‘black box’ the functions and reality of government processes and to generate new e-centre applications in key economic sectors alongside rather than integrated with existing government programmes.

(3) Renewing grassroots campaigning: From the beginning, the trust and confidence that people have in the Akshaya e-centres, highlighted by the large number of women trainees during e-literacy, was primarily due to the state’s strong role in projecting an image of Akshaya as a community development project. Although all the Akshaya e-centres were franchised to private entrepreneurs, the strong brand of government gave a signal that the activities of the e-centres were socially motivated. Traditional caste and community activism in Kerala provided a useful foundation for this and led to the recruitment of ‘social animators’ to bridge between the Akshaya entrepreneur and the local community. Indeed, the success of Akshaya’s entire e-literacy phase was heavily dependent on the vigorous grassroots campaigning that took place in order to generate awareness and support for the project among local communities and civil society forums. The e-literacy phase also gave entrepreneurs an opportunity to establish a close link with the community and with the gram panchayat for the generation of new activities.

However, in the aftermath of the e-literacy phase, images projected by the government regarding civil society mobilisation have been more limited in terms of their reach and more implicit. For example, since early 2004, declaring the role of social animators to be redundant, the government introduced a new function of block coordinators who were designated to network with entrepreneurs, but more in terms of their organisation and management rather than in terms of linking entrepreneurs with the local community. This explicit action taken by the government was accompanied by a gradual reduction in emphasis on grassroots campaigning based on the assumption that every family had already gained personal experience of interacting with the local Akshaya centre and with other community members through the e-literacy phase.

The current quest for content generation with the Akshaya project bears an important assumption – that people are willing to adopt new technologies in areas that are critical to their livelihoods. This may be so. On the other hand, evidence to date shows that people tend to use the Akshaya e-centres mainly for communication, which is not mission critical to livelihoods. While the extent to which a demand exists among the community for local content is an uncertain factor, our findings do suggest that the current lull in community-led demand for new activities is due in part to the less explicit thrust of the government in mobilising community interest in the project. Iterative patterns of interaction between state social policy initiatives and grassroots mobilisation, which had earlier been instrumental in carrying forward a strong image of political and economic support to encourage grassroots autonomous social organisations to flourish, are far less noticeable today.

(4) Involving the legislative system: The legislative implications of new activities being initiated at the Akshaya e-centres are only now becoming apparent as earlier interactions between various groups did not explicitly need to address these issues. This is particularly the case for the promotion of key sectors such as health where legislative support is needed to ensure that Indian data protection and human rights laws are in place to allow capture and usage of personal health data. At the present time, it is unclear whether the Akshaya e-centres have the legal dispensation to hold and manage such data, let alone pass it to private health providers. The state needs to develop an organisational framework to meet all legal obligations and to draw up and pass legislature in this regard. For example, a key question in health mapping will be where the data will be held physically. Holding local data in disparate locations, whether in Akshaya centres or in local panchayats, may be problematic in view of data security issues in which case a central server application held by the Department of Health or the IT Mission providing access to relevant parties may be a more viable option. The well-developed private health market in Kerala suggests that health mapping will facilitate market analysis of various health needs by private health providers including drug, vaccine needs and health insurance coverage for various segments of the population. Such activity will need to be closely regulated and in the absence of any formal agreement between the citizen and the government, it is unclear how the government plans to introduce safeguards to prevent the misuse of personal data and to ensure that there is transparency in the choice of clinics promoted for private health coverage.

The use and abuse of data has tremendous economic and political implications which need to be acknowledged, debated, conceptualised in legal terms, and explicitly articulated within society as the Akshaya telecentre project unfolds.

(5) Continued role of political champions: An important role was played in providing political support during the launching of the project by various central and state government ministers (including the President of India in the project’s inauguration). This symbolic role was important in generating an explicit image of Akshaya as a project dedicated to the broader development mandate of the political leadership.

The implementation of the project has been sensitive to local political processes. For example, the regulation of tensions throughout the early stage of the project between panchayat bodies operating at the different administrative levels of the village, block and district levels required sensitive handling. With the district panchayat body eager to claim the project under its jurisdiction, there was a risk of alienating the crucial village panchayat members who ultimately were the closest political unit to the community and could therefore have most influence on the project’s acceptance. This situation was salvaged by the strategic appointment of the Malappuram District Collector (previously the KSITM Chief) who was called in to handle sensitive local political tensions.

The continued support of political champions is now important with respect to current sustainability issues facing the project and with its recent roll out to seven districts in the state. New applications recently introduced at the e-centres suggest working closely with government departments and commercial enterprises with likely modification to established bureaucratic routines and passing of new legislature. At present, there is no explicit channel of communication established with the legislative system to institutionalise political support without the presence of a personality or particular political party. This situation has created an image of the project as dependent on the vagaries of the ruling party or on visionary and powerful administrators.

The scope of this section has been to move beyond an analysis of telecentre sustainability in terms of contemporary governance parameters. We have based our analysis on a broader definition of governance as interaction based on the assumption that interactions shape the way images are formed and evolve, and have an important influence on the unfolding of governance processes. Our style of analysis has been subtle and for each of the five themes we identified as important for sustainability of the Akshaya project, we have relied on the following theoretical constructs, which we described earlier. First, we have identified the nature and type of interactions between individuals and entities. This has involved describing the players involved and whether the interactions have been formalised or whether they remain informal. Second, we have traced the way images were formed among the players concerned from the inception of the project through to the present day. In some cases, images have been extensive and well articulated but more often than not images have been limited in scope and hover implicitly in the background. Third, we have tried to show that as images have changed over time due to changing priorities of the project and contextual factors, new information is now required in order to help different players create their own understanding of the project and its future.

Our analysis has derived from the sociology of governance theoretical lens. This approach has allowed us to expose the homegrown nature of governance reform in the particular case of the Akshaya project in Malappuram. In the next section, our analysis raises important theoretical and policy implications for the Akshaya project and more generally for telecentre sustainability and governance reform.

## Implications

The vision of governance presented in this paper, however preliminary, has strong implications for both theory and practice. Theoretically, adopting a sociology of governance theoretical lens leaves an open space for analysis of subtle interactions over time between state and society inviting us to illuminate critical aspects of social reality rather than applying a rigid theoretical governance template within which to ‘fit’ the reform process (Madon & Sahay, 2000). We strongly argue that any conceptualisation of governance that excludes such interactions will fail to capture important lessons about the complexity and diversity inherent in telecentre implementation. This analytical focus enables us to study how and why the project may have appeared cohesive in terms of integrating different players and their priorities at one point in time (for example, in the e-literacy phase), and how this situation changed due to contextual factors in the environment including the arrival of new players on the scene. In terms of future research implications, there is a need for further systematic investigation and comparisons of cases across diverse contexts using this theoretical approach.

The approach we have adopted to understanding telecentre sustainability can help to strengthen current literature on telecentre evaluation. While telecentre projects have been running for several years now, evaluation of their impact has been an enormously eclectic process, largely devoid of systematic research and planning. All too often, the evaluation exercise is premature tending to look at short-term impact in terms of good governance parameters of network coverage, network access, financial viability, transparency, accountability and public participation. For example, a conventional evaluation of Akshaya might have focused too intently on the achievement of 100% family e-literacy in the project as an important indicator of public participation and missed how over the last year this measure has become far less of a feature in the project’s long-term sustainability. Our focus on governance as interaction can hopefully provide a useful input towards reconceptualising the nature and scope of telecentre evaluation to move beyond an exclusive focus on good governance indicators.

A second major implication of our research is that the formation of images held by those who govern and those who are governed is crucially dependent on the existence and renewal of channels of information sharing and learning. For example, learning and knowledge creation of the local administration machinery is important to design relevant content for sectoral development priorities such as agriculture, education and health. Our findings also remind us of the need for implementers of the Akshaya project to improve understanding of the vertical orientation of local government and their systems of record keeping and data handling. This information is needed in order to present a realistic image of governance reform within the existing bureaucratic set-up and links up to Kerala state’s long-term reform agenda of administrative reform in local government. Information sharing and learning in other areas may also help in keeping the Akshaya project alive in terms of changes in societal conditions. For example, migration is an essential area for improved knowledgebuilding regarding telecentre sustainability in Kerala given the high percentage of migrant labourers outward bound from the state and the fact that migrants have so far been an influencing factor in the demand for communication services and e-literacy training programmes. While this is especially important in Malappuram, the district with the highest number of outwardbound migrants, improving understanding of migration patterns at the state level may be useful in creating an overall impression about possible demand for telecentres in Kerala.

A third implication of our research is that government has played an important role in constructing and managing interactions between state and society. In terms of building corporate confidence, a major policy implication from our findings is that the government needs to constitute and regulate a viable entity comprising the body of entrepreneurs to handle all procedures for contractual relationships between the Akshaya centres and the corporates. Given these implications, our findings strongly contradict the ideology of rolling back the state so typical in good governance policy manifesto. It is true that the telecentre principle brings with it a new mediator for bringing about socio-economic development, whereas historically this responsibility rested with the government working through its bureaucratic machinery. But the role of government remains crucial. The government needs to constitute an appropriate mediating body to maintain equity in terms of information sharing between large and small businesses to foster trust and cooperation between private sector companies. Given that one of the objectives of the Akshaya project is to promote local economic growth, it is crucial that the smaller players are made to feel secure. Consideration also needs to be given to building communication links whereby private companies are systematically consulted about potential changes in government policy, which may affect their relationship with the Akshaya centre. Such channels of interaction will provide an opportunity for learning and explicit image formation about the role of public–private partnerships and governance reform in the Kerala context.

One could argue about the generalisability of our findings to other states in India or other regions of the developing world. While it is true that Kerala’s uniqueness derives from the state’s particular history, geography, culture and politics, the Kerala model of development is not really based on any one existing model of development – Cuba, Costa Rica and Sri Lanka might be compared in certain respects. Without a doubt, Kerala’s development trajectory has provided fertile ground for Akshaya and regional profiles in the rest of India, especially in terms of population density and education, would suggest a far lower potential of telecentres contributing to governance reform than in Kerala. However, there are also important lessons to be learnt from our study. The most important is that social mobilisation and state intervention do not address all governance issues. Rather, the interactions between corporate, state and civil society players need continuous nurturing according to changing contextual circumstances. It is becoming increasingly clear that applications to supplement growth in key sectors of the economy such as health and education need improved policy planning and closer interactions with other institutions. For example, for health mapping, attention needs to be paid to the development of expertise and capacity within the state. This would include developing academic expertise in the areas of epidemiology and spatial statistics at local academic institutions. In the education sector, telecentre projects can also be thought of as deeply tied to learning systems in the areas they operate in since the level of existing basic written literacy and computing education in schools affects demand for projects like Akshaya. Equally, the existence of the ecentres can be used to fill in some gaps in the education system creating synergy with broader development goals.

The role of politics is rarely mentioned explicitly in telecentre sustainability literature. Indeed, the good governance policy manifesto is keen to downsize the political apparatus. However, evidence shows that promising projects have been dependent on the energy of champions who have either led the project from within government or have provided support to it when it has been implemented with private partners. This intersection of politics and operational efficiency must not be overlooked, particularly in the crucial roll out phase of telecentre projects. Our findings emphasise the importance of nurturing local political activity in which different groups of actors are involved and providing appropriate mechanisms for resolving tensions, which arise between players. Such activity is important to cultivate and sustain as it provides a base for grassroots social mobilisation.

Finally, and more broadly, our paper may have important implications for the topic of information systems in developing countries. Study in the field has gained strength theoretically and methodologically over the years through immersion in a broad range of social science literature. Yet, there remains much more work to do as we see evidence accumulating of failed or partially failed efforts to introduce information and communication technology to promote socio-economic development. Moreover, the perception that information and communication technology can bring about development needs to be tempered so that the gains achieved so far are not reversed by a backlash against such projects as witnessed in the Indian election results last year even in progressive states such as Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka. Despite the increasing integration of many parts of the developing world with the global economy, the fact is that large sections of these countries are still predominantly rural and the gap between urban and rural incomes will continue to increase unless attention is focused on improving the interface between the corporate sector and the government. Indeed, the sheer complexity of issues which exist in any particular situation calls for a need for further study to improve our understanding of how, why and in what ways

## Conclusion

Telecentre projects continue to be a driving force behind much of the good governance initiatives in India as in other developing countries. This paper proposes the application of sociology of governance concepts as a promising theoretical contribution to the telecentre literature. Interaction has a central place in this governance perspective and this approach raises new ideas about how and under what circumstances telecentres can be sustainable. In this paper, our analysis of the Akshaya project reveals that critical sustainability issues relate to how relations and exchanges between the myriad of players involved in implementing the projects are constituted and managed over time. This finding has important lessons for the way we think about telecentres as possible conduits for the promotion of governance reform.
