# Forming an intention to act on recommendations given via online social networks

Sabine Matook<sup>1</sup>, Susan A. Brown<sup>2</sup> and Johanna Rolf<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>UQ Business School, The University of Queensland, Brisbane, QLD, Australia; <sup>2</sup>Management Information Systems Department, Eller College of Management, The University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Volkswagen AG, Wolfsburg, Germany

Correspondence: Sabine Matook, UQ Business School, The University of Queensland, 3 Blair Drive, Brisbane, QLD 4072, Australia. Tel: +61 7 3346 8049; Fax: +61 7 3346 8166

## Abstract

Online social networks (OSN) are technology-enabled relationship tools in which a user creates a profile, connects to other individuals, and shares user-generated content with them. One of the many features OSN offer is the ability to post messages in the form of product and service recommendations. Although wordof-mouth research has examined this type of personal communication to intentionally influence consumer decisions, limited understanding exists regarding when a user acts upon a recommendation received from one of their contacts. In this study, we draw on relationship theories and research on trust to analyze the impact relationship characteristics and behaviors of the OSN contacts have on trust in the recommendation that subsequently leads a user to act on this OSN contact’s recommendation. The results of a survey of 116 OSN users showed that closeness, excessive posting behavior, and past recommendation experience have a positive impact on trust and intention to act on the recommendation. One characteristic of the relationships, that is, similarity, however, does not affect recommendation trust in the context of OSN. The findings enhance our understanding of relationships and their defining characteristics in OSN. The results also provide insights into how companies might leverage OSN in their marketing efforts.

## Introduction

Social media use has increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in the past few years. Online social networks (OSN), such as Facebook and Google+, have evolved as the major social information technologies (IT) for personal communication (Lin & Lu, 2010). Evidence of this can be seen in recent statistics for these sites: Facebook had a growth rate of 43% over the last 5 years (Anonymous, 2012a) building up to more than a billion monthly active users as of December 2012 (Anonymous, 2013b), while Google+, which was launched in July 2011, has become the second most popular OSN after Facebook, with 135 million active users only 18 months after its launch (Anonymous, 2012b). In addition, Internet users in the US committed almost 25% of their online time to social IT (Anonymous, 2011). Thus, it is not surprising when NielsonWire reports that OSN are among the top online destinations for users in markets like the US, Europe, and Australasia (Anonymous, 2011).

OSN are ‘web-based services that allow individuals to (1) construct a public or semi-public pro<sup>fi</sup>le within a bounded system, (2) articulate a list of other users with whom they share a connection, and (3) view and traverse their list of connections and those made by others within the system’ (Boyd & Ellison, 2007, p. 211). OSN support the formation of new relationships and the maintenance of existing social networks through the exchange of user-generated content (Ellison et al, 2007). User-generated content is the creative output of coproduced meaning in OSN communications and includes posting messages, sharing photos and videos, and social bookmarking (Kaplan & Haenlein, 2010). Status updates are a Facebook feature for users to post messages that are automatically broadcast to all contacts of the user’s network (Manago et al, 2012). These messages are about users’ actions, current locations, and emotional states, but also include opinions and views of products and services. For example, as of May 2013, 4.74 billion content items (e.g., posts and photos) are shared daily on Facebook (Anonymous, 2013a). Among all these different messages, the ones about products and services are of special interest to companies because they represent recommendations given by one individual to another, thereby in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the other’s consumption and adoption decisions (Anderson, 1998).

To be able to provide recommendations, an individual has to be a user’s OSN contact. OSN contacts can include best friends, current and past romantic partners, coworkers, teammates, and casual acquaintances (Walther et al, 2008). Despite the fact that Facebook labels all OSN contacts as 'friends', a user's OSN contacts include acquaintances as well as friends (Manago et al, 2012). However, OSN contacts are not strangers to the user, either. They are individuals with whom the user is already acquainted, has had mutual interactions, and may share connections with others (Tsai & Reis, 2009). Yet, the diversity of a user’s OSN contacts raises a question about whether or not the type of relationship between a user and his or her OSN contacts affects a user’s decision to rely and subsequently act on a recommendation received from a particular OSN contact.

Prior research on word-of-mouth (WOM) has extensively studied recommendation giving and seeking behaviors of consumers (see, e.g., Katz & Lazarsfeld, 1955; Anderson, 1998; Trusov et al, 2009). Overall, <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that people rely on various sources (personal or impersonal) for recommendations to aid with an overload of product choices and to obtain guidance in their purchasing decisions (Duhan et al, 1997). In fact, research has shown that recommendations made by personal sources have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on decisions than those made by impersonal contacts (i.e., companies and marketers) (Katz & Lazarsfeld, 1955). However, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not explain an OSN user’s decision to act on some recommendations and not others, when the majority of OSN contacts is personal (Ellison et al, 2009).

The technology-enabled environment of OSN further affects how a user might act on a recommendation because the technology allows for increased speed and reach of any WOM message (Hennig-Thurau et al, 2004). Subsequent electronic WOM research examined technology-enabled recommendations and aimed to understand how users are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the electronic WOM messages. However, <sup>fi</sup>ndings are limited because the studies were mainly focused on anonymous networks, such as online blogs and online forums (Hung & Li, 2007), and one-to-one communications via e-mail (Chiu et al, 2007). Little attention was paid to giving and receiving recommendations in OSN where the WOM message is disclosed publicly to a large network of known contacts.

Against this backdrop, this study seeks to develop improved theoretical insights into factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence a user’s intention to act on an OSN recommendation. We theoretically articulate and empirically test a research model that proposes OSN users act on recommendations if they have trust in the recommendation, which emerges based on relationship characteristics and speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT-related behaviors. Knowing the factors that affect recommendation trust is valuable from both a theoretical perspective (e.g., de<sup>fi</sup>ning relationships in the virtual OSN environment) and a practical perspective (e.g., learning what leads to OSN recommendation acceptance). The current research contributes to the body of literature on OSN in the information systems <sup>fi</sup>eld, on WOM in marketing research, and on the nature of friendships in the IT-enabled OSN context. This study builds on relationship theories and the research on trust to determine the impact of two key relationship characteristics (i.e., closeness and similarity) and two key behaviors of OSN contacts (i.e., past recommendation experience and posting behavior) on recommendation trust and the intention to act on that recommendation.

## Background

Overwhelmed by the amount of information available on the Internet, individuals increasingly face challenges regarding their consumption and adoption choices (Hennig-Thurau et al, 2004). In this decision process, individuals rely on WOM recommendations in the form of opinions from a diverse set of others (i.e., experts) in reducing decision uncertainty and the risks associated with different alternatives (Giddens, 1991). WOM recommendations are informal evaluations of goods and services that can be positive (e.g., sharing pleasant experiences) or negative (e.g., warnings about poor service) (Anderson, 1998). Recommendations are useful for people because they provide <sup>fi</sup>ltered information and point to interesting things one might otherwise have missed (Beatty & Smith, 1987; Heath et al, 2007).

Research on WOM has focused on actively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing WOM communication through target advertisements and promotions by identifying the information sources people rely on (Kozinets et al, 2010). People seek and obtain recommendations from a number of sources (Duhan et al, 1997). Yet, recommendations from personal contacts, such as friends, family, teammates, and co-workers, have a larger in<sup>fl</sup>uence than impersonal sources, such as shop assistants, strangers, and company experts (Katz & Lazarsfeld, 1955).

With the advent of the Internet, the speed and reach of WOM communication has accelerated because the technology reduces the cost and effort for undertaking WOM marketing (Riegner, 2007). Electronic WOM research aims to understand the possibilities the Internet provides to more effectively in<sup>fl</sup>uence WOM communication on goods and services among users (Hennig-Thurau et al, 2004). In this ITenabled environment, users undertake WOM to co-produce value and meaning on goods and services for an organization (Kozinets et al, 2010). Consequently, companies utilize social media (e.g., online blogs, online forums and communities, Twitter, and/or OSN) to engage users with the brand and to initiate co-creation of content (see, e.g., Culnan et al, 2010; Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). For example, Starbucks uses a variety of social media for their customer dialog management to deepen user-company communications but also to monitor user-user communications (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010).

Among the different social media, OSN are of special interest because sender and receiver of WOM recommendations are known to each other. Whereas WOM communication through social media, such as online forums and online blogs, is mostly impersonal, OSN contacts are a user’s personal network of social contacts (Boyd & Ellison, 2007). OSN are a virtual replication of an individual’s realworld relationships (Ellison et al, 2009) and the various features of the technology allow for both synchronous and asynchronous communication among all OSN contacts (Matook & Brown, 2008). Thus, OSN enable a user to manage and maintain different relationships (e.g., kinship, work-relationships, and friendships) in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and comprehensive way (Walther et al, 2008; Ellison et al, 2009). Among different types of relationships, friendships are crucial for human beings’ well-being because friends provide each other with companionship, communication, consideration, and affection (Hays, 1984; Hartup, 1993). OSN have even been called ‘friendship facilitators and promoters’ (Tu et al, 2011, p. 8) because they enable ‘voluntary or unconstrained interaction, in which the participants respond to one another personally’ (Wright, 1984 de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a friendship, p. 119). Interestingly, some OSN sites, such as Facebook, portray all contacts as friends. However, OSN contacts are diverse and by no means are they all best friends (Grimmelmann, 2009). A study of college students’ OSN contacts revealed that they include high school friends and past romantic partners (18%), acquaintances (27%), best friends, family and current partners (21%), teammates and co-workers (24%), but also strangers (4%) and online friends only (2%). The remaining 4% are missing data where respondents declined to answer (Manago et al, 2012). In the context of acting on OSN recommendations, it is important to consider that these OSN contacts are not anonymous, and a user has the ability to determine who the sender of a recommendation is. Thus, uncertainty about the motive for giving a recommendation to others is smaller compared with when the recommendation is made by an anonymous source (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004) Yet. it does not explain which recommendations from the known social network an OSN user acts on.

OSN reduce the effort required to make recommendations by facilitating the sharing of user-generated content with a large number of contacts and by enabling the contacts to respond to the entire network. The posting feature of OSN allows a user to post comments about any content deemed important, including recommendations (Ellison et al, 2007). Recommendations can take the form of direct posts as a comment about a product or service (e.g., a movie or a restaurant) or added as a post when a user browses through the Internet and hits, for example, Facebook's ‘Like’ button at a company’s webpage (Harris & Dennis, 2011).

Because it is estimated that, on average, a Facebook user has 130 contacts (Anonymous, 2012c), OSN postings reach large numbers of people simultaneously and – if their security settings allow – the contacts of these people as well. These postings can be differentiated as (1) post-initiating, where a user makes a posting without any preceding posts, or (2) post-responding, where the OSN contacts reply to and thus, comment on the original posting. User postings of individuals who want to build and maintain an OSN presence should be frequent, authentic, and engaging (DeVoe, 2009). However, ‘too much or repetitive content can become annoying’ (DeVoe, 2009, p. 420). If postings are perceived as annoying, the OSN contact may be blocked or removed from the contact list (Bevan et al, 2012).

In sum, personal recommendations have been extensively studied in the literature; scant knowledge exists regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of recommendations by an OSN contact on a user’s intention to act on these recommendations. Prior WOM research showed that recommendations by known sources are acted on (Garbarino & Strahilevitz, 2004), but because an OSN consists of only known sources (Krasnova et al, 2010a) limited understanding exists about which OSN contacts’ recommendations the user will act on. However, it is important to understand why a user acts on certain recommendations made in OSN, and not others, as it may enable companies in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumption choices, adoption decisions, and general consumer behaviors.

## Hypothesis development

This section presents the hypotheses. It proposes that intention to act on a recommendation is a function of trust in the recommendation, which is further determined by characteristics of the relationship (i.e., closeness and similarity) between the OSN user and different OSN contacts, and the behaviors of the OSN contacts who give the recommendation.

## Trust and intention

WOM recommendations assist people in their consumption and adoption decisions (Beatty & Smith, 1987; Heath et al, 2007). Research shows that people are twice as likely to select something when it has been recommended to them (Senecal & Nantel, 2004). Recommendations reduce uncertainty regarding products and services. However, uncertainty reduction theory (Berger & Calabrese, 1975; Berger, 1986) asserts that uncertainty reduction is contingent on trust. Given the foundational role of trust in human interactions (Altman & Taylor, 1973), we would expect trust to be an important element in determining the response to and acceptance of recommendations from others (Garbarino & Strahilevitz, 2004).

Trust is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a person’s ‘willingness to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action […], irrespective of the ability to monitor or control the other party’ (Mayer et al, 1995, p. 712). Vulnerability stems from incomplete knowledge and control over the actions and behaviors of others causing uncertainty and introducing risk into the interaction (Lewis & Weigert, 1985). Research in electronic commerce demonstrates that trust in the seller or the intermediary of an electronic marketplace reduces the perceived risk that the buyer will be disadvantaged in the transaction (van der Heijden et al, 2003). In essence, trusting implies favorable expectations that following recommendations from other people will not lead to harmful outcomes (Rousseau et al, 1998). Prior research differentiates between cognitive and affective elements of trust (Lewis & Weigert, 1985; McAllister, 1995) and emphasizes that ‘trust decisions usually involve both reasoning and feeling’ (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006, p. 943). Consequently, we next present the two dimensions of trust.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst dimension of trust is cognitive trust that captures the rational side of trusting. It is based on whether and under what circumstances an individual possesses certain attributes, such as integrity (honesty and likelihood of keeping promises), predictability (of the behavior of the trustee), and competence (the ability to behave as expected) (McKnight et al, 1998; Gefen et al, 2003). Trusting is based on having ‘good rational reasons’ (Lewis & Weigert, 1985, p. 972), available knowledge of the person to be trusted, and a conscious value assessment of the situation. A cognitive trust perspective leads the trustee to have a rational expectation that the other person embraces attributes that make them reliable and thus, renders the interaction less risky (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006).

The second dimension of trust is affective – or emotional – trust which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the con<sup>fi</sup>dence one places in a partner on the basis of feelings generated by the level of care and concern the partner demonstrates’ (Johnson & Grayson, 2005, p. 501). To arrive at an affective trust relationship, substantial emotional investments have been made that in turn safeguard the relationship against betrayal (Lewis & Weigert, 1985). Affective trust captures the extent to which a person feels secure and comfortable about relying on the trusted other (Komiak & Benbasat, 2004). Affective trust supplements cognitive trust particularly when complete information is unavailable and the trustee is faced with high uncertainty and risk. Hence, the decision to trust another person is based on sentiments and feelings, thereby making a ‘leap of faith’ (Sun, 2010).

In sum, trusting means that the behaviors, actions, and outcomes of others can be relied upon (Luhmann, 1979). Trust allows people to take risks under conditions of uncertainty because it provides them with con<sup>fi</sup>dence that others will not behave opportunistically and take advantage of them (McAllister, 1995). Therefore, when a person trusts an OSN contact, the person is willing to trust the recommendations made by that OSN contact. In turn, he or she is willing to become ‘vulnerable’ to this form of WOM message (Berndt, 1992) and subsequently, intends to engage in the recommended behavior. Consequently, we expect that trust in a recommendation given by an OSN contact will have a positive impact on the intention to act on the recommendation.

H1: Trust in an OSN contact’s recommendation positively influences the user’s intention to act on the recommendation.

## Relationship characteristics and trust

Two characteristics important in assessing relationships are closeness and similarity (Reis & Rusbult, 2004). Prior research has shown that an OSN network includes people from different life spheres, such as work, sport clubs, and high school (Ellison et al, 2009; Manago et al, 2012). Thus, OSN consist of people who have varying degrees of closeness and similarity. In general, people tend to trust recommendations that are made by individuals like themselves (Pitta, 2011). Members of OSN would seem to <sup>fi</sup>t this category, although the varying levels of closeness and similarity between a user and the contacts may lead some recommendations to be more trusted than others. Thus, it is important to understand how the relationship characteristics of closeness and similarity in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent to which trust in OSN recommendations manifests.

According to relationship theories (Berscheid <sup>Closeness</sup>et al, 1989; Reis & Rusbult, 2004), closeness is a key de<sup>fi</sup>ning factor in distinguishing among relationship types (e.g., co-worker relationships, friendships, kin relationships). The concept of closeness can be conceptualized as high interdependence between two people's activities over an extended period of time (Berscheid & Regan, 2005). Interdependence represents the strong, frequent, and diverse in<sup>fl</sup>uence one partner’s behaviors have on the other’s subsequent behaviors (Reis et al, 2000). Friendships and romantic relationships are typical of close personal relationships (Berscheid et al, 1989). These relationships exhibit high levels of communality, making them intrinsically rewarding and inherently enjoyable (Clark & Mills, 1993). Two individuals, who are close, provide support and express concern about each other’s well-being, show affection toward each other, and share experiences and activities together (Hays, 1984). The literature describes close relationships as warm and intimate bonds with positive emotions. These unique aspects of close relationships are the foundation for affect-based trust (Dunn & Schweitzer, 2005). Close partners feel that they can trust in each other’s actions and behaviors (Rempel et al, 1985).

Consequently, close relationship partners believe that they can trust in each other’s recommendations given via OSN.

In close relationships, partners mutually disclose personal details about their preferences, interests, and experiences (Hays, 1989). Through this self-disclosure, close relationship partners develop the ability to predict one another’s behaviors (Cozby, 1973). The personal details also allow for making customized recommendations that point to things the other person most probably likes (Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006). When interacting with an OSN contact, the personal details are used to make a rational assessment of closeness between the two contacts which is relevant for establishing cognitive trust. Once closeness is determined, uncertainty about the close partner’s behavior is reduced (Rempel et al, 1985). Hence, in close relationships, two OSN contacts have good reasons to trust in each other’s recommendations.

In sum, closeness provides the knowledge and affect necessary for another person to make an appropriate recommendation. Partners have strong bonds and know each other very well and thus, trust in each other’s judgment and advice. Yet, in OSN, the contacts a user has vary in their level of closeness. Consequently, we expect that recommendations made by those perceived as close are trusted more than recommendations made by OSN contacts with whom the user is less close.

H2: Closeness has a positive influence on trust in the recommendation of an OSN contact.

The concept of homophily, or similarity, is <sup>Similarity</sup>central to the formation of a person’s social networks (McPherson et al, 2001). The similarity-attraction paradigm posits that people prefer the company of others who possess characteristics (e.g., behaviors, attitudes, and values) that are similar to their own (Byrne, 1971, 1997). Thus, relationship similarity refers to a person’s tendency to establish and maintain a relationship with others who have similar interests, hobbies, education, and/or background (Jussim & Osgood, 1989; McPherson et al, 2001). Brass (1995) posits that ‘similarity is thought to ease communication, increase predictability of behavior and foster trust and reciprocity’ (Brass, 1995, p. 51). Although similarity can cover a wide range of aspects (Rubin, 1973), two primary categories of similarity are (1) status similarity, including the major socio-demographic characteristics, and (2) value similarity, representing internal states that direct a person’s future behaviors (McPherson et al, 2001).

Users employ OSN for social searching to obtain information about one’s OSN contacts (Lampe et al, 2006). In fact, social surveillance, which also includes exploring OSN contacts’ pro<sup>fi</sup>les, has been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as one of the most common OSN behaviors (Joinson, 2008). Through reading pro<sup>fi</sup>le information and status updates, OSN users are not only up-to-date on their contacts’ activities, they are also able to determine the degree of similarity they have with their contacts. This information in turn provides knowledge about the other person that can be used to reduce behavioral uncertainty (Berger & Calabrese, 1975; Berger, 1986). Thus, similarity facilitates reduction of risk and uncertainty associated with the OSN contact (Berndt, 1992). As discussed before, uncertainty reduction theory proposes that reducing uncertainty is positively associated with trust (Berger & Calabrese, 1975; Berger, 1986). In OSN, the contacts already possess some degree of similarity, either status (e.g., people from work) or value (e.g., people from church) similarity because a user in general only adds others to his or her OSN who are known to the user and with whom the user had some previous interactions (Tsai & Reis, 2009). Thus, OSN contacts are typically more similar than people in online forums or a shop assistant would be (Thelwall, 2009). This similarity among a user and his or her OSN contacts leads to the development of cognitive trust because the other person seems familiar, and it is (rationally) assumed the person thinks, acts, and behaves in the same way (Jussim & Osgood, 1989).

In addition, similarity makes people feel the other person is like them and increases a user’s positive sentiments toward the OSN contact. In this manner, similarity also enables affective trust because users feel that their similar OSN contacts resemble themselves (Hartup, 1993). Thus, a user feels more positive about the behaviors of a similar person and senses the recommendation can be trusted. For example, a user, who believes a contact is similar, is likely to feel that the contact’s recommendation can be trusted because the other person would only recommend a product that he or she enjoyed, and because of the similarity, would bring enjoyment to the user as well. Building on this line of thinking, we expect that the more similar a user believes to with an OSN contact, the more likely the user will trust in the others recommendation.

H3: Similarity has a positive influence on trust in a recommendation of an OSN contact.

## Behavior of OSN contacts and trust

In addition to the relationship characteristics, the behavior an OSN contact performs in the social media environment has an impact on the extent to which the user trusts in the recommendation. We focus on two behaviors of OSN contacts that are supported by the technology’s features – past experiences with the contact’s recommendation behavior and the contact’s posting behavior.

Extensive research has shown that past <sup>Past experiences</sup>behavior is the best predictor of future behavior (Rempel et al, 1985; Ouellette & Wood, 1998; Wood et al, 2002). Therefore, past experiences with a recommendation are an essential component of understanding trust in recommendations because the past experiences serve as the foundation upon which an OSN contact can form expectations of future recommendations (Herlocker et al, 2000). Knowledge about past recommendations is valuable for the OSN user because it allows for reducing uncertainty which in turn enhances trust (Berger & Calabrese, 1975; Berger, 1986). Trust in a recommendation is inferred from the match of a past recommendation with the person’s needs (Dunn & Schweitzer, 2005). A positive result (i.e., high match) will enhance trust in future recommendations (Herlocker et al, 2000), while a negative result (i.e., low match) will reduce trust toward the next recommendation, as the receiver will come to expect again a mismatch in the recommendation (Doney & Cannon, 1997). Thus, an OSN user makes a rational assessment of the match of past recommendations and the result affects the user’s cognitive trust in any future recommendation.

In the situation, when needs were met in the past, the OSN user feels comfortable relying on the contact for future recommendations (Herlocker et al, 2000). Because positive past experiences contribute to feelings of reduced uncertainty when the OSN contact provides another recommendation (Doney & Cannon, 1997), a user believes this contact’s recommendations can be trusted. The comfortable feeling that an OSN contact’s recommendations can be trusted is further ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed because the recommendation was given by a known source. Thus, affective trust in the OSN contact’s recommendation develops because the user feels this contact’s recommendation had a high match in the past. In summary, past experience with recommendations of a particular OSN contact give a benchmark to trust in the next recommendation.

H4: Positive past experiences with an OSN contact’s recommendations have a positive influence on trust in the recommendation of this OSN contact.

The primary motive to use OSN is for <sup>Posting behavior</sup>communication by sending private messages and posting messages to all of a user’s OSN contacts (Joinson, 2008). The postings are one form of self-disclosure that increases the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence among the contacts in an OSN environment (Posey et al, 2010). In these online environments, people <sup>fi</sup>nd it easier to disclose information because they can choose the time and location for sharing details without the need of another person listening or being physically present (Bonebrake, 2002). Postings in OSN consist of any information (e.g., status updates) a contact shares with the network, including recommendations about products and services. These postings are vital for the OSN to function and to keep users attracted to the OSN (Jones et al, 2004). However, overly high disclosure in an OSN manifests as excessive posting behavior. Excessive posting is a multifaceted concept, and we evaluate it based on an OSN contact posting too much (volume) and posting uninteresting information (interestingness).

When user posting behavior is excessive, it can be considered information pollution that results in information overload (Nielsen, 2003). Information overload means that the information processing requirements exceed the user’s processing capacity of this information (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). It should be noted however that individuals possess different processing capacities which makes it impossible to determine a common threshold across users (Chen et al, 2009). Under conditions of excessive posting behavior, users can experience information overload either through receiving too much information (Beaudoin, 2008) or more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to our context, through receiving too many recommendations (Leskovec et al, 2007). Excessive posting behavior in relation to the interestingness of a user posting can also lead to information overload as the processing of uninteresting postings requires more effort and deep concentration (Schiefele, 1999). Hence, the user’s processing capacity may be lower for uninteresting posts as compared with postings the user <sup>fi</sup>nds interesting. In fact, prior research on interestingness of text has shown that individuals recall uninteresting texts less frequently as compared with interesting ones when faced with large amounts of data (Garner et al, 1991).

An OSN user may treat too many recommendations as spam and hence, he or she may doubt the ability of a recommendation to reduce uncertainty in product choices (Leskovec et al, 2007). Indeed, ‘… as more recommendations are sent between the same two individuals, the likelihood that they will be heeded decreases’ (Leskovec et al, 2007, p. 3). Similarly, uninteresting postings are unlikely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals’ consumption choices because users only prescribe value to ‘informative’ postings, suggesting that uninteresting postings offer little value to them (André et al, 2012). The decrease in in<sup>fl</sup>uence of postings co-occurs with repetition wearout effects (Abendroth & Heyman, 2012) that capture the gradually diminishing effect of providing excessive recommendations (Bass et al, 2007). The wearout effect leads to a user losing interest in additional recommendations and <sup>fi</sup>nding the repeated recommendations worthless (Bass et al, 2007). When postings, such as recommendations, are perceived as having low value, an OSN user has no rational reason to trust in the advice or opinions of this recommender (Munson, 2011) which has a negative impact on cognitive-based trust.

In terms of affective trust, excessive postings due to too much and/or uninteresting information – and the related information overload – are perceived as annoying by the user and the user’s network (Koroleva et al, 2010), thus creating negative affect toward the recommender. In turn, it may lead the OSN user to question whether the other person cares about and is concerned for him or her, an important basis for affective trust. In addition, excessive posting of recommendations does not reduce uncertainty about product choices because the resulting information overload can lead to feelings of loss of control and being overwhelmed (Bawden & Robinson, 2009). As a result of excessive postings, an OSN user experiences emotional stress and confusion about which product he or she should choose (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). In such a situation, an OSN user will have lower affective trust in the OSN contact and the messages the contact posts. Based on the potential for a negative impact on trust, we expect that users will not trust recommendations of OSN contacts who engage in excessive posting behavior.

H5: Excessive posting behavior over OSN has a negative influence on the trust in the recommendation of an OSN contact.

## Methodology

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a quantitative study with 166 users from two different OSN sites. In the following, we present the recruitment of the participants, the data collection procedure, and the survey instrument.

## Participants and procedure

The unit of analysis in this study is the individual OSN user. Since OSN users represent a special population of Internet users, data can be collected using the snowball sampling method (Sudman & Kalton, 1986). Snowball sampling is a non-probability sampling method that relies on referrals made by people who know others who possess the required population characteristics (Salganik & Heckathorn, 2004). It is an effective method to recruit numerous participants because after an initial seed is planted the referral chain multiplies in different directions (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981). Hence, the connections of study participants to other individuals are utilized. Snowball sampling is uniquely designed for social science research (Coleman, 1958) and has been used in the context of social IT research before (see, e.g., Zhang et al, 2009; Tow et al, 2010; Warkentin et al, 2011).

After reviewing the advantages of snowball sampling, we decided to utilize this sampling method to recruit active OSN members for this study. It is important to note that snowball sampling is not without concern. With this method, participants are not randomly chosen but are dependent on each other; but as it is impossible to obtain user lists from OSN sites, such as Facebook, to undertake random sampling (Tow et al, 2010), snowball sampling seems an appropriate method for recruitment. To counterbalance issues of selection bias, which limits the validity of the sample (Atkinson & Flint, 2001), two separate seeds were planted in two different OSN sites. The seeds were placed in two distinctively different networks that had no overlap in OSN contacts. The invitations were posted to the OSN sites of Facebook (www.facebook.com) and the German VZ-network ‘StudiVZ’ (www.studivz.net), which is one of the most successful OSN sites in Europe (Sujka, 2010). Furthermore, by planting two seeds in two disjointed networks, we aimed to decrease the likelihood of participant repetition while increasing the overall validity of the study (Sudman & Kalton, 1986). Indeed, Salganik and Heckathorn (2004) suggest that with this nonprobability sampling method results can be produced that ‘are asymptotically unbiased no matter how the seeds are selected’ (p. 197). The invitation included an explanation about the research, the pledge to participate, the link to the survey, and a prewritten message for the relevant participants to be sent to their OSN contacts.

Data were collected using an online survey. A pilot survey was <sup>fi</sup>rst tested for clarity, language usage, comprehensiveness, and face validity with a small sample of 15 OSN users and three experienced IT researchers, as recommended by Churchill (1979). The pilot test identi<sup>fi</sup>ed some dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in understanding a few of the questions. As a result, edits were made to the questions prior to launching the <sup>fi</sup>nal study. The <sup>fi</sup>nal survey asked participants to think about a friend who had given the participant at least one recommendation in the past. We used the label of friend to be consistent with the wording used by the OSN sites.

## Measurement

Measurement items were adapted from the literature wherever possible. All items are listed in Table 1. Only two of our constructs had existing measures (i.e., trust, intention) which we adapted from existing sources. For the new measures, we followed standard psychometric scale development procedures (for details see, e.g., Bagozzi & Phillips, 1982; Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Boudreau et al, 2001). The domain of the relevant construct was initially speci<sup>fi</sup>ed, and the items were subsequently developed based on the conceptual de<sup>fi</sup>nition. New measures were developed for closeness, similarity, past experiences, and posting behavior. Measures for the constructs were developed based on the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions provided in the literature. All items were measured on a 5-point Likert scale. All constructs, with the exception of posting behavior, were measured as re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs.

## Results

The data were analyzed using SmartPLS 2.0 M3 (Ringle et al, 2005). Partial least squares (PLS) is most appropriate for research that aims to build theory (Chin et al, 2003) and for causal-predictive testing (Barclay et al, 1995). For our sample size of 116 participants, PLS provides a robust analysis enabled by its component-based estimation (Chin, 1998). A further strength of PLS relevant for this study is the ability to handle re<sup>fl</sup>ective and formative measures in a single model (Chin & Newsted, 1999). We also calculated the statistical power using G\*Power 3.0 (Faul et al, 2007). Assuming a moderate effect size of 0.15, an alpha of 0.05 for four predictors, and a statistical power of 0.8, we need a sample of the size of at least 85 (Faul et al, 2007). Hence, with our sample of 116 participants, the statistical power is acceptable.

Among the participants, the average age of respondents was 25 and 61.2% were female. On average, each respondent had 252 OSN contacts (min = 12; max = 1021). The data also demonstrated that respondents possessed good English pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency suggesting they were capable of understanding the English-language questionnaire despite not being native English speakers. In particular, data showed that in our sample 19% were native English speakers, 34% were <sup>fl</sup>uent in English, 29% were upper intermediate, 15% are intermediate, and only 4% assessed their English skills as low intermediate at the beginner level. Respondents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two OSN sites as their primary sites: 53% used Facebook, 42% used StudiVZ, and the remaining 5% of participants used either QQ – the Chinese OSN site (3%) or MySpace (2%) as their primary OSN site.

## Measurement model

The quality of the measurement model for the re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs is determined by content validity, construct reliability, indicator reliability, and discriminant validity (Bagozzi, 1982). To assess the quality of the formative construct, we examined the weights for the items (Chin, 1998) and their multicollinearity (Diamantopoulos & Siguaw, 2006).

Content validity is concerned with the extent to which the constructs represent the intended domain (Nunnally, 1978). Clear and precise construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions are essential for content validity. We discussed our de<sup>fi</sup>nitions with three experts in the domain of OSN and a cohort of <sup>fi</sup>ve Ph.D. students. The pilot study served as an additional check for content validity (Churchill, 1979).

Construct reliability for re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs demands a strong correlation among the items that are related to one particular construct (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Thus, we computed Cronbach α values and assessed the constructs’ composite reliability which were also above the recommended cutoff value of 0.7 and thus, deemed acceptable (Hair et al, 1998). Convergent validity was determined via the indicator loadings that all achieved levels above the recommended value of 0.60 (Chin, 1998) and are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at P<0.01, suggesting all indicators contribute well to their constructs. Finally, we calculated the average variance extracted (AVE). All AVEs are above 0.5, indicating an adequate level of convergent validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Liang et al, 2007). ## Structural model

Building on our examination of the measurement model, we then tested the structural model. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the proposed hypothesis was tested using the bootstrap procedure with 200 re-samples (Gil-Garcia, 2008). Our research model explains 47.9% of the variance in trust in a recommendation and accounts for 22.3% of the variance in the intention to act on a recommendation. Results of the analysis are illustrated in Figure 1.

Trust in a recommendation is positively associated with intention to act on the recommendation (β = 0.473, P<0.01). Thus, Hypothesis 1 is supported. Further, the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between closeness and trust in a recommendation (β = 0.308, P<0.01) is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant; therefore, H2 is also supported. We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient between similarity and trust in a recommendation; thus, Hypothesis 3 is not supported. Hypothesis 4 is supported because we found a positive signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect between past experiences and trust in a recommendation (β = 0.330, P<0.01). Finally, posting behavior (β = –0.270, P<0.01) is found to have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative effect on trust in a recommendation, supporting Hypothesis 5.

## Discussion

This research aimed to understand the factors that affect trust in a recommendation made via OSN and the extent to which an OSN user would act on the recommendation. Building on relationship theories and the literature on trust, we analyzed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of relationship characteristics (i.e., closeness and similarity) and the behavior of OSN contacts (i.e., past experiences and posting behavior) to understand how these factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust and intention to act on a recommendation. Our results show that users intend to act on a recommendation if they have trust in the recommendation which is affected by three factors, namely, how close the relationship is, positive experience with past recommendations by the OSN contact, and the contact’s posting behavior undertaken in the OSN. Closeness and positive past experience increase trust whereas excessive posting behavior reduces trust. Similarity between contacts was not associated with trust in a recommendation.

Trust matters when individuals interact in online settings. Prior research has shown that trust in online stores affects perceived shopping risks and purchase intention (van der Heijden et al, 2003). Similarly, trust affects a user’s intention to adopt a software recommendation agent (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). In the current study, we demonstrate that in the social media environment of OSN, trust in a contact’s recommendation is important when it comes to acting on the recommendation. We showed that closeness and past experiences are important determinants of trusting and following advice (i.e., intention to act). OSN offer an easy and convenient way for communicating with others (Krasnova et al, 2010b), including the seeking and giving of recommendations in the form of OSN postings. However, when postings are perceived as excessive, then, as our results show, there are negative impacts on recommendation trust. In fact, how individuals behave in the IT-enabled environment of OSN is more important for recommendation trust than perceived similarity between OSN contacts.

Research has demonstrated that among relationship partners, closeness and similarity may emerge (Jussim & Osgood, 1989; McPherson et al, 2001). Whereas our results indicate that closeness affects trust, the results do not show the same effect for similarity. Through social searching, OSN users are informed about the degree of similarity with their contacts (Joinson, 2008). However, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest differences in similarity are not a distinguishing factor when it comes to trusting in a recommendation. One possible explanation for the nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cance of similarity could stem from differences among types of OSN and types of recommendations given there. Our study focused on Facebook and StudiVZ, two OSN networks touted as friendship tools (Tu et al, 2011), where a user adds contacts because they are known to him or her, making closeness a de<sup>fi</sup>ning factor for these OSN relationships. However, these friendship-centered OSN are only one type of an OSN. Other networks exist that are based on a particular mission, for example environmental concerns (Oakley & Salam, 2011) or human well-being (Munson, 2011). Membership in these OSN is based on a common cause, the mission, or shared interests which means members are similar in that aspect. However, they may not be close, at least initially. In fact. it might be that members in these mission-centered OSN do not know each other at all and even never reveal their true (i.e., real-world) identity. We speculate that in these types of OSN, similarity may manifest as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factor when it comes to trusting a contact’s recommendations. Additional research is needed to better understand similarity in the context of OSN in general and across different types of OSN.

Another important <sup>fi</sup>nding of the current study is the impact of past recommendation experiences on trust and acting intention. Positive and negative experiences are the foundation for evaluating future recommendations. Individuals have more trust in recommendations given by others who are known and familiar to them, compared with recommendations made by unknown sources (e.g., companies and marketers) (Pitta, 2011). Considering past experiences with a contact’s recommendations helps individuals to reduce uncertainty and risk in future recommendations, leading to increased trust in a recommendation (Berger & Calabrese, 1975). However, we showed that excessive posting behavior negatively affects trust in recommendations.

An OSN contact who overuses the platform and posts in a manner such that the postings and recommendations exceed normal levels (e.g., posts too much) and/or are uninteresting cannot expect the same responses from the network as those contacts who do not overload the network with their postings. In addition to the issue that excessive posting behaviors reduce trust, the contact also risks that his or her recommendations will be ignored as users classify them as spam. In this case, the value and ultimately the effectiveness of the recommendation diminishes and users will not act on it (Leskovec et al, 2007). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the current study illustrate that OSN users should become sensitive to the posting norms in a network and adjust the volume (e.g., how many) and the content (e.g., interestingness) of postings and recommendations to what this particular network sees as normal posting behavior. Consequently, we suggest less excessive posting behavior may be more effective to build trust and in<sup>fl</sup>uence contacts regarding their product choices.

## Contributions to theory and implications for practice

This research addressed the understanding of user’s intention to act on a recommendation that is given by a user’s OSN contacts. Overall, our research model and results contribute to the literature in various ways. First, the current study contributes to the IS literature by developing an improved understanding about social media technologies. OSN have been examined regarding their ability to form, foster, and re-establish personal relationships, thereby enabling interactions that cross spatial and temporal boundaries. Rather than treating this type of social media as a singular monolithic artifact, in the current study we focused on the posting feature in OSN that enables OSN users to seek and give recommendations.

Separating and being explicit about OSN features would allow for examining other OSN characteristics on their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust and recommendation acting, such as media synchronicity (Dennis et al, 2008). Posting in Facebook’s OSN is asynchronous whereas talking via voice or video conferencing is synchronous. In a conversation both communication partners are present, which provides high transmission velocity (i.e., speed at which a message is delivered) but low reprocessability (i.e., ability to reexamine or process a message) (Dennis et al, 2008). While talking, the user can ask for more details and clari<sup>fi</sup>cations regarding a recommendation; however, once the talk is <sup>fi</sup>nished, the user needs to rely on his or her memories or notes taken during the talk to recall the recommendation. In contrast, postings on Facebook’s newsfeed can be read asynchronously, allowing for reprocessability (Dennis et al, 2008). Thus, this OSN feature increases the user’s ability to re-process and reconsider the recommendation before acting on it. Hence, we recommend that future research examines a user’s recommendation trust and intention to act associated with other technology characteristics, such as the use of synchronous vs asynchronous communication features (Matook & Brown, 2008).

Second, this work contributes to research in social psychology and the body of literature on relationships. Our research shows that closeness in<sup>fl</sup>uences trust in OSN recommendations, whereas similarity does not. Thus, our work points to the importance of thinking carefully about relationships in an online environment. Prior research has demonstrated that for average Internet users their of<sup>fl</sup>ine friendships were stronger in breadth, depth, and commitment compared with their online friendships (Chan & Cheng, 2004). Likewise, Boyd (2006) states that people have a loose de<sup>fi</sup>nition of friendship in an online context and add others to their contact list because ‘it would be socially inappropriate to say no because you know them’ (Boyd, 2006, p. 8). Our results provide a more nuanced picture as we identify closeness, but not similarity, as a key factor in an OSN environment. Hence, our study suggests that additional research attention is needed to explore the concept of relationships and particularly friendship in OSN settings. For example, in close relationships, partners disclose intimate details to each other, frequently and reciprocally (Hays, 1984; Hartup, 1993). In fact, disclosing and recommending through OSN requires less effort because of the location and time <sup>fl</sup>exibility the technology offers (Bonebrake, 2002). However, the positive impact of self-disclosure on closeness may be less strong for OSN relationships as users self-disclose publicly to entire networks of contacts (Manago et al, 2012).

Third, our work contributes to knowledge on peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence and recommendation sources that are of interest in marketing research. WOM is a well-known mechanism for spreading recommendations by customers (Brown & Reingen, 1987; Anderson, 1998). OSN provide a new dimension to this phenomenon through the ability to make recommendations to multiple people who are connected by some degree of familiarity. In this way, the OSN enables multiplication effects of a recommendation across an entire network, instead of a linear chain of recommendations given from one person to another. Closeness, past experiences, and posting behavior are crucial factors that facilitate the co-creation of WOM messages through interactions among personal contacts. In particular, closeness, in combination with the posting behavior of a WOM recommender, facilitates spreading that message. Consequently, we recommend that WOM research considers which and how OSN features are used when users communicate in OSN about products and services.

This work has a number of implications for practice. First, the study is relevant for marketers regarding how to leverage and utilize OSN for their advertising campaigns. People act on recommendations made by others because they trust in the opinion of people they know and feel close to. Because trusting is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by closeness, companies could think about strategies to decrease the customer-company distance thereby appearing closer to an OSN user. Research has provided examples of how companies can achieve closeness to OSN users; for example, prior studies examined how companies may set up effective virtual customer environments (Culnan et al,

2010) and how Starbucks uses different forms of social media for its customer dialog management (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010). At a company’s fan page, executives may post and thus, interact with OSN users on a personal level (Culnan et al, 2010), which may be seen by the user as something special and may not necessarily be perceived as a marketing initiative. Nevertheless, the user-executive communication may increase customer intimacy resulting in an OSN user feeling closer to the company. Similarly, Starbucks engages customers at their social media site by sourcing and subsequently, implementing their ideas to improve service delivery (Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010), which could be seen as a shared activity (e.g., cocreation) that in turn may increase the user’s felt closeness to the company (Randall et al, 2011).

In addition, more companies should provide easy-to-use opportunities, similar to that provided by Facebook with its ‘Like’ button, to allow users to spread recommendations to their OSN contacts (Harris & Dennis, 2011). Our study highlights the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of this feature for companies. However, OSN users might hesitate to publicly communicate to their entire network their preferences and behaviors (e. g., purchases made). Thus, companies may want to consider the use of incentive schemes to motivate this form of company recommendation, for example by offering discounts when a user agrees to have their purchases posted into the OSN. However, our results also highlight that uninteresting and too many postings may be seen as excessive behaviors in OSN that may result in a user not acting on recommendations. Thus, companies are advised to manage both the content, to keep it interesting, and the volume, to avoid too many posts of product-related messages.

Second, the current study has implications for OSN recommendations that are not related to evaluating products. Personal communications in OSN cover a wide range of recommendation topics, including providing support to stay on track with a current <sup>fi</sup>tness program or to conserve water and energy (Munson, 2011; Oakley & Salam, 2011). Although the current study is positioned within a marketing orientation, examining a user’s intention to follow a product recommendation, our results are also useful for recommendations regarding health and wellness behaviors. We anticipate that trust plays an important role in these contexts as well. The difference for these recommendations is that they may be triggered to a larger extent by similarity (e.g., goal of losing weight) among OSN contacts. While the contacts will have some degree of closeness because they get to know each other through the OSN over time, their levels of similarity may be relevant for trusting in and acting on the recommendation.

Third, this work provides important insights for developers and providers of OSN. Because our results demonstrate that closeness and posting behavior affect trust and intention to act on recommendations, OSN interfaces may be re-designed to consider these factors. For example, developers of OSN sites may want to provide a dedicated space on a user’s OSN for seeking and giving recommendations. Currently, all types of postings (i.e., users’ actions, current locations, emotional states, and recommendations) are made in the commenting feature, for example in Facebook’s newsfeed. However, a dedicated space for recommendations would allow decoupling recommendations from the other comments, thus, giving the impression that one OSN contact may post less only because two separate spaces exist.

Furthermore, options such as highlighting the interestingness of a recommendation and listing information regarding whether or not the OSN user followed the recommendation are valuable information for other OSN users that could be used for future consumption decisions. In the same light, mechanisms that enable a user to choose and <sup>fi</sup>lter which recommendations are displayed based on the closeness of different contacts may assist in reducing information overload. Thus, it might be possible to utilize the grouping feature in Facebook as a means to express closeness. Users should be able to tailor their interface such that recommendations made by a contact from a group ranked high in closeness are displayed <sup>fi</sup>rst, whereas recommendations from contracts of lower closeness are displayed thereafter or not at all.

## Limitations and future research

As with any empirical research, the current study has limitations that must be acknowledged. The <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation is associated with our sampling procedure. Our sample is not random; rather we used snowball sampling to recruit participants (Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981; Baltar & Brunet, 2012). This sampling approach could have introduced a selection bias; however, we believe any bias is minimal because the snowball was started by two members of the research team with disjointed OSN networks.

Another limitation may be the use of only an Englishlanguage questionnaire. In fact, the availability of a German-language questionnaire for the German-based OSN might have made the questionnaire accessible to additional respondents. However, a sensitivity analysis in which we split the sample into two groups (<sup>fl</sup>uent in English vs not <sup>fl</sup>uent English) and compared the means for the <sup>fi</sup>ve re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs (via an independent sample ttest) did not show signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the two groups. Furthermore, it is possible that when respondents were asked in the questionnaire to think of a friend from their OSN, they focused on their closest and most similar OSN contacts only. However, the variance in the constructs of closeness and similarity suggests that this was not a problem. Future research is encouraged to examine ‘real’ OSN recommendations and related user behaviors to augment our results. Such a study might also examine differences in recommendations directly posted in the OSN compared with recommendations distributed to the network as a result of Internet browsing, for example by using Facebook’s ‘Like’ button at a company’s webpage.

Another limitation relates to the conceptualization of an OSN contact’s recommendation. We treated the recommendations as monolithic and did not consider that different types of recommendations can be made. In the same light, we did not capture an OSN contact’s expertise on different products and services. It is possible that a contact’s recommendation is helpful for one type of product (e.g., restaurant), and less helpful for another product (e.g., vacuum cleaner). To address this issue, we have included in our model the factor of past recommendation experiences. Future research is needed to gain an understanding on the impact of expertise and product type on recommendation acceptance in OSN.

Another area for future research is to examine the network structure of a user's OSN contacts to understand the impact of a recommender’s position in the social network on the likelihood that the recommendation is accepted. This research could offer additional insights into trust in and acceptance of a recommendation by leveraging social network analysis measures (e.g., centrality, density) to examine the OSN contact network position in relation to all other connections (Wasserman & Faust, 1994) and subsequently, determine how the position relates to recommendation trust and intention to act. Although the current study focused on relationship characteristics and behaviors to examine a user’s trust in an OSN contact’s recommendation, we recommend expanding the concept of trust to include other forms of trust, such as trust in the technology-enabled environment of the OSN and trust in the recommender.

In addition, future research may want to extend our conceptualization of posting behavior. The current study focused only on post-initiating behaviors and how this behavior may affect trust in this person’s recommendations. Post-responding behavior may amplify the impact on trust and acting on the recommendation because the WOM message is broadcast to the entire network of OSN contacts. Furthermore, research may want to explore the extent to which post-responding behavior affects the closeness – suggesting frequent response may increase closeness – and on similarity – creating awareness for similar aspects, between two OSN contacts.

Finally, another area of future research could be to extend the current study to other types of OSN, particularly those associated with non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t endeavors, such as inspire.com for health and wellness (Kamel Boulos & Wheeler, 2007; Keckley, 2010). Individuals are motivated to join these OSN to interact based on similarity, either because of their personal health condition or that of a loved one. The communication on these sites provides support, accountability, and advice (Munson, 2011). Yet, unlike Facebook, these OSN are typically composed via networks of strangers. Given our results that similarity did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust in a recommendation, it would be interesting to understand how recommendations are trusted and acted on in OSN where, at least at the beginning, no personal relationships exist.
