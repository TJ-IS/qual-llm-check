# THE MORE, THE MERRIER? HOW THE NUMBER OF PARTNERS IN A STANDARD-SETTING INITIATIVE AFFECTS SHAREHOLDER'S RISK AND RETURN $^{1}$

Nitin Aggarwal
College of Business, San Jose State University, San Jose, CA 95192 U.S.A. {aggarwal\_n@cob.sjsu.edu}

Qizhi Dai

LeBow College of Business, Drexel University, Philadelphia, PA 19104 U.S.A. {qd24@drexel.edu}

Eric A. Walden

Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409 U.S.A. {eric.walden@ttu.edu}

Firms often collaborate with other firms to set information technology standards in order to decrease each firm's individual risk. But does this work? We propose that, in a capital market setting, establishing standards in a group does not decrease the total risk faced by an individual firm's shareholders. However, the market risks its investors face decrease and idiosyncratic risks increase, changing the risk profiles of the group members. We collected data on standard-setting events from 1996 to 2005. In our dataset, a firm obtained a 4.07 percent, three-day cumulative risk-adjusted return on stock price when engaging in a standard-setting initiative, after controlling event year, firm size, and group size. More importantly, we found that an increase in the number of firms in the group decreased the risk-adjusted abnormal return and the market risk (as measured by beta) of each firm, but increased the idiosyncratic risk (as measured by the variance of firm returns). Our findings suggest that firms electing to participate in a large standardization group obtain a reduction in abnormal returns on stocks on the days of the standard-setting events. They also expect to reduce market risks but increase idiosyncratic risks after the standard-setting events, as compared to firms choosing to participate in a smaller group or attempting to standardize their products unilaterally. This study contributes to the literature on IT standards and standardization, and expands our understanding of the implications of standardization strategy on shareholder risks.

Keywords: Standard, standardization, standard-setting, event study, returns on IT investment, risk of IT investment

## Introduction

Setting standards for information technology products can be risky. For example, the now bankrupt Circuit City took a \$130 million write-down when it discontinued its support of the DivX standard. $^{2}$ One strategy used by firms to address risk is to set standards with several partner firms through joint ventures (Koh and Venkatraman 1991). However, joint standard-setting activities can create new risks (Gellhorn and Miller 1995). For example, while the DVD standard was created by a large group and widely adopted, manufacturers saw very little profit because of intense competition from overseas manufacturers (Healey 2003).

In this paper, we consider how setting standards with other firms changes the risk and return of each participant. There are two types of risks that firms and their shareholders face: market risk, which shows the degree to which a firm's market value changes with the market and idiosyncratic risk, which reflects the extent a firm effectively responds to the market change to achieve competitiveness. We find that increasing the number of participants does decrease the market risk of each participant. However, it simultaneously increases the idiosyncratic risk of each participant. We propose that risk shifting occurs because increasing the number of firms in an IT standard-setting initiative decreases the risk to customers, making them more willing to buy, even when changes in the stock market reduce their wealth. At the same time, it shifts the basis of competition from the technical specification of products to the unique features of each product, which makes the idiosyncratic choices of each firm more important. In this study, we collected data on standard-setting events during the period 1996–2005 and found that a firm generated a three-day cumulative return of 4.07 percent on stock price by joining a standard-setting initiative. Using this data set, we also found support for our theory on how the number of partners affects risks.

In the next section, we review the research background and expound our theory on the returns and risks of standard-setting initiatives. Following the theoretical arguments, we describe the empirical method used to test our theory and to collect and analyze data, and report the analysis results. We conclude with a discussion of the implications of our findings, the future research direction, and the contribution of this work.

## Literature Review

## IT Standards and Standardization

Standards are specifications that provide solutions to potential matching problems, and that are approved and adopted by a substantial number of parties (de Vries 2005, p. 4). In particular, IT standards are technology specifications that “offer agreed upon, external points of reference to which the physical and performance characteristics of current or future technologies can be compared” (Lyytinen and King 2006, p. 405). Technical specifications detail how some parts of a product must be designed in order to work properly. For example, technical specifications for DVDs define the direction of rotation of a disk, the thickness of a disk, the reflectivity of a disk’s surface, the size of a burn mark, whether a burn mark represents a one or a zero, and a variety of other relevant characteristics. $^{3}$ The technical specifications must be followed exactly, or the technical product will not work.

Standardization is the activity of constructing and establishing solutions to matching problems; the solutions are intended to be used repeatedly or continuously by a large number of parties and thus become standards (de Vries 1999, p. 155). In the present research, we use the term standardization or standard-setting to refer to activities that show the commitment by one or more firms to develop a technical specification that is hoped to be adopted as a standard.

The value of standards has been studied theoretically and empirically. Brynjolfsson and Kemerer (1996) found that spreadsheet software packages that were compatible with the market standard sold at higher prices than those that were not. Even though the value of standards is proven, proposing and promoting them is always a challenge. For example, Hanseth and his colleagues (2006) conducted a case study on the standardization of electronic patient record systems in Norway, and pointed out that this standardization initiative failed to achieve its goal due to the complexity of technology and the standardization process itself. Thus, standardization is both risky and rewarding.

Furthermore, in competing to set the standards, firms adopt various strategies and institutional forms that can affect the value and risks of standard-setting initiatives (Lyytinen and King 2006). One strategy is for firms to unilaterally develop and promote their proprietary technologies as the standards, forcing others to offer compatible products (Shapiro and

Varian 1999). Another strategy is a strategic alliance between two or a few firms (Warner 2005). For example, Sony and Philips coordinated their technology development efforts in introducing CD technology and making it the industry standard (Peek 2010). Another institutional strategy is the formation of committees through which member firms can set up formal procedures to reach consensus on standards (Farrell and Saloner 1988).

One crucial dimension that differentiates these various standardization approaches is the number of parties that take part in developing and establishing the standards. Research in other areas such as joint ventures (Valdes-Llaneza and Garcia-Canal 2006) found that the number of parties in a coalition plays an important role in the performance of the coalition. Therefore, in this research, we launch our inquiry into the gains and risks of standardization by examining the effect of the number of participants.

## Event-Induced Shareholder Wealth and Risk

We test our propositions about shareholder risks in standard-setting initiatives using an extension to the standard event-study method, which allowed us to measure idiosyncratic and market risk as well as return (Dewan and Ren 2007). Event studies are used in economics, finance, and management research (MacKinlay 1997; McWilliams and Siegel 1997). The method is based on the assumption that capital markets can quickly and efficiently incorporate new information into the price of a stock. When events occur that have important implications in firm strategies, operations, and financial stances, capital markets will react quickly. Therefore, researchers have applied this method to a range of studies.

The event-study method has also been adopted in the Information Systems field. When a firm makes a public announcement of an IT investment, the market should rapidly revise the value of the company in light of the new information. IS researchers have used abnormal returns on stock prices and short-term changes in market value (typically over one or two days), as a proxy measure of the return to an IT investment. For example, Dos Santos et al. (1993) examined the effects of IT investment announcements on firms' market value during the 1980s, and Im et al. (2001) extended this study to measure changes in perceptions over time. Subramani and Walden (2001) used financial markets to examine returns from electronic commerce initiatives well before any accounting data was available. Dehning, Richardson, and Zmud (2003) looked into the value relevance of different types of IT investments from 1981 to 1996 with an event study, and found that transformative IT investments generated the highest abnormal stock returns. Recently, researchers have conducted event studies to investigate the factors that affect the effectiveness of IT investment and management decisions. For example, Duan, Grover, and Balakrishnan (2009) found that business process outsourcing is more likely to be successful when the firm outsourced internally cultivated primary processes. Roztocki and Weistroffer (2010) studied how the firm's cost management practice affected stock price reactions to IT investment events.

In addition to IT investment value, the event study is also used to investigate the effects of other IT announcements. For example, Chatterjee, Richardson, and Zmud (2001) examined how announcements of CIO positions and hires affected stock returns. Hovav and D'Arcy (2003) examined how security breaches affected the market returns of Internet and non-Internet firms. Also with an event study, Kannan, Rees, and Sridhar (2007) found no significant negative effects of information security breaches on firm stock returns. Aggarwal, Dai, and Walden (2005) examined the effect of XML standard announcements on shareholder returns. Oh, Gallivan, and Kim (2006) conducted an event study on the transactional risks in IT outsourcing announcements. Some of these studies (Anderson et al. 2003, Subramani and Walden 2001) produced extremely high estimates of returns to IT investments. This was partly because prior work did not consider changes in risks or changes in the betas in the market model. To consider changes in risk and betas, IS researchers have proposed better models of returns based on finance theory (Dehning et al. 2004).

In addition to the impacts of given events on shareholder wealth, researchers have studied the risk effects of various events using the event-study method. In event studies, event risks are measured by variances in the returns on stock prices after events occur, including market-based risks and firm-specific risks (Boehmer et al. 1991). For example, Ohlson and Penman (1985) found increased risk, measured by variability in stock returns, after stock splits. Bhargava and Fraser's (1998) study showed that banks faced higher market-based risks after the Federal Reserve Board allowed bank holding companies to engage in underwriting corporate debt and equity. Healy and Palepu (1990) showed an increase in firm-specific risks accompanying such events. This method of measuring risks is also used in studying the impact of firm strategies such as diversification on organizational risks (Aaker and Jacobson 1987; Miller and Bromiley 1990).

In IS research, Dewan and Ren (2007) investigated the risk effects in e-commerce events. The Dewan and Ren model recognizes that an IT event can change both the value and the risk profile of a firm. The model allows a study to control for risk and return simultaneously, and hence offers a better comparison among IT events and a potential explanation for the high but not risk-adjusted rates of return observed in prior research.

## Theory

## Risk-Adjusted Returns to IT Standard-Setting Initiatives

From an event-study perspective, we view a public announcement about a standard-setting initiative as an event. When such an event occurs, the market responds by changing the value of firms that take part in the initiative. The change can be captured by the returns on stock prices. Risk-adjusted returns are abnormal returns on stock prices after risks are controlled, that is, after accounting for the changes in betas. As IT events can change both the value and risk profile of a firm, risk-adjusted returns can better show the payoff of IT initiatives (Dewan and Ren 2007).

Firms can set a standard unilaterally or with other firms. In general, setting standards unilaterally offers the highest potential payoff, because it offers the standard-setter a monopoly power over the technology. This is summed up nicely in Dranove and Gandal's (2003) Principle 1: “A monopoly in the bush is often worth more than an oligopoly in hand.” A firm with monopoly power has two advantages: it controls a larger share of the market, and it can extract more surplus from consumers. Thus, we offer the following hypothesis:

H1 (Risk-Adjusted Return Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the less risk-adjusted abnormal return a participating firm's shareholders receive.

It is also likely that the risk-adjusted returns will depend on the size of the firm. In particular, since large firms are closely studied and hence more information is available about them, any particular event is less informative for large firms and hence reactions are muted (Reburn 1994). Moreover, we expect that large firms have many lines of business, and any particular standard will not affect all of those lines of business; small firms have fewer lines of business, so any particular standard will have a more relative impact on smaller firms. Therefore, we offer the following hypothesis:

H2 (Risk-Adjusted Return Size-Moderation Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the less risk-adjusted abnormal return a participating firm's shareholders receive, but the absolute effect is smaller for larger firms.

## Market Risk of IT Standard-Setting Initiatives

When customers invest in a new standard, they are concerned with the expected value of the standard, which depends on how long a standard will be viable. If a standard is accepted by more firms that show their support by participating in the standard-setting initiative, then prospective customers of the standard will have more confidence in its viability in the future. There are two reasons for this. First, with more firms backing the standard, the standard will retain sufficient support even if some advocates drop out due to acquisitions or changes in business. Second, if more firms support the standard, it is more likely to become the de facto standard through positive feedback effects (Katz and Shapiro 1994).

From the customer's point of view, therefore, a standard supported by the entire market has little uncertainty. The customer's only uncertainty arises from choice of vendor, who may have features that are more or less useful. On the other hand, a standard backed by a single firm has high uncertainty for customers, because the possibility is high that it will not become the de facto standard at the end of the standardization competition.

The stock market represents a large source of wealth to individuals and to corporations who are the customers of a standard. The Arrow hypothesis correlates risk and wealth: aversion for a particular risk should decrease with a customer's wealth (Arrow 1965). More generally, the Arrow hypothesis suggests that a gamble becomes more attractive as the customer's wealth increases, and less attractive as wealth decreases. When the stock market goes up, customers become wealthier; therefore, by Arrow's hypothesis, they become less risk-averse. When the stock market goes down, they become more risk-averse. An increase in risk aversion has a relatively greater negative impact on outcomes with high uncertainty than on outcomes with low uncertainty, for most reasonable utility functions.

Because customers view cooperatively set standards as less risky than unilaterally set standards, they have more confidence in cooperatively set standards than unilaterally set standards. Therefore, a firm that chooses to set standards in a cooperative manner faces less fluctuation in customer demand in response to changes in the stock market. We refer to an individual stock's sensitivity to changes in stock market as market risk, and measure it as a coefficient by fitting the market model of abnormal stock returns. Thus, we offer the following hypothesis:

H3 (Market Risk Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the less market risk a participating firm's shareholders must bear.

Firm size can moderate the effect of the number of firms on customer risk perceptions. A single large firm may have the same effect on the confidence of potential customers as several smaller ones. A larger firm is more likely to stay in business, less likely to be merged out of existence, and more able to support multiple standards. Furthermore, the support of a larger firm makes a standard more likely to become the de facto standard than the support of a smaller firm. This suggests that large firms benefit less from partnering than small firms, because large firms already reassure potential adopters. Therefore, we offer the following hypothesis:

H4 (Market Risk Size-Moderation Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the less market risk a participating firm's shareholders must bear, but the absolute effect is smaller for larger firms.

## Idiosyncratic Risk of IT Standard-Setting Initiatives

Besides market risk originating from fluctuations in the macroeconomic environment, there exist uncertainties in earnings that are determined by firm-specific factors (Dewan and Ren 2007). Firms have unique organizational knowledge and innovating capability that determine each firm's ability to absorb, interpret, and implement new technology and critical information quickly (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Having different resources, they respond to the environment in different ways (Barney 1991). Some of those responses may be more or less effective. We refer to the uncertainties about the effectiveness of unique responses as idiosyncratic risks. Idiosyncratic risk reflects the firm's ability, or lack thereof, to achieve competitive advantage in a particular situation (Chatterjee, Lubatkin, and Schulze 1999).

When firms develop a standard jointly, they negotiate the specifications of the standard through collective actions (Markus et al. 2006). During this process, proprietary knowledge becomes collective knowledge; participants learn from peers and obtain information about competitors (Leiponen 2008). Thus, the distribution of knowledge moves toward homogeneity among participants, reducing the competitive advantage for individual firms and leading to increased idiosyncratic risk.

Hence, when more firms participate in a standard-setting initiative, the benefit for each firm comes more from competition in the market than from expansion of the total market (Aggarwal et al. 2006). This increases the risk that any particular firm's idiosyncratic resources will be unproductive. We derive the measurement of idiosyncratic risk from the market model of stock returns, and propose the following hypothesis:

H5 (Idiosyncratic Risk Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the more idiosyncratic risk a participating firm's shareholders must bear.

A larger firm is more able to generate competitive advantage in producing a standardized product. If a firm already has other products placed in the market, then the companies that use those products can reduce relationship-building costs and enhance compatibility by staying with the same vendor. Moreover, larger firms have more resources to deploy in enhancing and promoting products. Thus, a firm's size moderates the effect of the number of firms on idiosyncratic risk because the larger firms can still exert monopoly power even in the presence of smaller competitors. Thus, we offer the following hypothesis:

H6 (Idiosyncratic Risk Size-Moderation Hypothesis): The more firms involved in a standard-setting initiative, the more idiosyncratic risk a participating firm's shareholders must bear, but the absolute effect is less for larger firms.

In summary, a smaller consortium leads to greater uncertainty about what standard technology will be used in the future. A larger consortium reduces the uncertainty about what standard will be used, but leads to uncertainty about which firm's products will be successful based on the idiosyncratic characteristics of each firm supporting the standard. ## Risk and Return Measurement Method

We follow Dewan and Ren (2007) in using a risk-adjusted market model (RMM) to estimate the risk changes. This is a variation of the event study (Brown and Warner 1985; MacKinlay 1997; McWilliams and Siegel 1997) that allows for the simultaneous estimation of both risk and return. Unlike a typical event study, however, this model includes both pre-event and post-event data. The model recognizes that the event may actually change the risk structure of a stock. To calculate changes in risk, it is necessary to estimate the risk both before and after the event.

![](/api/attachments/UXPNTTG2/fulltext/images/c81e21f4063cfa4a0f7610d465198ed50ed847e64aee9eac918ff82071fdb481.jpg)  
Figure 1. Risk and Returns in Standard-Setting

Event-study analysis allows researchers to examine how an event (the public announcement of a standard-setting initiative, in this case) affects the return on a firm's stock. The Dewan and Ren model posits that a firm's return is

$$
R _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \alpha_ {i} ^ {\prime} D _ {A} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t} + \beta_ {i} ^ {\prime} D _ {A} R _ {m t} + \gamma_ {i} D _ {0} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

The variables $R_{it}$ and $R_{mt}$ are the return on stock i at time t and the return to the market at time t, respectively. The parameter $\alpha_{i}$ is an intercept term measuring the average return of stock i over the estimation period in the absence of any changes in the market. The coefficient $\beta_{i}$ measures the sensitivity of stock i's return to changes in the market and is thus a measure of market risk. The variable $D_{0}$ is a dummy variable that is equal to one during the event window. The variable $D_{A}$ (short for dummy after) is a dummy variable that is equal to one on all days after the event window ends. A three-day event window is consistent with prior literature (Chatterjee, Richardson, and Zmud 2001) and is generally used to capture events that may have occurred before the trading day started or after the trading day ended. Thus, the coefficient $\gamma_{i}$ measures the return attributable to the event, and the coefficients $\alpha_{i}'$ and $\beta_{i}'$ measure the change in the parameters $\alpha_{i}$ and $\beta_{i}$ after the event. Finally, $\varepsilon_{it}$ is an error term that is assumed to have zero mean and finite variance. The variance of $\varepsilon_{it}$ may be different before and after the event, but is always finite.

The risk-adjusted return is $\gamma_{i}$ . The change in market risk is $\beta_{i}'$ . The change in idiosyncratic risk is the mean squared error of $\varepsilon$ after the event minus the mean squared error of $\varepsilon$ before the event.

Fama and French (1993) identify two factors other than market returns that help explain the returns on common stocks. The measures are commonly used in event studies to get better estimates of the parameters of interest. The first factor is SMB (small minus big), which measures the difference in the mean return of small market-cap stocks and big market-cap stocks. The second factor is HML (high minus low), which is the difference in the mean return to high book-to-market ratio stocks minus low book-to-market ratio stocks. The data were downloaded from French's website $^{4}$ on January 26, 2009. We also ran the models without the Fama-French factors, and the same results held. Our ultimate model of returns is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} R _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \alpha_ {i} ^ {\prime} D _ {A} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t} + \beta_ {i} ^ {\prime} D _ {A} R _ {m t} + \gamma_ {i} D _ {0} \\ & + \beta_ {s b} S M B + \beta_ {h l} H M L + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

This model allows the event to have both an immediate return and an enduring effect on risk, in contrast to a typical event study that allows for the measure of an immediate return only. In Figure 2, we offer a graphical explanation of the Dewan and Ren estimation. (This is a hypothetical example and not based on any actual event. We use a hypothetical example because we can manipulate the relative effect sizes for better visual demonstration.)

Before the event in Figure 2, the return of the firm's stock closely tracks the return of the market. On the event day, the firm's stock experiences a large return relative to the market. The interesting thing is that after the event, the variance of the firm's stock return is greater and its reaction to market changes is greater. Thus, before the event, if the market goes up, the firm's stock goes up by a smaller percentage; after the event, if the market goes up, the firm's stock goes up by a larger percentage. Furthermore, the error of the change is greater, so that the stock tracks the market less precisely. Here, the degree to which the firm's stock changes with the market is the market risk, and the error of this change shows the idiosyncratic risk.

![](/api/attachments/UXPNTTG2/fulltext/images/19ce64920ad1edaf7c6e39d6786aa24e416065bb2ca139b6076b92556c139e00.jpg)  
Figure 2. Graph of Hypothetical Event-Induced Risk Change

An example event that Figure 2 depicts is a firm's entering a new line of business through acquisition, when a firm takes liquid assets and converts them to business assets. Business assets have a greater potential for both failure and success and tend to track the market less precisely than liquid assets. Setting a new standard may be thought of as entering a new line of business, in that a firm must take some assets and devote them to creating and promoting a standard, as well as the products associated with the standard. This may be beneficial to the firm, but it does imply a change in risk structure. Thus, to fully understand the implications of a standard-setting initiative on a firm's stock return, it is important to estimate the risk changes.

## Process, Data, and Analysis

## Data Collection

We follow the typical event-study method in collecting the data (Aggarwal et al. 2006; Dehning et al. 2004; Im et al.

2001; Subramani and Walden 2001). We examined public announcements of standard-setting initiatives on news wires as archived by the LexisNexis database. We first performed a text search on PR Newswire and Business Wire announcements from 1996 to 2005 that contained the combination of terms standard or standardization; develop, create, introduce, or join; and data, communication, telecommunication, or process. From this search, we identified 1,983 announcements containing the specified text. To identify which announcements were the relevant kind of events (as opposed to extraneous announcements that just happened to contain the text), we counted the announced event as a standard-setting initiative if the announcement was about

1. product or technology compatibility or interoperability;

2. a new standard, instead of products implementing an existing standard; AND

3. the proposal, design, or development of a standard, instead of adoption, use, or promotion of a standard.

After removing repeats and nonevents, we identified 299 unique events based on the above rules. We then took additional steps to retrieve our sample from the collected announcements. First, we deleted firms with confounding events, which we defined as mergers and acquisitions, earning announcements, and changes in board directors, which occurred from two days before to one day after the public announcements on the standard-setting events.

Second, we focused on identifying the number of firms participating in the announced standard-setting initiatives. In some events, a single firm developed an IT standard. In other events, a group of firms joined forces to develop standards through alliances or industry consortia. For the former, we counted the number of firms in the standard-setting initiative as one. For the latter, we counted the number of firms that were identified in the announcement as participants in the development and design of the standard. We also created separate entries for individual firms identified in the same event announcement.

In this process, we did not always capture every firm in the event, because some were privately held, and some did not have sufficient data to pass our data tests. For example, on June 11, 1997, Firefly, Netscape, and Microsoft announced that they were cooperating with VeriSign on the Open Profiling Standard (OPS) proposal during the standards review process of the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) (Business Wire, June 11, 1997). For this event, we identified four firms that were leading the standardization initiative—FireFly, Netscape, Microsoft, and VeriSign—which led us to set the number of firms as four for this event. Among these four firms, FireFly was a private company, while the other three were publicly traded. Hence, we created three entries for this event, one for each of the three public companies.

In most events, the number of firms can be identified. But in some cases, the announcement does not reveal the exact number of companies that participated in the standard-setting initiative. For example, on November 8, 1999, 3Com Corporation announced the Mini PCI specification as approved standard by the PCI Special Interest Group, which was developed by 3Com and a consortium of notebook systems manufacturers and networking and data communications solution providers (Business Wire, November 8, 1999). In this case, the exact number of firms in the standard-setting initiative is unknown, although clearly more than one firm participated. Because the dependent variable of interest is the number of firms participating, we dropped these announcements. We applied this coding scheme to all events to further extract our sample.

Third, we collected market return data from CRSP. We collected the data for 180 calendar days before and 180 calendar days after the event date. This yielded approximately 120 trading days of returns depending on holidays, which is consistent with estimation models used in prior literature (Dewan and Ren 2007; Subramani and Walden 2001). We follow Dewan and Ren (2007) in excluding firms with an average trading volume of less than 50,000 shares and those with an average trading price of less than \$1. We then removed firms with less than 60 trading days of data before or after the event, and deleted firms that did not have entries for total sales or SIC code in COMPUSTAT. Finally, we collected 258 unique initiatives, and 789 firm-event entries in total. That is 789 unique combinations of firm and event that are the observations of usable data.

The number of the announcements and firm-event entries during the study period 1996–2005 is summarized in Table 1. Note that column 2 reflects the number of firms we are analyzing, not the total number of firms in the announcement. Many firms may have been dropped for the reasons above, or if they were not publicly traded. Our data tell us that firms were most active in developing and proposing IT standards during the years 1998–2001. They were also more collaborative in recent years; in general, more firms worked jointly in developing standards after the year 2000 than before.

Some firms were involved in more than one announcement. Table 2 shows the demographics of the sample by unique ticker symbols. There were 196 unique firms in our sample. The mean and mode number of times a firm appeared was one. The maximum number of times a firm appeared was 59 (both Microsoft and IBM appeared 59 times).

## Variables and Estimation Models

To test the hypotheses, we examined the impacts of the number of firms in standard-setting events on firm returns and the risks. Using the data we collected, we estimated equation (2) for every stock. We do not use White's (1980) heteroskedasticity-consistent estimator because ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates are unbiased, and are the same as what White's estimator produces. Heteroskedasticity affects the standard error of the estimates, but does not bias the residual. Because we only use the estimates and residuals, and not the standard error of the estimates, we use simple OLS.

In estimating equation (2), we used an equally weighted portfolio including distributions (CRSP variable ewretd) for the market return $R_{mt}$ and returns including dividends (CRSP variable ret) for the firm return $R_{it}$ . The estimated coefficient $\gamma_{i}$ is the measure of abnormal return after controlling for risk. The estimated coefficient $\beta_{i}$ is the measurement for market risk, $^{5}$ and we refer to it as $MktRisk_{i}$ (subscript i indicates the $i^{th}$ firm-event observation). We also estimated the residuals of market models before and after the event, and then obtained the difference between the average sum of squares of the two residuals as the measurement for idiosyncratic risk, which we refer to as $I_{doRisk_{i}}$ . After obtaining the measurements for returns, market risk, and idiosyncratic risk, we tested the hypothesized effects of the number of firms by estimating the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} D V _ {j} = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} \times \log \text {FirmCount} _ {j} + \delta_ {2} \times \log \text {Sales} _ {j} \\ \quad + \delta_ {3} \times \log \text {Sales} _ {j} \times \log \text {FirmCount} _ {j} \\ \quad + \delta_ {4} \times S I C 7 3 7 x _ {j} + \delta_ {5} \times S I C 3 5 7 x _ {j} \\ \quad + \delta_ {6} \times \text {Compliance} _ {j} + \delta_ {7} \times \text {Submission} _ {j} \\ \quad + \delta_ {8} \times \text {Upgrade} _ {j} + \sum_ {Y - 1 9 9 7} ^ {2 0 0 5} \delta_ {y} \cdot Y E A R _ {Y, j} + \mu_ {j} \end{array} (3)
$$

Equation (3) is a general form of the OLS model for estimating the effects of number of firms on stock return, market risk, and idiosyncratic risk. The subscript j indicates the $j^{th}$ firm-event observation in our data set. The dependent variable $DV_{j}$ can be either $Return_{j}$ , $MktRisk_{j}$ , or $IdoRisk_{j}$ . The independent variable $logFirmCount_{j}$ is the number of firms that participated in the standard-setting initiative announced in the event. We control for the effect of firm size by including the log of firm total sales, represented by the variable $logSales_{j}$ .

When $DV_{j} = Return_{j}$ , equation (3) tests hypotheses about returns (H1 and H2). And the estimated value of coefficient $\delta_{1}$ is expected to be negative because firms in a large group will obtain lower returns individually (H1). Moreover, the value of $\delta_{3}$ is expected to be positive because big firms will be affected less than small firms by the group size (H2).

When $DV_{j} = MktRisk_{j}$ , equation (3) tests our hypotheses regarding market risk (H3 and H4). The estimated coefficient $\delta_{1}$ is expected to be negative according to the Market Risk Hypothesis, which posits a negative main effect of the number of participating firms on market risk. As our Market Risk Size Moderation Hypothesis proposes an opposite moderating effect of firm size, the estimated coefficient $\delta_{3}$ of the interaction term is expected to be positive.

When $DV_{j} = IdoRisk_{j}$ , equation (3) tests our hypotheses on idiosyncratic risk (H5 and H6). The Idiosyncratic Risk Hypothesis proposes a positive main effect of the number of participating firms, so the estimated coefficient $\delta_{1}$ is expected to be positive. Conversely, the coefficient $\delta_{3}$ of the interaction term is expected to be negative because the Idiosyncratic Risk Size Moderation Hypothesis proposes an opposite moderating effect of firm size.

In addition, we control for IT industry by specifying dummy variables $SIC737x_{j}$ and $SIC357x_{j}$ for firms in SIC codes beginning with 737 and 357, which are roughly IT machines and IT services. Specifically, the variable $SIC737x_{j}$ equals one when the SIC code of firm j starts with 737, and equals zero otherwise. With these two dummy variables, we can control the possible effects of the IT industry structure, and thus to some extent separate providers from users of IT products.

We also include three dummy variables that control for the effects of different standard attributes. The first is compliance, which indicates whether the standard in the announcement was compliant with any existing standards. The second is submission, which indicates if the standard was submitted to a committee for review or approval. The third is upgrade, which indicates if the standard was an upgraded version of an existing standard.

The variable $Year_{Y,j}$ is a dummy variable that is equal to one in the year y when the event was announced for firm j, and covers all the years from 1996 to 2005. Because the intercept is included in the model, the dummy variable for year 1996, $Year_{1996,j}$ is not included, indicating that year 1996 is treated as the baseline year. Finally, $\mu_{j}$ is an independently and identically distributed random error term that represents other effects that are not captured by the independent variable and the control variables.

All correlations are below 0.3 except the interaction term, which is correlated with the number of firms, typical for interaction terms. ## The BEGLS Estimator

One problem that we may face in estimating equation (3) is that the error terms may covary. Specifically, we have a situation where there may be several observations from a single standard-setting initiative. It is likely that the abnormal returns attributable to firms doing the same thing on the same day will be correlated. Moreover, several firms appear more than once in the data, and it is also likely that the abnormal returns attributable to a specific firm from a standard-setting initiative will be correlated with the returns to that same firm when it engages in a different standard-setting initiative. Thus, we have a panel of data across firms and events.

Unfortunately, it is not a nice panel. It is unbalanced because not every event has the same number of firms and not every firm is in every event. Even worse (from a statistical perspective), it is sparse in that many firms only appear a few times and many events only have a few firms; in fact, many events only have one firm. Finally, the independent variable of concern is the size of a panel, $^{6}$ which means that removing the sparse observations also markedly removes the variable of interest. Thus, estimation of the parameters is not amenable to packaged software routines. Therefore, like Dewan and Ren, we constructed our own estimator for an unbalanced sparse panel to deal with the above event-clustering problem, which we term “bended estimated generalized least squares” (BEGLS) estimator. See Appendix C for full details.

## Analysis Results

The results of both the OLS and the BEGLS estimation are presented below. See Tables 5 and 6 for estimation results. The qualitative results are similar in that the coefficients are of the same signs and similar magnitude, the variables of interest are significant in both sets of regressions, and the relative significance of the variables remains. However, the BEGLS estimates fit much better than the OLS estimates. This is what we would expect to find if the data had a correlation matrix of the form we postulated. In that case, BEGLS is a better estimator. However, the qualitative results stay the same, suggesting that the effects are strong enough to be felt even in a misspecified OLS model.

Interpreting coefficients that are also part of interaction terms is often confusing because both the marginal effect and the standard error of the coefficient change as the interacting term changes. In our particular case, the effect of firm count on the dependent variables is a function of the sales. From equation (3), we can see that the marginal effect of $\log FirmCount$ on the dependent variable is

$$
\frac {\partial D V}{\partial \log F i r m C o u n t} = \delta_ {1} + \delta_ {3} \times \log S a l e s\tag{4}
$$

The variance of the estimate is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Var } \left(\frac {\partial D V}{\partial \log \text { FirmCount }}\right) = \text { Var } (\delta_ {1}) + 2 \text { Cov } (\delta_ {1} \cdot \delta_ {3}) \\ \times \log \text { Sales } + \text { Var } (\delta_ {3}) \times \log \text { Sales } \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Brambor, Clark, and Golder (2006) recommend plotting the marginal effect over the range of the variables to better understand the implications of the interaction term. We do so in Figures 4, 5, and 6.

## Summary of Results

Our results show significant negative coefficient of the variable $\log FirmCount$ on Return, which supports our hypothesis about the negative effect of the number of participating firms on firm market valuation (H1). While embarking on a standard-setting initiative is good in the sense that the intercept is positive and significant, the more firms that join, the less return each gains. On average, a one standard deviation increase in the number of firms will decrease the risk-adjusted abnormal return of a firm with the mean value of sales by 0.091 percent. $^{7}$ Moreover, the interaction effect on the return between the firm size and the number of participants is positive, which tells that large firms obtained higher returns than small firms when they participated in standardization coalitions of the same size. This supports the Risk-Adjusted Return Size Moderating Hypothesis (H2).

The results support the Market Risk Hypothesis (H3) as evidenced by the significant negative coefficient on the logFirmCount variable in the second column of each table. Thus, we cannot reject the theory that as more firms join an initiative, the market risk of each firm's stock decreases. Our theory suggests that this occurs because having more partners in an initiative makes a firm more robust in the face of changes in wealth in the market. On average, a one-standard-deviation increase in the number of firms will decrease the beta of a firm with the mean value of sales by 0.018. $^{8}$ In addition, the Firm Size Market Risk Hypothesis (H4) is supported.

Our results offer support for the Idiosyncratic Risk Hypothesis (H5), which is captured by significant positive coefficient of the variable $\log FirmCount$ in column three. Thus, we cannot reject the theory that as more firms join a standard-setting initiative, each faces more firm-specific risk. On average, a one-standard-deviation increase in the number of firms will increase the variance of a firm with the mean value of sales by 0.0071 percent. $^{9}$ We propose that this occurs because competition intensifies, which makes firm-specific characteristics more important to the individual firm's profitability.

Our results are weaker in relation to the moderating effect of firm size on changes in idiosyncratic risk (H6). The OLS estimates fail to reject the null of no effect at the 10 percent level of confidence, and the BEGLS estimates support it at the 1 percent level. Thus, we cannot reject the theory that larger firms' greater resources inoculate them against the negative effects of competition.

The number of firms in standard-setting initiatives is a significant predictor of the change in risks the initiatives cause for participating firms. To our best knowledge, no other work has measured the link between the number of firms and changes in risk factors. Moreover, the effects are opposite for market and idiosyncratic risk. This shows that the number of firms in an initiative does not simply increase or decrease risk; it fundamentally changes the risk structure of the firms participating in the initiative.

The variable upgrade shows positive effect on returns and negative effect on market risk. Since upgrading an existing standard shows incremental improvement or innovation in the present technology, it raises the expected returns from the investments. Furthermore, the upgraded standard is compatible with an existing standard of the same technology, and thus faces fewer obstacles in gaining the market adoption than other standardization efforts. Therefore, upgrading a standard has lower market risks. Moreover, the variables compliance and submission have negative coefficients on returns but positive coefficients on market risk. The first implication is that when firms propose a standard to be compliant with other existing standards or submit their own proprietary technologies to a committee as a candidate for a standard, they face decreases in short-term abnormal returns. In the meantime, the performance of the standard in the market will be affected by the performance of the existing standards, and thus becomes sensitive to the fluctuations in related market sectors along with its own sector. As a result, the market risk firms face goes up.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Future Research

An interesting direction for future research is incorporating more of the legal and industrial characteristics of specific standard-setting situations. A recent study by Bekkers et al. (2002) examined the legal and institutional issues for GSM standards. A deeper analysis is less generalizable, but more practically useful. In most standard-setting situations, stakeholders have different degrees of power and may have real or imagined advantages in the product.

Related to this are the intellectual property rights issues we allude to throughout the paper. It is worthwhile to create models that explicitly consider the intellectual property rights issues of standard-setting. One interesting observation is that standards are often set by firms that also happen to own the relevant patents.

In this study, the number of firms participating in standard-setting initiatives is considered exogenous. However, that may be a limiting assumption rather than a fact. In general, companies would like to appropriate the monopoly profits that come from the intellectual property rights on a standard. Firms cooperate with one another only when they think the benefits of cooperation outweigh the costs. Thus, the number of firms is chosen in the hopes of maximizing the return and minimizing the risks of the participants. It is not a random choice.

It is also possible that there are other factors not measured by this study. Press releases are relatively short (approximately 800 words) and do not contain much detail about the reasons and procedures for forming standard-setting coalitions, or about their subsequent negotiations. Circumstances such as the type of technology, the level of risk, the impact of the standard, abd the likelihood of adoption of the standard may influence a firm's decision to set standards unilaterally or as part of a group. There are factors at every step that influence whether coalitions are formed, how large they are, and how they conduct business, and these factors very likely affect the outcome. This is certainly a limitation of the method that would be worthwhile to investigate using a different method in the future. Another extension for the future study is to examine our theory when the macro-economy has changed, such as the recent economic turbulence which led to drastic declines in stock prices. Such changes would affect the investors' overall risk perceptions and would offer the context for testing the robustness of our theory.

## Conclusion

With every new technology, firms that sell the technology must choose a standard to use, and they must choose how to build that standard. Of particular concern is the choice of how many other firms with which to work. We have shown how the number of firms in a standard-setting initiative changes the risk and return of a firm's stock. Our findings give firms some basis for evaluating how many other firms to partner with in setting standards. From the perspective of risk management, firms that are sensitive to market risk would partner with many other firms, while firms that are willing to endure market risk would tend to work alone. From the perspective of resource management, firms with good competitive resources should join with other firms to establish the standard and then out-compete them in other areas. On the other hand, firms with fewer competitive resources would do better if they could control the standard themselves. The changes in risk must be balanced with the fact that firms will share the benefits of the standard with their partners.

These implications are evidenced by some anecdotes in industry. For example, in the late 1990s and early 2000, startup companies Ariba and Commerce One chose to develop XML standards for business-to-business (B2B) transactions with a large group of partners. By so doing, they reduced the market risk but increased the idiosyncratic risk. On the one hand, their XML technologies have been widely adopted, and both companies survived the broad dot-com burst. This can be viewed as a sign of reduced market risk. On the other hand, their competition in the same B2B software market generated different outcomes, which indicates the increased idiosyncratic risk. Commerce One filed for bankruptcy in 2004 and was finally sold to Perfect Commerce in 2006, while Ariba has grown to be one of the largest software service providers in B2B e-commerce. Recently, cloud computing is an emerging technology. As it is gaining adoption in the market, the development of standards has become a major issue (Greenberg 2008). At this point, the approach that firms take to set standards including the size of coalition will greatly affect the risks that each firm is going to bear and then ultimately the long-term firm performance.

Although prior research has acknowledged the risks in standard-setting initiatives, this study has taken one step further to identify and measure the risks. Moreover, we distinguished market risks from idiosyncratic risks, and found that the same standard-setting decisions shifted these two risks in opposite directions. This finding provides a marker for researchers to evaluate firms' standardization efforts.

This work establishes the great impact of standardization choices on firms. There has not been a large body of research about information systems standards, which led to the call for more work on standards and standardization by Lyytinen and King (2006). In response to this call, this research conducted an event study on standard-setting initiatives and found lasting changes related to a firm's stock in response to the standardization event. From the perspective of technology management, a standardization decision can be strategic about how a firm will invest and compete over a long period. Our study supports that a firm's choice of a technology and a coalition for standardization efforts can have an enduring impact on its market position. Atari, Amegia, Sinclair, Storage Tech, and Maxtor are all examples of companies that were harmed by choosing the wrong technology standard.

In conclusion, we empirically examined the stock market reactions to firm standard setting initiative announcements with 789 observations. We focused on the relationships between the number of partners in such initiatives and the returns and shareholder risks. In analyzing the data, we constructed our own estimator for an unbalanced sparse panel data. Our analysis results reveal that when there are more partners, a firm receives lower risk-adjusted abnormal returns; lower market risk but higher idiosyncratic risk. These findings provide guidance for firms in formulating standard-setting strategies, particularly in choosing standardization coalitions. Our research also expands our understandings of the impacts of standard setting initiatives by taking into account the changes in shareholder risks and returns.
