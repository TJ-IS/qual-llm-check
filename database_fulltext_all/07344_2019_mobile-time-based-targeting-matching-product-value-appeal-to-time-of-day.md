# Mobile Time-Based Targeting: Matching Product-Value Appeal to Time of Day

CHEE WEI PHANG, XUEMING LUO, AND ZHENG FANG

ABSTRACT: Mobile technologies, with their seamless integration into people’s everyday life, are said to enable “perpetual contact” with the users always accompanied by their mobile devices. This creates unprecedented opportunity to better understand the relationship between time and information technologies (ITs), in particular, how time of day may influence users’ IT behavior. With this understanding, companies may also be able to promote IT products by highlighting their value that appeals to users in a particular time segment of a day. Building primarily on the ego depletion theory and through conducting a combination of field experiment and survey, we show that matching IT product value appeal to people’s psychological state during different times of day can lead to more optimal outcome (higher adoption). The findings advance our understanding of how IT behavior, as a behavior embedded in time, is affected by time of day, and provide actionable guidance to practitioners in performing mobile time-based targeting.

KEY WORDS: AND PHRASES: mobile targeting, time-of-day targeting, IT product value, mobile Internet service, consumer psychology, randomized field experiment.

## Introduction

Mobile technologies, as a key contemporary form of information technologies (ITs), are known for their capability to enable “any place, any time” communication [1, 6, 15]. This has given rise to a new phenomenon called “perpetual contact” [16, 59, 64], whereby people are always with their mobile devices and are available for communication throughout the day. As an indication of this, a recent report by Ofcom shows that people check their smartphones, on average, every 12 minutes of the waking day.

This unique property of mobile technologies is by no means trivial, given the intimate relationship between time and ITs that has been highlighted in the literature [65, 77, 85, 106]. As Saunders [85] notes in an editorial article, “Perspectives on Time,” which discusses the importance of time in IS research: “What is something that plays an important role in most of the things that you do, but about which you often don’t consciously think? It’s time.” (p. iii) Earlier research highlights how ITs change the ways tasks are performed in organizations (e.g., Lee [65]) and how individuals organize their daily activities around time (e.g., Green [45]), generally noting a change in the tasks and activities from being more constrained to being more flexible. The intimate IT-time relationship has become more salient with the emergence of mobile technologies, owing to their seamless integration into people’s everyday life [22, 45, 87].

Research in the recent decade has tried to address the relationships between ITs and time by considering specific aspects of time, such as time duration, time difference, and time of day (see Table A1 in the Online Appendix A for a review). Our review shows that most extant studies have considered duration as a time factor and how it influences and is influenced by IT behaviors (e.g., de Guinea and Webster [28], Fuller and Dennis [39], Ko and Dennis [61], Williams and Pollock [101]), with some attention also paid to the effects of time difference in the virtual team context (e.g., Cummings et al. [24] and Sarker et al. [84]). A minimal amount of attention has, however, been paid to how time context<sup>2</sup> in people’s everyday life such as time of day may influence IT behaviors, despite the call for more research that considers time as a contextual factor in people’s experience of everyday technologies, of which mobile technologies represent a pertinent type [106].

Motivated to further our understanding about the relationships between time and IT behaviors, this study aims to investigate how different values, specifically utilitarian and hedonic values (being two dominant types of values provided by ITs) [47, 94, 100, 104], would appeal to people in their decision to adopt a mobile technology depending on different times of a day. We expect such an effect of time to be particularly salient for mobile technologies, which allow people to constantly gain access to both work- and leisure-related information anytime they want. Via mobile technologies, people are able to obtain IT services and information in a more natural manner in accordance to their psychological states across different times of a day. Indeed, based on large-scale data analyses, researchers have unveiled strong temporal regularities in people’s psychological states [43]. Concomitantly, previous research indicates that individuals’ psychological states, such as mood and emotion, play an important role in influencing their tendency to adopt a technology [10, 33, 98]. Thus, we can expect people’s demand for and receptiveness to an IT (e.g., what value they desire from the IT and whether to adopt it) such as mobile technology to demonstrate a time-dependent nature. We build primarily on the ego depletion theory [9] to systematically develop the corresponding hypotheses; the basic premise is that people’s mental resources may be depleted over the course of a day, and this may have implications to their psychological state and the kind of product value they find appealing.

In this study, we investigate mobile Internet service (specifically 4G) as the focal IT, which represents an important class of mobile technologies for people to gain access to information and services on the move [105]. As with many technologies, mobile Internet services are constantly evolving, and every upgrade bring with it a sea change to a large number of related industries. For instance, 3G Internet service has been noted to introduce huge impacts on “the way consumers could use their mobile devices- opening up a new world of services…and providing the infrastructure to support the mobile app ecosystem.”<sup>3</sup> Thus, when a new mobile Internet service is introduced (such as 4G that offers faster connection speed<sup>4</sup> than 3G), it is important to promote their fast adoption so as to ensure smooth transition and development of related industries as well as the whole technology ecosystem.

We leverage mobile time-based targeting capability to promote mobile Internet service (4G) adoption by customizing the product value appeal (utilitarian vs. hedonic) in the targeting message (short message service [SMS] sent to mobile users) based on time of day (morning vs. afternoon). We collaborated with one of the largest mobile service providers in the world to conduct a randomized field experiment. In the experiment, customers of our corporate partner were randomly assigned into each hour block starting from 8:00 am to 8:00 pm. This manipulation generated an exogenous shock in each hour block that can help gauge the causal impact. We find that consumer responses to the mobile targeting message varied systematically between the morning and afternoon hours. A follow-up survey further reveals the underlying psychological mechanism supporting our hypotheses arguments built on the ego depletion theory [9].

Our research may contribute to theory and practice in several ways. In particular, the findings of this study contributes towards a more complete understanding of the temporal aspect of ITs, specifically how time as a contextual factor may affect IT-related behaviors (in our case, adoption of mobile Internet service). With previous research indicating systematic diurnal variations in people’ psychological states [43] and that psychological factors play an important role in individual IT preference and adoption [10, 33, 98], this relationship is important to account for. Our study is conducted in an ideal technology context for this issue (i.e., mobile targeting technologies that can reach users at all times of a day). Furthermore, our study underscores that it is not just whether time has an impact on IT adoption, but also whether the right product value appeal is highlighted in accordance to specific daily time segments.

In addition, being the first, to our knowledge, in the IS and marketing literature to address mobile targeting by time of day, our study advances the mobile targeting literature. Specifically, our study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the unique opportunities afforded by mobile technologies (i.e., not only “any place”) but also “any time” communication. The findings obtained extend prior perspectives of contextual relevance [42, 81] by highlighting time of day as a practically valuable contextual factor to perform mobile targeting.

In the following sections, we first review the relevant literature, which highlights the gaps our study serves to address.

## Literature Review

Time and space are held to be the two most fundamental dimensions of human activities [76]. Indeed, previous literature has noted the pertinence of time in human psychology and behavior, in that what we do in everyday life is deeply interwoven with time [56]. Belk [11] notes the importance of time for the “demonstrable and systematic effect on [consumer] behavior” (p. 159). Echoing this, it has been argued that the study of human behavior without incorporating the role of time is likely to result in an incomplete understanding [41]. In the following section, we began by reviewing the literature on time and IT-related behaviors, which is the most relevant to this study, followed by the literature on time and consumer behaviors and mobile targeting.

## Time and IT-Related Behaviors

Early research that relates time with ITs has focused on the changes brought about by ITs on how tasks and events occur in a temporal sense in organizations. As people use ITs to perform tasks, ITs also change how people organize and utilize their time [65]. For instance, people may follow the procedures coded in an IT to perform tasks sequentially, or they may perform several tasks simultaneously as enabled by the IT. The advent of the Internet is also deemed to transform the way time is perceived, used, and managed by individuals. For instance, Hongladarom [51] notes how the Internet has created a globalized “Internet time,” which changes the ways people conduct their activities that are originally organized around local cultural time. Green [45] argues that mobile technologies affected peoples’ sequencing of everyday tasks and how they organize the cycles of work and family life. For example, “one can retrieve and watch news when one wants, not when it is delivered by broadcasting companies” [66, p. 49]. These earlier studies have in general highlighted how ITs (such as the Internet and mobile technologies) have made organization and human activities more fluid and flexible [45, 65, 80, 87].

A review of the more recent IS literature (2007-present) that addresses the time factor (see Table A1 in Online Appendix A) show that attempts have been made to consider specific aspects of time in investigating IT-related behaviors.<sup>5</sup> Specifically, a bulk of the studies has focused on time duration as an aspect of time in relation to IT behaviors. Of these, some have considered time duration over which an IT has been implemented and/or used [28, 39, 61, 101, 107]. For instance, de Guinea and Webster [28] demonstrate salient changes in the patterns of how an IS was used (from automatic to adjusting) over time. Fuller and Dennis [39] show that the importance of perceived task-technology fit on team performance diminishes after the technology has been used for some time. Ko and Dennis [61] find that the longer a knowledge management system (KMS) had been used, the greater the benefit it could bring to the user. These studies generally show that IT use itself and their use consequences (e.g., performance, trust) are influenced by how long they have been used.

There have also been studies that consider time duration as an important factor in information system design (e.g., Parsons and Ralph [78] that take viewing time as an input for designing product recommendation systems), and in explaining information system continuance (e.g., Ruth [83] that models waiting time for response as an antecedent of continuance in Q&A websites) and addiction (e.g., Turel and Serenko [93] that highlight an association between time spent in a social networking site and user addiction to the site).

A few other studies take time duration as a dependent variable that is influenced by IT artifacts [50, 67, 68, 99]. For instance, Lee et al. [68] show that the use of filler interface can reduce individuals’ perceived waiting time on a website. Venkatesh et al. [99] find that the use of e-healthcare system can enhance patients perception that their healthcare providers have spent sufficient time with them.

Apart from considering time in terms of its duration, studies in the context of virtual teams have noted the need to consider time difference across time zones, given that their members are typically geographically dispersed, and its effects on outcomes such as coordination delay [24] and perceived work-life conflict [84]. Due probably to the relevance of time difference in the virtual team context, there have also been conceptual discussions suggesting how research should consider the time factor in investigating virtual teams, such as the experience of temporal rhythms in virtual team interactions [85] and how virtual team members perceive and act with regard to time [86].

However, as can be seen from the literature reviewed, there has been scant attention on time as a contextual factor that influences IT-related behaviors, despite the call for greater attention to how everyday ITs (such as mobile technologies) experience is embedded in time [106]. We are only able to identify one exception, i.e., Bhatnagar et al. [14], which highlights how individuals’ online search durations are influenced by the visit day of week and time of day. Specifically, they find that users engage in longer search online during the day time compared to night time, and during weekdays compared to weekends. Our study that investigates how time of day influences consumer receptiveness to mobile targeting message to adopt mobile Internet service may thus extend and enrich this line of research on time and IT-related behaviors, and offer a more complete understanding of their relationships.

## Time and Consumer Behaviors

The use of the time factor to influence consumer behaviors can be traced to the dayparting practice in scheduling television advertising [46, 52, 71, 92].<sup>6</sup> Dayparting is the practice of dividing the day into several parts such as morning and afternoon segments, and customizing the display of advertisements during specific time segments [88].

However, such a practice is increasingly challenged by the changing habits of consumers in obtaining information and entertainment content due to technology advancements. As Morgan [71] notes, “Today, people consume almost all of their media—even television—where, when and how they want.” This has led to research to investigate the practice of dayparting in the online context [13, 97]. It is observed that people’s patterns of using the Internet vary across different times of the day. For instance, in the morning people tend to use the Internet primarily to read email and news, while in the afternoon work-related use slows down and entertainment-related use (e.g., checking movie times, online games) becomes more prominent [13]. Although useful for temporally customizing the type of information and products to be promoted to the users, a critical issue with such use of the desktop Internet is that it is hard to ensure the message reaches the users at the intended time of day. For instance, users may not have access to desktop computer when the message is sent. In this regard, mobile technologies with their portability and ubiquitous usage may serve as an ideal means to conduct time-based targeting.

## Mobile Targeting

Mobile technologies’ ability to alleviate both spatial and temporal constraints have attracted a sizeable body of research from both the IS and marketing communities in recent years. However, most of the extant research has focused on the spatial aspect of mobile targeting. For instance, earlier research attempts to find out if consumers are responsive to a promotional event that is located close to them (i.e., location-based targeting [5, 54, 89]). Subsequent research has tried to expand the understanding about location-based mobile targeting by incorporating other factors or by considering the location factor in different contexts (e.g., Andrews et al. [2], Fong et al. [38], and Provost et al. [81]). For instance, combining location with social factor, Provost et al. [81] suggested targeting performance based on the frequency of two users visiting the same geographical location. This is because these users are likely to belong to the same social network and, hence, are likely to influence each other’s purchase behavior. While offering valuable insights, these studies do not consider the temporal aspect of mobile targeting.

Among the limited studies that consider the temporal aspect of mobile targeting, Heilman et al. [48] indicate that mobile coupons that target customers in real time (at the point of purchase) could increase purchase amounts. Bang et al. [6] note that mobile channel is particularly suitable for time-sensitive purchase. Luo et al. [70] show that when targeting consumers at a near distance (<0.2 m), giving a short lead time (2 hr) is the most effective in stimulating purchase-making compared to longer lead times (1 day and 2 days). A common assumption in these studies is that by sending mobile coupons that require consumers to make timely purchase decision, a sense of urgency may be created that increases their impulsive purchase likelihood [91].

The extant mobile targeting research has not, to the best of our knowledge, considered another key aspect of time, that is, time of day. This is surprising because mobile technologies present an ideal opportunity to reach consumers at virtually all times of a day. People habitually carry their mobile devices on or near their body and check them out throughout the day [36]. Indeed, the “always with user” nature of mobile technologies led Green [45] to coin the term mobile “rhythms of everyday life,” whereby mobile use is integrated into people’s rhythms of work and leisure time.

Nonetheless, the practitioner literature recently notes that in the mobile advertising industry, attempts have been made to explore the possible effect of time on people’s demand and preference, apart from the popular location-based targeting. Specifically, companies try to leverage the “any time” communication capability of mobile technologies to send product promotional message to consumers across different hours of a day. Some companies indiscriminately send mobile promotional messages evenly across hours in an attempt to account for consumer heterogeneity. Some others selectively do so during specific hour blocks in a traditional advertising media fashion. Industry reports debate what time of a day is best suited for sending mobile promotional messages, with some claiming midday is the best [21], while others cite the evening [29, 72], or multiple peaks between 9:00 am and 3:00 pm [95]. Unlike location-based targeting, there has been a lack of consensus and scientific knowledge to guide companies on how to effectively leverage the time-based mobile targeting capability.

## Theoretical Foundation

In this study, we hypothesize how different product value appeal (utilitarian, hedonic) should be highlighted in the mobile targeting message promoting a mobile Internet service (4G) according to different segments of time of a day (morning, afternoon). We build primarily on the ego depletion theory to develop our hypotheses, as discussed in the following section.

## Ego Depletion Theory

Ego depletion theory [9] essentially posits that human’s mental resources, as with other kind of resources, are limited and prone to be depleted. The depletion of such resources may, in turn, have an implication on ones’ self-control, known as ego depletion.

Human’s limited mental resources can be depleted in many ways. Putting it in a temporal context of everyday life, people typically make many decisions and choices throughout the course of a day, and each of these decisions requires cognitive processing and use of executive functioning. Thus, over the day, the cumulative effect of repeated choice- and decision-making would gradually exhaust one’s mental resources [9]. Consequently, people’s self-control, or the exertion of control to override or inhibit urges and desires, becomes lower as doing so requires mental resources [74]. Baumeister [8] recently provided a review of research on ego depletion, concluding that depletion impairs inhibition both through weakening restraint and intensifying urges. In such a depleted state, people’s further self-control is impaired and is only restored after they are able to find rest.

In line with this, it has been found that people are more effective at self-control when well-rested (which makes them more energetic) than when tired [8, 9]. Research has provided evidences that people are generally more energetic in the morning after a night sleep, compared to in the afternoon [94]. Following from this, people are likely to be better at self-control in the morning than in the afternoon, given that their energy and thus resources to do so are higher in the morning after a night rest. This may have an implication on the IT product value that people find appealing in the morning vs. afternoon hours, which may be systematically seen through the utilitarian-hedonic value framework.

## Utilitarian-Hedonic Value Framework and Peoples’ Psychological State

Utilitarian and hedonic values represent two fundamental values underlying people’s consumptions, which guide their daily decision-making [7, 49, 63]. Utilitarian values are associated with task fulfillment, productivity, and means-ends needs; whereas, hedonic values are related to fun, enjoyment, and gratification needs [7]. In the IS context, the two values are also noted to form the basis on which users perceive a technology [47, 94, 100, 104].

Research has suggested that people’s psychological states may determine how they are attracted to utilitarian or hedonic product values. For instance, it has been shown that when people are in a negative (positive) mood, they are more (less) likely to seek hedonic product values [40]. Concomitantly, strong regularities are found in human psychological states across different times of a day. In general, people demonstrate higher physical and mental energy levels and, also, more positive affect/emotion (e.g., enthusiasm) in the morning than in the afternoon [90, 102]. More recently, based on large-scale data analyses, Golder and Macy [43] provide robust evidence to the consistent patterns in which people’s mood varies by time of day. People generally awaken in a good mood that deteriorates as the day progresses, and feel more relaxed with worse mood in the afternoon [43].

The temporal regularities in human psychological states may be linked to the notion of circadian rhythms [35]. Circadian rhythms are believed to be generated within the human body, and help coordinate not just internal bodily functions (e.g., sleeping and eating times) but also how individuals interact with the external world, such as perceiving stimuli and making judgments and preferences [20, 53]. In line with this notion, research has investigated the effects of time of day on consumers’ preference for new experiential activity [60], tendency to engage in mission-focused shopping [18], service demand [25], and variety-seeking behavior [82]. For instance, previous research shows that people are more likely to engage in mission-focused shopping in the morning (which is more utilitarian in nature) [18], while in the afternoon people tend more to seek novel experiential activity (more hedonic in nature) [60].

Taking the discussions above together, people’s psychological states may change over the course of a day (i.e., circadian rhythms of psychological states), and this may influence the product value (utilitarian vs. hedonic) that appeals to them during the morning vs. afternoon hours. Specifically, people may be more attracted to utilitarian value appeal presented in in the morning (with more energy and mental resources, and being more enthusiastic and mission-focused) in deciding to adopt a technology product. In contrast, people may be likely to find hedonic value more appealing in the afternoon, given the depleted energy and resources that lead them to become less restrained with lower self-control (i.e., being more relaxed). Based on these premises, we develop our research hypotheses in the next section.

## Hypotheses Development

Our central proposition is that all else being equal, mobile targeting in the morning (afternoon) hours will be more effective when utilitarian (hedonic) product value appeal is highlighted in the mobile message promoting a new mobile Internet service.

In general, products are multidimensional in nature and can have both utilitarian and hedonic attributes [23]. Nonetheless, previous research shows that a product can be framed to have a utilitarian or a hedonic focus, which has a significant impact on consumers’ attitude and purchase responses [62, 69; 100]. For instance, Wakefield and Whitten [100] showed that people’s perceptions and beliefs regarding a mobile technology (personal digital assistant) are affected by whether the technology is emphasized to serve primarily a utilitarian or hedonic purpose.

The two values are also salient in the context of our focal mobile technology product, i.e., mobile Internet service. Being a contemporary form of experiential technology [106], mobile Internet service not only can help in work (utilitarian value) but also provide leisure and entertainment (hedonic value). By connecting people to the Internet “any place, any time” [105], mobile Internet service allows people to gain better access to both work- and entertainment-related information and communication. Thus, mobile Internet service can offer both utilitarian and hedonic values to the users, and can be framed to have a focus on one of the values (i.e., a utilitarian or a hedonic value appeal framing).

In general, people have greater energy in the morning with a lifted mood after a night sleep [43] . The literature holds that a high energy state with a lifted mood is marked by a more task productivity orientation [73] and higher desire for success [31]. This may be explained from the perspective of the ego depletion theory [9], as the high energy level would enable people to engage in better self-control of what they desire to achieve. Given the achievement and productivity orientation early in the day [8], utilitarian product value appeal (i.e., highlighting the functional utility of the mobile Internet service such as helping one’s work) in the mobile targeting message should match better with people’s needs in the morning. In other words, for users high in energy with a greater focus on task and productivity early in the day, they will find utilitarian (rather than hedonic) value of the product (i.e., the capability of the mobile Internet service to enhance access to utilitarian information and services) more appealing to them in the morning. This is thus likely to promote consumer likelihood to adopt the mobile Internet service, as hypothesized:

Hypothesis 1: All else being equal, mobile targeting in the morning hours will be more effective when utilitarian (vs. hedonic) value appeal is used in the mobile targeting message to induce consumer purchase adoption of mobile Internet service.

Hypothesis 2: The psychology mechanism underlying Hypothesis 1 is that people in the morning are more energetic and pay more attention to the functional product benefits in utilitarian mobile communications.

Another hypothesis is that mobile targeting in the afternoon hours will be more effective with the use of hedonic (rather than utilitarian) product value appeal in the mobile targeting message promoting the mobile Internet service. According to the ego depletion theory [9], over the course of a typical day, people’s energy level and cognitive resources tend to be increasingly depleted after making various decisions and choices. The lower self-control may lead to a more relaxed mood in the afternoon, which makes people more likely to yearn for hedonic consumption. That is, with the depletion of mental energy and resources that are needed for self-control, people in a relaxing psychological state become more likely to engage in various self-gratifying or selfindulgent acts [8, 12]. This may then lead people to find hedonic product values, related to fun and enjoyment, to be more appealing to them [17, 32].

In the context of mobile Internet service, consumers may be more attracted by the service’s capability to enhance their access to hedonic information and services in the afternoon. This should thus promote consumer purchase likelihood in the afternoon, as hypothesized:

Hypothesis 3: All else being equal, mobile targeting in the afternoon hours will be more effective when hedonic (vs. utilitarian) product value appeal is used in the mobile targeting message to induce consumer purchase adoption of mobile Internet service.

Hyothesis 4: The psychology mechanism underlying Hypothesis 2 is that people in the afternoon are more relaxed and pay more attention to the fun and enjoyment product benefits in hedonic mobile communications.

## Field Experiment and Data

To test the hypotheses Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 3, we conducted a randomized field experiment sponsored by one of the largest mobile service providers in the world. The research design with a randomized field experiment was employed because it can control for consumers’ unobservable heterogeneity and thus avoid the endogeneity and causality biases that might confound the estimation results of mobile time-based targeting [3, 44]. Since any other systematic variations are randomized away across consumers, the differences in purchase likelihoods can then be attributed to the impact of the mobile time-based targeting. To generalize field experiment results and further test the underlying mechanism as stated in Hypothesis 2 and Hypothesis 4, we collaborated with a professional research company to survey mobile users about their relevant psychological perceptions.

## Experimental Design

Our targeted population consisted of mobile users from a large city in Asia who had bought a 4G-ready mobile phone but had not subscribed to the 4G service package. The subjects are regular customers of the company. The selected customers did not receive previous promotional offers to subscribe to the service. We also ensured that each mobile user would receive only a single mobile message during the study to ensure the between-subjects design.

Mobile time-based targeting was varied on an hour-by-hour basis in our field experiment design. To more precisely identify the effectiveness of mobile targeting by time of day, our experiment design involved 12 hours (from 8:00 am to 8:00 pm) x 2 product value appeal framing (utilitarian vs. hedonic) between-subjects design. We operationalized hour-by-hour treatments by ensuring each participant received a message at a specific selected hour. The hour treatment groups were established to receive a message within the hour, each hour between 8:00 am and 8:00 pm (i.e., the first hour block was 8:00 am-9:00 am, the last hour block was 7:00 pm-8:00 pm, altogether 12 hours). The mobile message used in the field experiment included a promotional offer that expired 60 minutes after the message was sent (e.g., for the participants assigned to the 7:00 pm-8:00 pm hour block, some might receive the message at 8:00 pm; for these participants, they could respond before 9:00 pm). The starting time (8:00 am) and ending time (8:00 pm) of the field experiment were determined to capture the main portion of participants’ active day while avoiding boundary conditions from participants who are not yet awake or already asleep. The 60-minute expiration of the mobile deal required purchase of the offered product (4G mobile Internet service) during the designated hour.

Our corporate partner could identify each consumer who had received a promotional message as part of the field experiment during each hour to guarantee no cross-hour contamination between the hour treatment conditions. All subjects are similar to the general users of the company and were randomly assigned into each hour block. We randomly selected consumers from among the company’s population of mobile users, on the condition that they had not subscribed to the 4G mobile Internet service and received no offer on this service before (this was to ensure a basic condition that the service is relevant to them). We then randomly assigned them to the experimental hour-by-hour groups. In other words, customers were similar (from the same population) and randomly assigned into each hour. As such, this randomization is free from any systematic bias and allows for identifying the causal impact of mobile targeting on consumers’ purchase responses to the mobile promotion.

Randomization was achieved by using SAS software’s random number generator and running the RANUNI function, which returns a random value from a uniform distribution [30]. Because our corporate partner is the mobile service provider, it can ensure the quality of the immediate message delivery so that the subjects can receive and read the mobile messages from the service provider immediately with over 99.2 percent of successful delivery (i.e., small sample selection bias). After all, users rely on the mobile service provider to make phone calls and obtain customer service supports, and such a high successful rate is often expected in the mobile telecommunication industry, especially for making urgent phone calls.

As previously mentioned, the product promoted in the mobile message was the 4G mobile Internet service with an extended data plan, which was just rolled out in the target market when this study was conducted. A fast adoption of this new mobile Internet service is not only important to help the company regain the huge infrastructural investments in rolling out the service, but also promote a smooth transition to a new ecosystem built on the enhanced mobile Internet that includes such industries as multimedia, advertising, gaming, and e-commerce.

The mobile message included a promotional discount to incentivize immediate purchase action. Prior research indicates that incentives in SMS promotions are expected and can lead to more positive consumer attitudes and purchase intentions [34, 70]. For those who purchased, the cost of the service would be charged directly to their regular phone bills. Two variations of promotional content of the same product were included in the basic message, emphasizing the utilitarian or hedonic value of the 4G mobile Internet service. All participants received a basic message that reads: “Get 20 times the surfing speed with the newly launched 4G! [utilitarian or hedonic content] Reply “Y” to subscribe to the 600M/month package at ¥50 and get an additional one-time 600M/month for free making this an incredible bargain at 1.2G per month of the first month! This promotion is only valid in the next 60 minutes.” The utilitarian product value appeal version highlights the functional values of the service: “this 4G service allows you to download big email attachments and enjoy handling official business anywhere you go.” In contrast, the hedonic product value appeal version emphasizes the fun and leisure values: “this 4G service allows you to download undamaged music video and enjoy playing games anywhere you go.” Results from a pre-test with 227 users of the mobile service provider supported the manipulation checks for the utilitarian/hedonic framing of the SMS message content [49]. Specifically, we used well-established scales for measuring utilitarian dimensions (useful for task fulfillment, practical for productivity enhancement, and functional for means-ends utilitarian needs) and hedonic dimensions (pleasant and fun, enjoyment, and gratifying for hedonic needs) in the marketing literature [7, 49, 63]. We adopted a between-subjects design whereby 108 of the 227 users provided their responses to the utilitarian-framed message, while the rest (119 users) provided their responses to the hedonic-framed message with respect to how they perceive the message in terms of the two consumption values. The results supported that the 4G service framed with the utilitarian product value appeal was indeed perceived by the 108 users as significantly higher (p < .01) on the utilitarian dimensions than on the hedonic dimensions. In contrast, the same 4G service framed with the hedonic product value appeal was indeed perceived by 119 mobile users as significantly higher (p < .01) on the hedonic dimensions than on the utilitarian counterpart. Thus, these findings supported the manipulation checks for the utilitarian/hedonic value appeals used in our field experiment.

The messages were sent to mobile users in the form of Short Message Service (SMS) messages in all field experiment conditions. We chose SMS as the mobile technology to attain targeting by time of day over other mobile marketing media, including multimedia message, app, and quick code, for several reasons. SMS is the most established and adopted mobile marketing medium today [26, 57, 58]. Its presence in virtually every mobile phone also means that its reach and chance of being read by the receivers are likely to be higher compared to other mobile media such as in-app and mobile web advertising [79]. Indeed, anecdotal evidences suggest that 98 percent of SMS messages are read (compared to, e.g., only 29 percent for Twitter), and 90 percent of them are open within three minutes by the consumers [75]. This makes it ideal for the mobile time-based targeting in our study. According to a report by 3C.com,<sup>7</sup> 48 percent of consumers prefer to receive mobile communication from brands via SMS (compared to only 20 percent for app). In addition, its simple interface implies that it is less likely to be subject to potential interferences compared to app-based and in-game advertising (e.g., the presence of social or game elements in the latter may create some forms of interferences). Text-based, though a disadvantage of SMS, also provides an opportunity to customize its textual content by different times of a day for better targeting. Online Appendix B provides a comparison of the advantages and disadvantages of SMS compared to other mobile media.

A total of 4,800 mobile users were randomly selected from the mobile service company and assigned into the 24 groups. Each hour block has 200 mobile users assigned. We also conducted a power analysis based on the average effect size with Cohen’s d of about 0.45, which would require a minimum sample of 74 subjects per condition using two-tailed t-tests with a power of 0.80. For a more conservative Cohen’s d of 0.30, a minimum of 127 subjects per condition are required. Thus, our experiment conditions each with 200 subjects have enough statistical power to test the hypotheses.

$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {i} ^ {H O U R} = a ^ {m} + \sum \beta_ {1, 2, \dots 1 1} ^ {m} \times H O U R _ {i} + \sum \zeta_ {1, 2, \dots 1 1} ^ {m} \times H O U R _ {i} \times U T I L I T _ {i} + \zeta^ {m} \\ \times U T I L I T _ {i} + \gamma^ {m} \times X _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i 2} ^ {m}, \end{array}

## Falsification Checks

We conducted several falsification checks. First, because field experiment can help randomize away consumer heterogeneity, the mobile hour treatment effects are robust to consumers’ mobile usage behavior (LnARPU, LnMOU, LnASMS, and LnGPRS). Furthermore, we check whether our results are robust to latent consumer segments. Again, because our field experiment was randomized, consumer heterogeneity due to latent segments based on mobile usage behavior should be randomized away. Thus, the mobile hour treatment effects should not be dependent or moderated by consumer latent segments. We conducted K-means cluster analyses based on the mobile usage behavior and found that in the field experiment, the optimal AIC and BIC statistics suggest two latent clusters (see Online Appendix D for the final means and ANOVA tests of the significantly different mobile usage behaviors across the two latent clusters). Importantly, after we entered the interaction terms with an HOUR x Cluster dummy, we found that that none of these interactions are statistically significant, while the mobile hour effects remain significant. Thus, our field experiment and the resultant conclusions are robust to these falsification tests.

## Follow-up Survey to Understand the Underlying Psychological Mechanism

To replicate the results in the field experiment and to test Hypothesis 2 and Hypothesis 4 in revealing the underlying psychological mechanism, we engaged a professional research company to conduct a scenario-based survey following recent IS research (e.g., Ayyagari et al. [4] and Carter and Grover [19]). The market research company maintains a large panel of general consumers from diverse backgrounds. They also employ quality assurance mechanisms, including verification of consumer demographic information, checking the quality of survey data, and identification of fraudulent acts. These mechanisms ensure access to a sample that spans across the general consumer population (vis-à-vis student samples).

## Data Collection

The professional research firm invited their panel of respondents to participate in the survey online during either one of two time periods: 1) morning, from 9:00 am to 12:00 noon; 2) afternoon, from 2:00 pm to 5:00 pm, on a random basis. These timings for the morning and afternoon sessions were selected as they garnered the relatively better responses as indicated in the field experiment, thus allowing differences in consumer responses and the underlying rationale to be better contrasted. They also largely coincide with previous research that investigates temporal variations in people’s physiological conditions [102]. The time logs indicate that the respondents completed the survey questionnaire within 30 minutes after being invited, thus ensuring they provided their responses within the right time frames (i.e., provided their responses in the morning if they were invited in the morning, and provided their responses in the afternoon otherwise).

For each respondent, a screening question was first presented to ensure they have not subscribed to the target mobile Internet package being offered in the message. For those who qualified, the survey system randomly presented to them one of two scenarios that corresponded to the message sent in the field experiment. The respondents were then requested to answer the items that follow based on the scenario. A total of 412 completed responses were obtained, of which 141 were from the morning session, while the remaining 271 were from the afternoon session. The relatively lesser responses in the morning session was likely due to the more hectic work schedule typical in the morning that discouraged respondents from spending time to complete the survey. The number of respondents for the two scenarios (utilitarian and hedonic contents) is roughly equivalent (205 and 207, respectively) across the morning and afternoon sessions, and also roughly equivalent within the two sessions (70 and 71, respectively, in the morning; 135 and 136, respectively, in the afternoon).

In accordance to our hypothesis arguments, our field survey measured people’s state of being energetic (argued to be higher in the morning) and relaxed (argued to be more so in the afternoon) in the follow-up survey. We also measured and controlled for the following major mood types that may be important: being happy, gloomy, bored, stressful, and angry. In addition, we measured the extent to which respondents have a mindset that favors or avoids engaging in cognitive deliberation at the moment; being simply lazy might lead people to favor hedonicframed messages with fun appeal (rather than how the product can help their job). We also controlled for individuals’ demographics (gender, age, income, education, number of years being a customer of the mobile service provider that reflects their loyalty with the firm) and a number of factors that may influence their receptiveness toward the mobile targeting message, including those related to individual traits, that is, utilitarian and hedonic shopping orientations, price consciousness, and impulsive purchase tendency; releated to the the advertising message, that is, perceived intrusiveness; related to the firm, that is, firm reputation. Some people may see buying products as mainly a utilitarian process just to obtain what they need, while others see it as a hedonic process that can provide fun and enjoyment. Such utilitarian and hedonic shopping orientations may determine how individuals respond to a product message regardless of the product value appeal being presented. Given that our mobile message contains a promotional offer, it may have a stronger appeal to individuals with a higher price consciousness. Also, those who tend to be more impulsive in purchase making could respond better when given the promotional offer. Related to the advertising message itself, individuals may find the mobile-communicated message to be intrusive given mobile technologies’ intimate nature [96], which may, in turn, affect their response. Lastly, consumer response may also be influenced by their perception of the firm that sent the message, such as the firm reputation.

Where possible, we employed and adapted existing measures from the previous literature. As some measures were self-developed to fit this study’s context, we conducted sorting exercises with four judges to assess the conceptual validity of the construct measurements. These resulted in minor modifications of the wording used in the measurement items (see Online Appendix E).

## Hypotheses Testing Results

We conducted ordinary least square (OLS) regression to analyze the data, given that many of the variables involved are single-item measurements (i.e., the different mood types, including being energetic and relaxed that are the focal variables). We first examine the descriptive statistics of the data (see Table 3). Within respectively the morning and afternoon sessions, people’s purchase intention was relatively higher when given the message with a utilitarian product value appeal in the morning (weakly significant at $\mathrm { ~ p ~ } = \ 0 . 0 7 5 )$ ; while it was the message with a hedonic product value appeal that led to a higher purchase intention in the afternoon $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ . These result patterns are consistent with our Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 3 and provide further supports to our field experiment results that people were more receptive towards utilitarian product value appeal in the morning, and otherwise (hedonic product value appeal) in the afternoon. It is also worth noting that, consistent with our expectations, people were relatively more energetic in the morning than in the afternoon $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ , and relatively more relaxed vice versa (weakly significant at $\mathfrak { p } = 0 . 0 6 8 )$ ).

We next assessed the measurement model followed by the structural model. As shown in Online Appendix E, the analysis of the measurement models indicates satisfactory reliability and convergent validity levels of the constructs, that is, Cronbach’s Alpha $\mathrm { ( C A ) } > 0 . 7 0$ , Composite Reliability $\mathrm { ( C R ) } > 0 . 7 0$ , and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) > 0.50. Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square-root of AVE of each construct against its correlations with other constructs. The results in Table E2 in Online Appendix E show that the diagonal elements (square-root of AVE) exceeded other entries in the same row or column, thus satisfying the discriminant validity requirement.

We proceeded to analyze the relationships among the variables, as the model demonstrated satisfactory reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity levels. Table 4 presents the analysis results. It can be seen that for the respondents presented with the utilitarian product value appeal in the morning, being energetic (which is relatively higher in the morning than in the afternoon) did play an important role in influencing their intention to purchase the 4G mobile Internet service offered in the message. In contrast, none of the other considered mental states (including being energetic) influenced their purchase intention when they were presented with the hedonic message in the morning. However, for messages with hedonic product value appeal presented in the afternoon (which was found to yield a better response in the field experiment), the survey results show that being relaxed (which is relatively higher in the afternoon than in the morning) has a positive effect on purchase intention. In other words, people who felt more relaxed were more inclined to purchase the mobile Internet service being framed with a hedonic tone. In contrast, none of the other mental states had an effect on people’s purchase adoption intention in the case of utilitarian message presented in the afternoon. These effects hold after controlling for individual mindset in terms of whether they approach/avoid engaging in cognitive deliberation. Taken together, these findings provide a viable account for our field experiment results. That is, the follow-up survey suggests that people would respond better to utilitarian (vs. hedonic) product value appeal in the morning, because during these hours, people are more energetic and pay attention to the functional product benefits in utilitarian mobile communications, supporting Hypothesis 2. Also, mobile targeting in the afternoon hours will be more effective when hedonic (vs. utilitarian) product value appeal was used because during these hours, people are more relaxed and pay attention to the fun and enjoyment product benefits in hedonic mobile communications, thus supporting Hypothesis 4.

## Discussion

With mobile technologies enabling “perpetual contact” [16, 59, 64], firms are presented with unprecedented opportunity to communicate messages to consumers at any time of the day. However, due also to their seamless integration into people’s everyday life, such message communications are particularly prone to be perceived as highly intrusive and are likely to cause annoyance when they are deemed irrelevant [96]. Thus, a critical issue to unleash mobile capabilities is to communicate mobile messages in ways that are consistent with people’s needs and preferences. To this end, time being a pervasive component of everyday life [85] plays an important role, especially given the regularities uncovered in how people’s psychological states vary by different times of a day [43, 90, 102]. Focusing on mobile Internet service as an important class of IT, this study posits that highlighting the utilitarian or hedonic value of the IT service according to different times of day can yield better user response in terms of their purchase adoption.

With both field experiment and field survey, this research provides consistent evidence for the distinct temporal patterns in user response to the mobile Internet service offer. Specifically, we find that mobile targeting with utilitarian product value appeal should be employed in the morning hours, whereas mobile targeting with hedonic product value appeal of the same product should be employed in the afternoon hours. Our use of the same product with different mobile communication content permits meaningful direct comparison of response rates. An additional analysis shows that the optimal strategy should leverage utilitarian value appeal from 8:00 am to 12:00 pm and hedonic framing for the remaining hours of the day. This strategy would produce an average purchase rate of 15 percent, which almost doubles the current industry practice of using utilitarian-only content in all hours. Specifically, current industry practices largely focus on the utilitarian product value in mobile promotions [21, 29, 72, 95], which receives 8.7 percent purchase rate. The findings offer several important implications for research and practice as discussed in the following section.

## Theoretical Implications

This research contributes to theory primarily in two ways. First, this study advances the notion that time of day is an important contextual element that influences the adoption of some types of ITs, such as mobile Internet service that can provide both utilitarian and hedonic values. A review of the IS literature (Table A1 in Online Appendix A) shows that some efforts have been taken to understand the role of time in IS research in the recent decade. However, most of the extant studies have considered time in terms of its duration, such as the time duration over which an IT has been used or implemented (e.g., Fuller and Dennis [39], Ko and Dennis [61], Williams and Pollack [101], Zahedi and Song [107]). This is consistent with the notion of time scale in the organization research [108], such as the time interval over which researchers conduct their observations on a phenomenon (e.g., IT use or implementation). Some attention has also been put on time difference, especially in research on virtual teams of which their members are typically geographically dispersed in different time zones (e.g., Cummings et al. [24] and Sarker et al. [84]). A minimal amount of attention has been put on time as a contextual factor in people’s everyday interaction with a technology. As Yoo [106] notes, it is important to consider how such a time dimension shapes the prevalent digitally mediated “embodied experiences” in our everyday activities through ITs today. Our study that unveils users’ different tendency to adopt a technology in different times of a day may serve to enrich our understanding of the relationships between ITs and time along this under-explored direction. Our findings suggest that investigations on user preferences for and adoption of everyday technologies should take time of day as an important factor in their research endeavor.

Second, this study also contributes to the mobile targeting research that has attracted considerable attention from both IS and marketing researchers. Most of the previous research on this issue, in both IS (e.g., Daurer et al. [27], Fang et al. [37], Provost et al. [81]) and marketing (e.g., Andrews et al. [2] and Danaher et al. [26]) areas, has explored the impact of mobile targeting based on user’s geographical location. The time factor, being another key dimension of mobile technologies’ “anyplace, anytime” capabilities, has been largely neglected.

Our study extends the scant literature on the temporal aspect of mobile targeting on two fronts. First, the extant limited research mostly examines temporal mobile targeting strategy in the forms of limited time offer to the consumers (e.g., Heilman et al. [48] and Luo et al. [70]). We address another important aspect of time, that is, time of day, which, if left unexplored, presents a missed opportunity given mobile’s “perpetual contact” with the users in their daily life. Second, we show that to leverage a time aspect of mobile targeting, it is important to understand the appropriate product value appeal to communicate based on different times of the day (such as morning and afternoon hours). Our research offers triangulated evidence and insights into a mobile time-based targeting opportunity.

## Practical Implications

To practitioners, our research highlights mobile time-based targeting as a viable form of mobile targeting. Such form of targeting, which is based on time of day, may be perceived by consumers as less intrusive compared to location-based targeting. For example, in order to use location-based targeting effectively, providers need to track consumer’s movements. Many consumers dislike disclosing this sensitive information to the mobile service provider and often fear that this private location data is stored without their consent [96]. For mobile time-based targeting, consumers do not need to reveal such private data, since such targeting is sent independent of the consumer’s current location (and other personal information). Furthermore, firms can employ this mobile targeting strategy to improve consumer responses.

In addition, we highlight to practitioners that not only targeting based on time of day is viable, but also what kind of product consumption values (utilitarian or hedonic) should be emphasized in the mobile communications. Businesses conducting a mobile targeting strategy should design their message in accordance to the time-varying patterns of consumer responses. For products with a mixture of both attributes, firms can choose to strategically highlight their utilitarian product values in the morning and hedonic product values in the afternoon. As shown in the previous literature, this is viable as IT products such as mobile technologies may be perceived to be utilitarian or hedonic, depending on whether their utilitarian or hedonic aspects are purposively highlighted to the users [100]. Thus, firms can draw on the products’ utilitarian or hedonic values to attain more effective mobile time-based targeting, that is, with higher return on investment (ROI) on targeting over different times of a day.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

As with other studies, there are several limitations in this research that need to be recognized. While our results are based on a carefully-designed field experiment and replicated by a field survey, the experiment took place in a single city and with a single product and service provider platform. Thus, the generalization of our findings to other cultures and contexts should be conducted with caution.

Also, our study cannot completely rule out the possibility that there could be seasonal or day-specific (e.g., weekdays vs. weekends) variations in the time-dependent mobile targeting effectiveness we uncovered. However, based on the study of Golder and Macy [43], which shows that the regularities in people’s psychological states are consistent across seasons and days of week, we believe our findings may, to some extent, be robust to such effects as well. Nonetheless, future research may replicate our study in different seasons and days, to validate the generalizability of our findings and assess if seasonal variations are salient.

In addition, our research only assesses the use of one form of mobile technology medium, that is, SMS. Considerations are needed in generalizing and applying our findings to other mobile marketing media. For instance, future research may tap into the effectiveness of targeting through different types of apps based on their nature, as some apps are utilitarian (e.g., financial tools) while others are more hedonic (e.g., entertainment apps). Future research may test how various communications channels with SMS, app, and social media can be used for more effective mobile time-based targeting.

Another limitation of our study is that the sample sizes of the follow-up survey are relatively small, especially for the morning groups given the potential constraints faced by the respondents in the morning (i.e., more hectic work schedule in the morning). Future research may, nonetheless, try to provide more attractive incentives to respondents to garner larger sample sizes to further validate our findings. Lastly, in our field experiment, we were unable to ensure that the mobile message was indeed read by all the participants. For instance, news prompts on the home-screen might be ignored and then disappear after the user unlocked the screen. Nonetheless, our use of the randomized field experiment approach may alleviate this concern by randomizing away the occurrence of such instances across the different experimental conditions; thus, the issue may not significantly affect our findings. Future research may try to introduce interventions that can strike a better balance between interfering with users’ actual response and ensuring the message is read by the participants.

## Conclusion

Mobile technologies offer a new opportunity to better understand the timeembedded users’ IT behavior, knowledge of which can be used for more targeted promotion of IT products. In this study, we primarily build on the ego depletion theory to propose a set of hypotheses on how users may be more attracted to the utilitarian versus hedonic value appeal of an IT product, that is, mobile Internet service, in the morning vs. afternoon time segments. Through a field experiment, we show that indeed paying attention to highlighting the different value appeal of the IT product in the two time segments of a day can result in higher adoption. We also show the psychological mechanism that underlies the field experiment findings, which is consistent with our theoretical arguments based on the ego depletion theory. The findings advance the notion that people can be attracted to different value appeals to adopt an IT product in different times of a day. Our research also advances the mobile targeting research by extending the contextual factors for targeting beyond location and time duration to a different factor, that is, time of day. Firms can readily employ this contextual factor to yield better targeting outcomes without causing serious users’ privacy concern such as in the case of location-based targeting. We hope our work can stimulate future research on the relationships between time and IT and on mobile targeting to further harness the omnipresent potentials of mobile technologies.
