Keywords: Mobile commerce Multichannel retailing Synergy effect Cannibalization effect

## A B S T R A C T

With the increasing popularity of mobile applications, increasingly more e-commerce websites are providing mobile shopping services that enable their consumers to access their products and services through an additional mobile channel. The question arises here as to whether the new channel brings new sales or merely shifts consumers from the web to the mobile channel. Using the 2½-vear transaction data obtained from an e-commerce company that expanded its web service onto a mobile platform, we investigated the impact of the newly introduced mobile channel on the sales of the incumbent web channel, and whether it could stimulate new consumption from consumers. Our empirical results indicate that after the adoption of the mobile channel, the purchases on the web channel were slightly cannibalized; however, the consumers’ purchases increased overall, suggesting that the positive synergy effect of the new channel overrode the negative cannibalization effect. Our investigation contributes to multichannel e-commerce literature by empirically testing the cross-channel effects of a new mobile channel and also providing insights for e-retailers interested in introducing a new mobile channel.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid adoption of mobile technology, consumers are increasingly relying on mobile devices for communication and shopping activities. As a result, mobile commerce, an extension of e-commerce on mobile platforms, has grown into an equally, if not more, important channel compared to the traditional web channel for online shopping. Mobile commerce (m-commerce) can be formally defined as all activities related to a potential commercial transaction conducted through communications networks that interface with wireless or mobile devices [40]. According to an eMarketer forecast, mobile commerce retail sales worldwide are expected to grow from \$38.84 billion to \$108.56 billion between 2013 and 2017, with an average growth rate of 29.3% [12]. In order to interact with consumers via multiple routes, many e-retailers have extended their existing business to the mobile platform by providing multichannel shopping services, as well as conducting mobile advertising and promotions, consumer support, and other relationship-building activities [25]. Researchers argue that the mobile technology brings retailers more opportunities to expand their markets and reach new consumers [39].

A key question for e-retailers on introducing a new mobile channel is whether it stimulates new purchases. According to the previous literature on multichannel shopping, introducing an additional channel can significantly influence consumer behavior in two ways. On the one hand, while the new channel attracts increasing consumer attention, sales on the incumbent channel are likely to be cannibalized (cannibalization effect) [9]. On the other hand, the new channel, as an additional distribution channel, may increase the availability of the e-retailer [33] as well as generate a mutually positive effect that enhances the e-retailers’ service capability on both the new and the incumbent channels (synergy effect) [29]. The question arises as to which effect plays a more dominant role when a new mobile channel is introduced to an existing web channel. Does the new channel bring new sales or merely shift consumer purchases from the web to the mobile channel?

The purpose of this paper is to empirically investigate how consumers’ purchase behavior changes with the introduction of the mobile channel in the multichannel context. In particular, we categorize the purchase behavior into purchase frequency, order size, and purchase amount in order to investigate the impacts of a newly introduced mobile channel in detail. This study was designed to address the following questions:

1. What is the effect of the new mobile channel on the consumer purchase frequency?

2. How does the consumer purchase basket change after the adoption of the new channel?

3. Does a consumer’s overall purchase amount increase after mobile channel adoption?

Our research data include detailed consumer-level transaction data of both web and mobile channels provided by an e-commerce company that recently launched a mobile channel in addition to its website. By investigating the consumers’ shopping behavior on both channels, we found that despite the cannibalization effect of the new mobile channel on the preexisting web channel, multichannel consumers purchased more frequently and spent more money after mobile channel adoption because of the synergy effect.

This study contributes to the literature of the multichannel context, consisting of the web and mobile channels, which have rarely been addressed in the existing literature. Our empirical examination of the consumers’ actual shopping transactions provides evidence that the synergy effect overrides the cannibalization effect after the introduction of the mobile channel. This study also reveals the behavioral differences between web and mobile shopping resulting from the unique characteristics of each. Moreover, most traditional cross-channel studies focus on sales changes of distinctive channels at the store aggregate level. The rich dataset of this study allowed us to discover the reason for sales changes at the individual consumer level. From a managerial perspective, our research suggests when and how a new mobile channel could lead to a higher consumer purchase amount.

This paper is organized as follows: First, the relevant literature on mobile shopping and multichannel shopping is introduced to develop the main hypotheses of the study. Next, we present a framework of our data and sample selection methods. In Section 5, we elucidate the combined impact of the interaction effects (cannibalization effect and synergy effect) on the consumer purchase frequency, order size, and purchase amount. We then report our research model and results with additional tests for improving the robustness. We then conclude with implications and directions for further research.

## 2. Literature review

Two literature streams are relevant to this study: mobile shopping and multichannel shopping. We review these studies to demonstrate the changes in the consumers’ shopping behavior with the introduction of a mobile channel alongside an incumbent web channel in the multichannel context.

## 2.1. Mobile shopping

Mobile shopping refers to the activities related to consumers’ shopping processes on a mobile device, implemented via a wireless telecommunication network [19]. Some researchers describe mobile shopping as an extension of the online shopping activities with the mobile terminal [28,31], while most other studies attempt to find the differences between these two shopping channels [47].

Researchers have different points of view on the key characteristics of mobile commerce in comparison with ecommerce. Tsalgatidou and Pitoura [41] suggested that, compared with e-commerce, mobile commerce has specific features of location awareness, adaptability, ubiquity, personalization, and broadcasting. Clarke [7] proposed four value propositions that may favor mobile commerce over e-commerce: ubiquity, convenience, localization, and personalization. Shankar and Balasubramanian [38] suggested that the key characteristics of the mobile media and mobile devices are local specificity, portability, and untethered/ wireless features. Wu et al. [47] argued that the most significant characteristics of a mobile service were mobility and its real-time nature. While the characteristics of location sensitivity, location correlation and ubiquity could be derived from its mobility, adaptability, convenience, and other characteristics could be derived from its real-time nature. These features indicate that the consumers are always ‘‘online.’’ As consumers can receive information and perform a transaction at any time anywhere, their product consideration, selection, and purchasing process would occur more frequently.

The literature also outlines the technological limitations of mcommerce compared to e-commerce enablers. Researchers have suggested that mobile devices have usability limitations [6] such as small screens that reduce the richness of information presented and limited display capabilities that require consumers to remember the content of a web page and then find more information by clicking or scrolling [13]. The mobile is also less effective for multitasking compared to computers; this increases the information search costs, as the consumers have to search product information separately. This higher information cost could influence the product type that consumers purchase from the mobile channel [6,13]; thus, the consumers may rely on computers to buy products that need heavy information search efforts.

## 2.2. Multichannel shopping

Introducing mobile shopping to online shopping adds a new context to multichannel management research. Multichannel retailing refers to the retailers’ use of two or more integrated channels to sell products and services to the customer [23], and it can be formally defined as the practice of simultaneously offering information, products, services, and support to consumers through two or more synchronized channels such as the brick-and-mortar channel, catalog channel, or web channel [34]. The combination of different channels may cause cross-channel interactive effects, which jointly influence the consumers’ multichannel shopping behaviors [9,30].

Firstly, the literature suggests that after adoption of the new channel, sales on the incumbent channel may be cannibalized partially or fully, due to the introduction of the new channel [3]. This is called the cannibalization effect. Various channels provide consumers with unique affordance but they also have constraints [32]. As consumers choose between channels to maximize their utility [4] or to pursue their specific shopping goals [10], the cannibalization effect is likely to occur when the new channel provides more appealing features to facilitate the shopping goals of the target audience [9]. Van Nierop et al. [44] empirically demonstrated that the introduction of a web channel has a negative effect on the consumers’ trips to brick-and-mortar stores. Researchers also suggested that substantial cannibalization is more probable if the newly established channel too closely mimics the entrenched channel [9].

Conversely, the introduction of a new channel may result in higher overall sales for several reasons. First, incrementally providing further channels is a form of increasing distribution of products and information. Multichannel consumers are more likely to be exposed to the retailers’ marketing efforts and be in closer contact with the retailer [33], thus inducing incremental purchases [33,30]. From the retailers’ perspective, their availability to reach new consumers and communicate with the existing consumers is enhanced; this was termed as the ‘‘availability effect’’ by Neslin et al. [30]. Second, a retailer provides similar services that consumers can choose to access from different channels [45]. For instance, consumers may be able to switch between the channels conveniently during the shopping process. In this circumstance, the diverse capabilities of separate channels can be combined to yield greater value [27]. Consumers can switch among channels to maximize their utility in consideration of each channel’s distinctive characteristics and are more likely to complete a shopping process than in a single-channel situation. This phenomenon is called the ‘‘goodness of fit between multiple service channels’’ [43]. For example, when a retailer is capable of contacting its consumers and conducting marketing activities through two different channels, the marketing efforts of one channel can enhance those of another [29]. Both the ‘‘availability effect’’ and the ‘‘goodness-of-fit effect’’ could improve the performance of the new and the incumbent channels, as well as enhance the overall effectiveness of reaching and serving consumers. We combine them to create the ‘‘synergy effect’’ in this research.

Appendix 1 describes the relevant multichannel literature on the cannibalization and the synergy effect. Among them, only one study examined the introduction of a mobile commerce channel to an e-commerce channel. Einav et al. [11] found that the introduction of mobile commerce induces a slight decrease in sales in the e-commerce platform, while the incremental purchases on the mobile platform are significant, thus leading to a higher overall purchase frequency. However, they did not explore the underlying mechanism of this sales change. In this study, we fill this gap by empirically investigating the impacts of both the cannibalization and the synergy effects caused by mobile channel adoption on the purchase behavior of consumers.

## 3. Hypotheses development

Based on the existing literature, the introduction of the new channel could change the consumers’ purchase behavior in many ways. In this section, we categorize the purchase behavior into three parts, that is, purchase frequency, order size, and purchase amount, and we discuss the impacts of the new mobile channel on them.

## 3.1. The effect of mobile shopping on purchase frequency

A specific shopping channel offers consumers particular shopping capabilities that in turn differentiate the way they shop on each channel [3]. The literature on channel cannibalization posits that consumers would migrate to a new channel if its capability were higher than that of the incumbent channel [1,8]. Schoenbachler and Gordon [37] found that the consumers chose purchase channels mostly for convenience. In the mobile shopping context, there are several capabilities that favor a mobile application over a traditional website. For example, a mobile application enables consumers to connect to the Internet and shop at any time anywhere, which could significantly increase visit and purchase incidence. Mobile applications also make the shopping process more convenient by eliminating the need for the consumer to make the effort required to enter an entire website address, by allowing them to simply click and open the application from the mobile device. Therefore, it is possible that with the abovementioned capabilities and convenience, some of the transactions from the traditional web channel will be cannibalized by the mobile channel, especially when the retailer provides similar services and products on the mobile and the incumbent channels.

The introduction of the mobile channel may also bring the synergy effect to sales on the web channel. For example, retailers’ promotion of the new mobile channel enhances the retailers image, which in turn increases the purchase frequency on the incumbent web channel.

However, we argue that the reversed synergy effect is small given the significant advantage of a new mobile channel on accessibility. Only purchases that required more intensive information search and comparison would be affected by this reversed synergy effect. Therefore, in accordance with the literature, we posit that the cannibalization effect overrides the reversed synergy effect of the new channel and thus reduces the purchase frequency of the incumbent channel. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H1a. Mobile consumers’ web channel purchase frequency wil decrease after mobile channel adoption.

Regarding the purchase frequency of both channels, the literature suggests that creating an additional channel is a form of increased distribution, which lowers costs and increases sales [30]. The distribution capabilities are enhanced by the convenience of accessing a mobile device at any time and anywhere. The availability of a PC device is much more limited compared to an ‘‘always on’’ mobile device. The increased accessibility and availability of the mobile channel facilitate impulse purchases induced by advertisements or recommendations from friends, especially for low-value and low-involvement products [17]. The ubiquity of the mobile devices also works perfectly for flash sales when there is limited time for the consumer to enjoy better discounts or wider choices. Moreover, an e-retailer can target its consumers more precisely and communicate with them more closely through the enhanced marketing capability of the mobile devices [14]. Researchers suggest that the consumers who receive more marketing contact will respond to the retailer more strongly in terms of purchase incidence [2]. Furthermore, the web and the mobile channels would create a two-way positive effect. With the potential existence of synergies, the added value of separate channels exceeds the sum of their individual values [45,20]. In the case of m-commerce and e-commerce, consumers can have the benefits of both ubiquity and ease of information, leading to a positive incentive for more purchases [24]. For example, consumers can put the candidate products into a virtual shopping cart at any time using their mobile to avoid missing a good purchase lead, and then conduct the necessary information search later via PC to make the final decision. Conversely, consumers could first search for information via PC and then make the decision via mobile. Using a combination of the two distribution channels, the particular advantages of each work jointly to support the completion of a shopping process, which results in a higher overall purchase incidence.

Combining the higher availability and mutual synergy effect, we expected that the introduction of a mobile channel would lead to new purchases on the additional mobile channel as well as incremental purchases on both channels. While the cannibalization effect may lead to sales transfer from the web channel to the new mobile channel, we posit that the increase in sales on the mobile channel exceeds its decrease on the web channel. We therefore hypothesize the following:

H1b. Consumers’ newly added purchase frequency on the mobile channel is larger than the decreased frequency on the web channel, which leads to a higher overall purchase frequency after mobile channel adoption.

## 3.2. The effect of mobile shopping on order size

The introduction of the mobile channel could conceivably also influence the consumers’ shopping order size. Shopping order size – defined in this study as the average monetary amount of each transaction – is determined by two factors: the price and quantity of the products purchased in each order. Mobile shopping might influence both factors.

A channel’s capability in product information search and transaction fulfillment determines the shopping efficiency on this channel [16]. With the rapid technical progress in mobile shopping applications and mobile payment, the capability difference between the mobile and web channel in transaction activities is fast disappearing. However, information-searching activities on the mobile channel are still limited by its smaller screen size and cumbersome input mechanisms [13]. Some studies suggest a positive relationship between the product price and search capabilities across a variety of products [18,36]. In this regard, consumers may use the web channel to conduct more information search activities to support their purchase decisions when they buy high-price products. On the contrary, the information required for a purchase decision is often less when consumers buy relatively inexpensive products, in which case consumers might be indifferent as to which channel they use for these.

The inherent limitations of mobile commerce might also restrict the complexity of the tasks processed on the mobile channel [46]. The number of items included in a shopping basket is positively correlated with the purchase task complexity given that more items may involve more searching and comparison tasks. Consumers may purchase more items per basket through the web channel given its higher ability to process complex tasks. Meanwhile, the mobile channel, by contrast, may lead to a smaller number of items per basket.

The two aspects mentioned earlier suggest that the order size on the new mobile channel should be smaller than that of the incumbent web channel. As the cannibalization effect suggests that the consumers shift some purchases from the web to the mobile channel, the order size on the web channel may change for two reasons. On the one hand, the web channel’s capability to handle complex tasks might tip the web channel in the direction of an increased order size as consumers shift simpler tasks, which contain relatively cheaper and fewer items per basket, to the mobile channel. On the other hand, the order size of the web channel will decrease as the web channel now handles only a part of the consumer’s shopping as a consumer’s total shopping is now allocated to both the web and mobile channels.

Therefore, from the cannibalization perspective, these two opposite effects are likely to lead to unchanged order sizes on the web channel.

However, with regard to the synergy effect, while the new mobile channel stimulates new purchases on the web channel, or transfers some unfinished purchase processes to the same, it is reasonable to believe that the incremental transactions on the web channel brought by the synergy effect of the mobile channel is more ‘‘mobile like’’. In other words, the order size feature of the incremental transactions would be similar to those of the transactions on the mobile channel, which is smaller when compared to the original web transactions.

With these effects on the web channel, we posit that the web channel’s shopping order size may decrease after the adoption of the mobile channel. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H2a. Mobile consumers’ shopping order size on the web channel decreases after their mobile channel adoption.

As discussed earlier, from the perspective of the channel’s ability, the newly introduced mobile channel is typically used to buy less expensive products and involves a smaller quantity in the basket. Consequently, the order size of the new mobile transactions would be much lower than that of the web transactions. This in turn would influence the overall average order size. Because the order size of the mobile channel would be smaller than that of the web channel and it might also decrease further, we could infer that the consumers’ overall average order size would decrease after the introduction of the mobile channel. We then hypothesize that

H2b. Mobile consumers’ shopping order size on the mobile channel is smaller than that on the web channel, which leads to a lower shopping order size after their mobile channel adoption.

## 3.3. The effect of mobile shopping on purchase amount

The two hypotheses mentioned earlier focus on the combined impact of the cannibalization and synergy effects on the consumers’ purchase frequency and order size after the introduction of the mobile channel. The consumer purchase amount is determined by both monthly purchase frequency and order size. We have hypothesized that on the incumbent web channel, the consumers’ purchase frequency (H1a) and the order size (H2a) decreases. Therefore, for the purchase amount, we can easily infer that

H3a. Mobile consumers’ purchase amount on the web channel decreases after their mobile channel adoption.

With regard to the overall purchase amount on both the channels, we hypothesized an increase in the purchase frequency (H1b) and a decrease in the order size (H2b); hence, we may be unable to infer the change of purchase amount by doing simple algebraic calculations. We have argued that the consumers shift some purchase activities from the web to the mobile channel due to the cannibalization effect, as well as make a smaller order size on the mobile channel. Therefore, the shifted purchase amount on the mobile channel caused by the cannibalization effect may not be sufficient to recover the loss on the web channel. Nonetheless, the synergy effect could create additional purchases on the mobile channel and lead to a higher purchase frequency on the web channel [15]. Therefore, with similar arguments as for H1b, we posit that the consumers’ additional purchase amounts on the mobile and the web channels would offset the decreased sales caused by the cannibalization effect through an increased purchase frequency, leading to an increased purchase amount. This is consistent with the findings of Han et al. [15] that the retailers’ total sales increased after the introduction of a new tablet channel. Therefore, we hypothesize that the consumers’ overall purchase amounts would increase after the mobile channel adoption, and the magnitude of the change depends on the relative magnitude of the synergy effect against the cannibalization effect.

H3b. A mobile consumer’s newly increased purchase amount on the mobile channel is larger than the decrease on the web channel, which leads to a higher overall purchase amount after the mobile channel adoption.

## 4. Data description

Our research data have been obtained from one of the most popular B2C e-retailers in China. Its main product categories are electronics, office supplies, and household appliances. The company launched its mobile shopping application in November 2011 as an additional channel to its incumbent website. It offers identical products through its web and mobile channels, including identical information and transaction processes. Consumers can use a unique login ID to carry out transactions on both the mobile and web channels. As Deleersnyder et al. [9] have suggested, ‘‘substantial cannibalization is more likely to take place if the newly established channel too closely mimics the entrenched channel’’. Thus, because of the similarities between this e-retailer’s web and mobile channels, the retailer’s web channel faces potential cannibalization.

The data used in our analysis were extracted on June 30, 2013. The firm generated a random selection of 4434 individual

consumers for analysis. We asked the firm to provide data from before the mobile application’s release date, which allowed us to create a ‘‘pre’’ period for comparison. The company provided transactional data from January 1, 2011. The consumer transaction data obtained from both the web and mobile channels were extracted for this window period. For each transaction, we have detailed information such as the date of transaction, the basket information of each transaction, consumer ID, and the (web or mobile) channel used for the purchase.

In this research, we considered the mobile channel adoption<sup>1</sup> as an ‘‘event’’ in a natural experimental setting during our 2½-year sampling window. In order to explore the economic impact of the mobile channel introduction, we divided the full sample into two groups and compared the average purchase amount per consumer: (1) consumers who only purchased through the web channel and (2) those who purchased through both the mobile and the web channels. In the subsequent discussion, we designated the consumers with both mobile and web purchases as mobile consumers (treatment group) and the rest as web-only consumers (control group).<sup>2</sup> Our dataset contains 2566 web-only and 1868 mobile consumers within our window. As the web-only consumers had never used the mobile application, we chose the median of the period during which the mobile consumers began to use the mobile channel intensively as a reference point. A large portion of the mobile consumers started using the mobile channel between March and May 2012; hence, the median of this period – 0:00 on April 1, 2012 – was chosen as the cutoff point to further separate the control group into a ‘‘pre’’ and ‘‘post’’ group. However, only 1437 of the web-only consumers had registered before April 1, 2012. We thus considered only these consumers in our control group.

Given that the assignment of the treatment and control groups was not random, those consumers who adopted mobile shopping may have been inherently more likely to be active purchasers (e.g., they may be more wealthy, find new shopping technology more acceptable, etc.), leading to a potentially biased result in the analysis. It is thus necessary to address the self-selection problem to rule out a potential endogeneity issue. In this research, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to control for selection on observables. We calculated the propensity scores for each consumer using the logit function with observed explanatory variables, including duration (duration and duration\_sqr), consumer credit level on this e-commerce website<sup>3</sup> (credit\_level and credit level\_sqr) used as a proxy for disposable income, PC purchase frequency (MpurchaseFreqtotalbf and MpurchaseFreqtotalbf\_sqr), and logarithmic PC purchase amount (log(MpurchaseAmttotalbf)) before the mobile channel adoption, with the common support conditions. We also included several squares of covariates in the model. The results along with the fit statistics are reported in Appendix 1. We then performed the process of matching the treatment and control consumers using the estimated propensity score. Because of the lack of a sufficient control group, we matched a mobile consumer to a web-only consumer using the nearest matching method with replacement. We then created a matched sample based on PSM. The application of PSM limited the sample to 1865 mobile consumers and all 1437 of the web-only consumers.

We then checked whether the underlying assumptions of the PSM process held. In order to insure the common support condition, we checked the existence of an overlap between the treatment and control groups. We performed a visual analysis of the propensity score distributions through box plots and histograms as recommended by Lechner [22] and Rishika et al. [35], as shown in Fig. 1. The results indicate that the consistence between the control and the treatment groups improved significantly after matching.

We further checked the quality of the PSM procedure. We conducted balancing tests suggested by Rishika et al. [35], and the results are shown in Table 1. We calculated the mean difference between the treatment and control groups before and after matching for each covariate. We also reported the t-statistics for the difference in means as well as the variance ratio for each covariate by dividing the variance of the treatment group by that of the control. The t-test shows no significant difference for the covariates between the treatment and the matched control groups, and the variance ratio is close to 1, which suggests a good quality of the selected comparison group.

After the PSM procedure, we had 52,759 transaction records, of which 10,589 were carried out on the mobile channel. Table 2 reports the basic data structure. We found that mobile consumers purchased more than web-only consumers, either before or after the introduction of the mobile channel, which suggests that the multichannel consumers were more active than the single-channel consumers even after we controlled for the selection bias. This phenomenon is consistent with the previous research findings in other multichannel contexts that heavy buyers are more likely to be multichannel users [21]. In addition, Table 2 shows that the average order size was smaller for consumers who used both channels. More specifically, the average order size on the web channel decreased slightly after the mobile channel adoption; however, the average order size on the mobile channel (249.19 RMB) was significantly smaller than that on the web channel (380.43 RMB).

## 5.3. The effect of mobile channel introduction on purchase amount

As discussed earlier, the monthly purchase amount reflects the relative magnitude of the synergy and the cannibalization effects, and it is influenced by both monthly purchase frequency and order size. If we simply timed the row of average transactions with that of the average order size given in Table 2, we could obtain a significant increase in the purchase amount for the mobile consumers (from 3366 to 5155 RMB). ## 6. Econometric analysis and result

In addition to mobile channel adoption, consumers’ shopping behavior could also have been driven by other time-variant factors, such as increasing marketing expenditure, an increasing awareness and reputation of this e-retailer, a wider product collection, and consistent price drops. In order to mitigate these effects, we utilized the difference-in-differences (DID) method to investigate the consumer overall purchase behavior change induced by mobile channel adoption. DID is one of the most popular designs for finding the effects of a treatment in social science.

## 6.1. Empirical model

The setting of the model is to compare the average difference in the dependent variables before and after the ‘‘treatment’’ for the treatment and control groups. This approach removes the biases that could result from permanent differences between the groups, as well as those due to changes over time in factors that are common to the treatment and control groups. Our sample was thus divided into four groups: mobile consumers before (MobileConsumer = 1, Time = 0) and after the adoption of the mobile channel (MobileConsumer = 1, Time = 1), and web-only consumers before (MobileConsumer = 0, Time = 0) and after the cutoff point (Mobile-Consumer = 0, Time = 1). Table 5 shows, in brief, some measures of the change in consumer purchase behavior after the adoption of the mobile channel. We observed that the overall monthly purchase frequency and purchase amount increased significantly, while the order size decreased significantly after the introduction of the mobile channel. For web transactions, the purchase frequency and purchase amount decreased slightly after the mobile channel adoption. The order size of web transactions of mobile consumers remained the same after the adoption. But compared with the web-only consumers, the order size of the mobile users’ web transactions decreased, which may have been due to the variance of web-only consumers’ purchases.

Besides the main DID effect, we included the time lagdependent variables, for example, ‘‘MpurchaseFreq<sup>total</sup><sub>t-1</sub>’’, to control the dynamic impacts of the consumers’ shopping behavior. We also controlled for the consumer’s average order size of all past purchases (AOS) to reflect the heterogeneity of the consumer’s purchase behavior. We added the month index dummy variables (M ) to determine the potential time effect. The covariates of the PSM procedure were also included in the model noted by $X _ { i j } ,$ with Q representative of its estimated coefficients.

## 7. Robustness check results

## 7.1. Individual DID test

The time of mobile channel adoption in our context was not the same for everyone in the treatment group; some may have bought via mobile later or installed the application later than others. Aggregating the treatment effect at the group level may cause biased estimation results. In order to mitigate this problem, we conducted an individual-level DID approach as suggested by Manchanda et al. [26].

The nature of this approach is to assign an individual-level value to the ‘‘after treatment’’ variable. More specifically, we aggregated the detailed purchase data (purchase frequency and purchase amount) into two periods (T and T ), in which T and T represented the period before and after mobile channel adoption, respectively. This approach helped in mitigating potential serial correlation and grouped the error term effects rather than leveraging a more fragmented time series [5]. The specification of the model was the same as stated in Eqs. (1)–(6), the only difference being that t 2 {T , T } and each consumer only had two observations, that is, before and after. ## 7.2. Alternative cutoff points

We chose the middle of the time period – March, 2012 to May, 2012 – as our cutoff point, during which most mobile consumers adopted the new mobile channel. Thus, the results obtained for the comparative study of web-only consumers would depend on our choice of the cutoff point. For comparison purposes, we constructed two additional samples by setting the cutoff point for web-only consumers as March 1, 2012 and May 1, 2012, respectively. We then conducted PSM in terms of the two selected samples as done earlier. We conducted econometric modeling using the two samples separately, and the results are shown in Appendix 3. We found that the result pattern of the two compared samples was very similar to our original results (Table 6). This suggests the robustness of our analysis under the original cutoff point.

## 8. Conclusion

The objective of this study was to empirically analyze the effects of new mobile channel adoption on the change in consumers’ purchase behavior. With the rapid development of mobile services, consumers have shown a tendency to switch from a web to a mobile channel. The question arises as to how the newly introduced mobile channel changes the overall online commercial landscape. The findings of this research shed light on this question and provide insights from both theoretical and practical perspectives.

## 8.1. Theoretical implications

Compared to the previous literature that mainly focused on mobile shopping characteristics, or other cross-channel interactions in multichannel contexts not consisting of a mobile channel, this study contributes to the body of literature on mobile shopping and multichannel shopping in several ways.

First, the prior literature mainly focused on the impact of a new web channel on the existing (traditional) channels [9,33,3]. Han et al. [15] investigated the impact of a new tablet channel on the mobile and PC channels, but they found that the tablet devices contributed to only a small fraction of the total mobile transactions. Very few studies have examined the effects of the introduction of the mobile channel comprehensively. The shopping processes of the web and mobile channel are similar in many respects in terms of online shopping, such as product search, online payment, and package delivery. However, the web channel works better for information searches, while the strength of the mobile channel lies in its being more ubiquitous and convenient. It has been suggested that cannibalization is likely to occur when the new channel closely mimics the incumbent channel [9]. Our research reaches similar conclusions that the mobile channel cannibalizes the web channel.

Second, we investigated the impact of the combined crosschannel effects, namely cannibalization and synergy on sales on the existing channel and the total sales. The extant literature studied either the cannibalization effect alone to discuss the decrease in sales on the incumbent channel [9] or the combined effect of cannibalization and synergy on the incumbent channel, ignoring the effect on the retailer’s overall sales [29]. Pauwels and Neslin [33] put forward the theories of the cannibalization and synergy effects, but they treated the order size as unchanged, which simplified their effects on total sales. In our research, we empirically tested the existence of the cannibalization effect on the mobile channel by investigating the decrease in the monthly purchase frequency and monthly purchase amount through the web channel. We discussed in detail how these factors influenced the consumers’ overall purchases and empirically tested the relative magnitude of the synergy effect against cannibalization. The result shows that although the consumers’ overall order size decreased, the synergy effect exceeded the results of the cannibalization effect and led to a higher purchase frequency and purchase amount.

Third, we contribute to mobile shopping literature using mobile shopping features to explain how mobile commerce influences the consumers’ shopping behavior. The ubiquity and convenience of the mobile channel resulted in higher availability, which induced incremental purchases on the mobile channel. We also found that the consumers tended to purchase less expensive products and fewer products in each transaction, because the mobile channel’s information-processing capability and ability to aid decisionmaking was weaker than those of the web channel, thus resulting in smaller order sizes than those on the web channel.

## 8.2. Practical implications

This study also provides significant insights for e-retailers interested in introducing a new mobile channel. Our results show that adding an additional mobile channel does stimulate incremental total sales, despite the fact that a fraction of the incumbent web channel’s sales is cannibalized after the mobile channel adoption.

Second, although the cannibalization effect does exist, the new mobile channel is an additional distribution channel that provides appealing features to the consumers, leading to potentially increased sales overall. However, in order to achieve those, the retailer needs to carefully design features on the mobile channel to generate more synergy effects. The retailer can stimulate the synergy effect by taking advantage of the ubiquity and convenience of mobile commerce, or utilize mobile marketing tools to attract more traffic from the mobile channel. The retailer can also improve the connections between the two channels so that the consumers can seamlessly switch between the two to leverage the synergy effect. In other words, the retailer should enable a consumer to complete the shopping process through either channel with the same process, and to switch at any segment of the process.

We end with a few limitations of our research – primarily due to data availability issues – that could be addressed in future research. First, some studies have suggested that consumers choose different channels for different stages of the buyer behavior process [4]. For instance, they might research a product online but choose to purchase it in store [23]. However, because of the lack of information on the consumers’ searching activities, we were unable to distinguish between the transactions of consumers who searched and purchased through the mobile channel and those who searched online and purchased through mobile devices. Further clickstream data would provide more insights into the cross-channel integration process. Second, as the dataset of this study was based on transactions, we were unable to distinguish between the consumers who did not use the mobile channel and those who used it without making any transactions. This could have led to the underestimation of the reversed synergy effect from the mobile to the web channel. Further study with app log information could eliminate this bias and improve the robustness of the results. Third, we did not consider the dynamic change of purchase behavior in our model. As shown in Fig. 2, the consumers’ mobile channel purchases increased immediately after the adoption of the mobile channel, and in the long term, the pattern became more stable. Literature also suggests that the consumers’ purchase decision processes may evolve over time [42]. Investigating the temporal effects on multichannel shopping could be very insightful as the new channel will become widely adopted and well developed over time.
