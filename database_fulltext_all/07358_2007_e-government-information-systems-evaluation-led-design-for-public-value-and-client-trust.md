# e-Government information systems: Evaluation-led design for public value and client trust

## Abstract

e-Government systems differ from commercial information systems (IS) in that they frequently encompass strategic goals that go beyond efficiency, effectiveness and economy, and include political and social objectives such as trust in government, social inclusion, community regeneration, community well-being and sustainability. Designing e-Government systems that help to meet these objectives is a significant challenge for the future. This paper develops an evaluative design framework for e-Government projects that complements traditional approaches to IS evaluation. The framework is based upon Moor’s concept of public value. It focuses upon citizens’ and clients’ experiences of service provision and service outcomes as contributors to the formation of public trust. Trust is shown to be related to the extent to which people feel that an e-Government service enhances their sense of being wellinformed, gives them greater personal control, and provides them with a sense of influence or contingency. The framework’s development and validation are founded upon analyses of a two live case studies in south-east England and London, U.K.

## Introduction

The United Kingdom (U.K.) government, in common with many others, is actively promoting on-line access to government and public services through its e-Government initiative. At one level, the goal is economic – efficiencies in public service provision release resources to address other dimensions of citizens’ expectations and preferences, be they revised rates of taxation or new and improved public services (HMT, 2004; CIO, 2005). But there are also prominent socio-political dimensions to many e-Government initiatives.

One such dimension is the unifying concern across many U.K. government departments that deployment of e-Government systems be undertaken in ways that promote social inclusion, community development, well-being and sustainability (ODPM, 2004, 2005). A second such dimension relates to identifying and meeting citizens’ expectations of public services and their governance. Beyond personal satisfaction or contentment, citizens attach value to the entitlements of others, for example, in respect of quality of healthcare, threshold standards of education, access to civil and criminal justice – the so-called distributive equity (Kelly et al., 2002). Citizens also expect accountability and transparency in respect of service provision, and acknowledge the additional burdens that these entail. The very nature and diversity of the broader objectives associated with the domains within which e-Government information systems (IS) are introduced serves to distinguish their evaluation from commercial IS.

In the public sector, the dominant approaches to evaluation-led IS design and management tend to mirror those of the private sector, focusing predominantly on the functional alignment with requirements and economic performance measures such as cost reduction, profit, return on investment and so on (Willcocks & Lester, 1999; Irani & Love, 2002). While such functional and economic measures are clearly important in the context of e-Government, they do not naturally support the attainment of the broader socio-economic and sociopolitical goals that characterise so many e-Government projects. Hence, the objective of this paper is to elaborate elements of a conceptual framework that supports evaluation-led design of e-Government systems in which outcomes such as those we have described are prominent. The framework is intended to complement existing approaches, so elements that incorporate more familiar technical, performance and economic objectives are not explicitly included here.

In developing a framework, we take as our point of departure the observation that the broader expectations or preferences of citizens and governments find expression in Moore’s conceptualisation of public value (Moore, 1995; Kelly et al., 2002). Public value can be interpreted as the value that citizens and their representatives seek in relation to strategic outcomes and experience of public services (Moore, 1995). It includes the value attached to relatively concrete outcomes, such as reduced levels of homelessness or universal access to healthcare independent of ability to pay, and the more intangible, such as increased trust in government and public service providers. Public value also captures the quality of citizens’ experiences of public services and government, and incorporates the ‘production’ of trust within society (Kelly et al., 2002). Public value, in this sense, does not equate to ‘value for money’; instead, it complements such measures of value.

In order to develop an initial evaluative framework supporting design for public value we use a case study, Home Connections (HC), an early e-Government project that introduced information and communication technologies (ICT) into the allocation of public and social housing in central London, U.K. Engagement with this case study project allowed us to simultaneously abstract concepts and ground intuitions that led to a (partial) framework supporting design for public value. This framework was then validated via a survey-based study of clients from a similar project, Thurrock Choice Homes (TCH).

In giving an account of the development of the framework, the remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In the following section, we outline our approach to IS evaluation, describe the research context, and give an account of the major phases of the research project and of the methods used. Then the evolution of our conceptual framework, rooting this in literature, earlier research on attitudes to public services and the HC case study are described. The succeeding section gives an account of the validation of the framework in the TCH project. The penultimate section introduces a specific tool to support evaluation-led design and management, based upon the earlier elements of the framework. The last section provides some concluding remarks and observations, and summarises some additional ‘lessons learned’.

## Evaluative approach and methods

The development of any evaluative framework or instrument must be rooted in a chosen evaluative paradigm. We begin this section with an outline and justification of our choice of paradigm: interpretive evaluation (Walsham, 1999). Subsequently, we describe the two main phases of the framework building project and outline the methods used at each stage.

## Approach to e-Government evaluation

Willcocks & Lester (1999) observe that, as far as IS design and development is concerned, evaluation is one of the most neglected areas. However, this is not simply the result of omission; rather, it reflects the degree of difficulty inherent in developing appropriate evaluative criteria. The most frequently cited reasons for absence of evaluation are problems identifying and quantifying benefits and opportunity costs, unfamiliarity with evaluation techniques, difficulty in interpreting results, and lack of time, data, information or interest (Ballantine et al., 1999, p. 142). Not only are there well-recognised problems with objective, usually financially based, evaluation metrics, but there is also little experience (at least outside the academic world) of using methods, which seek to interpret and/or quantify the subjective (Powell, 1999, p. 159) or the intangible (Farbey et al., 1999, p. 184).

In the context of e-Government, the problems of traditional IS evaluation described here are rendered even more complex. Stakeholders are particularly diverse, including elected representatives, government officials, public-not-for-profit and private-for-profit organisations. Users are not simply customers; they are clients. That is to say that their relationship with the service provider may be governed by statutory entitlements, and professional and ethical codes. And in a separate and distinct role, users are also the citizens who express preferences and demand accountability via the ballot box. The range of evaluative criteria is also extended. Tangible criteria include cost cutting, enhanced efficiency, service enhancement and development, and, for private sector participants, (long-term) profit maximisation. But to these we add the more intangible criteria associated with the creation of public value, such as perceptions of equity and social inclusion in respect of services, and outcomes such as community cohesion, sustainability, well-being and trust.

In response to the problem of evaluation in traditional IS contexts, some academics within the IS community have argued for interpretive evaluation, which seeks to accommodate subjective assessment at every stage of a project in order to complement objective techniques (Walsham, 1999; Hirscheim & Smithson, 1999; Jones & Hughes, 2001). Interpretive evaluation is neither prescriptive nor proscriptive in respect of the techniques that an evaluator may use, other than requiring the techniques to draw upon the views of stakeholders in contributing to a subjective understanding (in contrast to an objective ‘Olympian’ deliberation or judgement – if such is ever possible).

Walsham (1999, pp. 374–377) identifies the main elements in the design of an interpretive approach to IS evaluation as follows:

\- Context arises from an analysis of stakeholder assessments, both current and historical.

\- Purpose is related to the stage of development of an IS. At the early stage it supports feasibility assessment, during development it feeds back on design progress, and post-implementation it focuses on achievement of goals. For at all stages, purpose is concerned with achieving understanding (preferably shared).

\- Content relates to the system goals (functional, economic, human, organisational, social and political), acknowledging that stakeholders will have different perspectives and motives in relation to the project.

\- Facilitation requires the interpretive evaluator to assume a number of roles: facilitator of reflection, learner, teacher, reality shaper and change agent.

The sustained emphasis upon stakeholder engagement and the breadth of scope in respect of project goals aligns well with the e-Government context. In a comprehensive survey seeking to marry IS project contexts with approaches to evaluation, Farbey et al. (1999) consider qualitative, interpretive evaluation as the method of choice for the class of projects that are radical (as opposed to conservative) and for which objectives are fuzzy (as opposed to crystallised); a category into which we believe many significant e-Government initiatives fall. However, the very attributes that suit interpretive methods to radical projects and fuzzy contexts, also mean that they offer relatively little by way of specific guidance to designers and managers. The elements of interpretive evaluation above provide direction at a very high level. Hence, the aim of this paper is to elaborate elements of a conceptual framework that enhances and refines this support by identifying specific elements of an evaluative framework suited to projects featuring evident public value objectives.

## Project context, phases and methods

The work described here was undertaken in two main phases: framework building and framework development. Each phase was facilitated by senior managers and staff in two similar e-Government projects in the south-east of England. To provide context, we begin with an account of the e-Government projects and then describe the project phases and the methods used within them.

Case study organisations HC and TCH are e-Government projects supporting the allocation of public and social housing, so-called choice-based letting (CBL) schemes. In the U.K., local government authorities (councils) maintain a stock of public housing that serves a number of economic and social functions. Many local councils administer their housing stock through a housing department, accountable to the elected council, but managed and staffed by professional council officers. Historically, many housing departments have been organised as bureaucracies; that is, they are characterised as being the locus of expertise, power and authority in relation to their clients. Culturally, they adopt professional attitudes to clients, administering housing stock ‘in the public interest’.

The client group for CBL schemes is very diverse. In the two areas concerned, much public social housing exists in pockets and small estates. Some are strongly identified with established local communities, reflecting the economic and ethno-demographic history of an area. In south-east England and London, demand for public and social housing is far in excess of supply. Allocations are prioritised according to criterion-referenced need that reflects many factors, including the size of the family, health and disability (Borough of Camden, 2003). Most of those seeking accommodation will have to wait for long periods of time and many will not succeed in being allocated a home from their local borough housing stock. Typically, CBL schemes seek to promote consideration of alternative forms of housing and alternative locations, especially in parts of the U.K. where there was relatively low demand, or even surplus.

Even cursory consideration of the factors outlined above will make it clear that CBL schemes are particularly demanding in respect of attaining client satisfaction and public value outcomes.

From an e-Government perspective, the main goals of the two projects may be summarised as follows: by taking advantage of ICT, change and improve clients’ experience of applying for and finding a solution to their housing need. The ICT systems seek to avoid digital exclusion, supporting disadvantaged groups, such as people with disabilities, the elderly, people for whom English is not their first language. They also aim to integrate advice and guidance on training and employment, thereby taking a more holistic view of people’s needs.

Both HC and TCH integrate Internet, mobile phone text messaging (SMS) and automatic telephony, together with local commercial print media, as an interface to a reengineered personnel-based system. The systems’ principal functions enable clients to: explore their eligibility for housing, search for suitable properties, apply for properties, monitor the progress of their application relative to others, receive basic feedback on outcomes and reflect upon their application histories. Officials still make the initial assessment of need, respond to clients’ queries, receive applications for specific properties from clients and determine the allocation of each property.

At the time of the study, HC was deployed across four of the five councils in central London covering about 60,000 registered clients, and TCH was deployed in Thurrock, a borough in the Thames Gateway in southeast England, with about 6000 clients.

Phase 1 – development and refinement of the initial framework An initial conceptual framework was based upon an analysis of Moore’s concept of public value and consideration of its application to U.K. public services. The analysis was based upon original literature (Moore, 1995) and discussion papers produced for U.K. government (Donovan et al., 2001; Kelly et al., 2002) and sought to identify and relate the major elements of a model for producing public value.

The initial evaluative framework was then developed and refined by undertaking an interpretive evaluation study, HC (2005–6). Through this reflexive use of interpretive evaluation, we sought to identify and refine generic evaluative constructs (concepts and relations between concepts) associated with public value that afforded re-use in future projects of a similar nature. This included drawing upon earlier work that explored citizens’ perceptions of traditional public services (Grimsley et al., 2003, 2004a, b).

This phase concluded with a preliminary qualitative validation of elements of the revised framework within the HC project.

In order to manage the development of the framework during this phase, we adopted the structured-case approach of Carroll & Swatman (2000). Here, the term ‘case’ is used to define the object of study; for instance, a person, a group, a project, an organisation, a process, an IS, etc. The goal of structured-case is to produce new or revised knowledge and theory that describes relationships between (abstract) concepts but is demonstrably rooted in observation. A structured-case study is distinguished from other forms of case study by using three steps in a process cycle that features:

\- An evolving conceptual framework representing the current state of a researcher’s (evaluator’s) aims, theoretical foundations and understandings. The researcher begins with an initial conceptual framework based upon prior knowledge and experience, CF<sub>1</sub>, iteratively revising it (CF ) until the enquiry terminates, producing CF<sub>n</sub>.

\- An iterative research cycle featuring data collection, analysis, interpretation and synthesis.

\- An ongoing literature-based scrutiny that is used to compare and contrast the (evolving) outcomes of the enquiry with extant literature which may either support or contest them.

Structured-case had already been used to develop understanding of ways in which local authorities undertake ex ante evaluation of e-Government projects (Irani et al., 2005).

An enquiry within which theory building unfolds in accordance with the steps of structured-case may be compared to Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Glaser, 1992). They are similar in respect of the way a researcher engages with the research context for data collection, and then undertakes analysis, interpretation and theory building. However, structured-case differs from Grounded Theory in that it is less insistent upon the initial emergence of theory from the research context (Glaser, 1992) and more permissive of traditional hypothetico-deductive approaches to theory development. Typically, structured-case allows for an a priori conceptual framework (as is the case in the work presented below), which is then validated and refined using both qualitative techniques for inductive theory building, and theory testing using empirical studies and quantitative analysis (as is also the case here).

Data to support the reflexive interpretive evaluation of HC, and subsequent ‘framework building’, were acquired in a variety of ways including:

\- system walk-through (providing the ‘narrative’ of the project manager/system developer);

\- formal project documentation (including project proposal, commissioning, hand-over);

\- public presentation (public-facing narrative presented at a promotional launch);

\- website observation;

\- analysis of the primary records of at five focus groups involving 40 clients in total and conducted as part of the post-launch evaluation;

\- executive report derived from primary focus group reports and

\- interviews with project staff (HC manager, project manager and system developer).

Phase 2 – validation of the extended model The framework resulting from phase 1 was validated by means of a survey of user experience for TCH (3rd quarter 2006). A questionnaire was designed that explored a number of aspects of users’ experiences of the TCH system. Specific measurement items included were designed to assess, from a client’s perspective, the existence and/or relevance of the constructs featured in the hypothesised framework derived in phase 1. The user questionnaire was sent to 2315 clients registered to use the ICT-mediated system of whom 244 (11%) responded. This is a relatively low response rate for a self-completion postal survey, where response rates between 10 and 30% may be expected. Factors that may have influenced response rates include the length of the survey form (70 items over nine pages), time of year (the survey coincided with the end of the summer holiday period and the return of children to school), a relatively short period within which to respond, no reminder letters were issued due to time and resource constraints. Nonetheless, the numbers of clients who did respond enable us to be confident about statements we make about the experiences of respondents, if less so about statements concerning all clients.

Completed questionnaires were electronically scanned. Analyses used a variety of multivariate tools (available in SPSS 14). Further details of the questionnaire items and of the specific multivariate methods used, are given later, when describing the detailed results of the validation.

## An evaluative design framework for public value

In this section, we document the evolution of our public value framework. We begin with an account of the initial conceptual framework (CF ), based upon an analysis of the nature of public value as articulated in the core literature. Thereafter, we describe its refinement $\left( \mathrm { C F } _ { 2 } \right)$ as we interpreted $\mathrm { C F _ { 1 } }$ in the light of earlier work on citizens’ attitudes to public services while simultaneously engaged with the HC project. We conclude this section with an account of the initial qualitative evaluation of our framework, based upon analysis of clients’ evaluation of the project within its first year of operation.

## The nature and production of public value (CF )

In articulating the notion of public value, Moore (1995) grounds the concept in an observation that societies express ‘normatively compelling collective purposes’ (p. 30). Such purposes are most clearly identifiable in respect of ‘universal entitlements’ such as health, education, housing and sanitation, personal security and justice. They appear related to social values such as fairness, equity or equality. Moore argues that the nature of public value means that it should not be evaluated in terms of the economic market place of individual consumers but in the political sphere of citizens’ aspirations and the collective decisions of representative democratic institutions. Citizens expect efficiency in respect of public expenditure, so this is not to imply that economic evaluation is inappropriate; rather, it points to the need for complementary measures. These, Moore argues, must relate to citizens’ desires and perceptions.

In mapping the territory of what it is that clients and citizens value in respect of government and public services, Kelly et al. (2002) identify three categories: outcomes, services and trust. These are recognised as being distinct but related, although the authors do not fully articulate how this works and focus on the way in which factors may be mutually reinforcing or in tension. We briefly consider each category in turn.

Outcomes Examples of direct service-related outcomes might include high employment, low crime rates, low recidivism following criminal conviction, clean streets and so on. More generic outcomes might include community well-being, environmental and economic sustainability. Outcomes are distinguished from service targets in that they are more broadly defined so as to be less prone to distortion or contrivance in respect of their attainment. Outcomes are experienced both by individual clients and by citizens as a body, many of whom may never interact personally with the provider of a public service itself.

Services Services may be identified as meeting a relatively enduring need, for example, provision of education, healthcare or policing. Jointly or severally, services contribute to the achievement of outcomes. Again, it is helpful to distinguish between experience of clients and citizens. Clients have a direct and live engagement with services; citizens, in general, do not, but have endorsed the provision of the service for a ‘compelling collective purpose’.

Trust Within any community, individuals relate to a wide range of groups and institutions, such as family, cultural and political associations, public service providers, formal democratic and legislative fora. Relations with these institutions operate on the basis of some level of trust. Kelly et al. (2002) view trust largely in terms of the legitimacy it confers upon government, but others suggest that trust makes possible the achievement of community objectives that would not be attainable in its absence (Coleman, 1990; Fukuyama, 1995). Community trust relations are an expression (possibly the principal expression) of a community’s capacity to achieve a better quality of life than would otherwise be available if its members acted merely as individuals (Lin, 2001; Warren, 2001). Trust is positively associated with community sustainability, including economic, social and psychological well-being (Grootaert, 1998; Green et al., 2005; ODPM, 2005). Viewed in these terms, production and propagation of trust is a sine qua non in respect of public value.

In one respect, trust might be judged as another example of an outcome. However, other outcomes we have considered have a direct service-function origin (e.g., schools teach pupils and hospitals treat patients) whereas trust in government and public services appears more rooted in the nature and quality of citizens’ collective relationship with and experience of the provider. Trust does not always depend upon quality of outcome. For example, in the case of public housing allocation, very many clients are unlikely to secure their preferred outcome; similar situations arise in relation to care of people who are terminally ill or in policing and criminal justice as experienced by felons.

At this stage of analysis, the elements of public value (outcomes, services and trust), and clients’ and citizens’ relations to them are captured in Figure 1, which represents the initial conceptual framework $\mathrm { ( C F _ { 1 } ) }$ . Clients experience both an enduring relationship with a provider and also the outcomes of the service as they relate to them as individuals, and as they relate to family, friends and the community as a whole in respect of Moore’s ‘normatively compelling collective purposes’.

## Refining the initial framework

The value of CF<sub>1</sub> is that it is very general, applicable in a wide number of contexts. However, this is also its principal limitation: its elements, especially trust, are ill-defined and contain little information to help in the design of e-Government systems mediating peoples’ experiences. To support evaluation-led design in different e-Government domains, we need measurable constructs that are general enough to be independent of a particular service context but specific enough to support the realisation of goals such as client satisfaction and trust. We need constructs and measures of intermediate resolution, compared to those of CF<sub>1</sub>.

In the sphere of public value, as elsewhere, measures may be quantitative or qualitative, and each of these may be objective or subjective. The predominance, even the desirability, of quantitative measures is questioned by Moore (1995). And Kelly et al. (2002) point out that, in the U.K. at least, the quantitative measures and targets of the New Public Management (NPM) of the 1980s and 1990s often failed to address the key intangible issues of public service quality. NPM was, they say, ‘premised on the applicability of management techniques across both public and private sectors’ and that government value would be created ‘by mimicking organisational and financial systems used by business’ (p. 9). The result, they assert, was an inappropriate emphasis on narrow concepts of cost-efficiency and a downplaying of nonfunctional objectives that were difficult to measure (Kelly et al., 2002, p. 9). Moore (1995) echoes this view, suggesting that simplistic targets lend themselves to manipulation and contrivance in their attainment.

The most common and natural subjective measure of service experience and service outcome is satisfaction. Interestingly, there is evidence that people distinguish between satisfaction and dissatisfaction (Donovan et al., 2001; MORI, 2001). Satisfaction appears to be driven by service attributes such as a sense of attentiveness, friendliness, commitment, aesthetics and security. On the other hand, dissatisfaction is driven by service attributes such as integrity, functionality, flexibility and responsiveness. Some attributes span the two, such as responsiveness and care.

Trust, too, is measured subjectively. In a large-scale longitudinal study, Grimsley et al. (2003, 2004a) have demonstrated a positive correlation between satisfaction with public services and trust in public services. Further, they establish that both trust and satisfaction are driven, in part, by three distinct measures of citizen/client experience: a sense of being well-informed, a sense of personal control in one’s life and a sense of influence. The motivation of these measures of experience was that:

\- People need to feel well-informed about a public service, what to expect of it, and how to engage with it. Obtaining information that might otherwise be available has an associated opportunity cost.

\- People need to feel that engagement with the service fits in with the way they juggle the diverse demands made upon their time by work, family care and social life. Public services that are inflexible or unresponsive are also ‘opportunity costly’ and diminish peoples’ sense of personal control in life.

\- People need a sense of influence in relation to any institution of public service. Influence is expressed through discussion and negotiation in which there is a sense of each party acknowledging the needs of the other. Sometimes, influence may be experienced by the service adapting to individual needs, for example, disability. But it can be equally experienced when people receive an explanation, understandable and acceptable to them, as to why a particular event or outcome (positive or negative) has arisen.

Note: the distinction between personal control and influence is that the former relates to the clients choosing ‘what to do’ and ‘when to do it’, whist the latter relates to the psychological need for responsiveness from the other party. In this sense, influence is ‘contingency’, in which an action in the world leads to a more or less anticipated response. Denial of contingency is known to induce mental illness (Skinner, 1996 ).

The link between these three measures of experience and the production of both trust and satisfaction is evident when we observe that the service attributes associated with satisfaction (attentiveness, friendliness, commitment and aesthetics, as considered above) tend towards the informational – as mediated via personal interaction, whereas the service attributes associated with dissatisfaction (integrity, functionality and flexibility) tend to impact on a sense of personal control and a sense of influence (see MORI, 2001, p. 18). This analysis suggests that the three experiential dimensions (wellinformedness, personal control and influence) may warrant focal interest when undertaking evaluation-led design of e-Government systems as they give designers and managers a more tangible sense of the underlying experiences citizens are seeking.

We can now represent this elaborated conceptual framework (CF<sub>2</sub>) in Figure 2, which features four ‘pathways’ in the production of trust and satisfaction in clients and citizens:

A – effect of service provision on well-informedness, personal control and influence,

B – effect of experience of outcomes on well-informedness, personal control and influence,

C –the promotional effect of well-informedness, personal control and influence on satisfaction and

D –the promotional effect of well-informedness, personal control and influence on trust.

At this point Figure 2 is read as a hypothesis, resting upon the empirical research described in the literature we have considered. Accordingly, we needed to validate the use of well-informedness, personal control and influence as usable evaluative measures of satisfaction and trust in an e-Government context.

## Preliminary validation of elements of ${ \mathsf { C F } } _ { 2 }$

We analysed primary records from five focus groups convened to evaluate clients’ experiences of HC. The focus groups were conducted by experienced research staff from the Chief Executive’s Department of the London Borough of Camden. From the primary texts, we identified 101 recorded statements from clients that are related to satisfaction or to issues of trust. Inevitably, some client statements were in effect identical, and so the 101 individual responses were reduced to 39 distinct statement types. These were then coded according to whether they related to one or more of the factors: wellinformedness, personal control or influence (as defined earlier). Many statements were related to more than one dimension, though rarely all three. The degree to which this coding seemed quite natural, is, itself, indicative of the applicability of the three experiential dimensions as evaluative measures when examining expressions of stakeholder views in preference, say, to trust itself. \- Clients were evaluating the process as a whole, making a clear distinction between the ICT mediation of their relation with the council and the council function itself.

\- Threats to trust appeared to arise when there is a dislocation between the ‘implied promise’ of HC as an ICT system and the conduct of the ‘back office’ processes. This would explain why so many comments related to issues of information and/or lack of influence (in moving the process through).

\- The relative absence of issues with negative effects (  ) on personal control in Table 1 would support the observation that the ICT element of HC has appreciably enhanced the flexibility people enjoy in engaging with the housing process, and thus increased their sense of control in their personal lives.

In considering issues related to the role of information in clients’ views of HC, it was possible to discern three related but nonetheless distinct informational problems, suggesting that, for purposes of evaluative design, each may be usefully considered in turn. The first concerns situations where there is a perceived lack of initial information; the second is when there is seemingly contradictory information (most often between the website and the statements of officials) and the the third is the absence or significant delay of feedback information when a client triggers some ‘event’ in the overall process – appropriate feedback reinforces a sense of influence/contingency.

In respect of client satisfaction and trust as considered here, the high-level lesson might well be summarised as ‘satisfaction and trust may be jeopardised when the expectations of being better informed and having more influence are not met; enhancing a sense of personal control may promote satisfaction and trust’.

## Incorporating antecedents of trust and satisfaction into the evaluative design framework (CF<sub>3</sub>)

The final element of our framework seeks to draw upon the results obtained above to help inform the design of ICT systems mediating experience of public services and their outcomes so that they promote satisfaction and trust. We do this by incorporating the three experiential dimensions into an instrument for use by evaluation-led designers of citizen and client-facing ICT systems. Taken with CF , this elaboration comprises CF . This element of the framework, described below, is an Experience Management Matrix (Grimsley et al., 2004b). It supports each party in a trust-based relation in determining its own participative strategy.

The Experience Management Matrix reflects the interrelation of the three experiential factors (well-informedness, personal control and influence) contributing to trust and satisfaction in the relationship between a public service provider and clients/citizens. If we consider ourselves in the role of a manager of an e-Government system that mediates a client’s interaction with our public service, we may formulate three related trustmanagement strategies: an Information Strategy, a Control Strategy and an Influence Strategy. Each strategy must address the desired experiential outcomes for the citizen/client. For example, the Information Strategy must consider three questions: how to induce a sense of well-informedness; what information is needed to facilitate exercise of enhanced personal control and what information is needed to convey a sense of influence. Three similar questions arise in respect of the Control Strategy and three more for the Influence Strategy. Figure 3 presents an Experience Management Matrix featuring a set of nine design-related questions that are oriented to trust promotion, and which can be used within the evaluative design of e-Government systems delivering public services.

We briefly illustrate consideration of these questions in relation to the case study systems we have described. Both HC and TCH feature key aspects of a successful Information Strategy. A high, indeed a largely unprecedented, degree of openness about the housing available and the criteria for allocation appreciably reinforced wellinformedness. In respect of providing information to enhance a sense of personal control or autonomy, the approaches integrated the ICT communications channels through which clients were able to initiate system events relating to their needs and actions at times that suited their personal circumstances. In respect of promoting a sense of influence, feedback mechanisms were planned to reassure clients that the system was aware of their most recent actions. (In the initial stages of the HC deployment, problems with delivering this feedback in a timely and/or consistent fashion threatened the trust relation.) In terms of communication of information across all experiential dimensions, ensuring that information from all sources is consistent was found to be important.

In respect of the control strategy, the first question is how could control be used to promote well-informedness? The key lesson from both case studies was the need to be proactive in initiating communication. The second question introduces a major design task as it concerns the distribution of control (initiative) between the parties across all ‘subtasks’ in the overall process. There is no one optimal solution to this: different clients will need, or just prefer, one distribution of responsibilities over another. The third question concerns the issue of reinforcing a sense of influence by being responsive to actions by the other party. Particular problems arise if each party assumes that the initiative lies with the other in terms of the next process event. (We point to the extensive literature on design of the so-called mixed initiative systems for designers unfamiliar with these issues.)

Finally, we consider Influence Strategy. In respect of promoting well-informedness, there needs to be information that legitimates the experience the other party has of current policy and practice. Shortly after an initial deployment of HC, it became clear that users were unhappy when they did not understand the responses of the system. In particular, problems arose when officials published system responses that suggested (though it was untrue) that there was a hidden set of ‘unofficial’ rules being applied to influence the allocation outcome in a non-transparent way. CBL presents a particularly challenging problem in relation to the use of influence to manage clients’ expectations without inducing a sense of loss of control or autonomy. The reality of housing allocations in central London is that, for most applicants, the outcome will lie not in obtaining public social housing in their area of choice, but rather in moving into the private sector or to a different area of the capital or even a different region of the U.K. The problem here is to influence the clients’ perception of the solution space without this being experienced as a diminution of control/autonomy. HC and TCH both strive for this by taking a more holistic view of clients needs. While effectively constraining the scope for the desired solution, they open up the possibility of alternative solutions that addressed the need for employment, family education, family healthcare, and so on. By proactively directing clients to complementary sources of advice and guidance in relation to these options, the organisation was addressing the final aspect of an Influence Strategy that concerns the style of ‘negotiation’ to be employed. Modern negotiation theory tells us that, in contrast to traditional zero-sum strategies, coordinative and integrative styles in which the principal goals of the other party are at least acknowledged and even accommodated, induce stable relations based upon a long-term trust between parties (Pruitt, 1981).

## Some specific lessons learned and concluding remarks

## Some specific lessons learned

The structured inquiry that produced the framework also provided some generic ‘lessons to be learned’ and these may be summarised. In no particular order, we draw out the following issues and lessons:

\- Evaluative design should apply to the whole system as the client/citizen perceives it and not just the ICT platform – a socio-technical perspective.

\- The experiential dimensions of well-informedness, personal control and influence are more tangible than satisfaction and trust, and their use points to design actions. They also facilitate an open style of evaluative discussion.

\- In respect of promoting a sense of being well-informed, there are three informational issues to focus on: initial availability, consistency and timely feedback information.

\- In terms of promoting a sense of personal control, attempts should be made to introduce multiple ways of achieving the same end (and provide rapid and prominent feedback in relation to any ‘event’ that affects the client).

\- In terms of promoting a sense of influence, again, timely feedback is valuable. Many clients sought some/ any expression of personalisation.

\- It is possible to maintain trust even if the desired outcome is very difficult to attain or is even unattainable by taking a holistic view of clients’ needs and proactively supporting the client in addressing their wider needs.

\- Introduction of e-Government raises client and citizen expectations and it is these raised expectations that must be met. This is particularly challenging for developers and managers of e-Government systems as the introduction of the system itself raises the benchmark by which it will be judged trustworthy. As the HC Development Manager commented: ‘it is like walking on eggshells’.

\- One response to the challenge is to use the framework elaborated here to guide the process of obtaining stakeholder feedback at all stages of the design process. This can then be used to revise aspects of the service, as has been done by both of the projects considered here. Figure 3 supports this activity. The feedback could be explicitly represented by making a number of pathways in $\mathrm { C F } _ { 2 }$ bi-directional.

As we look forward to being able to articulate a theoretical basis for the relationship between the three experiential dimensions underpinning trust and satisfaction, currently we interpret them as ecologically rational heuristics (Gigerenzer & Todd, 1999). Ecological rationality is a form of bounded rationality where the existence of the bound is managed by the application of simple decisionmaking heuristics. In choosing actions in the world, actions that do not surrender future personal control or autonomy, that move towards future states in which influence is not diminished and which maximise the information available are seen to confer advantage – even survival. The underpinning of trust by these decision heuristics suggests that trust may be an emergent phenomenon of ecologically rational action. For IS supporting persons on the edge of homelessness and other critical life states, building and maintaining trust is essential in avoiding social exclusion and alienation (see Duffy et al., 2003).

## Concluding remarks

This paper has given an account of an attempt to develop a framework for evaluation-led design of e-Government projects with a particular focus on the concept of public value as concerning outcomes, services, satisfaction and trust. The initial inquiry was grounded in an e-Government project that sought to introduce transparency and client engagement into the process of meeting their housing needs. The elements of the conceptual framework that emerged in this first study articulate the relationship between outcomes, services and trust in terms of three experiential dimensions: a sense of being well-informed, a sense of personal control in one’s life and a sense of influence, which appear to drive trust and satisfaction. It demonstrated that these dimensions could be used to analyse evaluative comments from stakeholders in a way that pointed to issues of trust and satisfaction.

This framework, read as an hypothesis, was validated in an empirical study in a second, similar, e-Government context.

Finally, we introduced an Experience Management Matrix as a ‘tool’ that supports the consideration of how an e-Government system may be designed to promote trust (and satisfaction) by developing information, control and influence (and negotiation) strategies that promote a client’s sense of well-informedness, personal control and ability to influence.

In seeking a generic framework, we have sought to avoid introducing any elements that were specific to our chosen domain, CBL schemes. The ICT ‘architecture’ of CBL – the technology used (Internet, mobile and auto telephony, locally available print media) and the clients’ interface to traditional human enquiry services when needed, is common to many other e-Government services. Hence, the framework as represented by Figures 2 and 3, is designed to correspond to a wide range of ICTmediated public services.
