# USING ORGANISMIC INTEGRATION THEORY TO EXPLORE THE ASSOCIATIONS BETWEEN USERS’ EXERCISE MOTIVATIONS AND FITNESS TECHNOLOGY FEATURE SET USE<sup>1</sup>

Tabitha L. James, Linda Wallace, and Jason K. Deane Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, 1007 Pamplin Hall, Blacksburg, VA 24061 U.S.A. {tajames@vt.edu} {wallacel@vt.edu} {jdeane1@vt.edu}

Wearable devices and applications (apps) that offer a variety of features intended to support exercisers have flooded the marketplace. Organismic integration theory (OIT) proposes that motivations to exercise can vary along a spectrum of self-determination. To best serve exercisers and assist organizations that are developing and promoting fitness technologies, we need a better understanding of how individuals’ exercise motivations influence their fitness technology feature set use. We also need to determine the impact of fitness technology features on enhancing or undermining wellness outcomes—such as subjective vitality. Our results suggest that almost every subtype of exerciser, where the subtype is defined by OIT motivations toward exercise, has a unique use profile. Our findings also suggest that the social interaction and data management features of current fitness technologies show promise in assisting well-being outcomes, but only for the more selfdetermined and amotivated subtypes of exercisers. This leads us to suggest that providing every type of exerciser the motivational support that best fits their motivational profile may not be a trivial task, but it ultimately may be necessary for fitness technologies to be universally useful in supporting wellness outcomes.

Keywords: Fitness technology, wearables, health information technology, self-determination theory, organismic integration theory, intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation

## Introduction

Wearable devices and applications (apps) (e.g., Fitbit, MapMyFitness) that are designed to support fitness are inundating the technology marketplace. These fitness technologies provide features that allow users to perform activities such as compiling, analyzing, and monitoring performance data; searching for fitness support information; obtaining reminders and rewards; managing goals; gaining access to coaching; and integrating social sharing, comparison, encouragement, and competition. Fitness technologies are one of the most popular examples of technologies supporting the “quantified self” or “personal informatics” (Epstein,Kang et al. 2016; Patel et al. 2015; Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017), and they aspire to ultimately improve wellbeing by providing functions that support a user’s motivation to exercise. Fitness technologies generated approximately 238 million dollars in revenue during 2013 (Economist 2014), and projections are for revenues to reach 2.8 billion dollars by 2019 (Dolan 2014). Onetime leaders in the fitness tracker market, such as Fitbit, Nike, and Jawbone (Economist 2014), have seen increasing competition from a host of companies with new product entries.

Fitness technologies are novel and emerging, with analysts suggesting they are still in the diffusion phase and that the next phase of their development will be determining how to best use collected data to lead to better wellness (IDC 2017). Furthermore, one early report suggested that fitness technologies were being targeted at “fitness fanatics” (Economist 2014). These statements not only suggest different potential uses of fitness technologies but also the possibility they may appeal differently to different types of exercisers, which could ultimately have ramifications for both sales and wellness outcomes. If individuals with different motivations toward exercise do use fitness technologies differently, such insight could be instrumental in determining how best to design and deploy future generations of such technologies to obtain better wellness outcomes.

While fitness technologies are becoming increasingly popular, little is known about how individuals use them and how that use may contribute to well-being outcomes. Motivation theory, specifically self-determination theory (SDT), tells us that individuals have different motivations toward exercise. What has not been determined is whether individuals with different exercise motivations use fitness technologies differently. Hence, our first research question is: How do individuals’ exercise motivations influence their fitness technology feature set use? The ultimate goal in employing fitness technologies is to improve the well-being of the user. Information regarding the impact of fitness technology feature set use on enhancing or undermining wellness outcomes—such as subjective vitality—is also limited. Thus, our second research question is: Does the use of fitness technology feature sets moderate the relationships between users’ motivations toward exercise and subjective vitality?

Our study draws from SDT (Deci and Ryan 2012; Ryan and Deci 2000), which offers a high-level picture of human motivation and states that there are three primary states of motivation to perform an activity (e.g., exercise): amotivation, extrinsic motivation, and intrinsic motivation. An intrinsically motivated individual performs the activity for the pure enjoyment of it, an extrinsically motivated individual responds to external pressure, and an amotivated individual lacks any motivation. Organismic integration theory (OIT) is a sub-theory of SDT that delves into the subtypes of extrinsic motivation, suggesting that some forms of extrinsic motivation can be more self-determined than others. Each subtype of extrinsic motivation presented in OIT is distinguished by the degree to which the desire to perform the activity for the individual’s own sake is internalized.

SDT also proposes that there are characteristics of the environment or social context in which the individual is performing the activity that can be supportive (or unsupportive) of the individual’s basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness that are crucial to intrinsic motivation. Specifically,

The concept of psychological needs provides the basis for describing characteristics of the environment that support versus undermine the organism’s attempts to master or engage each new situation. To the extent that an aspect of the social context allows need fulfillment, it yields engagement, mastery, and synthesis; whereas, to the extent that it thwarts need fulfillment, it diminishes the individual’s motivation, growth, integrity, and well-being (Ryan and Deci 2002, pp. 8-9).

Hence, environmental or social factors can support or thwart an individual’s basic psychological needs and thereby enhance or diminish the impact of the individual’s motivation on their activity outcomes (e.g., well-being, mastery of an activity). Stated more precisely, individuals have particular motivations to perform an activity that may lead to different activity outcomes, and environmental factors are variables that are inserted into the environment in which the activities are being performed that may influence that relationship. In the case of fitness technologies, the hope is that their features are environmental factors that will support better activity outcomes for all subtypes of exercisers.

Much of the early work in SDT focused on rewards as one such environmental factor and found that rewards given to individuals for performing an activity thwarted their need for autonomy, undermining their intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999; Deci and Ryan 2012). Another environmental factor, positive feedback, has been suggested to support individuals need for competence and thus foster or enhance intrinsic motivation for an activity and improve activity outcomes (Deci et al. 1999; Deci and Ryan 2012). In our study, we suggest that the fitness technologies provide a selection of environmental factors (e.g., rewards, reminders, directives, feedback, social encouragement) from which exercisers can choose to help support their exercise. We will refer to these features as environmental motivational supports because they are intended by the fitness technology makers to be environmental factors to support exercise and thereby to help improve exercisers’ wellbeing.

The previous discussion of SDT and environmental motivational supports illustrates a unique aspect of our fitness technology context. SDT suggests that exercisers may have different types of motivation toward exercise, but unlike many contexts in which SDT has been studied, our exercisers with varying motivations can choose their own selection of environmental motivational supports. Contrast this scenario with a classroom context within which a student is performing an assignment (i.e., the activity), and the teacher decides if environmental motivational supports, such as rewards, threats, or reminders, are a part of the student’s environment. Hence, a unique aspect of our context is that the exercisers get to decide which features of fitness technologies they will use to support their exercise.

Drawing from work in information systems (IS) on affordances, Leonardi (2013) argues that people may exhibit different patterns of use of the same technology. His argumentation builds on work by DeSanctis and Poole (1994) that examines the use of technology at the feature set level and finds variation in feature use among different people. We suggest that this is true for different subtypes of exercisers, where the exerciser subtype is defined by OIT motivations toward exercise. For example, users that are intrinsically motivated to exercise may especially prefer environmental motivational supports that promote competence and allow them to gauge their exercise performance. To intrinsically motivated users, support that attempts to control their activity may be viewed as annoying, unhelpful, or unnecessary and may diminish their enjoyment. However, users that are extrinsically motivated to exercise may be looking for environmental motivational supports that add additional layers of control to help sustain their exercise because they do not enjoy it and are responding to external pressure.

Based upon this reasoning, our study examines which subtypes of exercisers use which environmental motivational supports, when given the choice. In answering our first research question, we gain an improved understanding of the relationship between users’ motivations to exercise and their use of the feature sets offered by leading fitness technologies. Such insight can help fitness technology makers understand how to develop their technologies or customize usage suggestions for exercisers with different motivations toward exercise. Prior research on exercise motivation has primarily looked at the extent to which motivations predict exercise behavior (for a review of such research, see Teixeira, Carraça et al. 2012). Our study is unique in that it examines the influence of motivations to exercise on the way fitness technologies are used.

Many entities (e.g., parents, physicians, fitness technology makers, the exercisers themselves) would ultimately like to see fitness technologies provide environmental motivational supports that enhance well-being. One measure of well-being that is commonly employed in SDT studies is subjective vitality, which is defined as “a positive feeling of aliveness and energy” (Ryan and Frederick 1997, p. 529). Subjective vitality “results from satisfaction of the basic psychological needs, is an important indicator of health, and provides the necessary energy for effective self-regulation and coping with challenges” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 427). Following SDT, the more self-determined motivations toward exercise should lead to higher subjective vitality, and the environmental motivational supports may moderate this relationship. That is, the environmental motivational supports may enhance or diminish the relationships between the different motivations and subjective vitality. Thus, our second research question examines the effect of the fitness technology features on enhancing or undermining this wellness outcome for different subtypes of exercisers. Exploring this question provides insight into which feature sets may successfully support exercisers’ wellness outcomes—specifically, subjective vitality. These findings are helpful because they provide a partial indication as to why fitness technologies may be effective for some exercisers and not for others as well as which fitness technology features may be most useful for different types of exercisers.

## Model Development

Our research is grounded in SDT, which is a meta-theory of human motivation that has been an active area of research for several decades and has been separated into several subtheories, including OIT. We provide a brief overview of SDT and the key sub-theories below.

## Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

At a high level, SDT distinguishes between autonomous (i.e., self-determined) and controlled (i.e., non–self-determined) motivation (Ryan and Deci 2000). Intrinsic motivation, where an individual performs an activity for the pure enjoyment or satisfaction derived from its performance, is autonomous or self-determined. Alternatively, extrinsic motivation, where an individual performs an activity due to perceived external pressure to do so and in order to attain some separate outcome (e.g., praise), is controlled or non–self-determined.

OIT is a sub-theory of SDT that resulted from consideration of more autonomous forms of extrinsic motivation and led to a “differentiation of the varied types of internalized extrinsic motivations” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 421). Similar to intrinsic motivation, Deci and Ryan (2012) suggest that supporting the basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness are important to facilitating internalized extrinsic motivations, whereas thwarting the basic psychological needs can impair these types of motivations.

Early work on SDT focused on intrinsic motivation and considered it to be “an inherent characteristic of human beings” that “could be either undermined or enhanced depending on whether the social environment supported or thwarted the needs for competence and self-determination” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 417). As studies of intrinsic motivation attempted to explain “increasingly complex experimental phenomena” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 418), it became necessary to theorize how factors in the social environment contributed to the satisfaction of the basic psychological needs and consequently shaped motivational outcomes. Hence, the SDT sub-theory called the cognitive evaluation theory (CET) was proposed “to explain the effects of extrinsic factors on intrinsic motivation” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 418).

The previous discussion illustrates that central to work on intrinsic and extrinsic motivations is the idea that to enable the preferred (i.e., more self-determined) types of motivation that lead to better well-being, the social environment must support individuals’ basic psychological needs. SDT eventually proposed three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Autonomy “refers to being the perceived origin or source of one’s own behavior” (Ryan and Deci 2002, p. 8). Competence “refers to feeling effective in one’s ongoing interactions with the social environment and experiencing opportunities to exercise and express one’s capacities” (Ryan and Deci 2002, p. 7). Relatedness is defined as “feeling connected to others, to caring for and being cared for by those others, to having a sense of belongingness both with other individuals and with one’s community” (Ryan and Deci 2002, p. 7). Another sub-theory of SDT, the basic psychological needs theory, focuses on how satisfactions of the basic psychological needs are associated with well-being outcomes (Deci and Ryan 2012).

## Organismic Integration Theory (OIT)

While early SDT research concentrated on intrinsic motivation, the OIT sub-theory was developed to explore the idea of autonomous extrinsic motivation (Deci and Ryan 2012). OIT resulted in the identification of four types of extrinsic motivation (see Figure 1), which are differentiated by the extent to which they are internalized (Deci and Ryan 2012). To explore the idea of internalization, it is useful to look at the two extremes. On one end of the spectrum, an individual’s behavior can be completely externally regulated; the individual is simply complying with a demand and thus fully controlled, without any interest in the activity itself or an internal drive to partake in it. On the other end, an individual can be performing the activity simply for the intrinsic satisfaction that results. This distinguishes pure extrinsic motivation (the former) from pure intrinsic motivation (the latter). OIT proposes that these extremes do not paint the entire picture and that there are actually levels of extrinsic motivation, where the stimulus is extrinsic but the individual starts to want to perform the activity to some degree (i.e., internalizes the behavior) rather than simply complying with the outside force.

## Fitness Technology Feature Sets

OIT provides the foundation to classify exercisers based upon their motivation toward exercise. Fitness technologies provide features that exercisers can choose to insert into their environment to support their exercise. Our context is novel because the environment is not a static feature in which the activity is taking place or a variable being manipulated by the researchers (Wilson et al. 2008); rather, the exercisers can customize the environment in which their exercise takes place. Following the logic of the theory of affordances, we argue that different OIT subtypes of exercisers will choose to use different fitness technology feature sets.

## Theory of Affordances

We explore fitness technology use at a “feature level of analysis” and view fitness technologies as “a collection of specific feature sets” (Jasperson et al. 2005, p. 529) that provide environmental motivational support. Following previous literature, we suggest that fitness technology features can provide multiple utilities that individuals with different exercise motivations can enact (Leonardi 2013). In his work on affordances, Leonardi (2013) argues that people may exhibit different patterns of use of the same technology. In addition, Leonardi incorporates the theory of affordances credited to the ecological psychologist Gibson, which proposes that “we perceive in order to operate on the environment, [that] perception is designed for action, [and] called the perceivable possibilities for action affordances” (Ware 2013, p. 18). Leonardi (p. 751) describes the concept of affordances as “objects have properties (or features, in the context of IT use) and animals that make use of objects have their own physical characteristics and a host of needs” and suggests that “IS researchers who adopt a relational view of affordances stress that people’s goals shape what they come to view the features of the technology as affording them the ability to do.” Based on SDT and the theory of affordances, we propose that users’ specific exercise motivations (i.e., characteristics) will shape which of the feature sets of fitness technologies they ultimately use.

Following DeSanctis and Poole (1994) and Poole and DeSanctis (1990), many researchers have examined technology use at the feature set level instead of the artifact level (e.g., Jasperson et al. 2005; Leonardi 2013; Maruping and Magni 2015). Researchers have focused on the range of system features that an individual will use when they add technology to a task or take into account when evaluating a technology’s usability (Hoehle and Venkatesh 2015). Focusing on features rather than the technology as a whole is essential for understanding how technology can contribute to the behavioral outcomes of use (Markus and Silver 2008), and researchers (Hoehle and Venkatesh 2015; Hong et al. 2013; Venkatesh et al. 2012) have argued that being considerate of contextual differences (e.g., differences in the form and function of mobile, web, and PC applications) is important because there is “a general tendency to seek causal explanations at lower rather than higher levels of analysis, a tactic referred to unflatteringly as explanatory reductionism” (Johns 2006, p. 403). Our exploration of the use of fitness technologies at the feature set level is further justified by these arguments for contextualized consideration of features that may provide more granular explorations of the use of technologies that are highly customizable and differ from each other in function and form.

## Categorizing Fitness Technology Features

Fitness technologies typically combine the use of some type of hardware (e.g., a wearable bracelet, token, or mobile phone) with the use of software (e.g., an app provided with the wearable, third-party apps that can be paired with a device the exerciser owns, or both). Not only can a user choose between multiple form factors for the hardware, but they can also pair the hardware with multiple software options, and these software options may cooperate. Because the wearables can be paired with multiple apps, exercisers have a great deal of flexibility in customizing their environment.

We grouped the fitness technology features into three feature sets,<sup>2</sup> as shown in Figure 2.

The first feature set, social interaction features (SIFs), is made up of features that allow the exerciser to interact with other people. The SIFs enable social interaction for the purposes of data sharing, encouragement, competition, comparison, and coaching. The second feature set, exercise control features (ECFs), consists of features that are intended to control the users’ exercise. ECFs offer rewards and reminders that prompt a user to exercise, and goal management allows the user to create and manage the goals (i.e., rule set) the fitness technology uses to determine when the rewards and reminders are triggered. The third feature set, data management features (DMFs), collects and manages the exerciser’s data. The DMFs collect, analyze, and feed data from the device/app to the users regarding their exercise activity as well as provide data from other sources (e.g., maps of running and walking routes). The feature sets were developed to be generic representations of environmental motivational support categories available to exercisers by pairing a wearable, a mobile phone, or both with one or more apps.

## Exercise Motivations and Fitness Technology Feature Set Selection

There have been indications in the popular media that different types of exercisers may perceive the utility of or use fitness technologies differently. For example, it has been suggested that the fitness technology companies are targeting “fitness fanatics” (individuals that are intrinsically motivated toward exercise) (Economist 2014), implying that the fitness technology makers think the technologies may be more attractive to the intrinsically motivated exerciser.

Ultimately, the selection of features implemented as environmental motivational supports is up to the exerciser. CET describes how environmental motivational supports relate to the psychological needs of individuals. This line of research suggests that the environmental conditions can be manipulated to support someone’s motivation toward an activity either positively or negatively (where positive support refers to environmental conditions conducive to self-determined motivations for an activity through the nourishment of the individual’s basic psychological needs) (Wilson et al. 2008). What fitness technologies are attempting to do, in simplified terms, is to manipulate the environmental conditions for exercisers in all motivational subtypes. OIT provides a categorization of motivations based upon how self-determined—or, conversely, how externally regulated—the performance of the activity is.

“Externally regulated behaviors are dependent on the continuous presence of the controls” (Ryan and Deci 2006, pp. 1569-1570), suggesting that less self-determined exercisers may see utility in fitness technology features that are more controlling because these add continuously present controls to their environments. SDT suggests that more self-determined exercisers should demonstrate a preference for environmental motivational supports that are supportive of the basic psychological needs. SDT has also explored the relationships between the exercise motivations proposed in OIT and subjective vitality, which is a commonly employed measure of well-being. In what follows, we expand this discussion to introduce our hypotheses and research models.

## Exercise Motivations and Social Interaction Features (SIFs)

Fitness technologies have features that incorporate other people into exercisers’ fitness regimes, such as fitness data sharing, encouragement, competition, comparison, and coaching. A straightforward description of the SIFs would be that they facilitate external social pressure, which should transfer the locus of control to outside the person and reduce autonomy. However, the use of these features facilitates a social connection between a user and their exercise community. That community could include both people who are trying to pressure the user to exercise (e.g., a parent watching how many steps their child takes) and people the exerciser is socializing with for entertainment (e.g., sharing data to take part in a challenge with friends). In the former case, the fitness technology facilitates external social pressure. In the latter case, the fitness technology facilitates socialization that could satisfy the need for relatedness (i.e., encouraging feelings of belongingness or connectedness with the individual’s exercise group). In fact, socially motivated exercise goals have been harder to classify in the exercise SDT literature (Ryan et al. 1997). Previous literature has suggested that social motives for exercise were extrinsic to exercise but that the social interaction may lead to increased enjoyment (Ryan et al. 1997) which may enhance intrinsic motivation. We suggest that the social features of fitness technologies may be simultaneously supporting exercise and socialization, and in doing so, they are at least partially supporting the exerciser’s relatedness need.

Hence, the SIFs can be supportive of the relatedness need of exercisers as well as facilitate the connection between the exerciser and extrinsic social pressures that may reduce autonomy. Including a social aspect to exercise is not a new concept, but technology-mediated social support for exercise is somewhat novel. Prior research has provided some support for the idea that exercising with another person can lead to increased enjoyment and psychological functioning (Plante et al. 2003; Plante et al. 2011). In addition, previous work has found that social motives for exercise may increase enjoyment (Ryan et al. 1997; Wankel 1993), which enhances intrinsic motivation and can lead to better adherence to the exercise program (Spink and Carron 1992); however, the social support features offered by the current generation of fitness technologies are somewhat asynchronous. That is, it is may be before or after exercise that the user can share data, obtain coaching, set up a competition, compare statistics, or receive feedback or acknowledgment from another person. For example, a user can go for a run with the fitness technology and then post the length and path of that run to Facebook through the app. While the technology enables relatedness with an exercise group before or after exercise, the features may not truly support the completion of a simultaneous exercise act. However, it has been suggested that the social features of fitness technologies can serve as successful environmental motivational support (Kelly 2013).

In agreement with how social motives for exercise have been considered in previous literature, we consider the SIFs to be relatedness-supporting, because they may facilitate communication with a desired social group, but also autonomythwarting to some degree, because they may facilitate external pressure to exercise. This leads us to suggest that users who are amotivated or extrinsically motivated to exercise will be more likely to use the SIFs because the external social pressure may be viewed as an additional control on their exercise. However, we also argue that intrinsically motivated exercisers will be more likely to use this feature set because they may derive enjoyment from the social aspect (i.e., their relatedness need is being met to some extent).

H1a: Intrinsic regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

H1b: Integrated regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

H1c: Identified regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

H1d: Introjected regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

H1e: External regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

H1f: Nonregulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of SIFs.

## Exercise Motivations and Exercise Control Features (ECFs)

Research has long suggested that rewards decrease individuals’ autonomy (i.e., are controlling) and hence are ineffective means to motivate behavior because they do not support intrinsic motivation for an activity (Deci 1971; Deci et al. 1999). Research has also suggested that “threats, deadlines, directives, pressured evaluations, and imposed goals diminish intrinsic motivation because, like tangible rewards, they conduce toward an external perceived locus of control” (Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 70). The goal management and reminder features of fitness technologies are deadlines, directives, and imposed goals, and so, as with rewards, they transfer the locus of control to the fitness technology. The fitness technology is set up to control the user’s actions, the user feels pressured by it to complete the activity and reach the goal, and such control reduces the exerciser’s autonomy.

Reports imply that ECFs may be perceived as controlling by some users. For example, one user stated to a reporter that using a Fitbit-like device was akin to “having a tiny, overbearing mother always on your person” (Garber 2015, p. 1). Another fitness technology user stated

My walks were no longer delightful excursions to marvel at nature or delight in old back alleys. They were forced marches to accumulate steps. The fun of it all took a dramatic, stage-left exit and I was left alone to take ever-more steps (Garber 2015, p. 1).

In the latter example, the exerciser expresses enjoyment of the walks, and this intrinsically motivated exerciser perceives the ECFs as controlling and expresses disdain for them.

Intrinsically motivated exercisers enjoy the activity and are more likely to adhere to their exercise routines (Seghers et al. 2014; Wilson et al. 2008; Wilson et al. 2004) without seeing a need to use a fitness technology to remind or reward them to exercise. In fact, it has been suggested that the quantified aspects of fitness technologies as they are currently designed may take the fun out of exercising (Garber 2015) by undermining the exerciser’s intrinsic motivation (Etkin 2016). The environmental motivational supports that the ECFs mimic have been found to diminish intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci 2000), and so it is unlikely that users who are intrinsically motivated to exercise would be drawn to such features.

All extrinsic motivations rely at least partially on external pressures to motivate the exercise and are thus more likely to depend on the constant presence of controls for motivational support (Ryan and Deci 2006). We suggest that extrinsically motivated users will be more likely to use the ECFs because these features provide controlling environmental motivational supports similar to those in non-technological environments (e.g., rewards, reminders, goal setting). These features mimic or reinforce other external pressures (e.g., a parent reminding a child to exercise), and since non–self-determined users are turning outward for their motivation to exercise, fitness technologies are another instantiation of external pressure and fit their exercise motivational profile. For example, one user stated that the “Fitbit’s real secret is to make you more intentional about parts of your life you’d normally ignore” (John 2014). Any exercisers that are classified as integrated, identified, introjected, externally regulated, or amotivated are likely relying on the fitness technologies, which act as continuously present controls, for “pressure” to exercise. While the ECFs may not be the ideal feature set for externally regulated or amotivated individuals, these features are very likely to be the ones extrinsically motivated and amotivated exercisers are drawn toward.

H2a: Intrinsic regulations for exercise will be negatively associated with the use of ECFs.

H2b: Integrated regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of ECFs.

H2c: Identified regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of ECFs.

H2d: Introjected regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of ECFs.

H2e: External regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of ECFs.

H2f: Nonregulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of ECFs.

## Exercise Motivations and Data Management Features (DMFs)

SDT research has suggested that environmental motivational supports “that conduce toward feelings of competence during action can enhance intrinsic motivation for that action” and that “optimal challenges, effectance-promoting feedback, and freedom from demeaning evaluations were all found to facilitate intrinsic motivation” (Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 70). Research has confirmed (Deci et al. 1999) that a social environment that provides information that promotes competence for autonomous activity will meet the competence and autonomy needs of the individual to some extent, and this will positively support intrinsic motivation (Deci and Ryan 2012). Autonomy and competence-supportive environments have been shown to be important to exercise and wellness outcomes (Hsu et al. 2013; Moustaka et al. 2012; Teixeira, Silva et al. 2012), although studies manipulating the sociocontextual environment of exercisers have been limited and the results somewhat mixed (Wilson et al. 2008). Several fitness technology features support exercisers’ competence and autonomy needs; for example, fitness technologies provide effectance-promoting feedback through the collection and analysis of exercise data that helps users better understand their performance. Features of fitness technologies such as exercise progress updates or analysis of a user’s exercise data serve as feedback that promotes competence, and an information-searching feature provides the user with autonomy in planning, carrying out, and evaluating their exercise routines. Therefore, data analysis, data collection, progress updates, and information searching are all informational supports afforded by fitness technologies that are supportive of exercisers’ competence and autonomy needs.

What distinguishes the DMFs from the other feature sets is that they are not instructing users to do something. Rather, these features simply provide information about what users have already accomplished or allow them to look for information to support their exercise, which makes these similar in nature to effectance-promoting feedback in real life. Thus, the key difference between the DMFs and the other feature sets is that the fitness technologies are not providing or facilitating external pressure; instead, they are serving as an informational source that exercisers can use to gauge competence or to help make future exercise decisions. Therefore, the locus of control is still with the exerciser.

We would expect intrinsically regulated exercisers to be more likely to use the DMFs because these features will provide them with information to evaluate their competence (i.e., how well they are performing) and help them make decisions about what to do next (e.g., new trails to run, how much more or less exercise they would like to do in a week). Selfdetermined exercisers should prefer the DMFs because these support autonomy and competence and provide environmental motivational support conducive to intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci 2000).

While we would expect intrinsically motivated exercisers to be the most likely users of the DMFs, we also propose that individuals who are extrinsically motivated but fall on the more self-determined end of the scale (i.e., individuals with integrated or identified exercise regulations) will be more likely to use them. Exercisers with integrated regulations, while perhaps still exercising in part because of external pressures, feel that exercise or its outcomes are a part of their self-concept. Individuals with integrated regulations see value in the exercise itself and view exercise as personally important, and this is the closest of the extrinsic motivations to intrinsic motivation. Exercisers with identified regulations value the benefits of exercising even though they may be exercising because of external pressures, and so they have only partially integrated exercise into their self-concept. We argue that these more self-determined extrinsically motivated exercisers should also prefer the DMFs, which are supportive of autonomy and competence.

Moving through the spectrum in Figure 1 from the more selfdetermined exercise regulations (intrinsic, integrated, and identified) into the more non–self-determined exercise regulations (introjected and extrinsic) and finally into a state of nonmotivation (amotivation), we would expect individuals at the non–self-determined or amotivated end of the spectrum to be less likely to use the DMFs. Exercisers with introjected regulations are those who are exercising to avoid guilt or feelings of failure. They have integrated exercising into their selfconcept sufficiently to feel guilty for failing to do so, but they are still exercising because of external pressures. These exercisers would be most closely related to purely extrinsically motivated exercisers, who are exercising solely because of external pressure. Amotivated exercisers are not motivated to exercise. These individuals simply do not want to exercise or do not see the point of exercising. When the exerciser is not partaking for the pleasure of the activity, they are less likely to be interested in spending time considering the activity (e.g., analyzing their performance or searching for information on new exercise locations or methods). Similarly, amotivated exercisers are likely looking for something to help stimulate their exercise, and DMFs that require user interest or participation are not likely to be seen as providing that easy stimulus.

H3a: Intrinsic regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of DMFs.

H3b: Integrated regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of DMFs.

H3c: Identified regulations for exercise will be positively associated with the use of DMFs.

H3d: Introjected regulations for exercise will be negatively associated with the use of DMFs.

H3e: External regulations for exercise will be negatively associated with the use of DMFs.

H3f: Nonregulations for exercise will be negatively associated with the use of DMFs.

## Exercise Motivations, Environmental Motivational Supports, and Well-Being

Subjective vitality assesses a person’s aliveness and energy in a physical activity context and is thought to reflect eudaemonic well-being (Gunnell et al. 2014; Ryan et al. 2013). Eudaemonic well-being, focusing “on meaning and selfrealization and defin[ing] well-being in terms of the degree to which a person is fully functioning,” is contrasted with hedonic well-being that “focuses on happiness and defines well-being in terms of pleasure attainment and pain avoidance” (Ryan and Deci 2001, p. 141). Researchers using OIT have shown that more self-determined motivational regulations are positively associated with well-being (McDonough and Crocker 2007). Ryan and Deci (2002) have also suggested that more self-determined activity leads to greater well-being. Furthermore, they describe integrated and identified regulations as more self-determined and introjected regulations as more controlling or closest to purely extrinsic. Thus, we expect the more self-determined regulations (intrinsic, integrated, and identified) to lead to higher levels of subjective vitality when exercising. We would similarly expect the more non–self-determined regulations (introjected and external) to lead to decreased subjective vitality when exercising. We would not expect amotivation to be associated with subjective vitality when exercising.

H4a: Intrinsic regulations for exercise will be positively associated with subjective vitality.

H4b: Integrated regulations for exercise will be positively associated with subjective vitality.

H4c: Identified regulations for exercise will be positively associated with subjective vitality.

H4d: Introjected regulations for exercise will be negatively associated with subjective vitality.

H4e: External regulations for exercise will be negatively associated with subjective vitality.

H4f: Nonregulations for exercise will not be associated with subjective vitality.

Subjective vitality has been conceptualized as “a feeling of personal energy associated with agency, which can be diminished by factors that block or hinder autonomy or competence” (Ryan and Frederick 1997, p. 560). We expect the use of feature sets that are supportive of the basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness to enhance subjective vitality outcomes. In other words, these feature sets should provide positive environmental motivational support to exercisers. We argue that the SIFs and the DMFs positively support at least some of the basic psychological needs. Therefore, we expect these feature sets to have a positive effect on the relationships between the exercise motivations and subjective vitality because they help provide an environment supportive of the basic psychological needs.

Prior research has indicated that environments supportive of the basic psychological needs lead to better exercise and wellness outcomes (Hsu et al. 2013; Moustaka et al. 2012; Teixeira, Silva et al. 2012). ECFs most closely mimic environmental motivational supports that are said to be controlling and to thwart the autonomy need. Thus, we expect the ECFs to diminish the relationships between the exercise motivations and subjective vitality because they do not help provide an environment supportive of the basic psychological needs.

H5a–f: Use of the SIFs will positively moderate the relationships between the exercise motivations and subjective vitality.

H6a–f: Use of the ECFs will negatively moderate the relationships between the exercise motivations and subjective vitality.

H7a–f: Use of the DMFs will positively moderate the relationships between the exercise motivations and subjective vitality.

## Methods and Analysis

## Scale Development, Data Collection, and Participant Profile

We used existing scales to create our survey instrument whenever possible. The behavioral regulation in exercise survey version 3 (BREQ-3) (Markland and Tobin 2004; Wilson et al. 2006) was used to measure the six OIT exercise motivations. The latest version of the BREQ scale, version 3, contains items that measure the exercise motivations for each of the categories in Figure 1 (Wilson et al. 2006). BREQ’s direct mapping to the OIT spectrum seen in Figure 1 provides “a focus on underlying, source level motives for exercise, represented by the behavioral regulation continuum, rather than surface level motives (such as weight control, socialisation, and fitness),” which “may increase our understanding of the way in which perceived self-determination for action influences behavior” (Mullan et al. 1997, p. 751). The BREQ-3 was used without modification because it satisfied our needs and has been very widely tested and used in previous exercise motivation research (Markland and Tobin 2004; Teixeira, Carraça et al. 2012; Wilson et al. 2006).

The items to measure fitness technology feature use had to be developed for the current study. The process we used to identify these features and develop the items is described in Appendix A. The subjective vitality scale (SVS) was developed and tested in previous research by Ryan and Frederick (1997) and Bostic et al. (2000). The original SVS contained seven items, but work by Bostic et al. found that the removal of one item increased the scale’s effectiveness, so we used the improved six-item scale. The survey items along with the mean and standard deviation for each item are provided in Appendix B.

An online survey was developed using the Qualtrics<sup>3</sup> online survey platform provider. Data was collected on Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (mTurk)<sup>4</sup> crowdsourcing platform. The mTurk platform has been adopted by many behavioral researchers because it has been demonstrated to provide a somewhat more diverse demographic sample than using college students and data that is as reliable (or more so) as traditional methods (Buhrmester et al. 2011; Lowry et al. 2016; Mason and Suri 2012). For our purposes, respondents needed to currently use or have used a fitness technology (a device, an app, or both). A filter question was posed at the beginning of the survey asking respondents to indicate if they met this requirement. Those that were not current or past users of a fitness technology were ejected from the survey. Those respondents indicating that they were current or past users of a fitness technology were asked to specify which devices, apps, or both that they use (or had used). The results of this query for the full data collection are given in Table 1.

The full survey was subject to a pilot test to examine the quality of the new items and confirm the soundness of the previously implemented scales. For the pilot test, data was collected on mTurk until 100 usable responses were obtained. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the pilot data in IBM’s SPSS. The pilot data possessed the desired factor structure, and the full data collection was allowed to proceed. For the full data collection, a total of 1,432 responses were obtained from mTurk. Of those 1,432 responses, 154 respondents did not complete the survey, resulting in unusable data that was discarded. Another 212 respondents did not pass the filter question and were eliminated. There were an additional 186 responses where at least one of the “attention trap” questions was incorrectly answered, which were also discarded. After this data cleaning process, the resulting data set included 880 responses.

Demographic information for the sample is provided in Table 2, and some basic device and app demographics are provided in Tables 1 and 3. Table 1 shows that many of our respondents are Fitbit, Nike Fuelband, or Samsung Gear device users (past or present) and Fitbit, Nike+ MyFitnessPal, or RunKeeper app users, which is to be expected because these devices/apps were the most popular at the time of the data collection. The fitness technologies included in this study are relatively new to the marketplace, so it was not surprising to find that the length of ownership was typically reported as less than two years (87%).

## Model Specification and Testing

We used SmartPLS Version 3.2.1 (Ringle et al. 2015) to test our model. Partial least squares (PLS) regression is a form of structural equation modeling (SEM) that can be used to test multiple relationships between multiple independent and dependent variables simultaneously (Anderson and Gerbing 1988; Gefen et al. 2000; Lowry and Gaskin 2014). In SEM analyses, a two-stage approach, first examining the measurement model and then the structural model (Anderson and Gerbing 1988; Kline 2011), is recommended. Our models contain six reflective exogenous motivations toward exercising constructs: nonregulation, external regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, integrated regulation, and intrinsic regulation. Subjective vitality when exercising is specified as a reflective endogenous construct.

Three second-order factors represent the fitness technology feature sets: SIFs, ECFs, and DMFs. These three secondorder factors are formative (Petter et al. 2007). Formative second-order factors in PLS are handled using a repeated indicator approach (Lowry and Gaskin 2014) such that firstorder factors are created that contain the indicators for each of the first-order subconstructs. Those first-order subconstructs are used to predict the second-order construct, which contains all the items for all the first-order subconstructs. When the second-order constructs are endogenous, the R-squared values for these become 1.0 (i.e., are “swamped out”) because the first-order factors perfectly predict the second-order factors. To overcome this issue, we followed the recommended twostep approach to test our models (Lowry and Gaskin 2014). In the first step, the measurement model is tested using the repeated indicator approach to obtain latent variable scores. In the second step, the latent variable scores are used as the indicators of the constructs to test the structural models.

Before we could examine the structural model, the measurement model needed to be explored to determine convergent validity, discriminant validity, and reliability. The statistics reported in Appendix C suggest that all three are acceptable. We also checked for multicollinearity and performed checks to establish a lack of common method bias. The results indicate that multicollinearity and common method bias are not issues for our model. Detailed results for all tests are provided in Appendix C. The measurement model provides statistics (path coefficients and p-values) for the formative second-order constructs. The path coefficients are shown in Appendix D, and all are significant at p < 0.001.

The structural models were also tested using SmartPLS Version 3.2.1 (Ringle et al. 2015). Our structural models with path coefficients, R-squared values, and significance are shown in Figures 5 and 6. A summary of the results can be found in Tables 4, 5, and 6.

## Discussion

We posed two research questions:

(1) How do individuals’ exercise motivations influence their fitness technology feature set use?

(2) Does the use of fitness technology feature sets moderate the relationships between users’ motivations toward exercise and subjective vitality?

Answering the first question determines the fitness technology use profile for individuals with different motivations toward exercise. Answering the second question determines whether the use of the feature sets enhances or diminishes the known relationships between exercise motivations and subjective vitality, which enables us to provide insights that may explain why the current generation of fitness technologies are more effective for some exercisers. An overview of our results for each exercise motivation subtype is provided in Table 7.

Broadly speaking, we argued that more self-determined exercisers would gravitate toward less controlling and more informative feature sets (i.e., prefer the feature sets that are supportive of at least some basic psychological needs and avoid those that thwart these needs). Similarly, we argued that the less self-determined and amotivated exercisers would prefer the more controlling feature sets. Our results indicate that the story is more nuanced than this. Table 7 shows that most exercise motivation subtypes had quite different feature set use profiles.

Individuals with intrinsic regulations exercise for the enjoyment and satisfaction derived from it. Table 7 shows that exercisers with intrinsic regulations were more likely to use the SIFs, as hypothesized. However, there was no association between exercisers with intrinsic regulations and either the ECFs or the DMFs. The latter reveals that our findings do not support the argument that exercisers with intrinsic regulations would be more likely to use features that would assist them in gauging their competence and informing their exercise. Nevertheless, use of both the SIFs and the DMFs were found to positively moderate the positive relationship between intrinsic regulations and subjective vitality, which implies that the use of both of these feature sets can help promote positive well-being outcomes for intrinsically motivated exercisers. These findings suggest that the use of both the SIFs and the DMFs may be beneficial, although not necessarily attractive, to users with intrinsic regulations.

Exercisers with integrated regulations are the closest on the spectrum to intrinsically motivated users. These exercisers have integrated the activity into their value and belief system, but while their exercise is more self-determined, it is still done to obtain separate outcomes they value (e.g., rewards) rather than purely for enjoyment (Ryan and Deci 2000). Our findings reveal that these exercisers are more likely to use the SIFs and the ECFs, as hypothesized. Although we hypothesized that exercisers with integrated regulations would be more likely to use the DMFs, we found no relationship. Exercisers with integrated regulations also have a positive relationship with subjective vitality, and as with the intrinsically motivated exercisers, the use of the SIFs and the DMFs positively moderated this relationship. Hence, exercisers with integrated regulations have a use profile similar to that of exercisers with intrinsic motivations, with the addition of the use of the more controlling ECFs that we argued would appeal to all the extrinsically motivated exercisers.

Exercisers with identified regulations endorse the activity and the value it brings (Ryan and Deci 2002). While they are less self-determined than exercisers with the previous two regulations, exercise is considered part of these individuals’ identity, and its benefits are recognized (Markland and Tobin 2004; Wilson et al. 2006). Our findings reveal that exercisers with identified regulations have a very different use profile than the other subtypes (see Table 7). These exercisers are the only subtype to be more likely to use the DMFs, supporting hypothesis H3c, but less likely to use the SIFs, contrary to our hypothesis. Exercisers with identified regulations were also found to have no relationship with the ECFs or subjective vitality, rather than the positive ones hypothesized. These results suggest that exercisers with identified regulations use fitness technologies simply for data analysis and information.

Introjected regulations are tied to the exerciser’s self-esteem and feelings of guilt about not exercising (Deci and Ryan 2012; Gillison et al. 2009). Researchers have suggested that introjected regulation is “a relatively unstable form of internalization…in which people adopt an ambient value or practice and are motivated to maintain it, as they ‘should,’ in order to maintain self-approval or avoid guilt” (Deci and Ryan 2012, p. 421). We argued that these exercisers would be more likely to use the ECFs and SIFs, which integrate external factors into the exercise regime, but less likely to use the DMFs, which support competence. Table 7 reveals that exercisers with introjected regulations have no significant relationships with the use of any of the feature sets. Furthermore, as expected, introjected regulation had a negative relationship with subjective vitality, and the use of the SIFs and DMFs negatively moderated this relationship. Our results suggest that no feature sets of current fitness technologies provide support that is useful to this subtype.

Exercisers with external regulations are purely extrinsically motivated. “Externally regulated behaviors are dependent on the continuous presence of the controls” (Ryan and Deci 2006, pp. 1569-1570), and therefore, we argued that exercisers with external regulations would be more likely to use the feature sets that placed additional controls on their exercise. The ECFs add pressure in the form of beeping and buzzing notifications to prompt the user to stand or walk, and the SIFs integrate others into the users’ exercise regimes, creating the possibility of perceived social pressure. The results show that there is a positive relationship between exercisers with external regulations and the use of both the ECFs and the SIFs, as hypothesized (see Table 7). No relationship between external regulations and the use of the DMFs was found. As expected, external regulation had a negative relationship with subjective vitality, and the use of the SIFs and DMFs negatively moderated this relationship. These results largely confirm our expectations that extrinsically motivated exercisers would gravitate toward features that inserted additional controls on their exercise.

Amotivated exercisers are simply not motivated in any way to exercise. Exercisers with nonregulations typically do not perform the activity, or do so passively, and may not value the activity or the outcomes (Ryan and Deci 2002). Our findings reveal that, as hypothesized, amotivated exercisers are more likely to use the SIFs and less likely to use the DMFs (see Table 7). However, we hypothesized a positive relationship between amotivated exercisers and the ECFs that was not supported. We expected neither the positive relationship between nonregulation and subjective vitality nor the positive moderation of this relationship by both the SIFs and DMFs. The results suggest that amotivated exercisers may benefit from using some features of fitness technologies.

## Contributions to Research and Theory

Drawing on the theory of affordances, we examined the use of fitness technologies at a feature set level of analysis, arguing that individuals with different motivations toward exercise would use fitness technologies differently. The foundation of this argument is supported by prior work exploring how individuals view the utilities of technology, perhaps best summarized by the following statement: “IS researchers who adopt a relational view of affordances stress that people’s goals shape what they come to view the features of the technology as affording them the ability to do” (Leonardi 2013, p. 751). Our results indicate that there are unique patterns of use for almost all subtypes of exercisers. Our findings that those with different motivations toward exercise have unique fitness technology use profiles provide a theoretical contribution, and the novelty of our study’s context elevates the importance of this discovery.

Fitness technologies are currently the most prominent example of the increasingly popular (Lamkin 2017) set of technologies supporting the “quantified self” or “personal informatics” (Epstein, Kang et al. 2016; Patel et al. 2015; Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017; Ware 2013). As these terms imply, the overarching idea of these technologies is that they serve as environmental motivational supports for specific “personal” activities (e.g., exercise, finances, education) to encourage individuals in obtaining desirable outcomes associated with the performance of the activities (e.g., better wellness, more money, a degree). These technologies offer support by collecting and analyzing data related to the activities, providing options to socialize or add gamification to activities in new ways (Liu et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017), and offering electronic stimulation (automated notifications) as a partial substitute for a life coach or assistant (Spence and Grant 2007). Motivational theory states that individuals differ in how they think about or approach an activity or even in their desired outcomes (Deci and Ryan 2012; Ryan and Deci 2000; Ryan et al. 2008). However, early generations of “personal informatics” technologies are concerned with building a user base (IDC 2017), because these technologies are still quite new and quickly evolving (Lamkin 2017) and have not yet turned to customizing their use (IDC 2017), leaving it to the user to select which features to employ. This one-sizefits-all approach may provide a partial explanation for some discouraging early findings regarding the use of fitness technologies and certain outcomes (e.g., losing weight, enjoyment) (Etkin 2016; Jakicic et al. 2016). Research is only beginning to examine how to present the collected information to users, frame messages, or best add gamification (Epstein, Caraway et al. 2016; Epstein, Kang et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Shin and Biocca 2017). Our study contributes to this emerging area by illustrating that to achieve the most effective outcomes from the use of such personal technologies, it may be wise to consider personalizing use to the users’ characteristics.

Our study extends SDT in the exercise context by combining it with the theory of affordances to examine (1) how fitness technology users with different motivations toward exercise construct their environments and (2) how the insertion of the fitness technology feature sets into users’ exercise environments impacts a wellness outcome. Our results suggest that traditional motivational theories will need to be extended to accommodate advances in personal informatics technologies that allow users to customize the environments in which they are performing activities, as well as to further explore the ramifications of using such technologies as environmental motivational supports.

Advocates of SDT have long extolled the virtues of creating “adaptive” environments via providing sociocontextual supports that promote the fulfillment of basic needs, facilitate more self-determined regulation of behavior, and contribute to overall feelings of eudaemonic well-being and health (Wilson et al. 2008, p. 253).

Fitness technologies provide users with such an adaptive environment, and our study contributes to the motivation literature by revealing what kinds of environments users with different motivations toward exercise construct when left unguided and how that use contributes to their well-being. The implication for researchers is that studies may be limited by both excluding user characteristics when exploring the use of highly personalized technologies and ignoring that users can customize their use to construct their own personalized environments, which necessitates a feature set level of analysis.

In addition to these broader implications to research and theory, our findings also provide granular insights that may be helpful to researchers in the study of such personal and customizable technologies. First, the hypothesized positive relationship between intrinsic regulations and the DMFs was not supported. One implication of this result may be to partially explain prior findings that suggested that “by highlighting a quantitative outcome of enjoyable activities, measurement makes such activities seem more like work, which undermines intrinsic motivation” (Etkin 2016, p. 980). Specifically, purely intrinsically motivated exercisers may not need or even want the quantification provided by the fitness technology for at least two possible reasons: (1) it may take the fun out of an activity they enjoy by making it feel like it is something they have to do (Etkin 2016) or (2) it may become boring because the current data analytics mostly inform them of what they already know (Cotgreave 2015; Rapp and Cena 2016). Hence, it is important for future researchers to address such mismatches between the users motivational characteristics and the available features or the guidance users are given for the technologies. Such mismatches could even lead to annoyance or anger at the technology if not properly managed (Lowry et al. 2015).

We should caution that our findings do not suggest that exercisers with intrinsic regulations should not use fitness technologies. In fact, the benefit of our feature-level approach lies in pinpointing which feature sets naturally attracted different subtypes of exercisers and examining the impact of the feature sets on a well-being indicator. The ECFs and DMFs may not appeal to the intrinsically motivated exerciser, but the SIFs did, indicating that socializing or adding gamification may be appealing. Research in IS calls for more investigation of gamification (Liu et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017), and fitness technologies offer such elements (e.g., challenges and leaderboards). Furthermore, both the use of the SIFs and the DMFs positively moderated the relationship between intrinsic regulations and subjective vitality, which suggests that using both feature sets may enhance well-being outcomes for intrinsically motivated exercisers. Thus, researchers should explore how socializing or adding gamification may benefit wellness outcomes for intrinsically motivated exercisers and how DMFs could be redesigned to be more appealing to this type of exerciser (Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017).

The SIFs were the feature set that four of the six subtypes of exercisers were more likely to use, although those with identified regulations were less likely to use them. These results suggest that carefully designed social and gamification features that are mindful not to decrease the exerciser’s need for competence and autonomy may prove a fruitful avenue to assist the greatest number of subtypes of exercisers. Previous literature has found that social motives for exercise may increase enjoyment (Ryan et al. 1997; Wankel 1993), perhaps leading to better adherence to the exercise program through satisfaction of the relatedness need (Spink and Carron 1992), which our results indicate may be more attractive to more types of exercisers than any other currently available feature set. An editorial in the Journal of the American Medical Association suggests that fitness technologies need to provide environmental motivational support that helps sustain extrinsic motivations, although it advises that this is difficult to accomplish (Patel et al. 2015). The authors conclude that fitness technologies need to be combined with engagement strategies, which carefully designed socialization and gamification could provide. However, our findings were not universally positive with regard to all exercise motivation subtypes and the use of the SIFs. It may be that some of the extrinsically motivated exercisers see the SIFs as a way for people to view and further control their fitness progress rather than as a source of enjoyable social interaction. Research needs to delve into perceptual differences of technology-mediated socialization to inform customized designs that provide benefits to all types of exercisers.

Our findings also provide insights for each subtype of exerciser, broadly suggesting that research needs to build on both SDT and our work to determine what guidance can be provided to users with different motivational characteristics on how to use the technologies as well as to inform technology creators regarding how to design new features that are better for different types of exercisers. Our results for exercisers with introjected regulations are particularly notable. These individuals are exercising to avoid guilt or to buoy selfesteem, and our findings suggest none of the current feature sets may appeal to them. Furthermore, studies that examined the influence of introjected exercise motivations on exercise participation found inconsistent results, leading to the suggestion that “null or unreliable results from introjection are theoretically expected within SDT, in which introjection is seen as an unstable basis for motivation without positive long-term utility” (Teixeira, Carraça et al. 2012, p. 20). Prior research has also found gender differences in introjected regulations, suggesting that self-worth gauged by peer acceptance and sport may be more critical to males (Gillison et al. 2009). Exercisers that are motivated by self-esteem issues and guilt may have a mismatch with all the current feature sets, which may reinforce such feelings by encouraging negative social comparisons, for example. Hence, research needs to consider that personal informatics technologies as currently designed may not be effective for all types of users. Our findings for exercisers with introjected regulations reinforce the importance of extending SDT to the study of fitness technologies to further clarify the motivational characteristics that are central to achieving beneficial outcomes for all users.

For exercisers with identified regulations, exercise reflects values or goals that are important to them but distinct from the pure enjoyment of the activity. An example of the uniqueness of identified regulation in the exercise context is given by Teixeira, Carraça et al. (2012a, p. 22), referring to Edmunds et al. (2006), who

suggested that because sustaining a physically active lifestyle presumably requires a high degree of effort, often for mundane or repetitive activities, regulation by identification with the outcomes may be more important than exercising for fun and enjoyment.

Thus, one explanation for our findings for this subtype may be that the outcomes associated with exercise are valued over enjoyment. Individuals with identified regulations may feel that health and well-being are important objectives and use the DMFs to measure progress toward these goals. This result may actually illuminate an option for new features that integrate exercise goals (e.g., appearance, health, social, enjoyment) (Etkin 2016; Ryan et al. 1997; Ryan et al. 2008) in more depth rather than relying primarily on simpler quantification. This discussion also calls attention to the need for researchers to extend our work to examine other motivational characteristics (Deci and Ryan 2012; Ryan et al. 2008), such as goal content, in relation to the use of fitness and other personal informatics technologies. Research is only beginning to explore the personal and societal ramifications of personal informatics technologies. Our study used only one sub-theory of SDT; there is much to be gained by further integration of SDT into the study of personal informatics technologies.

## Implications for Society and Practice

Our results provide insights into the use and design of the current generation of fitness technologies that may be useful to both users and developers. The ECFs are arguably the most prominent features of the current generation of fitness technologies. The ECFs are often the default in fitness technologies to, for example, send exercisers a reward badge when they exceed a certain number of steps or to remind them to stand, breathe, or take more steps. It is not especially promising that our results revealed only two of the extrinsic motivation subtypes, integrated and external, were more likely to use the ECFs. Moreover, motivation theory has long suggested that rewards, imposed goals, deadlines, and directives all steer individuals’ focus toward external controls, diminishing intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci 2000). Furthermore, our findings show that the ECFs had no moderating impact on the relationships between the exercise motivation subtypes and subjective vitality. The theory and our results both suggest that the ECFs, as currently designed, may not be the best feature set for most exercisers.

One suggestion for fitness technology users would be to spend time exploring the other feature sets that may offer more effective environmental motivational support. The SIFs and the DMFs are becoming increasingly sophisticated as fitness technologies mature. These features often require more setup, learning, and customization from the user than the ECFs, but our results suggest this additional time and effort may be worth it. Given our findings for the ECFs and that too many notifications may overwhelm or annoy users, fitness technology makers may want to carefully consider how they use notifications. It may be imprudent to send rewards or reminders that may not be as effective, when notifications could be used to provide feedback supportive of the basic psychological needs through carefully crafted messaging from the DMFs and SIFs that may lead to better outcomes for many users. Furthermore, gamification elements should be granularly investigated in future research, because some may function more as rewards (e.g., badges) where others may be found to be more supportive of the competence or relatedness needs (e.g., leaderboards, challenges). Research has begun to explore messaging and customizing analytics (Epstein, Caraway et al. 2016; Epstein, Kang et al. 2016; Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017), but there is much work remaining for both researchers and fitness technology makers. Furthermore, while our results for the DMFs showed more promise in assisting well-being outcomes for some exercisers, only exercisers with identified regulations were more likely to use this feature set. There are some indications in the literature that the DMFs may become boring after a while as exercisers become familiar with their normal levels of daily activity and that they may be discouraging to those whose use of fitness technologies has lapsed (Epstein, Caraway et al. 2016; Epstein, Kang et al. 2016; Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017). Changes in how the data is displayed or to the framing of messages may help improve the DMFs to make them more effective (Epstein, Caraway et al. 2016; Epstein, Kang et al. 2016; Rapp and Cena 2016; Shin and Biocca 2017). Fitness technology makers may be well served to improve the DMFs, but users could also explore changes in how they use their own fitness data. For example, adding friends and using a leaderboard to view their step counts compared to others would be a way to add gamification to the data analytics. The SIFs appealed to the greatest number of subtypes of exercisers (intrinsic regulations, integrated regulations, external regulations, and nonregulations) and also positively moderated the relationships between the intrinsic, integrated, and nonregulations and subjective vitality. This suggests that the SIFs may be a very promising feature set for both users and makers to explore. Furthermore, gamification elements such as challenges and leaderboards are often a part of the SIFs, which provides another valuable option for engagement (Liu et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Patel et al. 2015).

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study was limited because our data collection was conducted at a single point in time. We could only observe associations between current exercise motivations and current fitness technology feature set use. Future research should include longitudinal studies, which could capture and confirm changes in the use of fitness technologies, users’ perceptions regarding fitness technologies, and users’ exercise behaviors. For example, mismatches between motivational characteristics and fitness technology features could lead to continuance issues. Hence, future longitudinal studies could explore the influence of fitness technology feature sets on exercise adherence, regimen changes, and habit as well as investigate discontinuance among users with different motivational characteristics. Such studies could inform design approaches; early research on discontinuance and design is already yielding interesting results (Epstein, Caraway et al. 2016; Epstein, Kang et al. 2016).

Our study was limited to the feature sets of fitness technologies at the time of data collection. These features are likely to expand substantially with further maturation of the product lines, and future research may benefit from a broader set of features to examine. As the number and sophistication of the features of fitness technologies expands, future research may also need to employ more formal methods of developing feature sets (e.g., a card sort procedure). In addition, the form of fitness technologies is changing rapidly. Technology to measure and collect exercise and health indicators, as well as nutrition and sleep data, is being integrated into a wide variety of items (e.g., mobile phones, smart watches, bands, tokens, shoes). We did not restrict our respondents to the use of a particular combination of devices and apps because our interests lie in determining what features they would implement if the choice were left up to them. However, different technologies have different characteristics. For example, perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, privacy, and trust could all vary for different devices and affect their use and the wellness outcomes obtained thereby.

Future research may also want to delve into other antecedents of use, such as trust in the accuracy of the device, which has recently been highlighted in the media (McPhate 2016). Another interesting antecedent to different wellness outcomes or to use may be to explore motivation to use fitness technologies (e.g., Ke et al. 2013; Malhotra et al. 2008) instead of or in addition to the exercise motivations. Future research could also explore an analysis of use that is more granular; for example, frequency of feature use (i.e., adapting our scales to obtain frequency of use information rather than merely use), manipulations of the social environment, consideration of various social psychological constructs in models of motivation and fitness technology use, or implementation choices (i.e., form factors, combinations of apps and devices).

## Conclusion

Our study, grounded in SDT, provides a fine-grained examination of the associations between exercise motivations and fitness technology feature set use. Our results suggest that almost every subtype of exerciser has a unique use profile. In addition, we examine the impact of fitness technology feature set use on the relationships between users’ exercise motivations and subjective vitality. Our findings suggest that two of the feature sets (SIFs and DMFs) show promise in enhancing well-being outcomes, but only for the more self-determined and amotivated subtypes of exercisers.

Fitness technologies are still relatively new to the marketplace, and little is known regarding their ultimate effectiveness. Some recent research even suggests they may not support other wellness outcomes, such as weight loss (Jakicic et al. 2016). Our findings regarding subjective vitality indicate that there is some promise to the technologies as environmental motivational supports, but our study also provides the insight that a one-size-fits-all approach to their design and use may not be the most effective. Our findings reveal that providing every type of exerciser with the motivational support that best fits their motivational profile may not be a straightforward task, but it ultimately may be required for fitness technologies to be universally useful in supporting wellness outcomes.

## Developing the Fitness Technology Feature Sets

Fitness technology feature use items were not available in the literature, and thus, were developed for the current study. Items were developed for each feature set following the procedure described next. We used a four-step process to determine the fitness technology features that make up our first-order subconstructs. First, we compiled a list of currently available fitness devices and their associated apps using lists of wearable published in popular media outlets. The original list contained 72 devices and was compiled using lists of wearables from CNET, PC Magazine, The Wall Street Journal, Engadget, Gizmodo, and others. While not comprehensive, the redundancy across multiple lists suggests that our list, at the very least, contains the most popular devices in the wearables category at the time the data was collected. Second, the three researchers independently visited every website of each device/app on the list and collected the features the company advertised for the fitness technology. Third, all three of the researchers’ feature lists were compared, discussed, and used to create an integrated list. Fourth, once survey items were created, an expert panel was convened to examine the feature list and scales as described below.

Accepted procedural methods (Churchill 1979; MacKenzie et al. 2011) were followed in developing the fitness technology use items. Once the items were developed, an expert panel was convened. The expert panel consisted of two faculty members who are well versed in survey based methodologies, two faculty members who were active users of fitness technologies, and two employees of a fitness technology company. The expert panel was instructed to examine the entire survey instrument for clarity and to provide feedback on wording and note if any fitness technology features were missing. The expert panel did not provide any new fitness technology features, which indicated our list was reason ably comprehensive. The expert panel did suggest wording changes to the fitness technology features set use items and the addition of a few more fitness device and apps to our list (primarily new versions of devices already present in our list). We compiled the expert panel wording suggestions and considered each suggestion. Most wording suggestions from the expert panel were implemented, improving the clarity of the survey items.

## Discriminant Validity

To establish discriminant validity, the cross-loading matrix can be examined for troublesome cross-loadings between the indicators. Discriminant validity is confirmed if it can be illustrated that “measurement items posited to reflect (i.e., ‘make up’) that construct differ from those that are not believed to make up the construct” (Straub et al. 2004, p. 389). Loadings should be an order of magnitude greater than the nearest cross-loading (i.e., the difference between the primary loading and any other loading should be greater than 0.1) (Lowry and Gaskin 2014). This is the case for all of the items seen in Table C2 with the exception of encourage2, compare5, and compete1. However, these three items all load highest on their primary factor and cross-load with other first-order subconstructs of the same second-order construct where we might expect some correlation, so we retained these items and performed the second check for discriminant validity. For the second check, we examined the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for a construct in comparison to the construct correlations of that construct with every other first-order construct in the model. These results are shown in Table C3. The right portion of Table C3 contains the construct correlations. The bolded values that appear down the diagonal of the table are the square roots of the AVEs found in the second column for each construct. Any correlation below an bolded value should be lower than that bolded value (Fornell and Larcker 1981), which is the case for all of our constructs. Taken together, these results indicate discriminant validity.

## Reliability

Reliability was examined using the AVE, composite reliability, and Cronbach’s alpha for each construct. These values were calculated by the PLS algorithm and provided as output. For our model, these values are provided in Table C3. Reliability scores are intended to provide an indication of how reliable the scales will be over time (Straub 1989). Ideally, the composite reliability should be above 0.7 (Hair et al. 2006) and greater than the AVE. Both are true for all constructs: all composite reliabilities are above 0.7 and the AVE is less than the composite reliability. It is recommended that the AVE be 0.5 or above (Fornell and Larcker 1981; Hair et al. 2006), which is the case for all of ou constructs. Cronbach’s alphas above 0.7 are recommended and above 0.5 are acceptable (Davis 1964; Peterson 1994). The Cronbach’s alphas are above 0.7 for all of the constructs in our model. Thus, reliability was confirmed for all of the scales used in the study.

## Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity refers to the situation where predictors are highly correlated with each other. To check for multicollinearity, the variance inflation factors (VIF) obtained from SmartPLS Version 3.2.1 can be examined. It is suggested that the VIF be below 10 (Hair et al. 2006; Neter et al. 1996). A VIF of greater than or equal to 5 has been suggested to be indicative of moderate multicollinearity and greater than or equal to 10 suggestive of severe multicollinearity (Larose and Larose 2015). VIF values for the items for this study are given in Table C4. All of the VIFs are below 10 and most are below 5, which suggest that multicollinearity is not an issue in our model.

## Common Method Bias

Our study design incorporated recommendations to reduce common method bias following leading literature (MacKenzie et al. 2011; Podsakoff et al. 2003). The survey was implemented on the Quatrics platform. The Qualtrics survey platform was used because it is an approved survey administration tool by the researchers’ institutional review board (IRB) and allows for data to be anonymously collected on the Amazon Mechanical Turk (mTurk) platform. The survey items were randomized within blocks based upon the Likert-scale response anchors for the items (e.g., strongly disagree to strongly agree). Providing anonymity to the survey respondents has been recommended as an approach to reduce common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003) by reducing the tendency of respondents to answer in a way that they think the researchers would prefer. Randomizing the survey items has also been suggested as a way to decrease common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). “Attention trap” items were inserted throughout the survey. Attention trap items ask the respondent to select a particular response from the Likert-scale responses (Oppenheimer et al. 2009). For example, the respondent may be asked to “Please answer ‘Agree’ to this question.” The purpose of the trap items is to identify those respondents that are not cognitively engaged in responding to the survey and to discard those responses.

In addition, the construct correlation matrix can be examined to determine if any constructs are correlated above 0.90, which could indicate a common method bias issue (Pavlou et al. 2007). An examination of the construct correlations in Table C3 reveals that none of our constructs are correlated above 0.90. Harmon’s single-factor test (Lowry and Gaskin 2014; Podsakoff et al. 2003) was also employed to check for common method bias. We examined the unrotated factor solution in SPSS for all the items of our first-order constructs. The factor analysis revealed 12 distinct factors with the largest factor accounting for only 26.109% of the variance. This further suggests a lack of common method bias (Lowry and Gaskin 2014).

## Developing the Fitness Technology Feature Sets

Fitness technology feature use items were not available in the literature, and thus, were developed for the current study. Items were developed for each feature set following the procedure described next. We used a four-step process to determine the fitness technology features that make up our first-order subconstructs. First, we compiled a list of currently available fitness devices and their associated apps using lists of wearable published in popular media outlets. The original list contained 72 devices and was compiled using lists of wearables from CNET, PC Magazine, The Wall Street Journal, Engadget, Gizmodo, and others. While not comprehensive, the redundancy across multiple lists suggests that our list, at the very least, contains the most popular devices in the wearables category at the time the data was collected. Second, the three researchers independently visited every website of each device/app on the list and collected the features the company advertised for the fitness technology. Third, all three of the researchers’ feature lists were compared, discussed, and used to create an integrated list. Fourth, once survey items were created, an expert panel was convened to examine the feature list and scales as described below.

Accepted procedural methods (Churchill 1979; MacKenzie et al. 2011) were followed in developing the fitness technology use items. Once the items were developed, an expert panel was convened. The expert panel consisted of two faculty members who are well versed in survey based methodologies, two faculty members who were active users of fitness technologies, and two employees of a fitness technology company. The expert panel was instructed to examine the entire survey instrument for clarity and to provide feedback on wording and note if any fitness technology features were missing. The expert panel did not provide any new fitness technology features, which indicated our list was reason ably comprehensive. The expert panel did suggest wording changes to the fitness technology features set use items and the addition of a few more fitness device and apps to our list (primarily new versions of devices already present in our list). We compiled the expert panel wording suggestions and considered each suggestion. Most wording suggestions from the expert panel were implemented, improving the clarity of the survey items.

## Discriminant Validity

To establish discriminant validity, the cross-loading matrix can be examined for troublesome cross-loadings between the indicators. Discriminant validity is confirmed if it can be illustrated that “measurement items posited to reflect (i.e., ‘make up’) that construct differ from those that are not believed to make up the construct” (Straub et al. 2004, p. 389). Loadings should be an order of magnitude greater than the nearest cross-loading (i.e., the difference between the primary loading and any other loading should be greater than 0.1) (Lowry and Gaskin 2014). This is the case for all of the items seen in Table C2 with the exception of encourage2, compare5, and compete1. However, these three items all load highest on their primary factor and cross-load with other first-order subconstructs of the same second-order construct where we might expect some correlation, so we retained these items and performed the second check for discriminant validity. For the second check, we examined the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) for a construct in comparison to the construct correlations of that construct with every other first-order construct in the model. These results are shown in Table C3. The right portion of Table C3 contains the construct correlations. The bolded values that appear down the diagonal of the table are the square roots of the AVEs found in the second column for each construct. Any correlation below an bolded value should be lower than that bolded value (Fornell and Larcker 1981), which is the case for all of our constructs. Taken together, these results indicate discriminant validity.

## Reliability

Reliability was examined using the AVE, composite reliability, and Cronbach’s alpha for each construct. These values were calculated by the PLS algorithm and provided as output. For our model, these values are provided in Table C3. Reliability scores are intended to provide an indication of how reliable the scales will be over time (Straub 1989). Ideally, the composite reliability should be above 0.7 (Hair et al. 2006) and greater than the AVE. Both are true for all constructs: all composite reliabilities are above 0.7 and the AVE is less than the composite reliability. It is recommended that the AVE be 0.5 or above (Fornell and Larcker 1981; Hair et al. 2006), which is the case for all of ou constructs. Cronbach’s alphas above 0.7 are recommended and above 0.5 are acceptable (Davis 1964; Peterson 1994). The Cronbach’s alphas are above 0.7 for all of the constructs in our model. Thus, reliability was confirmed for all of the scales used in the study.

## Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity refers to the situation where predictors are highly correlated with each other. To check for multicollinearity, the variance inflation factors (VIF) obtained from SmartPLS Version 3.2.1 can be examined. It is suggested that the VIF be below 10 (Hair et al. 2006; Neter et al. 1996). A VIF of greater than or equal to 5 has been suggested to be indicative of moderate multicollinearity and greater than or equal to 10 suggestive of severe multicollinearity (Larose and Larose 2015). VIF values for the items for this study are given in Table C4. All of the VIFs are below 10 and most are below 5, which suggest that multicollinearity is not an issue in our model.

## Common Method Bias

Our study design incorporated recommendations to reduce common method bias following leading literature (MacKenzie et al. 2011; Podsakoff et al. 2003). The survey was implemented on the Quatrics platform. The Qualtrics survey platform was used because it is an approved survey administration tool by the researchers’ institutional review board (IRB) and allows for data to be anonymously collected on the Amazon Mechanical Turk (mTurk) platform. The survey items were randomized within blocks based upon the Likert-scale response anchors for the items (e.g., strongly disagree to strongly agree). Providing anonymity to the survey respondents has been recommended as an approach to reduce common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003) by reducing the tendency of respondents to answer in a way that they think the researchers would prefer. Randomizing the survey items has also been suggested as a way to decrease common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003). “Attention trap” items were inserted throughout the survey. Attention trap items ask the respondent to select a particular response from the Likert-scale responses (Oppenheimer et al. 2009). For example, the respondent may be asked to “Please answer ‘Agree’ to this question.” The purpose of the trap items is to identify those respondents that are not cognitively engaged in responding to the survey and to discard those responses.

In addition, the construct correlation matrix can be examined to determine if any constructs are correlated above 0.90, which could indicate a common method bias issue (Pavlou et al. 2007). An examination of the construct correlations in Table C3 reveals that none of our constructs are correlated above 0.90. Harmon’s single-factor test (Lowry and Gaskin 2014; Podsakoff et al. 2003) was also employed to check for common method bias. We examined the unrotated factor solution in SPSS for all the items of our first-order constructs. The factor analysis revealed 12 distinct factors with the largest factor accounting for only 26.109% of the variance. This further suggests a lack of common method bias (Lowry and Gaskin 2014).
