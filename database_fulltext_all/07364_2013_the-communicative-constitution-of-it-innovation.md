## a b s t r a c t

This paper contributes to studies of IT innovation, by approaching discourse and technology not as alternating causalities of change, but rather as constitutive to processes of change. Drawing on a communicative constitution of organization (CCO) perspective, the paper provides an analysis of oral and written evidence on innovations in the English National Programme for IT (NPfIT) from 1998 to 2011. The paper makes two key contributions to the literature. First, it offers a longitudinal empirical understanding of how IT innovation is constituted in the triadic relationship between human and nonhuman actors, and the narrative texts in which the delegation between the <sup>fi</sup>rst two occurs. The paper explores the implications of this renewed understanding of IT innovation for IS research in sociomateriality. Second, the paper contributes to CCO-informed research by adopting a methodological approach that draws on both a historical analysis of the constitution of material objects in speci<sup>fi</sup>c narrative texts and a rhetorical analysis of communicative actions. The paper explores the methodological implications of this approach for addressing the challenge of understanding the scaling-up of micro communicative actions to macro actions towards the constitution of IT innovation.

## 1. Introduction

Over the last three decades, innovation studies have pointed to the complex processes through which new information technologies (IT) and organizational practices are created, developed or reinvented (King, 1992; Rogers, 1995). In a seminal paper, Swanson and Ramiller (1997) developed the notion of “organizing vision” to explain how an IT innovation embeds and utilizes processes of social construction. An organizing vision is best viewed as a “discursive construction” and “rhetorical” in nature since it “is commonly full of disputation, contradiction, challenge, and attempts to persuade” (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003: 16, emphasis in the original). This is because an organizing vision is produced and sustained through a heterogeneous set of commercial and professional interests, including IT vendors, practitioners, academics, etc., “all intent upon talking the associated innovation into existence in one or another form or shape” (Ramiller & Swanson, 2003: 16, emphasis added).

The emphasis here is based on the premise that discourse generates, not merely expresses, key organizational realities. This is among the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions of the linguistic turn in organization studies: an understanding of communication (or discourse) as constitutive (Cooren, Taylor, & Van Every, 2006; Putnam & Nicotera, 2009). Put simply, communication is not merely a conduit of message transmission between sender and receiver where the two express already formed realities to one another. Rather, the realities of the situation (e.g. the capacity to speak and be heard) are not fully formed outside of communication and simply awaiting expression. They are shaped by language and are open to new possibilities in the communication process.

Despite this signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution, however, the application of constitutive models of communication has had limited impact on organization studies, primarily because these models have thus far stressed symbolic over material aspects of organization (see Ashcraft, Kuhn, & Cooren, 2009; Leonardi & Barley, 2010). That is, most studies have focused on the ways by which organizations are realized in discursive communication, as represented by broader institutional structures, relations of power, and material constraints (Fairclough, 1995; Reed, 2000, 2010).

It has been argued that recent advances in the communicative constitution of organization (CCO) perspective can overcome the symbolic-material duality in organizational studies (Ashcraft et al., 2009; Brummans 2006; Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009; Cooren, Fairhurst, & Huët, 2012; Güney & Cresswell, 2012; Robichaud, 2006). The CCO perspective places great emphasis on the relationality of agency: material objects (e.g. information technologies) interact with human actors to constitute a (macro) collective voice that communicates action from a distance and across time on behalf of a network of actors within an organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld (Cooren, 2006; Robichaud, 2006).

In providing a relational ontology of organizations, the CCO perspective goes beyond other important streams of theorizing organizational phenomena, by putting forth the idea that “an organization is not rei<sup>fi</sup>ed and given [in structures], but, on the contrary, its perpetuation is continuously at stake and necessitates a continuous reproduction of communication” (Blaschke et al., 2012: 883). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the CCO perspective places strong emphasis on the study of “relationality rather than thinghood” (Cooper, 2005: 75), in order to trace the emergence of organizations as distinct and processual entities, the boundaries of which are brought forth by communication activities (Luhmann, 2003).

This paper draws on the CCO perspective to pursue the following research question: how is an IT innovation communicatively constituted and with what consequences?

The paper explores this question in the context of the English National Programme for IT (NPfIT). The paper focuses on two key narrative texts in the NPfIT's trajectory that have been made available to the public: two IT proiect report meetings between the Committee of Public Accounts (CPA) – assigned to examine the progress made in the NPfIT – and the NPfIT project leaders (CPA, 2007, 2009). In these meetings, oral and written evidence was presented to the CPA by the NPfIT project leaders, IT suppliers (e.g. British Telecommunications, Accenture, Fujitsu, etc.), the National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, and by user bodies (i.e. healthcare professionals), among others. The paper focuses on the communicative actions between policymakers and NPfIT project leaders, and the ways by which those actions become mediated by information technologies. Even though the research does not report on direct engagement with information technologies, the relationality of agency across both human actors and material objects is traced in the historical evolution of the NPfIT over the period 1998–2011. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings are also contrasted with empirical research on the actual adoption of various technologies of the NPfIT in speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational settings (Greenhalgh et al., 2010; Hendy, Fulop, Reeves, Hutchings, & Collin, 2007; Robertson et al., 2010).

The paper makes two key contributions to the literature. First, it offers a longitudinal empirical understanding of how discourse and technology actually work in constitutive entanglements, thus overcoming the symbolic-material duality of IT innovation studies. This renewed conceptualization of IT innovations departs from extant perspectives that usually follow representationalist thinking, i.e. they view IT innovation as a symbolic representation of practice, which often becomes a management fashion (Abrahamson & Fairchild, 1999) or an organizing vision (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997). In doing so, the paper brings to the forefront the agency and power of material objects in speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational contexts, thus also addressing the paucity of research in this area (Cooren et al., 2006). IT innovations are conceptualized as constitutive entanglements of what matters and what is meaningful and what is excluded from mattering (i.e. immaterial) and sense-making, at particular points in time and space. This renewed conceptualization of IT innovation raises implications for the recent wave of research on sociomateriality (e.g. Leonardi, 2013; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008; Robey, Raymond, & Anderson, 2012) that are discussed in the end of the paper.

Second, the paper contributes to CCO-informed research by adopting a methodological approach that draws on both a historical analysis of the constitution of material objects in speci<sup>fi</sup>c narrative texts and a rhetorical analysis of communicative actions. This approach addresses the challenge of understanding the scaling-up of local communicative actions to global actions towards the constitution (Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009; Putnam & Nicotera, 2010) or breakdown of organization (Bisel, 2010). In particular, the paper analyzes patterns of communicative acts that unfold into communication episodes, which in turn recursively interlock to form narrative texts that constitute an IT innovation. It is argued that this approach is particularly suitable for capturing the inevitably processual and historically situated character of IT innovation.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. The next section provides a review of the literatures on IT innovation and the communicative constitution of organizations. This is followed by a description of the case study, including details about the data sources, data analysis and theorizing from the data. Then, following this, there is an in-depth analysis of the case study using the CCO perspective. The paper concludes by <sup>fl</sup>eshing out the key contributions of the research.

## 2. A communicative constitution perspective to understanding it innovation

Extant research has argued that IT innovation is a complex process requiring integrated frameworks to classify innovation into different types and attributes (Fichman, 2000; Gallivan, 2001; Swanson, 1994). For example, Swanson (1994) proposed a model of IT innovation involving technical, administrative, and functional cores, and proceeded to a taxonomy of different types of innovation based on combinations of the aforementioned cores. These efforts to develop a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model and to classify the various types of innovation observed in different organizational settings have been argued to provide a more coordinated approach to the study of IT innovation (see Grover, Teng, & Fiedler, 1997).

At the same time, efforts towards a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed model of IT innovation have been criticized for classifying all innovations with the same attributes, while failing to account for different technologies' interpretive <sup>fl</sup>exibility at different phases of the innovation process, and, in consequence, their diverse trajectories (Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2001). In turn, it has been argued that IT innovation needs to be understood through processes of social construction across a heterogeneous community of policymakers, business managers, academics, journalists, etc. (Swanson & Ramiller, 1997, 2004).

In a seminal paper, Swanson and Ramiller (1997) developed the notion of “organizing vision” to explain how an IT innovation embeds and utilizes processes of social construction. Specifically, they argue that organizing visions shape IT innovations through (Swanson and Ramiller, 1997: 460–461): “interpretation” (i.e. interpretations of initial exploratory initiatives), “legitimation” (i.e. linking the innovation's rationale to broader business concerns), and “mobilization” (i.e. motivate and structure entrepreneurial and market forces that support the materialization of the innovation). More importantly, Swanson and Ramiller (1997: 556) explain that organizing visions come to be produced and sustained through the “discourse” of a heterogeneous community, which “provides a focus for the innovation's interpretation, aids in legitimizing it, and helps to mobilize associated material … processes” (emphasis added).

According to this view, an IT innovation is constituted through the communicative actions of the innovators. Yet, the materiality of an IT innovation is understood as an epiphenomenon that is only mobilized after the communicative act. Thus, greater emphasis is placed on symbolic over material aspects of the constitution of IT innovation.

Recent research on the communicative constitution of organizations (CCO) has argued that the symbolic-material duality can be overcome by focusing on the ways by which organizations evolve through associations among human and nonhuman actors whose present/local interactions transcend space and time (Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009). As Cooren and Fairhurst (2009: 123–124, emphasis in the original) explain:

interactions are never completely local. Instead, they are what we call, using a neologism, “dis-local,” that is, their local achievement is always mobilizing a variety of entities – documents, rules, protocols, architectural elements, machines, technological devices – that dislocate, i.e., “put out of place” (Webster's New Encyclopedic Dictionary, p. 289) what initially appeared to be “in place,” i.e., local. … the “here and now,” which is traditionally privileged in the micro-analyses of organizational interactions, is always contaminated by the “there and then” (whether in the past or future). However, and this is the main point of our argument, this “there and then” was or will be another “here and now.” … the immanent (micro) is also always already transcendent (macro).

The key goal here is to pay attention not only to what human actors are doing in interaction, but also to what speaks or acts through them, i.e., what constitutes them and what they constitute in the continuum of their communicative actions (Cooren, 2006; Cooren et al., 2012; Robichaud, 2006). Thus, there is a need to study the different ways by which nonhuman actors (e.g. information technology) participate in the communicative actions of human actors; what matters and is meaningful to human actors is materiality always already transcendent in their conversation (Cooren et al., 2012). Human actors are not simply and only enacting a given situation based on their sensemaking activities (Weick, 1979), they are also giving a voice to the nonhuman actors that transcend those sensemaking activities by pointing at what matters and what is immaterial in narrative texts.

It is, thus, crucial to remain as open as possible about who or what appears to act or be active in a given situation. Human actors are certainly active in co-constructing and co-orienting to a given situation (Taylor, Groleau, Heaton, & Van Every, 2001; Taylor & Robichaud, 2004). However, it will also be crucial to recognize that they are not alone in this process. What appears to materialize itself in a given discussion should also be deemed as what participates in the co-construction of the situation. For instance, if someone brings out the poor performance of an IT project in a discussion, this materializes itself in a discussion — i.e. it matters to at least one person who starts to speak on behalf of the material object (i.e. the IT), making this object present and active in the conversation. An object that suddenly appears to matter in a given discussion might, for instance, in<sup>fl</sup>uence or even dictate how the interaction unfolds because it shows how something might be highly consequential regarding the situation at hand. That is, human actors are enabled and/or constrained actors vis-à-vis the material consequences of their actions (Pentland & Singh, 2012).

The next section provides a more detailed description of how the CCO perspective has been applied in the analysis of IT innovations within the English NPfIT. The section starts with a short description of the English National Programme for IT (NPfIT) as a background to the analysis. This is followed by a description of the methods for data analysis. This is then followed by an in-depth analysis of the data using the CCO perspective.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Context of the research: the English NPfIT

The NPfIT was initiated in the context of the reforms implemented by the Blair government in the English National Health Service (NHS) in 1998 (NHS Executive, 1998). The central vision of the NPfIT was to standardize the previously fragmented IT delivery in all NHS organizations by introducing an integrated infrastructure (Currie & Guah, 2007).

This ambitious and wide-ranging information strategy included a vision of lifelong electronic health records for every person in the country, and 24/7 online access to patient records and information about best clinical practice, for all NHS clinicians, enabling “genuinely seamless care for patients through GPs, hospitals and community services sharing information across the NHS information highway” (Department of Health (DoH), 2002: 9). Following consultation with a wide range of stakeholders a 700-page speci<sup>fi</sup>cation for the architecture of the NPfIT was produced (Department of Health (DoH), 2003).

The architecture of the NPfT consisted of two main elements. The first was the Local Detailed Care Record, for use within local healthcare communities where the overwhelming majority of patient care was delivered. This was intended to integrate and coordinate the information which needed to be available to community care centers, general practitioners (i.e. family doctors), and hospitals (such as pathology test results, drugs prescribed or hospital discharge noti<sup>fi</sup>cation), while enabling both managers and medical professionals to deliver better quality services. The second element was the National Summary Care

Record which aimed, for example, to support emergency care for people injured or taken ill while away from home. The NPfIT was also intended to provide additional services, such as electronic transmission of prescriptions, an email and directory service for all NHS staff (NHSmail), computer accessible X-rays and other medical <sup>fi</sup>les (Picture Archiving Communications Systems), a facility for patients to book <sup>fi</sup>rst outpatient appointments electronically (Choose & Book) and a broadband network (see Fig. 1).

The strategy adopted was to break the programme into two clusters comprised of <sup>fi</sup>ve regions, namely, the London and the Southern cluster and the North-East, Eastern, North-West and West Midlands cluster. Contracts were then put out to tender for systems-integrator-led consortia to act as local service providers for each region. The <sup>fi</sup>ve contracts were originally awarded to <sup>fi</sup>ve different companies, including Accenture, Fujitsu, Computer Sciences Corporation (CSC), and Capital Care Alliance (CCA) in joint with British Telecommunications (BT). CCA left the programme in 2006 and BT took over the London region entirely (NAO, 2006). BT also held the two major contracts for supplying IT services at the national level, those for the N3 broadband network and the National Data Spine, the backbone of national databases and functions. The four remaining service providers subcontracted some areas of their work to smaller, more specialized companies.

Because there were constant changes in both the material con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the NPfIT and the way different information systems were being procured and how the overall programme was being managed, in 2006, the National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce (NAO) was commissioned to examine the progress being made in delivering the various information systems of the NPfIT against original plans and costs (NAO, 2006).

Following the 2006 NAO report, the House of Commons assigned the Committee of Public Accounts (CPA) to examine the progress made by the English Department of Health in implementing the NPfIT. At the <sup>fi</sup>rst meeting, the evidence presented is concerned with developments during the period 2006–2007 (CPA, 2007), whereas at the second meeting the evidence is concerned with developments during the period 2007–2009 (CPA, 2009).

The evidence presented at the two meetings amounts to more than 200 pages (CPA, 2007, 2009) and is accompanied by a series of other texts, including three National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce reports (NAO, 2006, 2008, 2011) and three Health Committee reports (HC, 2007a, 2007b, 2007c), which together amount to more than 700 pages of evidence. This evidence covers the evolution of the NPfIT from its initiation in 1998 to its several phases of development and implementation across different healthcare organizations in the English NHS up until 2011 — the date of the last National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce report.

## 3.2. Methods of data analysis

This paper drew on rhetorical analysis (Gill & Whedbee, 1997) to analyze the data presented at the two CPA meetings. The focus of the analysis was on the “enthymemes” (Heracleous & Barrett, 2001) of the two key stakeholders participating in the meetings, namely, the CPA members and the NPfIT project leaders. Enthymemes are rhetorical structures of argumentation and are usually not fully expressed, with one or more of their premises being taken for granted or assumed by the audience (Eemeren, Grootendorst, Jackson, & Jacobs, 1997). By paying speci<sup>fi</sup>c attention to enthymemes that as a whole constitute human actors' rhetorical strategies, the aim was to elucidate these actors' own arguments used in favor of or against certain courses of action.

The method of identifying communication processes in the NPfIT is outlined in Table 1 and explained below in more detail.

First, central themes were searched for in the evidence presented in historical narrative texts. This involved exploring each of the two CPA meeting reports (CPA, 2007, 2009) for central themes that were explicitly stated or assumed and then comparing the results inter-textually to discover themes that were present in other texts including the three National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce reports (NAO, 2006, 2008, 2011) and the three Health Committee reports (HC, 2007a, 2007b, 2007c). Secondly, the connections among these central themes were analyzed and their role in communication processes identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Then the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the <sup>fi</sup>rst two steps were combined to determine the enthymemes underlying the argumentation between the CPA members and the NPfIT leaders. Lastly, inter-textual analysis was conducted, by moving from the part (single reports) to the whole (discourses constituted of the totality of evidence) and vice versa, in order to examine the commonalities and differences between different patterns of argumentation.

E1 and E2 represent persistent enthymemes of the two key stakeholders' argumentation. The main role of these enthymemes is normative, acting to support or reject practice-oriented arguments that are more openly and explicitly stated in A1 and A2. The contents of A1, A2 and CA (Consequential Action) were found to shift from the time of the first meeting (CPA. 2007) to the second (CPA. 2009) in response to the materialization of certain actions over others, including actions towards the physical implementation of NPfIT technologies at different locations in the NHS.

In this sense, the CA points to the materiality of communicative actions on the technologies of the NPfIT project. This is consequential in the sense that it transcended what mattered and what was meaningful for certain communities in a particular context (Brummans, 2006; Robichaud, 2006). Materiality was, thus, conceptualized as that which transcended future courses of action in relation to the concerns and values of particular communities (Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009). To identify this materiality, an analysis of the compromises and proposed solutions to different concerns raised in the two meetings, as well as other reports on the NPfIT, was conducted.

The key matters of concern emerging from discussions across the two meetings (CPA, 2007, 2009) and from written evidence presented in other reports (HC, 2007a, 2007b, 2007c; NAO, 2006, 2008, 2011) are linked to some common issues found in the literature. In particular, one of the key matters of concern was whether to opt for a centralized or decentralized solution (e.g. see Sambamurthy & Zmud, 1999) with subsequent issues of control emerging in the discussions (Ciborra, 2001). Also, another matter of concern was around the competency of suppliers to deliver on time and efforts to standardize local applications (e.g. see Hanseth, Jacucci, Grisot, & Aanestad, 2006), with associated risks of exceeded timescales, costs, and returns on investing in IT in the public sector (e.g. see Dufner, Holley, & Reed, 2002). These concerns were raised in the communications between the CPA and the NPfIT project leaders and transcended what mattered in the NPfIT. ## 4. Analysis

As mentioned above, the analysis of the discussions between the CPA and the NPfIT project leaders can be grouped under two key concerns: (a) the option for a centralized vs. a decentralized solution and associated questions of control and security, (b) the competency of the suppliers and efforts to standardize local applications. Arguments around these key matters of concerns were driven by the respective enthymemes of the two key stakeholder groups. Brie<sup>fl</sup>y, the CPA members' arguments were driven by a goal to minimize delays in the delivery of key information systems and decrease the overall costs of the NPfIT, while securing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (including both <sup>fi</sup>nancial savings and service improvements). The NPfIT project leaders' arguments were driven by a goal to centrally integrate and control the previously fragmented IT delivery in all NHS organizations. The two enthymemes were not necessarily opposed but they were not totally aligned either, as it is illustrated by the argumentation between these two stakeholder groups around the two key matters of concern. In the process of time, though, the materialization of certain actions over others and at different locations in the NHS led to speci<sup>fi</sup>c consequential actions that constituted the innovation trajectory of the NPfIT.

## 4.1. Matter of concern A: Centralized vs decentralized solution

The <sup>fi</sup>rst key matter of concern raised in both the <sup>fi</sup>rst and the second meetings was whether the centralized solution that the project leaders opted for was in fact causing a lot of problems in the NPfIT's timescale and costs. “Why are you seeking to impose such a massive system from above on the NHS instead of building on local initiatives?” the CPA Chairman asked at the opening of the <sup>fi</sup>rst meeting (CPA, 2007: Ev2<sup>1</sup>). The project leaders' response and general belief was that the centralized solution would improve control and mitigate security risks, while also minimizing costs to future upgrades (CPA, 2007: Ev2). In addition, the Director General of IT at the time, the Managing Director of BT Health, and the NHS Leadership all offered strong assurances that the centralized solution was the best option for improving security, while arguing that unlawful access to the National Data Spine would be nearly impossible (CPA, 2007: Ev10, Ev89, and Ev121 respectively).

However, doubts were raised by different NHS Trusts about the decision to create a national data spine for storing and transferring information, as opposed to allowing individual NHS Trusts to handle their own data locally. For example, a group of 13,500 users in the Greater Manchester region argued that they were getting real bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by sharing a divided system already in place across NHS Trusts (CPA, 2007: Ev55). In addition, the UK Computing Research Council, and the British Computer Society added that a single system accessible by all NHS employees from all Trusts maximizes rather than minimizes the risk of a security breach (CPA, 2007: Ev125 and Ev38 respectively).

The key debate between a centralized and decentralized strategy continued on during the second CPA meeting. The centralized strategy raised the question as to how much scope there was for the NHS more generally, and for individual NHS Trusts more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to renegotiate contracts for national services. The following is a discussion from the second CPA meeting pointing at this very issue (CPA, 2009: Ev11):

CPA member: If I earmark a hospital, I do not really have an option other than to go to BT for the core services, do I? … On the National Data Spine, again you are using the Oracle database server platform for that, and presumably at some point in time that might become very expensive to use. Is it a realistic option to <sup>fi</sup>nd another supplier?

Chief Operating Officer for the NPfIT: Bearing in mind that we have an enterprise-wide agreement with Oracle to supply unlimited – within the parlance it is all you can eat – so as much of the Oracle products as you can buy at a <sup>fi</sup>xed price.

CPA member: That may be a very good deal, but if you do not like the deal you are offering is it realistic or sensible or highly disruptive to go elsewhere?

Chief Operating Officer for the NPfIT: It would be disruptive. That particular decision as to which database platform they use is the supplier's, since they are getting it for nothing effectively.

CPA member: So in one case you are stuck with BT, in the other you are stuck with Oracle.

In fact, in both cases, BT was the major supplier and, thus, essentially, the NPfIT was “stuck” with BT for national IT services. Through the maxim of “centralized and secured” networks and “all you can eat” database systems, NPfIT project leaders presented BT as the best option among alternative IT options. The NPfIT project leaders argued that the main problem was not the centralized strategy – including the fact that BT was the main and only supplier at the national level – but rather that, the problem rested with the local service providers and their repeating failure to deliver on time and according to the needs of individual users.

## 4.2. Matter of concern B: Competency of suppliers and standardization of local applications

This was the second key matter of concern that the CPA picked upon during both CPA meetings, especially after the failure of two of the <sup>fi</sup>ve local service providers to deliver. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Accenture had reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cant losses and eventually left the NPfIT (HC, 2007a: Ev3, Ev8, Ev105, Ev121), followed by two of its subcontractors, IDX and Commedica (CPA, 2007: 6, 15, Ev8). Following in the footsteps of Accenture, Fujitsu also left the programme in 2008 leaving the NPfIT with only two main suppliers, CCA and BT, to carry the responsibility for major components of the programme. In turn, the CPA cautioned that “the programme's high dependence on just two major suppliers has implications for the programme's capacity and capability, and for the department's leverage” (CPA, 2009: 3). There were also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant delays to the two main patient administration systems, Millennium and Lorenzo, supplied by BT and CSC Alliance respectively, with iSoft (the Lorenzo subcontractor), also showing signs of <sup>fi</sup>nancial strain (CPA, 2007: 15, Ev7, Ev105, Ev121).

During the second CPA meeting, and through an oral exchange between Fujitsu's Group Director of UK Public Services and the Chairman of the CPA, it became clear that the suppliers' main issue was that, apparently, the NPfIT was always late in their installment payments, while there were repeated requests for system customizations not agreed in the initial contracts (CPA, 2009: Ev15–16). The same arguments were being made by Accenture a few years earlier (CPA, 2007: Ev102).

The NPfIT project leaders supported the arguments of the suppliers by bringing forward the issue of standardization vs. customization. Their general belief was that the key challenge faced by the suppliers and by the NPfIT more broadly was “the level of customisation that the suppliers are having to make for individual organizations,” which was found to be “more extensive than we imagined at the beginning of the programme” (CPA, 2009: Ev2). The Chief Executive of the NHS continued in the same exchange (CPA, 2009: Ev3):

Chief Executive of the NHS: … I think we have more experience now, more knowledge and understanding, and we are working much better with certainly the two suppliers that are left and understand each other, I think, quite well, and have a level of trust and understanding based on a lot of detailed work of testing what people are saying. That is the <sup>fi</sup>rst thing, and secondly, we have a product now, or are very close to having a product. I think some members of the Committee will have seen the Lorenzo demonstration. It has been very widely welcomed by the clinical community.

Chairman of the CPA: Let me stop you there. Thank you for arranging that demonstration last week but let us remind ourselves that Lorenzo has not been deployed in a single hospital yet. It is one thing to show it to members of the Committee in Richmond House, and it works; it is quite different to deploy it into a busy hospital and have the thing not crashing. How much con<sup>fi</sup>dence can we really have in this? There have been so many delays up to now with Lorenzo. Convince us that you would be right to keep faith with it.

The Chairman's question of con<sup>fi</sup>dence was repeated by almost all CPA members (CPA, 2009: Ev5–9, Ev12, Ev16–19), who seemed unconvinced that the NPfIT could be indeed turned around any time soon and begin to show real bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in actual organizational settings. The general belief of the CPA was that the projected value of the NPfIT was questionable and, therefore, all plans for the general roll-out of systems should be stopped until that value was re-established (CPA, 2009: 5–6).

In consequence to these developments, many Trusts had begun to implement a number of locally-driven “interim” solutions, until the general roll-out of the NPfIT reconvened. From the NPfIT project leaders' point of view, these interim solutions should have been subjected to both technical and legal control mechanisms to ensure that no IT choices worsened conditions in the NPfIT and more broadly the NHS — i.e. by adding to interoperability problems and by increasing the <sup>fi</sup>nancial burden of the public sector (CPA, 2009: Ev3, Ev6). However, although the intentions of the NPfIT leaders were fused with efforts to salvage the NPfIT, the reality was that it was fraught with problems due to the complexity of the NHS. Different organizational settings required different functionality from their IT systems and, thus, the vision of a national, standardized IT system was problematic.

## 4.3. Consequential action: Constituting what matters and is meaningful for the NPfIT

The concerns about the centralized solution for the NPfIT, the challenges faced by the suppliers, and the associated delays and increasing costs to the programme, led the CPA to propose a locally-driven model, while giving more autonomy to NHS Trusts and others to select from a wider range of information systems than those currently available, provided that these conformed to national standards.

Many user communities, including NHS Trusts, and also broader medical bodies such as, the Royal College of General Practitioners and the British Medical Association, recognized the need to move away from a centrally-controlled national infrastructure towards a set of interoperable local systems (HC, 2007b Ev 93–94, and Ev 41–45 respectively). Taking this evidence on board, the CPA argued that (CPA, 2009: 6):

unless the position on care records system deployments improves appreciably in the very near future (i.e. within the next six months), the Department should assess the <sup>fi</sup>nancial case for allowing Trusts to put forward applications for central funding for alternative systems compatible with the objectives of the programme.

In the end, due to this increasing pressure by both user communities and CPA members, BT, the key supplier of national IT services, and one of the two remaining local service providers, put forward plans to move towards a “best of breed” approach. This was accepted by NPfIT project leaders and the necessary contractual arrangements were drafted as early as 2008 (NAO, 2008). The best of breed approach was to use different system suppliers for different NHS care settings.

At the same time, the CPA stressed that the decision to opt for a best of breed approach should be able to demonstrate the economic value and manageable risk of such a system, before they are selected for implementation. On this note, the NPfIT project leaders asked the CPA to show more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the remaining suppliers and to consider keeping systems already deployed by the NPfIT to avoid generating more problems than solutions (CPA, 2009: Ev16–17).

Despite these proposals, however, due to the history of suppliers' failure to deliver on time, there were a series of public statements made by policymakers in relation to the NPfIT that in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the CPA's <sup>fi</sup>nal recommendations. In one example, the Shadow Health Secretary<sup>2</sup> invited NPfIT project leaders to “have the grace to admit that the programme has been an abject failure” that had “sti<sup>fl</sup>ed IT innovation in hospitals” (Whit<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2009). These criticisms eventually led to the CPA to question the objectives and scope of the NPfIT and to suggest instead that the DoH should even reconsider the need for a national IT programme.

These communicative actions generated consequences for the material con<sup>fi</sup>guration of innovations in the NPfIT. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, although there had been considerable success in national level deployments – especially in relation to the N3 broadband network and Spine – deployments at the local level were wrought with failures. Deployments of local patient administration records fell below 30% across the three clusters of London, South, and North and Midlands, and most successful deployments came from interim solutions (NAO, 2008). Most importantly, the contracts originally agreed with Fujitsu and later BT for the London and South regions for the delivery of patient administration systems were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduced. Generally, the DoH expected to make more use of existing IT systems by linking them together rather than replacing them wholesale (NAO, 2011).

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in tune with research that reports on direct engagement with information technologies in the NPfIT (Greenhalgh et al., 2010; Hendy et al., 2007; Robertson et al., 2010). For instance, Robertson et al. (2010) conducted a service evaluation of three centrally procured hospital applications (Lorenzo and Cerner Millennium for acute hospitals and RiO for mental health) across 12 healthcare trusts in London, North, Midlands and East, and Southern England. As they report (Robertson et al., 2010: 4570):

Both NHS Trust interviewees and local service providers expressed fears that progress in developing the central NHS IT infrastructure and shared detailed electronic health records could be lost, with a considerable waste of public money and effort already expended … major perceived risk now was a possible radical swing back to a “bottom-up” approach that would leave responsibility for NHS IT developments with individual trusts and at best aspires to link multiple local systems using interoperability standards.

As this and other studies of the NPfIT have shown, the innovation trajectory of the NPfIT has been constituted through historical communicative actions between key stakeholders as mediated by the materiality of technology on the situated practices of different organizations in the NHS.

## 5. Discussion & implications

The paper offered an analysis of how an IT innovation is communicatively constituted and examined the consequences of this constitution for the technologies and organizations involved. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, although originally found to diverge on their arguments on what mattered in the NPfIT, the CPA members and the NPfIT project leaders eventually arrived at a communicative constitution of future courses of action: the NPfIT should be deployed in smaller, more manageable and locally-led systems, in line with healthcare organizations' local practices. This materialization was constituted over historical processes of communication in associated narrative texts, with the various technologies being deployed transcending the local face-to-face argumentation between human actors. In other words, these historical communication processes did not just represent the abstract/symbolic arguments of the CPA members and NPfIT project leaders as expressed in situated interactions, but rather they materialized what mattered and what did not matter outside those interactions for the whole of the English NHS.

For instance, in relation to the concern of opting for a centralized procurement and control of local systems, the NPfIT project leaders argued that this option would provide lower costs overall, better control and security over the whole project, and higher integration and standardization of systems. The CPA members argued that such an option was highly in<sup>fl</sup>exible and added complexity to the project, while also increasing costs due to delays of delivering systems and the disruptions to existing work. In the end, as reported in the more recent National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce report (NAO, 2011), although the N3 broadband network and the National Data Spine had been fully delivered, the National Summary Care Record – i.e. the core of the NPfIT for all clinical services – was only delivered in 10% of healthcare organizations (Robertson et al., 2010: 17).

An extensive independent review of the NPfIT found that the main reason for this failure was that, “A system that is appropriate for a district general hospital may not be adequate for use in a complex teaching hospital with tertiary care, academic and research needs” (Hays et al., 2009: 82). In other words, the complexity of the English NHS – with distinct materialities present in participating organizations – made it impossible to just impose a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed, national IT system from the top

Evidence that the (bottom-up) decentralized approach to developing national health IT systems works better than the (top-down) fully integrated systems envisioned and rejected in the UK can also be found in the USA (Adler-Milstein, McAfee, Bates, & Jha, 2008), but also in India (Sahay, Monteiro, & Aanestad, 2009), and other developing countries (Braa, Monteiro, & Sahay, 2004). The bottom-up (i.e. locally-driven) decentralized approach to health IT innovations is preferred because these innovations “unfold and are shaped over time through interactions with the healthcare context” (Cho & Mathiassen, 2007: 747) and they depend on a “co-constitutive relationship between the users and the technology” (Jensen & Aanestad, 2007: 673).

Despite these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, research on health IT innovations has primarily focused on the socio-political aspects of IT diffusion and adoption, with great emphasis being placed on the communicative actions of key actors. This paper has moved this line of research forward by showing how those communicative actions are not solely driven by socio-political aspects, but rather are co-constituted by the materiality of the technology. This renewed understanding of health IT innovation offers rich insights into why certain innovations drift into successful trajectories whereas others fail.

In the NPfIT case study, the NPfIT project leaders failed to acknowledge this co-constitution by refusing to speak for the materiality that was relevant to the various healthcare organizations in the English NHS. This eventually led the latter to resist the NPfIT project. For example, in 2006 the Royal College of Nurses (RCN) survey of 4500 members found 74% had not been adequately consulted about the relevance of the NPfIT to their practices (HC, 2007b: Ev97). Similarly in another survey conducted by Ipsos MORI in February 2006, 64% of GPs answered that they were “unaware” of when they would be expected to start uploading their patients' clinical details to the national database as part of the National Care Records Service (HC, 2007b: Ev158). Using the excuse that they did not understand the scale and objectives of the IT innovations proposed by the NPfIT, these and other healthcare professions began to express their resistance to the NPfIT in public forums (e.g. Ford, 2011; Greenhalgh & Bowden, 2011; West, 2009).

By 2009, this lack of substance and relevance of the NPfIT (cf. Cooren et al., 2012), led many healthcare organizations to start implementing their own systems, thus, also communicating what mattered to them. This option became a possibility after the repeated failures of the local suppliers to deliver on time and according to the needs of individual healthcare organizations. Even though these local service contracts were repeatedly audited and assessed, there was no a priori certainty around what, how and when these contracts were supposed to deliver. Rather, there were constant negotiations around both the contracts themselves and the competencies of the suppliers — with two major suppliers, namely, Accenture and Fujitsu, leaving the programme, with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant consequences for the rest of the NPfIT. There are now strong arguments put forward that alternative solutions implemented in different healthcare organizations will be hard to replace as time goes by (NAO, 2011: 8):

With fewer systems being provided through the Programme and more use being made of a variety of existing systems, there is an increased risk of not achieving adequate compatibility across the NHS to effectively support joined up healthcare. The Department estimates that achieving interoperability will cost at least £220 million.

This was exactly what the NPfIT project leaders feared the most and fought against: that too much choice would lead to interoperability problems and more costs. However. based on figures provided by the National Audit Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, even with a series of agreed reductions in the number of systems being delivered by the remaining suppliers, “under the terms of the current contracts it is unlikely that the remaining work can be completed by the end of the contracts in 2015-16” (NAO, 2011: 10).

In summary, the local communicative actions between the NPfIT project leaders and the CPA members constituted, and were constituted by, both the materiality of NPfIT technologies and the end users of those technologies across time and different locations in the NHS. In other words, the (micro) actions of the NPfIT project leaders and the CPA members were always already transcended by the (macro) actions of the network of actors in the NHS, as voiced in associated narrative texts. Whatever arguments and decisions were being made by the two main stakeholder groups were always “tele-acted” on by the materiality (e.g. legacy systems, interim systems, willingness of users to adopt new technologies, budget, etc.) that preceded them and that were (consequentially) due to follow them (Cooren, 2006; Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009). The current outcomes of the NPfIT are, thus, not merely con<sup>fi</sup>ned to technical issues or to solely leadership issues, but rather to the collective actions of the network of actors in the NHS in relative and transactional terms (Cooren et al., 2012; Pentland & Singh, 2012). The next subsection explores the implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5.1. Implications for theorizing sociomateriality in IS research

Information systems (IS) research on sociomateriality draws from a long tradition in sociotechnical studies (e.g. Callon, 1986; Latour, 2005; Law, 2004). For instance, Latour (2005) has long argued that phenomena cannot be adequately understood unless we abandon arti<sup>fi</sup>cial distinctions between human and nonhuman actors and direct our attention to the empirical reality that all are joined together in an intricate network of associations that develop momentum over time. This is exactly what early conceptualizations of sociomateriality in IS research have sought to do: to understand “the constitutive entanglement of the social and the material in everyday organizational life” (Orlikowski, 2007: 1438; see also Orlikowski. 2010: Orlikowski & Scott. 2008).

Despite this commendable objective, these early conceptualizations of sociomateriality in IS research have been criticized for a lack of a clear ontology of what exactly constitutes sociomaterial agency (Mutch, 2013). This criticism is reminiscent of the early criticisms of ANT theory, in that it is directed at distinctions of what is material and what is human, with humans having intentions and social structures driving their actions, and nonhumans merely mimicking, or following instructions of, human action (Collins & Kusch, 1998). Nonhuman agency can only be found in resistance through the material affordances and constraints inscribed in technological objects, which mediate human agency (Pickering, 1995).

Leonardi (2013: 66) has further supported this criticism by arguing that, although it is insightful to attempt not to separate the social from the material in IS research, “this philosophical stance presents empirical problems because actors in the world do not perceive the material and the social … as interpenetrated entities.” A solution to this problem is to embrace an “analytical dualism” between structure and action, whereby materiality is a “structural property” or “affordance” and human agency is an action that follows structure and transforms it (Leonardi, 2013). This solution, he argues, “accords with [human] actors' categorization with and experience of phenomena” (Leonardi (2013: 66)). Similar arguments are made elsewhere (Faraj & Azad, 2012; Robey et al., 2012).

Although insightful, this ‘solution’ still suffers from the epistemological problem of how IS researchers can identify an affordance in the context of their research, and where should they start, methodologically, to trace the relationality between human actors and materiality.

The CCO perspective offers a way forward by placing emphasis on the triadic relationship between narrative texts, human, and nonhuman actors (Robichaud, 2006). Through the interaction with nonhuman actors (information technologies, etc.), human actors generate narrative texts (e.g. IT project reports) that act as macro-actor voices for organizing a broader organizational <sup>fi</sup>eld. In this sense, materiality is not a structural property that is de<sup>fi</sup>ned a priori, but rather it emerges in time and space through the consequences of human–nonhuman interaction. Thus, though important to maintain an analytical distinction between the social and the material, their relationality can only be studied in their consequential actions over time and space, as those become part of collectively produced narrative texts.

The emergence of sociomaterial agency can only be studied in the historical narrative networks of human and nonhuman actors (cf. Constantinides & Barrett, 2012). This enables an understanding of the dynamics between the ‘here and now’ (or micro action) with the ‘there and then’ (or macro action — not structure) (Cooren & Fairhurst, 2009). As illustrated through the case, what appears to matter at a speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time (i.e. a national programme for IT) can lose parts of its materiality because interactants come to the conclusion that what could have been a matter of concern for them does not happen to be so (i.e. a communicative constitution towards locally-led solutions). The point here is not to say that a certain materiality suddenly vanishes, but that it is transformed from something that is meaningful ‘here and now’ to something that is immaterial ‘there and then’. Not mattering anymore means, as pointed out previously, that certain matters will not substantiate any concern, but also that they will lose their materiality as matters of concern. In other words, as a technological infrastructure – a material object – the NPfIT will not lose its materiality, but as a concern, it will, precisely because it does not matter anymore in the collective practices of those involved, as reported in associated narrative texts. In this sense, intentionality emerges out of the relationships in which human actors <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves connected to various material objects and which becomes part of their collectively produced narrative texts; “any inherent [‘structural’] property of an agent, be it intentionality or power, must be described as the result of action, not its source” (Robichaud, 2006: 106). This pragmatist epistemology of the CCO perspective (Brummans 2006; Robichaud, 2006) raises implications for which methods of conduct IS researchers should employ when studying sociomateriality.

## 5.2. Implications for conducting CCO-informed research in sociomateriality

As discussed above, by adopting a relational ontology, sociomaterial agency is understood to emerge in a triadic relationship between human and nonhuman actors, and the narrative texts in which the delegation between the <sup>fi</sup>rst two occurs (i.e. acting on behalf of one another). The complex relationality between human and nonhuman actors in IT innovation can be historically traced in narrative texts (e.g. an IT project report). In other words, the fact that the act of innovating is aimed at someone (a group of human actors) with something (a material object of innovation), ipso facto embeds the act of innovating within a narrative context — a text (project report) presenting a conceivable development (to slightly paraphrase Robichaud, 2006: 111).

Unlike more general organizational research, IS research is always focused on speci<sup>fi</sup>c material objects with abundant narrative texts reporting on their evolution, as in the case of the NPfIT. This means that IS research is in a position to not only be informed by the CCO perspective, but to also inform it by providing empirical case studies that can move theoretical developments forward.

Methodologically this means that studies in IT innovation need to adopt discursive methods of data gathering and analysis, while acknowledging the voice of nonhuman actors as tele-acted through narrative texts. The methodology employed in this paper is a useful extension of extant discursive approaches to studying IT innovations. First, by focusing on the underlying enthymemes or rhetorical structures of argumentation of key human actors around an IT innovation, this approach enables the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the transcendent values and beliefs that drive the practice-based arguments of those actors. These enthymemes are only discernible though a historical understanding of the (con-)text of an IT innovation, requiring a longitudinal, in-depth hermeneutic analysis of central themes across different narrative texts (e.g. policy documents on national IT strategies). Second, this approach enables the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of patterns in the communicative actions of different human actors through a rhetorical analysis of the oral and written exchanges between them. Through such rhetorical analysis, this approach enables an understanding of the shifts in both the enthymemes and practice-based arguments of different actors. Even though enthymemes appear to be more stable over time, their nature can shift to some extent, as central beliefs are adapted to the new materiality of IT innovation. which mediates and is mediated by the interactions between human actors and the new information systems being introduced. This is the third and most important aspect of this methodological approach to studying IT innovation, namely, the focus on the consequential actions of networks of human and nonhuman actors as those become visible in speci<sup>fi</sup>c narrative texts. This approach enables the study of sociomateriality in temporal narrative texts that speak on behalf of a network of actors, as their actions scale up from micro (present) to macro (past– future) interactions.

## 6. Conclusion & re<sup>fl</sup>ections

This paper has adopted a CCO perspective to studying IT innovation. It has been argued that the CCO perspective helps to overcome the tendency of extant discursive approaches to stress symbolic over material aspects of IT innovation. The paper has argued that IT innovation is constituted in the triadic relationship between human and nonhuman actors and the narrative texts in which the delegation between the <sup>fi</sup>rst two occurs.

This perspective has been applied in the analysis of historical narrative texts in the development and implementation of the English NPfIT over the period 1998–2011. The analysis of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings extends current literature by showing how innovations in the NPfIT were communicatively constituted over consecutive narrative texts that spoke on behalf of key human and nonhuman actors in the English NHS. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, although CPA members and NPfIT project leaders seemed to be making the key decisions, their communicative actions were always tele-acted on by the materiality (e.g. legacy systems, interim systems, willingness of users to adopt new technologies, budget, etc.) that preceded them and that were (consequentially) due to follow them. The interactions between these human and nonhuman actors constituted the innovation trajectory of the NPfIT.

Although, the discussion has drawn primarily on <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the English NPfIT case, the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings are relevant to other public and private sector settings. Further research could examine other IT innovation efforts by paying attention at how technology becomes materialized and meaningful through historically competing communicative actions, and how such mattering and sensemaking become visible in collectively produced narrative texts. Such longitudinal research could help develop rich insights into the dynamics of IT innovation.

Re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, it is acknowledged that the research has not empirically engaged with information technologies, but rather relied solely on narrative texts to develop its key arguments. This limitation is recognized and the paper has made a conscious effort to cross-reference its <sup>fi</sup>ndings with empirical research on the actual adoption of various technologies of the NPfIT in speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational settings (Greenhalgh et al., 2010; Hendy et al., 2007; Robertson et al., 2010). More importantly, however, the onto-epistemological assumptions upon which the paper grounds its arguments challenge the ‘need’ to materially engage with technology in order to justify arguments. The key emphasis is on “relationality rather than thinghood” (Cooper, 2005: 75) understood to transcend situated interactions between human and nonhuman actors across time and space. Thus, by focusing on historical narrative texts that report on those situated interactions over long periods of time, this paper has addressed this limitation.

The use of narrative texts to study and understand sociomateriality raises important ethical questions on attributions of agency (Brummans, 2006). As evident from the two CPA reports, even between the CPA members and the NPfIT project leaders, there were attributions to agency – i.e. who or what did what and with what consequences (e.g. Accenture failed to meet the terms of contract, the software had bugs, the NPfIT ran over the budget etc.) – that were continuously questioned. These questions of agency are communal in the sense that, actors participate with their own enthymemes and practice-based arguments, but are always confronted by those of other actors. At the same time, however, IS researchers need to ask themselves which of those actors are being conceptualized as micro or macro from an ongoing stream of activities, and who participates in this ‘authority’ of agency conceptualization (Constantinides, Chiasson, & Introna, 2012). Our accounts of organizational and IS phenomena produce our individual research agendas, our sociomaterial interactions, and our collective future. We, thus, need to ethically assess our accounts in light of the kind of research <sup>fi</sup>elds we wish to produce.
