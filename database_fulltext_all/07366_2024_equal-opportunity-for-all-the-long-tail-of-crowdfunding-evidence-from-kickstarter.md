# EQUAL OPPORTUNITY FOR ALL? THE LONG TAIL OF CROWDFUNDING: EVIDENCE FROM KICKSTARTER<sup>1</sup>

Hilah Geva Coller School of Management, Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv, ISRAEL {hilahlev@tauex.tau.ac.il}

Ohad Barzilay Similarweb, Tel-Aviv, ISRAEL {ohadbr@gmail.com}

Anat Goldstein Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Ariel University, Ariel, ISRAEL {anatgo@ariel.ac.il}

Gal Oestreicher-Singer Coller School of Management, Tel-Aviv University, Tel-Aviv, ISRAEL {galos@post.tau.ac.il}

Crowdfunding platforms are believed to create a more equal-opportunity environment for fundraising by removing entrance barriers found in traditional entrepreneurial markets. Our work investigates whether in creating more equal opportunities for entrepreneurs to enter the market, crowdfunding platforms also create a more equal distribution of funds across ventures. To this end, we utilized a natural experiment in the form of a policy change on Kickstarter.com that resulted in opening the market to more players. Using platform-level analysis, we show that opening the platform shifted demand toward the head of the distribution: More funds and backers became concentrated in a smaller number of head-offers (the superstar effect). Using individua backer-level analysis, we show that these changes in demand distribution are likely to have resulted from changes in investors’ investment behavior, beyond changes in the composition of the demand side (that is, changes in the types of investors entering the market). Together, our results suggest that efforts to level the playing field in crowdfunding platforms can ultimately result in a less equitable distribution of funds across market participants, and drive investors to focus their investments on a smaller set of campaigns.

Keywords: Long tail, crowdfunding, Kickstarter, platform openness, policy change, peer economy, peerto-peer platforms, Gini, demand distribution, natural experiment, superstar effect, quality signals

## Introduction

Crowdfunding platforms enable entrepreneurs to fund their ventures by raising small amounts of money from large numbers of backers, instead of turning to angels or venture capital investors. The emergence of crowdfunding as an alternative to traditional investment channels has simplified the fundraising process and increased its accessibility, enabling a wider and more diverse range of entrepreneurs to fund their ventures. In fact, many platforms consider such inclusivity as part of their core missions, viewing it as a means of bettering society at large.<sup>2</sup> To this end, some platforms have intentionally replaced selective acceptance policies with more “open” policies that refrain from enforcing control over which campaigns can launch (Sundararajan, 2013; Howe, 2008).

Naively, one might think that opening the doors to more entrepreneurs could be enough to advance the inclusiveness goal. Indeed, as might be expected, the introduction of open acceptance policies has increased the number of suppliers operating in the market and the variety offered to consumers (Burtch et al., 2018a; Geva et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2017). Yet, to our knowledge, no research to date has empirically quantified whether inclusiveness and easier access to a crowdfunding market translate into inclusiveness in terms of the distribution of revenue and funds. Our paper aims to close this gap. Specifically, we suggest that though open acceptance policies provide prospective entrepreneurs with equal opportunities to enter the market, they might unintentionally prevent entrepreneurs from obtaining equitable access to funds, i.e., investments on the demand side of the market. In other words, we study the following question: Following the implementation of an open acceptance policy and the resulting increase in the number of campaigns, does the distribution of funds across offerings become more or less equitable?

As we are focused on changes in the concentration of funds (demand) across campaigns (products), we naturally turn to the rich literature on the long-tail phenomenon. In the context of ecommerce, papers in this stream have provided evidence that after a significant increase in supply, demand concentration can either decrease (the “long-tail” effect; e.g., Anderson, 2006; Brynjolfsson et al., 2003, among many others) or increase (the “superstar” effect; e.g., Elberse, 2008; Lee & Hosanagar, 2019). Combining these contradicting findings with considerations regarding the distinctive characteristics of the crowdfunding context, we present competing hypotheses as to whether platform openness and the associated increase in supply lead to a more or less concentrated distribution of funds and backers across campaigns. While our work focuses on the distribution of demand, our analysis controls for potential changes on the supply side of the crowdfunding platform, such as changes in quality and risk level.

To test our hypotheses, we relied on a natural experiment on the crowdfunding platform Kickstarter.com, in which the implementation of a policy change made it much easier for entrepreneurs to launch campaigns, effectively raising the level of openness on the supply side of the platform. Using both platform-level and individual backer-level analysis, we found that after Kickstarter opened its platform to include more participants, the distribution of demand became more concentrated and less equitable. Our analyses enabled us to determine that the effect indeed represents an intensification of inequality and is not merely an artifact of changes in the number of campaigns, their quality signals, and risk levels. Crucially, we also show that this shift reflects changes in backers’ behavior rather than a mere change in the types of backers operating in the market. In particular, we observe that after the policy change, recurrent backers—i.e., backers who funded campaigns both before and after the policy change—shifted the composition of their investment portfolios to include a larger portion of top-performing campaigns.

From a theoretical perspective, our work contributes to the growing body of literature on crowdfunding platforms and the established literature on the long tail of e-commerce. We add new evidence to the complex debate regarding whether an increase in the number of offerings on a platform leads to a long-tail effect or a superstar effect. Further, and even more importantly, we extend the long tail literature to a completely new context: the context of crowdfunding platforms. Whereas prior literature has primarily considered the distribution of sales across products on large-scale online retail platforms (online stores), our study is the first to focus on the context of investment decisions on crowdfunding platforms. This focus has the potential to provide meaningful new insights, as P2P crowdfunding platforms possess fundamental characteristics that distinguish them from traditional online retail platforms and create unique systems of incentives and risks that warrant investigation.

From a managerial perspective, we add to the discussion about the societal effects of the emerging sharing economy and crowdfunding platforms on equality and opportunity. Specifically, we show that equality in access does not necessarily translate into increased equality in revenue and success.

## Theoretical Background and Hypotheses

The notion of the “long tail” was introduced by Anderson (2004) to describe how demand distribution might be expected to change as a result of the emergence of the digital economy. Since then, the academic community has devoted extensive effort to understanding how demand distribution actually responds to technological advancements that drastically change market conditions. This research has not produced a clear-cut answer. More than a few studies have supported the idea that following such technological change, demand becomes less concentrated (the “long-tail effect”; e.g., Anderson, 2006; Brynjolfsson et al., 2003; Brynjolfsson et al., 2009; Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Peltier & Moreau, 2011; Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012; Zhou & Duan, 2012; Zentner et al., 2013; Kumar et al., 2014; Gallino et al., 2017; Ratchford et al., 2020). Yet numerous studies have presented evidence that contradicts this idea, showing that the digital economy has created a more concentrated distribution, favoring hit products (the “superstar” effect; e.g., Elberse, 2008; Elberse & Oberholzer-Gee, 2007; Fleder & Hosanagar, 2009; Goel et al., 2010; Smyrnaios et al., 2010; Zhong & Michahelles, 2013 Lee & Hosanagar, 2019; Tan et al., 2017; Taeuscher, 2019; Park et al., 2020; Jain & Tan, 2022). These mixed findings, obtained across various types of digital markets (e.g., traditional online retail, mobile commerce) suggest that specific market characteristics may shape the response of demand concentration to technological change. To our knowledge, we are the first to directly examine longtail and superstar effects in the context of crowdfunding. Accordingly, in what follows, we first review processes through which technology has been shown to elicit long-tail or superstar effects in other markets and then speculate on how these processes might interact with the distinctive features of crowdfunding to shape demand.

## Technological Drivers of Long-Tail/Superstar Phenomena in the Literature

In a comprehensive research commentary, Brynjolfsson et al. (2010) identified two main avenues through which technological drivers can influence demand concentration in a market. The first is by directly affecting consumers’ behavior on the demand side, that is, by altering how consumers search for and discover products (e.g., through the introduction of new search or recommendation technologies; see, e.g., Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012) and changing the types of products they seek out. Some of these demand-side technological drivers have been linked to long-tail effects, whereas others have been linked to superstar effects; interestingly, some drivers (e.g., recommendation systems; Oestreicher-Singer & Sundararajan, 2012; Lee & Hosanagar, 2019; Fleder & Hosanagar, 2009; Sun et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2010; Celma & Cano, 2008) have been shown to lead to both phenomena in different contexts.

The second avenue through which technological drivers shape demand concentration is by changing the landscape on the supply side of the market—typically, by reducing production and stocking costs, thereby enabling producers to offer a greater variety of products to consumers, which, in turn, triggers a shift in demand concentration. We suggest that the technological factor at the focus of our study—i.e., the implementation of a more open acceptance policy on a crowdfunding platform—is likely to serve as such a supply-side driver rather than as a demand-side driver, as it enables more suppliers to enter the market, thereby expanding the set of unique campaigns that users can choose to back.

Notably, a basic premise of Anderson’s (2004) original conceptualization of the long tail was that the digital economy would influence demand by increasing the variety of products that consumers can access. Yet, as noted by Tan et al. (2017), “very limited research has been done to empirically evaluate [this premise].” Even less research has explored what happens when, as in our context, technological change expands the variety of products available within a single channel, as opposed to creating a shift to a new channel, as in the case of the shift from traditional retail to e-commerce. The few studies that do address this question (Zhou & Duan, 2012, who examined online software downloading; Hinz et al., 2011, who investigated the video-on-demand market; and Tan et al., 2017, who examined the movie rental industry) do not clearly indicate whether an increase in supply within a single channel leads to a superstar or a long-tail effect—aside from the work of Tan et al. (2017), which points to a potential superstar effect.

## Distinctive Characteristics of Crowdfunding

We identify three key features of reward-based crowdfunding platforms that are likely to influence how demand concentration responds to an increase in supply. The first such feature is the all-or-nothing revenue model adopted by many crowdfunding platforms, including Kickstarter. In this model, a campaign must reach a predetermined funding goal in order to receive the funds pledged to it; if it fails to meet this goal in a given period of time, the entrepreneur and the platform do not gain any profits. The all-or-nothing model has been shown to affect various aspects of consumer behavior (Burtch et al., 2018b; Cumming et al., 2019) and, as such, is likely to shape backers’ responses to a more open acceptance policy; however, research has yet to examine this question explicitly.

A second distinctive feature of crowdfunding platforms is substantial uncertainty and risk from the buyer’s perspective, both with regard to the trustworthiness of the transaction partner (i.e., the entrepreneur/campaign owner) (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Taeuscher, 2019) and with regard to the expectation that the campaign owner will ultimately deliver the advertised product (given that crowdfunding campaigns typically raise funds for products that are still in development; Agrawal et al., 2014; Greenberg & Gerber, 2014; Mollick, 2014). Moreover, crowdfunding does not provide a “regret” option. That is, there is no ability to return the product or to cancel one’s commitment to pay (after the funding period has ended).

In general, risk has been shown to affect demand on P2P and crowdfunding platforms: A recent study by Kim et al. (2022) documented a direct association between crowdfunding campaigns’ performance and consumers’ awareness of the risks associated with those campaigns. Taeuscher (2019), in turn, provided preliminary evidence regarding the direction of the effect of risk on demand concentration, showing that the uncertainty characterizing P2P platforms is associated with higher demand concentration than would be expected according to the 80/20 Pareto principle. Though research has yet to explicitly examine how demand concentration on P2P platforms responds to an increase in supply, it seems plausible that a more open acceptance policy might intensify the uncertainty and risk associated with such platforms even further—which may lead to an increase in demand concentration.

A third distinctive characteristic of P2P platforms, in general, and crowdfunding platforms, in particular, is consumers’ high propensity for herding behavior—as many of the backers’ decisions are visible, and consumers can take these decisions into account as quality signals when deciding which campaigns to back. In particular, studies in the context of crowdlending (e.g., Zhang & Liu, 2012; Caglayan et al., 2021) present evidence of rational herding behavior. Few studies have examined drivers of herding motivation in reward-based crowdfunding, with the exception of Burtch et al. (2018b), who compared herding behavior under all-or-nothing funding models versus alternative models. Yet if we assume that crowdfunding and crowdlending platforms exhibit similar dynamics, then a more open acceptance policy is likely to affect demand concentration to the extent that it affects backers’ motivation to herd. In that case, a superstar effect might be observed.

## Hypothesis Building

The discussion above offers preliminary speculation on the ways in which the implementation of a more open acceptance policy on a crowdfunding platform (the “opening” of a platform)—which leads to an increase in supply—might affect the distribution of demand on the platform. However, by and large, current evidence does not converge to a clearcut prediction as to the direction of the effect—namely, whether the tail should become longer and fatter, in line with the classic long-tail effect, or longer and flatter, in line with the superstar effect. This question is therefore empirical by nature. To address it, we present competing hypotheses:

H1a: Platform openness leads to a more concentrated distribution of (i) funds and (ii) backers across campaigns.

H1b: Platform openness leads to a more equal distribution of (i) funds and (ii) backers across campaigns.

To understand the essence of any changes that arise in demand concentration, it is crucial to identify the user population(s) driving these changes. Are existing users changing their investment behavior in response to changes on the supply side? Or do supply-side changes trigger the entry of a new type of user, or the exit of existing users, leading to shifts in the composition of the user population and its corresponding backing behavior? To address these questions, we focus on recurrent backers, backers that were active on the platform before and after the policy change, and we study whether they adjust their investment behavior in response to changes on the supply side. In accordance with the reasoning above, we formulate two competing hypotheses regarding the direction of such an adjustment:

H2a: For recurrent backers, platform openness leads to a more concentrated distribution of demand at the individual level.

H2b: For recurrent backers, platform openness leads to a less concentrated distribution of demand at the individual level.

We note that our goal in investigating H2 is to identify the presence of change in investment behavior; we do not attempt to isolate the cognitive mechanisms that bring about this behavioral change. In general, as discussed above, such mechanisms might include herding, social influence, risk aversion, or heightened reliance on quality signals, among others.

## Empirical Context: A Policy Change on Kickstarter as a Natural Experiment

## Kickstarter.com

Kickstarter is a reward-based crowdfunding platform that follows the all-or-nothing business model discussed above. Kickstarter’s financial model is based on charging campaign owners 5% of all funds successfully raised on the platform. Since the platform’s founding in 2009, nearly 600,000 campaigns have launched on the platform, raising more than 7 billion dollars from over 22 million users.<sup>3</sup> Kickstarter’s rules<sup>4</sup> specify that each campaign must create something to share with others in one of 15 categories: Art, Comics, Crafts, Dance, Design, Fashion, Film & Video, Food, Games, Journalism, Music, Photography, Publishing, Technology and Theater. Kickstarter forbids fundraising for charity. Campaign success rates range from 22% for technology campaigns to 64% for comics campaigns, with an average success rate of 40% across categories.

## Policy Change: The Opening of the Platform

For the first five years or so after its founding, Kickstarter was selective in determining which campaigns could launch: Entrepreneurs were subjected to stringent rules that excluded numerous product categories,<sup>5</sup> and Kickstarter’s team verified compliance in a manual review process lasting several days. On June 3, 2014, Kickstarter announced<sup>6</sup> a policy change (denoted as PC) with respect to campaign screening. The PC included two components:

1. Simplified rules: Kickstarter condensed its elaborate rules for creators into three simple guidelines: (a) campaigns must create something to share with others, (b) they must be honest and clearly presented, and (c) they cannot fundraise for charity, offer financial incentives, or involve “prohibited” items. In an interview, platform cofounder and then-CEO Yancey Strickler emphasized that many of the previously excluded items (e.g., eyewear, personal care products) were now allowed and that the new policy “only prohibit[s] things that are illegal, regulated, or dangerous” (Jeffries, 2014).

2. The new “Launch Now” feature: The “Launch Now” feature introduced in June 2014 provided an automated alternative to Kickstarter’s manual evaluation process. Specifically, for each campaign, an algorithm is used to test whether the campaign is ready to go live, by analyzing thousands of campaign attributes.<sup>7</sup> If the algorithm approves the campaign, the creator can decide to “Launch Now”—that is, go live immediately—or proceed to request additional manual feedback. If the algorithm rejects the campaign, it is sent for manual review by Kickstarter's team. The decision to “Launch Now” is not made visible to potential investors. Moreover, all campaigns—even those whose owners do not select the “Launch Now” option—are subjected to the automated screening process prior to any manual review.

According to reports in several high-profile media outlets (e.g., Jeffries, 2014; Etherington, 2014), Kickstarter’s founders explicitly intended for the PC to create a “simpler, friendlier Kickstarter” (Jeffries, 2014), i.e., to make the platform more accessible, easy-to-use, and open. And indeed, as shown in the following section, our data suggest that the PC achieved its goal of increasing the platform’s openness, leading the number of campaigns offered on the platform to increase by more than 70%. Accordingly, in line with prior studies (e.g., Wessel et al., 2017), we propose that Kickstarter’s PC (the opening of the platform) serves as a natural experiment to examine the effects of platform inclusivity on demand distribution. We further note that the two components of the PC were implemented simultaneously; hence, we were unable to separate their empirical effects.

## Data and Descriptive Statistics

Our main analysis uses data collected about campaigns on Kickstarter.com that were launched between June 3, 2012 and June 3, 2015, i.e., during the two years before the PC and the year after the PC. This dataset contains a total of 173,993 campaigns. For each campaign, we collected the following data: description, financing goal, financing duration, use of a video (yes/no), amount of money pledged to the campaign, location, whether the campaign was successful (i.e., reached its financing goal), and the category the campaign belongs to. Additionally, we collected a list of campaigns that the campaign’s owner had previously created, from which we derived a variable indicating the number of successful campaigns he or she had previously owned. To capture demand, we focus on two outcome variables of interest: the total sum of pledges (“AmountPledged”) per campaign and the number of backers (“NumberOfBackers”) per campaign. Table 1 provides detailed descriptions and descriptive statistics of all variables used in our analysis, broken down by year: two years before the PC [“PC−2”]; one year before the PC [“PC−1”]; one year after the PC [“PC+1”].

Table 2 presents descriptive statistics with regard to the demand side and the supply side, at the platform level, from the periods before and after the policy change (June 3, 2014). As expected, we observe that the number of campaigns available for funding on the platform increased dramatically after the PC (by 72%, when comparing the years PC+1 versus PC−1; columns C and B in Table 2, respectively). By comparison, the number of campaigns launched during the year PC−1 (Column B) was only 14% higher than the number of campaigns launched during the year PC−2 (Column A). Together, these statistics suggest that, as expected, the opening of the platform constituted a shock to Kickstarter’s supply side in that it led to a stark increase in the number of offerings available.

We do not observe a parallel shock on the demand side. For example, we see that during PC−1, the number of backers was 12% greater than it was during PC−2. During PC+1 (Column C), in turn, the number of backers was only 13% greater than it was in PC−1. More broadly, we observe that the increase in offerings on the supply side was much stronger than the corresponding increase in demand, suggesting that the demand distribution across products is likely to have changed.

## Empirical Methodology and Results

H1: Effects of Platform Openness on the Concentration of Demand (Platform-Level Analysis)

Our identification strategy is based on a traditional difference-in-differences (DiD) approach and aims to establish a causal link between the PC and changes in the demand distribution; these changes are measured using Gini coefficients, as elaborated below.

## Construction of the Dependent Variables

In their 2010 commentary, Brynjolfsson et al. identified three measures that are commonly used to capture demand concentration: the absolute long tail, corresponding to absolute numbers of products sold; the relative long tail, corresponding to the relative share of sales of products above or below a certain rank; and the exponent of the relationship between ordinal rank and cardinal sales (which often follows a Power Law distribution). In our analysis, we followed Tan et al. (2017) and Taeuscher (2019), who adopted the relative long tail definition, and specifically used the Gini coefficient. We suggest that, as in those works, the relative long tail is the most relevant option for our context, as it is scale-invariant and more appropriate for measuring changes over time within the same platform. Thus, the outcome variables of interest for our DiD analyses were the Gini coefficients for the total sum of pledges per campaign, and for the number of backers per campaign.

Specifically, separating our data according to campaign category (so as to be able to control for this factor in our analyses), we computed the weekly category-level Gini coefficients of (1) AmountPledged (denoted as WeeklyCatGiniPledged) and (2) NumberOfBackers (denoted as WeeklyCatGiniBackers).<sup>8</sup> Complete details on the construction of these variables can be found in the following section.

## Identifying the Causal Effect of the Policy Change on the Demand Distribution

To establish a causal link between the PC and any post-PC changes observed in the demand distribution, a proper control group must be constructed. An ideal setup for establishing causality would involve comparing Kickstarter (our “treatment platform”) during the time period surrounding the PC (i.e., the “treatment”) with another crowdfunding platform (the “control platform”) that was not treated, i.e., did not undergo similar changes in its acceptance policy during this period. As we had Kickstarter data at our disposal, but no data from other platforms, we used the next best thing: Kickstarter data from a control period of time, prior to the initiation of the PC. In doing so, we followed recent work (Han et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2020; Sim et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Danaher & Smith, 2014) that used time shifts within the same platform to construct such a “pseudo-control” group.

Specifically, we used Kickstarter data from the period between June 3, 2012 and June 3, 2014, i.e., the two years preceding the PC. For this pseudo-control platform, we considered the date June 3, 2013—the midpoint of the period and the same calendar date as that of the actual PC, one year prior—to represent the PC date of the pseudo-control group (which we refer to as the “simulated PC”). Accordingly, for the pseudo-control dataset, the period spanning June 3, 2012-June 3, 2013 was designated as the before period, and the period spanning June 3, 2013 - June 3, 2014 was designated as the “after” period. ## Model Construction

We estimated the following traditional DiD model using each of our two dependent variables. We estimated the model using fixed-effects OLS with category fixed effects:

We estimated all models using clustered standard errors on the campaign category. For robustness, in addition to the main analysis, we estimated Model (1) using two additional subsets of the main dataset:

1. Shorter time segments and no overlap between groups: As discussed above, limitations of our dataset resulted in a situation in which, in order to cover a full year of data for both the treatment group and the control group, the time periods used to define these groups partially overlapped. For robustness, we created a new dataset using shorter periods of time for the before and after data, enabling us to define treatment and control groups without this overlap. Specifically, we used the following time periods: treatment before: January 1, 2014 - June 3, 2014; treatment after: June 3, 2014 - January 1, 2015; control before: January 1, 2013 - June 3, 2013; control after: June 3, 2013 - January 1, 2014. In other words, the five months before the PC and the seven months after the PC constituted our treatment group, whereas the previous 12 months constituted the control group. A shortcoming of this analysis is that it does not control well for seasonality, as the before and after groups cover different months of the year. Accordingly, this analysis cannot stand alone but is intended as a complement to our main analysis. Notably, this dataset enabled us to examine the more immediate effects of the PC.

2. Using a matched sample: We carried out a propensity score matching (PSM) analysis to rule out the possibility that the post-PC increase in the concentration of demand was merely an artifact of the 72% increase in the number of campaigns, and to control for potential differences in the observable composition (i.e., the variety) of campaigns. This is especially important in our context, as we need to account for potential differences in observed quality (risk) signals after vs. before the PC. Thus, for each dataset (i.e., the treatment group and the control group), we created a matched subsample,<sup>9</sup> ensuring that we accounted for the changes in quantity, risk, and quality signals and that for each group (treatment and control), the number of pre-PC observations would be equal to the number of post-PC observations. Following previous literature (see Geva et al., 2019; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Mollick, 2014; Burtch et al., 2013; Gafni et al., 2019; Kim et al., 2022), we matched campaigns along the following features: (1) specific campaign characteristics—goal, category, duration and (2) known quality and risk signals—number of words in the description, number of previous successful campaigns by the campaign’s owner, whether the campaign had a video, number of previous backings by the campaign owner, and geographic location (whether the campaign is located in or outside the U.S.). (We note that prior studies have interpreted the latter variables both as indicators of quality and as indicators of risk; indeed, the two constructs are closely related.) This matching process resulted in 96,624 campaigns in the treatment group (48,312 campaigns “before” and 48,312 campaigns “after”), and 85,124 campaigns in the control group (42,562 campaigns “before” and 42,562 campaigns “after”).<sup>10</sup>

To ensure the internal validity of the DiD model, we must show that the parallel trends assumption is met within our dataset. The parallel trends assumption requires that in the absence of treatment, the difference between the treatment group and the control group is constant over time (Abadie, 2005). To support this assumption, we conducted two analyses. The first analysis examined the trends over time of the dependent variables in the pre-PC period (that is, the weeks before the PC); the results showed no significant difference between the trends of the treatment and control groups. The second analysis was a time falsification check that included three yearly quarters of pre-entry (i.e. pre-PC) indicators as placebos. In the presence of a pre-entry effect, that is, if the parallel trends assumption does not hold, the placebo indicators should produce positively significant coefficients. The results of this analysis show that the placebo indicators did not hold significant coefficients. These two analyses suggest that the parallel trends assumption is fulfilled and that the observed relationships between the PC and the increases in WeeklyCatGiniPledged and WeeklyCatGiniBackers are not attributable to events that happened prior to the PC.<sup>11</sup>

## Additional Robustness Analyses

For completeness and to further support our results, we conducted the following additional robustness analyses for H1: (1) We controlled for temporal changes in the economy, financial uncertainty, and the general investment environment in the US economy during the time of the analysis. We did this by collecting data on the weekly values of the S&P 500 index and controlling for them in the regression model. (2) We controlled for weekly risk on the platform. We followed the definition of Kim et al. (2022) and created a new aggregated risk variable for each campaign. We then constructed weekly risk indicators for each category and controlled for them in the analysis. (3) We removed campaigns that started before and ended after the PC. (4) We used a dependent variable based on actual revenue rather than the amount pledged. (5) We used a dependent variable that approximated weekly demand per campaign. The results of all these analyses are in the same direction and magnitude, further supporting H1a.<sup>12</sup>

## H2: Effects of Platform Openness on the Behavior of Recurrent Backers (Individual-Level Analysis)

Having established H1a—that platform openness leads to a more concentrated distribution of funds and backers across campaigns—we proceed with the analyses for H2. Recall that this hypothesis explores whether platform openness leads to changes in the investment behavior of individual backers and, specifically, of recurrent backers—backers who were active on the platform both before and after the PC. Formally, we define recurrent backers as users who backed at least one campaign that launched before the opening of the platform (i.e., between June 3, 2013 and June 3, 2014) and at least one campaign that launched after the opening of the platform (i.e., between June 3, 2014 and June 3, 2015). These individuals (n = 875,584) constituted about \~15% of the total sample of backers. We carried out two analyses, followed by several robustness analyses, to examine how the mix of campaigns backed by recurrent backers changed after the PC.

## Analysis 1: Share of Top Campaigns in Each Recurrent Backer’s Portfolio

In the first analysis, for each recurrent backer and for each period (i.e., pre-PC and post-PC), we calculated the share of the campaigns that the backer pledged to that were in the set of “top campaigns” during that period. In this analysis, a set of top campaigns for a given period was defined as the top 10, 50, 100, or 500 campaigns (in terms of funds raised) out of all campaigns offered on the platform during that period. For example, let us assume that, during the pre-PC period, User A backed five campaigns, two of which were among the top 10 campaigns during that period. This would mean that for Backer A during the pre-PC period, the share of backed campaigns belonging to the top 10 campaigns would be 40%.

As shown in Table 5, on average, the share of top campaigns in recurrent backers’ portfolios increased after the opening of the platform for different sets of top campaigns. All differences were statistically significant (paired t-test, p < 0.005, where the pairing was done per recurrent backer, i.e., comparing each recurrent backer’s behavior before versus after the PC).

## Analysis 2: Quantiles

In the second analysis, for each recurrent backer and for each period, we calculated the share of campaigns the backer pledged to that belonged to different quantiles (in terms of funds raised), namely, Q25, Q50, Q75, Q95, Q99, and Q99.5. For example, if Backer B backed four campaigns before the policy change, one of which belonged to Q99.5 (that is, the top 0.5% of campaigns), this would mean that for Backer B during the pre-PC period, the share of backed campaigns belonging to Q99.5 would be 25%.

Table 6 shows that the average number of recurrent backer investments in quantiles below Q75 decreased after the PC, whereas the number of investments in Q75 increased. The differences are statistically significant (paired t-test, p < 0.001). Breaking down Q75 into subpercentiles, we see the increase stems mostly from quantiles Q99-Q99.5 and Q99.5–-Q100. In particular, we found that the increase was most substantial in investments in campaigns above Q99.5: an average of 34.66% after the PC compared to an average of 28.07% before.

Together, these results suggest that recurrent backers’ demand became more concentrated after the PC, supporting H2a. Thus, they show that the PC brought with it a change in behavior, beyond any change in the types of backers operating on the platform.

## Summary and Concluding Remarks

The objective of our work was to study how a platform owner’s choice to open a crowdfunding market might affect the distribution of demand on the platform. Our analysis relied on a natural experiment in the form of a change in Kickstarter’s acceptance policy, which resulted in lowering the entry barriers to the platform, thereby opening the market to more players. We obtained the following key insights regarding the effects of this PC on the distribution of demand on the platform: First, the distribution of demand after the change was less equal than that before the change, and it was more concentrated in the head. (H1a). Second, focusing on the individual-level behavior of recurrent backers, we found that these backers shifted their investments toward top campaigns after the PC. This finding suggests that the change in the distribution of demand is likely to be attributable to a change in investors’ behavior, beyond any changes in the composition of the demand side (that is, changes in the types of investors entering the market). (H2a)

On the basis of these results, we conclude that a more equalopportunity crowdfunding market does not lead to more equitable access to potential funds. Rather, it leads to a less even distribution of the funds, in line with the superstar effect observed in the long tail literature. This phenomenon is likely to be driven, at least in part, by the fact that opening a platform triggers changes in backers’ behavior.

Though our data did not enable us to isolate the cognitive mechanism for recurrent backers’ behavioral change, this question certainly warrants further exploration. Our findings suggest that a plausible explanation might be backers’ heightened risk aversion in the presence of heightened risk/uncertainty, a phenomenon observed in financial markets (Bernanke et al. 1994, Beber et al. 2008, Kabir 2018).

In interpreting our results, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of our empirical analyses: The use of a natural experiment entails a lack of control over extraneous variables that could bias the results or introduce alternative factors beyond market openness and might drive the phenomena observed. Accordingly, an ideal identification strategy would have been to use a control group that was not affected by the natural experiment within the time frame of our analysis. In our context, data from a competing crowdfunding platform, in which no policy change was implemented, could have served as such a control group. As we acknowledge in the Empirical Methodology and Results section, we did not have access to such data. Our solution, following previous works (Han et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2020; Sim et al., 2022), was to create a pseudo-control dataset using time-shifted data from Kickstarter. Given that the control and treatment groups encompass different time periods from the same platform, our identification is naturally susceptible to environmental changes over time and to other policy changes that might have taken place on Kickstarter. Regarding the latter factor, we made an effort to identify such policy changes but did not find major events. One exception is the addition of two categories together with the PC— Crafts and Journalism. Notably, these two categories existed as subcategories before the PC, and it seems unlikely that their reclassification as top-level categories would have driven the effects we observed.

Further, as is often the case with natural experiments, our treatment event triggered multiple phenomena—here, an increase in campaign variety and a decrease in quality, alongside an increase in quantity—whose effects we were unable to fully distinguish. Our PSM analysis, which accounted for quantity and for observed quality signals, controlled for some of these differences. However, since we could not fully separate between these effects, we limit our conclusions to cases where there is an increase in variety and quantity at the same time as a decrease in signaled quality. We note that such phenomena are generally likely to occur simultaneously when digital platforms adopt an open acceptance policy. Separating their effects constitutes an interesting avenue for future work.

We also acknowledge that the policy change was initiated and implemented by Kickstarter, which had full control over the form of the new policy and the timing of its introduction. In that sense, ours is not a traditional natural experiment involving a fully exogenous treatment event. We note, however, that other studies have used similar endogenous events as natural experiments (e.g., Kim et al., 2022). Moreover, our extensive robustness analyses provide some measure of confidence that the effects we observe indeed reflect backers’ responses to shifts in the campaign supply available on Kickstarter, as opposed to some other unobserved event that was tied to the initiation of the PC.

Its limitations notwithstanding, our work offers both theoretical and managerial contributions. From a theoretical perspective, we contribute to the IS literature on the tension between the long-tail effect and the superstar effect, extending this literature to the crowdfunding context and, more generally, expanding its coverage in the context of P2P platforms. We observe that the opening of a crowdfunding market does not lead to a long-tail effect and, in fact, leads to a superstar effect. As discussed in the Theoretical Background section, these findings are likely to be linked to the distinctive characteristics of crowdfunding settings: an all-or-nothing revenue model, heightened uncertainty and risk, and a substantial potential for herding.

Our findings subvert a key premise of share economy markets, namely, the notion of inclusivity for all agents. Accordingly, they suggest that if practitioners and policy makers indeed value such inclusivity, they must take additional steps to make the likelihood of crowdfunding success more equitable across suppliers—for example, through changes to their platforms’ recommendation, word-of-mouth, and filtering mechanisms. From a broader perspective, our results suggest that creating more equal opportunities for entering online marketplaces does not necessarily lead to more equalized distribution of funds and, in fact, may intensify inequality. That is, a side effect of creating more seats at the table may be a reduction in the number of slices of pie distributed. This concept may be worthy of further investigation not only in online marketing contexts but also as a broader societal question.

## About the Authors

Hilah Geva is an assistant professor of technology management and information systems at the Coller School of Management, Tel Aviv University. She holds a Ph.D. in information systems and an M.A. in philosophy from Tel Aviv University, along with a B.Sc. in computer science and cognitive science from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. Her research primarily focuses on online user behavior, the economics of digital platforms, and the transformative effects of generative AI on consumer behavior. Her work has been published in leading information systems venues.

Ohad Barzilay is the head of data science at Similarweb. After earning his Ph.D. in computer science, Ohad joined the Department of Information and Technology Management at the Coller School of Management at Tel Aviv University, where he studied the economics of digital platforms and electronic markets. Before joining academia, Ohad worked for some technology companies including Google, Jungo, VocalTec, and BMC. He also served at the Israeli Chief Scientist Office on the committee for incubator and early-stage company funding and on the scientific committee of the Blavatnik Interdisciplinary Center for Cyber Research.

Anat Goldstein is an assistant professor of knowledge and data engineering in the Department of Industrial Engineering and Management at Ariel University and a member of the Data Science and Artificial Intelligence Research Center. She holds a Ph.D. in information systems, an M.B.A., and a B.A. in computer science and economics, all from Tel-Aviv University. Her research includes the development and application of data science and machine learning methods in the context of electronic commerce, peer-to-peer platforms, medicine, and agriculture. Anat has published her work in numerous journals and conference proceedings.

Gal Oestreicher-Singer is The Mexico Professor of Information Systems and the associate dean of research at the Coller School of Management at Tel Aviv University in Israel. She received her Ph.D. from the Stern School of Business at New York University. Her work has been published in the top journals in the fields of both information systems and marketing. She is the recipient of numerous awards and recognition including several best paper awards and career awards. In addition, she has received several grants, most recently the ERC grant.
