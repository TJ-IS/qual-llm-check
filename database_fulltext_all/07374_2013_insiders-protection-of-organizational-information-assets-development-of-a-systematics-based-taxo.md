# INSIDERS’ PROTECTION OF ORGANIZATIONAL INFORMATION ASSETS: DEVELOPMENT OF A SYSTEMATICS-BASED TAXONOMY AND THEORY OF DIVERSITY FOR PROTECTION-MOTIVATED BEHAVIORS<sup>1</sup>

Clay Posey Department of Information Systems, Statistics, and Management Science, Culverhouse College of Commerce and Business Administration, The University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa, AL 35487 U.S.A. {cposey@cba.ua.edu}

Tom L. Roberts Department of Management and Information Systems, College of Business, Louisiana Tech University, Ruston, LA 71272 U.S.A. {troberts@latech.edu}

Paul Benjamin Lowry Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong PEOPLE’S REPUBLIC OF CHINA {paul.lowry.phd@gmail.com}

Rebecca J. Bennett and James F. Courtney Department of Management and Information Systems, College of Business, Louisiana Tech University, Ruston, LA 71272 U.S.A. {rbennett@latech.edu} {courtney@latech.edu}

Protecting information from a variety of security threats is a daunting organizational activity. Organization managers must recognize the roles that organization insiders have in protecting information resources rather than solely relying upon technology to provide this protection. Unfortunately, compared to negative insider behaviors, the extant literature provides sparse coverage of beneficial insider activities. The few beneficial activities in the literature represent only a small portion of the diverse collection of insiders’ protective actions.

This research focuses on protection-motivated behaviors (PMBs), which are volitional behaviors enacted by organization insiders to protect (1) organizationally relevant information and (2) the computer-based information systems in which the information is stored, collected, disseminated, and/or manipulated from information security threats. Based on systematics, we propose a six-step methodology of qualitative and quantitative approaches to develop a taxonomy and theory of diversity for PMBs. These approaches integrate the classification techniques of multidimensional scaling (MDS), property fitting (ProFit), and cluster analyses. We leverage these techniques to identify and display how insiders collectively classify 67 unique PMBs and their homogeneous classes. Our taxonomy provides researchers and practitioners a comprehensive guide and common nomenclature for PMBs. Our methodology can be similarly used to create other theories of diversity.

Keywords: Protection-motivated behaviors, behavioral information security, systematics, theory of diversity, multidimensional scaling, cluster analysis, taxonomy

## Introduction

The primary goal in managing a firm’s information systems security is to protect the information resources of the firm (Dhillon and Torkzadeh 2006; Herath and Rao 2009b; Siponen and Vance 2010). Consequently, organizations worldwide dedicate a substantial amount of financial resources to protect information against external and internal threats (Cavusoglu et al. 2004). Global information security spending was approximately \$60 billion in 2011 and is expected to grow at close to 10 percent every year over the next 3 to 5 years (PWC 2011). As a recent example of this trend, although the U.S. federal government is decelerating spending on information technology, U.S. federal spending on IT security is predicted to have an annual growth of nearly 9 percent between 2011 and 2016 (Peterson 2011).

Although traditional IT security efforts focus almost entirely on technical methods to achieve protection (Zafar and Clark 2009), many researchers now recognize that security efforts must adequately account for individual, social, and organizational influences to be effective (Choobineh et al. 2007; Dhillon and Backhouse 2000; Vroom and von Solms 2004). Particularly, researchers are interested in the impact of organization insiders’ behavior on information security within firms (Boss et al. 2009; D’Arcy and Hovav 2007; Moore et al. 2008). Organization insiders are all individuals (e.g., fulland part-time employees, temporary workers, board members) who have access to organizationally relevant information while fulfilling their duties (Shaw et al. 1998). These individuals’ actions, whether accidental or intentional, exert significant influence on the organizational information security chain (Im and Baskerville 2005; Schneier 2000; Vroom and von Solms 2004).

Several studies highlight the potential negative influence of insiders on organizational information security efforts (D’Arcy et al. 2009; Straub 1990; Warkentin and Willison 2009; Whitman 2003; Willison and Siponen 2009). As an illustration, the Identity Theft Resource Centre reported that 13.4 percent of publicly disclosed breaches in 2011 were known forms of insider theft (ITRC 2012). Another recent survey of health care employees in August 2011 reported that 71 percent of employees had experienced a breach of personal health information in the last year (Veriphyr 2011). According to the survey, insiders were responsible for the majority of breaches, with 35 percent snooping into the medical records of fellow employees and 27 percent accessing records of friends and relatives (Veriphyr 2011). The 2011 CyberSecurity Watch Survey also found that insider attacks are viewed as more costly than external attacks and are becoming more sophisticated (CSO Magazine 2011). According to the 2011 Global Fraud Report, the key element of fraud incidents is the insider, whether inadvertently or by choice. The report found that in 2011, 60 percent of all fraud incidents involved an insider (Kroll Advisory Solutions 2011).

The silver lining juxtaposed against this mountain of negative insider behavior is that insiders also have untapped potential to help protect organizational information and systems. Security practitioners and researchers consequently need to broaden the myopic view that insiders are only security threats (Albrechtsen and Hovden 2009; Stanton and Stam 2006; Stanton et al. 2005). Specifically, organizational information protection can be achieved with a simultaneous understanding of (1) how to deter detrimental human behavior and (2) how to motivate insiders’ beneficial activities (Stanton et al. 2005). Benevolent activities are extremely desirable since the timely detection of security attacks against an organization often depends on the awareness and actions of authorized insiders (Hamill et al. 2005).

Despite the importance of protecting organizational information via insiders, few studies have expanded the knowledge proffered by the field’s initial investigations of protective behaviors (Aytes and Connolly 2004; Bulgurcu et al. 2009, 2010; Ng et al. 2009; Workman et al. 2008). Namely, little research has explored the overall superset of beneficial protective behaviors, its structure, and the interrelationships, commonalities, and differences among the behaviors. Neither have researchers investigated the relationship between this superset of beneficial behaviors and the portfolio of roles that insiders need to perform.

This research gap could prove increasingly problematic because knowledge created from a focus on a single behavior or subset of behaviors does not necessarily generalize to the grand structure of behaviors. In fact, the act of studying behaviors as isolated events—instead of as an aggregate model—can inhibit researchers’ understanding of the complex psychological processes surrounding the overall superset of human behaviors of interest (Hanisch and Hulin 1991; Hanisch et al. 1998). In our context, studying protective behaviors in isolation inhibits researchers’ understanding of insiders’ ability to perform multiple protective behaviors.

Taking inspiration from this opportunity, our research embraces a systematics approach (McKelvey 1978, 1982) to understanding protective information security behaviors. We do so by focusing on the science of diversity rather than the science of universals or functional science (Lee and Baskerville 2003). Accordingly, the purpose of this paper is to create a systematics-based taxonomy that explores the diversity of these protective activities, which we term protectionmotivated behaviors (PMBs). PMBs are volitional behaviors enacted by organization insiders to protect (1) organizationally relevant information within their firms and (2) the computer-based IS in which that information is stored, collected, disseminated, and/or manipulated from informationsecurity threats. Examples of PMBs include “an organization insider immediately reports a coworker’s negligent information-security behavior to the proper organization authorities” and “an organization insider opens e-mail attachments only if he/she knows the e-mail’s sender and was expecting the e-mail.” A complete listing of PMBs and their classes is provided in Appendix C. The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows: We review the necessary background literatures on systematics, the science of diversity versus the science of universals, and PMBs. We then apply the qualitative and quantitative methodological approaches to examine and explain the diversity among and classification of 67 PMBs that result in our proposed taxonomy. Finally, we discuss the findings of the study and its contributions to theory and practice, along with potential future research.

## Why a Systematics Study of Protection Behaviors Is Crucial

The systematics approach asserts that the diversity or differences among behaviors and their classifications must be enumerated and effectively disseminated (i.e., the science of diversity) prior to an attempt to discover “the universal laws which govern the behavior, functions, and processes of the objects under investigation” (i.e., the science of universals) (McKelvey 1978, p. 1428). Simply put, theoretical development for a population of interest is hindered until the members of that population are adequately described and differentiated (Mayr 1969; McKelvey 1982). Accordingly, our goal is to provide a theory of diversity about the overall set of protective information security behaviors via the development of a formal taxonomy and classification schema.

Systematics focuses on three separate but related tasks: (1) the development of taxonomies, which describe the differences among population members; (2) the understanding of the evolution of population members and how their state changes over time; and (3) the identification of homogeneous classes within the taxonomy and the assignment of population members to their respective classes (McKelvey 1978; Ross 1974).

Systematics is a prerequisite to—rather than a byproduct of—sound scientific method (McKelvey 1978, 1982). Again, this is because the science of diversity symbiotically precedes the science of universals in that researchers must examine differences to enhance the understanding of the behaviors and functions of the population members exhibiting those differences, prior to applying universal laws or theories. Without understanding the population of PMBs, it is virtually impossible to make universal predictions about them. Researchers must consequently strive to describe and classify the similarities—and dissimilarities—among objects of interest from which functional studies and subsequent theoretical advancements can emerge (Ulrich and McKelvey 1990). We assert that this is also the case when trying to establish PMBs because these behaviors and their relationships are not simple.

To wit, if ideally managed and motivated, insiders have a sizeable portfolio of protective roles and associated behaviors they must engage in to protect organizational information assets. This portfolio of roles creates behavioral complexity for insiders, which makes a systematics investigation more vital. Behavioral complexity is the performance of a portfolio of functions that allow an individual to “respond to complex demands” (Hooijberg 1996, p. 919). Behavior complexity has two dimensions: behavioral repertoire and behavioral differentiation (Hooijberg 1996; Wu et al. 2010). Behavioral repertoire is the portfolio of roles that individuals perform and their ability to perform multiple roles and behaviors within their organizational context (Wu et al. 2010). Behavioral differentiation is the ability to switch from role to role at appropriate times to handle paradoxes and contradictions mandated by one’s job (Hooijberg 1996). Via systematics, we seek insight into the diversity and nature of PMBs so that we can better understand the behavioral repertoire and differentiation that result in PMB complexity. This insight then allows researchers and practitioners to explain, motivate, and manage PMBs properly.

Many disciplines have benefitted greatly from systematics. In particular, the natural sciences of biology, botany, ecology, and zoology could not have contributed to humanity to the degree that they have if it were not for initial taxonomic efforts. As McKelvey (1982) states, chemistry saw significant progress following Mendeleev’s creation of the periodic chart, and functional science in physics advanced significantly following Dalton and Bohr’s conception of the atom. Despite its youth relative to the aforementioned disciplines, IS has also experienced growth due to researchers’ taxonomic efforts. The subjects of these non-exhaustive efforts include a research program for the IS discipline (Mason and Mitroff 1973), taxonomies of IS success antecedents (Larsen 2003), technology structures and their influences on organizational structures (Fiedler et al. 1996), individual responses to potential invasions of privacy (Son and Kim 2008), knowledge management strategies (Earl 2001), the diversity of information (McKinney and Yoos 2010), and others. These research efforts provided the foundation and requisite guidance to the functional studies within their respective areas to develop universal theories about the phenomena. These efforts were vital for scientific progress because theoretical development is a methodical progression that must emerge from rigorous foundations (Gregor 2006).

For these reasons, we assert that the area of IS security can benefit from systematics investigations. Accordingly, our study evaluates PMBs from a systematics approach. To understand PMBs better, the natural starting point for a systematics approach is to first elicit the major behaviors that comprise the PMB taxonomy. After eliciting the major PMBs, the next step with a systematics approach is to then develop a theory of diversity of PMBs. Namely, once the domain space of PMBs has been defined and the representative behaviors have been elicited, it is important to determine how insiders, as a collective unit, cognitively differentiate and comprehend the diversity among these behaviors. Understanding the structure of the PMB perceptual map and how PMBs relate to each other conceptually will allow researchers and practitioners to focus on the individual components of the PMB structure (i.e., homogeneous groups or classes) and to examine how they form the overall PMB taxonomy.

Notwithstanding the compelling reasons to develop a theory of diversity around PMBs, methodologists have argued that theories of diversity should be developed only if an evidencebased explanation for the existence of such a concept of interest is provided a priori (Doty and Glick 1994). We provide this justification here. Accordingly, we posit that insiders can become motivated to engage in behaviors that help protect their organizations from information security threats. Just as employees can become motivated to perform in-role and positive, extra-role behaviors within organizations (MacKenzie et al. 1998; McNeely and Meglino 1994; Organ et al. 2006), techniques can be leveraged that motivate individuals to defend their organizations in the information security domain from security threats.

However, it is important to emphasize that PMBs should not be confused with positive extra-role employee activities, which have been defined as organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs). Although both PMBs and OCBs are focused on employee behaviors that benefit organizations, OCBs are defined explicitly as extra-role behaviors (Organ 1988). This conceptualization has caused concern among organizational behavior scholars because employees’ perceived boundaries between in- and extra-role activities are often ill defined (Morrison 1994; Van Dyne et al. 1995). PMBs do not make such a distinction; in fact, PMBs may very well be in-role and thus employees could be disciplined for not performing them, which is never the case with OCBs.

Behaviors within the taxonomy of PMBs are likely to be classified according to several dimensions currently unknown to the research community (Ng et al. 2009). Hence, all behaviors are not expected to be the same in the minds of insiders and should be structured as several homogeneous classes. In fact, insiders’ behavioral repertoires will vary by individual. Whereas an insider with a broad behavioral repertoire will be able to assume the roles needed to protect the organization, an insider with a narrow behavioral repertoire may have limited capacity to perform the same functions.

Theory-based evidence helps provide this requisite foundation for the development of a theory of diversity. Protectionmotivation theory (PMT) (Rogers 1975, 1983) is the theoretical foundation that has guided much of the extant information security research on protective security intentions and behaviors (Anderson and Agarwal 2010; Herath and Rao 2009b; Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Junglas et al. 2008; Lee and Larsen 2009; Liang and Xue 2009; Siponen et al. 2007). PMT specifies the cognitive processes that individuals undergo following the reception of threat information; these processes result in individuals being motivated to engage in adaptive behaviors (i.e., behaviors that effectively minimize the threat) (Rogers and Prentice-Dunn 1997). This motivational force toward adaptive behaviors is termed protection motivation, which is “an intervening variable that has the typical characteristics of a motive: it arouses, sustains, and directs activity” (Rogers 1983, p. 158). Individuals may become motivated to engage in adaptive behaviors to protect themselves (Floyd et al. 2000; Milne et al. 2000), other individuals (Beck and Feldman 1983; Flynn et al. 1995), or organizations (Beck 1984) from threats. PMT thus supports the concept of an overall protective taxonomy and the development of a theory of diversity. To clarify, PMT is the theoretical justification sought by Doty and Glick (1994) in our context.

## Protection-Motivated Behaviors

Given the motivation and justification for engaging in systematics research on PMBs, we now further explain PMBs to provide the requisite background for developing a systematicsbased taxonomy of them. A variety of research in behavioral information security has taken place in the PMB domain. Behavioral information security<sup>2</sup> research encompasses all of the complexities of human activity that influence the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information and information systems (Stanton et al. 2006). This research stream is composed of two general areas in regard to insiders: the areas focus on (1) individuals as the weakest link in the security chain (Dhillon 2001; Im and Baskerville 2005; Vroom and von Solms 2004) and (2) employees as protective stewards of sensitive information (Stanton and Stam 2006; Stanton et al. 2006).

Although some of the original behavioral IS security research emphasized that IS practitioners and managers have key roles and responsibilities to protect the privacy and confidentiality of organizational information (Backhouse and Dhillon 1995; Oz 1992; Straub and Collins 1990; Walsham 1996), only more recently have researchers suggested that these roles and responsibilities be expanded to include all individuals with access to sensitive organizational information (Stanton and Stam 2006). These roles and responsibilities form the basis of research exploring individuals’ motivation to adopt protective technologies and to engage in PMBs within organizations.

As a useful foundation for potential PMBs, several individual protective behaviors have been studied by researchers regarding personal protection outside an organizational context (Dinev et al. 2009; Dinev and Hu 2007; Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Lee and Kozar 2008; Woon et al. 2005). These studies have examined protective behaviors such as individuals’ adoption of home wireless security technology (Woon et al. 2005), the use of anti-spyware technology (Johnston and Warkentin 2010; Lee and Kozar 2008), and intentions to become “conscientious cybercitizens,” who actively protect their own computers and the Internet in a home setting (Anderson and Agarwal 2010). Crossler (2009) highlighted many activities that individuals enact to protect themselves, such as using anti-virus software, running software updates, securing wireless networks, using firewalls, carefully storing credit card information, and using pop-up blocking software.

Turning from individual protection, researchers have also studied how employees can become protective agents of information within their organizations (Stanton and Stam 2006; Stanton et al. 2005). These studies are important because insiders possess insight into business knowledge in creating effective organizational security measures (Spears and Barki 2010). Insiders are also vital to the success of organizational security efforts because they have immense control over protecting organizational information for cases in which technical methods of IS security are ineffective:

As a group, employees have access to most or all of the organization’s most valuable information assets. Their actions have a profound influence on the safety and protection of those assets, even in situations in which information technology professionals have put monumental efforts into imposing mechanical controls on what users are allowed to do with the company’s computers and networks (Stanton and Stam 2006, p. 38).

Because of the vital potential role of insiders, organizations need insiders who are willing to take expansive, active roles as protection-motivated stewards of organizational information (Herath and Rao 2009a; Ng et al. 2009; Straub and Collins 1990; Van Niekerk and von Solms 2010) and to expand their information security behavioral repertoires. Understanding PMBs is key to understanding these roles. A variety of PMBs within organizations has emerged in the IS literature. These PMBs include insiders’ intention to comply with (Bulgurcu et al. 2009, 2010; Herath and Rao 2009a, 2009b; Siponen et al. 2007) and actual adherence to information security rules and policies within organizations (Myyry et al. 2009; Siponen et al. 2007). Researchers have also investigated individual behaviors within organizations such as creating data backups, performing e-mail attachment virus scans, using safe e-mail practices, and changing and sharing passwords (Aytes and Connolly 2004). Stanton and Stam (2006) also provided an in-depth analysis of potential benevolent behaviors—including attending training programs, reporting discovered vulnerabilities to appropriate authorities, and actively choosing not to release nonpublic company data to external sources.

This literature clearly indicates an emerging knowledge of ways that employees can be positive agents who protect their organizational information assets through PMBs. Nonetheless, the literature currently does not explain the relationship between these PMBs; neither does it explain the relative importance of each behavior, which may vary from organization to organization. It is on this foundation that we build our taxonomy.

## A Six-Step Methodology to Establish the Theory of Diversity of Protection-Motivated Behaviors

Again, the primary purpose of our research is to establish a theory of diversity of PMBs. To develop a theory of diversity, researchers must ensure the content validity of the concept of interest and its components. Developmental research efforts that fail to incorporate all representative and relevant issues, items, or behaviors that belong to the larger population of interest will ultimately limit the progress of subsequent research (Hanisch and Hulin 1991; Hanisch et al. 1998). A key reason for this limited progress is that such research fails to ensure the content validity of the concepts of interest (Straub et al. 2004). To be clear, in referring to content validity in a taxonomy context, we are not referring to psychometric measurement. We are referring specifically to the degree to which the taxonomy itself represents all the important facets of the targeted behaviors. Thus, a taxonomy of PMBs that has high content validity is one that largely represents the grand population of all possible PMBs. Without content validity, a taxonomy cannot claim to represent the “essence” of the overall structure. Moreover, researchers who rely on a single approach to achieving content validity often make trade-offs between the adequacy of content validity and the generalizability of findings with practicality (e.g., time and cost) (Tojib and Sugianto 2006).

We have chosen to maximize both content validity and generalizability by leveraging a methodologically pluralistic approach that combines six steps of qualitative and quantitative analysis that center on the use of multidimensional scaling (MDS). A methodologically pluralistic approach (Baskerville and Wood-Harper 1998; Chen and Hirschheim 2004; Landry and Banville 1992; Mingers 2001) is an integration of qualitative and quantitative techniques, which is uncommon but useful in IS research (Straub et al. 2004; Tojib and Sugianto 2006). Such integration increases researchers’ ability to more fully understand phenomena of interest by not restricting the emergence of knowledge to any one approach (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). Such pluralism limits the potential for errors of exclusion—that is, errors that inhibit researchers ability to accurately represent reality—which are significant detriments to content validity and theories of diversity (Kruskal and Wish 1978; Straub et al. 2004).

The reason that MDS is pivotal to our methodology is that it allows the creation of perceptual maps of PMBs, which maps then allow us to explore the diversity of how users think about such behaviors. Nevertheless, before using MDS, researchers need to conduct three preliminary, interdependent steps. Accordingly, these three steps are also the foundation of our methodology for creating theories of diversity: (1) behavior elicitation, (2) removal of redundant behaviors, and (3) acquisition of similarity ratings that comprise the data matrix, which ratings MDS interprets. Given that these three preliminary analyses define the behaviors within the content space covered by PMBs, we then perform three empirical steps to explore the diversity comprising the insiders’ collective perceptual map regarding PMBs: (4) MDS analysis to determine the number of dimensions existing within the insider’s perceptual map; (5) ProFit analysis to label these dimensions objectively and formalize the taxonomy; and (6) cluster analysis to identify homogeneous classes of PMBs within the collective perceptual map. Appendix A provides further rationale for each of the six steps in our proposed methodology to theorizing diversity. The next few sections explain how these steps were conducted.

## Step 1: Behavioral Elicitation

Behavior elicitation requires an initial in-depth review of the unique PMBs listed in the literature. As noted, few academic (Albrechtsen and Hovden 2009; Ng et al. 2009; Stanton and Stam 2006; Stanton et al. 2005) and professional (Price 2007) studies have attempted to provide this information because the protective-based, organization-insider literature is still developing. To bridge this literature gap and to ensure content validity, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 11 information security professionals and 22 ordinary organization insiders to obtain a more complete view of PMBs. The information security professionals were organization insiders whose primary expertise and job functions were directly related to protecting the organization’s information systems infrastructure. An ordinary organization insider is an individual whose job is related to the day to day operations of the organization. Examples of an ordinary organization insider jobs include accountant, loan officer, engineer, sales representative, and so forth. Ordinary insiders’ job functions do not directly relate to the organization’s information systems or security. Table 1 highlights the qualifications of the information security experts and ordinary organization insiders used in the structured interviews.

The purpose of interviewee selection was to elicit possible PMBs from two disparate groups of organization insiders: both security professionals and ordinary organization insiders—managerial and nonmanagerial employees—who were employed in a wide variety of industries. Qualitative research should incorporate the opinions and experiences of all individuals involved in the phenomenon of interest by attaining a triangulation of subjects (Rubin and Rubin 2005). This approach helps eliminate “elite bias,” which would occur if only one set of individuals were queried (Miles and Huberman 1994; Myers and Newman 2007).

Each of these one-on-one, telephone-based interviews lasted approximately 25 minutes. We provided the interview protocol (available upon request) to each interviewee via e-mail two weeks prior to the actual interview, with a reminder e-mail sent a few days before the interview. The goal of this approach was to allow the interviewees the ability to reflect on the purpose of the interview adequately, to clarify any ambiguities and questions before the interview, and to utilize their time effectively during the interview process. The same interview protocol guided all interviews, but we allowed for follow-up improvisation.

## Step 2: Removal of Redundant Behaviors through Two New Sets of Expert Review

This set of behaviors was further refined by two rounds of external review. First, two qualified independent judges with expertise in IS and research methods assessed the behaviors to remove potential redundancies.<sup>3</sup> This review process reduced the total number to 92 unique behaviors. The remaining behaviors were further assessed by 10 different subject-matter experts (SMEs) (three professors of IS, one professor of management, one professor of organizational behavior, and five graduate IS students with significant professional experience) who rated each unique behavior along a seven-point Likert-like scale on three factors: (1) the behavior’s fit with the definition of PMBs, (2) the clarity of the behavior’s wording, and (3) the behavior’s applicability to a wide range of occupations and industries. These efforts served as an external validation check that the behaviors were unique, free from technological jargon, and appropriate for inclusion in the domain space suggested by the definition of PMBs. The SMEs’ ratings were averaged, and behaviors receiving a 4 or less on any of the three factors listed above were considered for minor wording alterations or exclusion from further assessment, depending upon their clarity and fitto-definition and applicability scores, respectively (Robinson and Bennett 1995). Following this second review, 67 behaviors emerged as the unique set of PMBs to undergo assessment with the MDS technique.

## Step 3: Acquisition of Similarity Ratings through a Survey of Insiders

We contracted with an online, research-panel provider to obtain data for the MDS similarity matrix. Online panels have received increasing attention in the academic literature (Awad and Ragowsky 2008; Gibney et al. 2009; Posey et al. 2010; Shang et al. 2010) and provide the diversity (e.g., varied work experience, professional background, and industry) of sample respondents necessary for the development of a generalizable taxonomy of PMBs. In total, 492 panelists from a wide variety of industries participated. Comparable to other studies (Robinson and Bennett 1995), similarity ratings could not be obtained for all behavior pairs from each respondent due to the overall set size; therefore, each respondent was issued a single behavior to compare against the other 66 PMBs in the behavior set.<sup>4</sup> These judgments were averaged across all respondents, thereby making the technique an aggregate MDS approach (Hair et al. 2009). In addition to rating each behavior-pair comparison on a nine-point bipolar scale (1 = not at all similar; 9 = very similar), respondents were asked to comment, within openended comment boxes, as to how they arrived at each of their comparison decisions.

## Step 4: Use of MDS to Determine the General Structure and Dimensionality of Insiders’ PMB Perceptual Map

MDS is a powerful classification methodology that allows a set of objects or behaviors to be differentiated on one or more cognitive dimensions that may be unknown to the researcher (Hair et al. 2009; Kruskal and Wish 1978). To wit, MDS graphically describes how a target population cognitively views a set of objects or behaviors of interest even when researchers have little to no a priori information about the means used by the target population to distinguish among the objects or behaviors. Ergo, this technique maps the latent psychological distances physically between a set of objects or behaviors that exist collectively in the minds of the respondents (Rabinowitz 1975; Tan and Hunter 2002). This approach is important in novel fields of study as it assists researchers to comprehend how the target population assesses a topic of interest cognitively, and the population’s perceptual maps and the relevant dimensions within that map must be understood by researchers (Green and Carmone 1970).

MDS is not a single methodology. Instead, MDS is a group of data classification techniques that allow researchers to position similar objects or behaviors within their respective perceptual dimensions (Kruskal and Wish 1978), which usually number two or three dimensions (Huang et al. 2006; Schiffman et al. 1981). MDS is similar to factor and cluster analyses but does not rely on a specified variate, which reliance often introduces researcher bias into studies of an exploratory nature (Hair et al. 2009). MDS also normally provides “more readily interpretable solutions of lower dimensionality” than factor analysis (Schiffman et al. 1981, p. 13). MDS techniques are also more effective than cluster analysis at recovering the structure of perceptual maps when dimensionality is not yet known by investigators (Kruskal 1977).

IS researchers have used MDS to graphically relate types of business-to-business e-marketplace activities (Matook and Vessey 2008), users in the e-mail network space (Rice 1994), group memory (McQuaid et al. 1999), expert visualization (Huang et al. 2006; Wright and Ayton 1987), decision support systems (Eom 1996), business intelligence (Chung et al. 2005), and variations among different system development methodologies (Sircar et al. 2001). MDS has also been applied to investigate the concept of organizational size in IS research (Goode and Gregor 2009). Accordingly, we use this powerful technique to understand the mindset of a population of individuals regarding PMBs in the workplace empirically.

We used ${ \bf M D S } ^ { 5 }$ to determine the number of dimensions that best represent the structure of the respondents’ ratings in a parsimonious manner. Various dimensional configurations were created and assessed using standards set forth by methodologists (Cattell 1966; Hair et al. 2009).<sup>6</sup> Three dimensions were found to best represent insiders’ mental structure regarding PMBs.

## Step 5: Using ProFit Analysis to Label the Dimensions with another Set of Insiders

Once we determined the dimensionality of the respondents perceptual space, we labeled the dimensions using an objective and rigorous technique termed property fitting (ProFit) analysis. Notably, this technique is recommended strongly by leading MDS researchers for dimension labeling (Kruskal and Wish 1978; Padgett and Mulvey 2007; Robinson and Bennett 1995). This approach is similar to previous MDS research (Robinson and Bennett 1995) and other positivist qualitative approaches (Lacity and Janson 1994). A ProFit analysis regresses respondents’ ratings of several possible dimension labels on the behaviors’ position in the three-dimensional coordinate space as indicated by the MDS technique. Variance explained, calculated F values, standardized regression weights, and correlations among the ratings were used in combination to determine the appropriate dimension labels (Padgett and Mulvey 2007; Robinson and Bennett 1995). We also added property attribute vectors to the perceptual map to help visualize the appropriate dimension labels (Dillon and Goldstein 1984).

## Step 6: Using Cluster Analysis to Find PMB Classes or Subgroups

Although MDS techniques are appropriate for determining the dimensionality of the structure that best represents the configuration of similarities among a large number of objects, cluster analysis techniques are more effective at recovering the smaller structures within a prespecified larger configuration—such as one suggested by MDS (Kruskal 1977; Padgett and Mulvey 2007). This effectiveness is due to the fact that cluster analysis does not concern itself with the geometric representation of objects in low-dimensional space but rather is a technique used to classify objects into groups within a predefined space (Timm 2002). Consequently, we conducted cluster analyses to determine whether homogeneous classes of PMBs exist within the respondents’ perceptual map.<sup>8</sup> Fourteen unique clusters emerged from this analysis. We labeled the first dimension of their perceptional map as degree of criticality, which is how important the behavior is for everyone—regardless of occupation, status, or organizational rank or position—to perform on an ongoing basis within an organization to protect an organization’s information assets. We labeled the second dimension as promotion difficulty, which is how difficult it is to promote ongoing adherence to the behavior within an organization due to the underlying burden, focus, and effort required to engage in the behavior. Finally, we labeled the third dimension as degree of common sense, which is the degree to which insiders’ existing knowledge and experience leads them naturally to understand a protective behavior as having clear logic and rationale.

Furthermore, we conceptualized all three dimensions as ranging from low to high in value and then categorized the results from the collectively held perceptual map. The results of these categorizations are displayed in Figures 1–4 and Table 3. Figures 1–3 are two-dimensional diagrams that reveal the combination of attributes in a 2 × 2 quadrant format that allows each PMB class to be categorized clearly for the taxonomy. Figure 4 is a three-dimensional diagram that details the complete categorization of PMB classes in a 2 × 2 × 2 format. Table 3 provides a detailed description of the fit of the attributes into each dimension and contains detailed information about our theory of diversity and its homogenous classes.

Given the results of our taxonomy, we now further explain the meaning of the eight taxonomy categories that logically group the 14 clusters. The first category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (high), promotion difficulty (high), and degree of common sense (high). This category creates a major paradox for most organizations because it is critical to the organization but should be common sense to most insiders. This paradox is precisely why the promotion difficulty is high. This is a difficult category for organizations to address and to develop positive behaviors in because insiders will be more likely to feel they are being trained on the obvious. The only PMB class to fall into this category was PMB Class #4, appropriate data entry and management.

This category includes secure data entry, data sanitization and disposal, data accuracy efforts, and data backup.

The second category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (high), promotion difficulty (high), and degree of common sense (low). This category is difficult for most organizations because it is critical to the organization and difficult to promote to organization insiders. The fact that the category does not have a high degree of common sense adds to the difficulty because many organization insiders never grasp the reasons or the importance of the PMB classes in this category. Two PMB classes fell into this category. They include PMB Class #3, policy-driven awareness and action, and PMB Class #8, wireless installation. The reason for the promotion difficulty of these classes is clear. Policy-driven awareness and action has always been a major difficulty for organizations, which has spawned leading IT policy compliance research on the matter (e.g., Bulgurcu et al. 2010; Herath and Rao 2009b; Hu et al. 2011; Siponen and Vance 2010; Warkentin and Willison 2009). It is challenging to get organization insiders to understand how and why security policies are created, implemented, and administered. Often organizations do not adequately inform insiders about new policies. Likewise, wireless installation has always presented organizations with a struggle between user access/ freedom and information security. This conflict is not easily understood by organization insiders and is definitely difficult to promote within most organizations. Ultimately, this category is vital to organizations because it contains one of the most vital PMB classes for organization information security —policy-driven awareness and action—which will likely always be difficult for organizations to manage.

The third category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (high), promotion difficulty (low), and degree of common sense (high). This category is critical to organizations’ security efforts and easy to sell to organization insiders, which contrasts with the first category because of major differences in promotion difficulty. Three PMB classes fall in this category: PMB Class #2, protection against unauthorized exposure; PMB Class #7, verbal and electronic sensitive-information protection; and PMB Class #9, widely applicable security etiquette. Of course, information protection will always be critical to the organization in every form, including verbal and electronic forms. Most organization insiders understand that they must protect sensitive information such as business records and documents, personnel records, financial information, or even classified documents in organizations associated with national security. Additionally, they understand that even organization insiders do not need complete access to the organization’s information. It should be noted that widely applicable security etiquette contains detailed information protection components.

The fourth category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (high), promotion difficulty (low), and degree of common sense (low). This category is critical to organizations’ security efforts and easy to sell to organization insiders just like the previous category. However, this category does not possess high common sense. This means that PMB classes that fall within this category might be difficult for organization insiders to grasp, and thus requires more training and oversight. The PMB Class #12, account protection, was the only class to fall in this category. This result is not really a surprise simply because of the internal conflict for organization insiders between completing their actual job functions and the organization’s need for information security. For example, allowing another project team member to use their computer to complete a task might seem like common sense to an insider who is more concerned about completing a critical work task—even though it violates organizational rules for account protection.

The fifth category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (low), promotion difficulty (high), and degree of common sense (high). This is the first category with a low degree of criticality. In addition, PMBs classes in this category are difficult to promote to organization insiders, even though they are highly practical and often necessary. PMB class #5, coworker reliance, and PMB class #13, immediate reporting of suspicious activity, fell into this category. These two PMB classes are not a surprise in this category because few organization insiders desire to be a whistleblower; neither do they want to be part of the organization’s police force. A substantial base of literature establishes the difficulty of encouraging insiders to engage in prosocial behaviors and to whistle blow on other insiders, primarily because of fears of retaliation, the bystander effect, and social concerns (e.g., Miceli et al. 2008; Near et al. 1993; Rehg et al. 2008). For this reason, it is not a surprise that insiders did not find these PMB classes as critical to the organization’s information security efforts.

The sixth category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (low), promotion difficulty (high), and degree of common sense (low). PMB classes in this category are difficult to promote because they often seem irrational to organization insiders and are of low criticality. This category should raise serious concerns in practice because PMB Class #1, legitimate e-mail handling, and PMB Class #6, secure software, e-mail, and Internet use, fell into this category. It is likely that these are the two PMB classes that most organization insiders hear about the most. Yet, organization insiders evaluated these PMB classes as having low criticality and low common sense. In addition, insiders pointed out the difficulty that organizations have promoting these PMB classes. These results are troubling, given how much effort has been expended with these particular PMBs by many organizations in the past and the possible danger for the organization that might occur when organization insiders ignore this PMB class. The organization could face lawsuits if pirated software is installed on organization machines.

Furthermore, organizational systems and networks can be damaged if they are infected by viruses from downloaded attachments, or sensitive information might be compromised through phishing schemes related to e-mail.

The seventh category has the following attributes: degree of criticality (low), promotion difficulty (low), and degree of common sense (high). PMBs in this category should be easy to promote and highly practical for organization insiders. PMB Class #5, document conversion, and PMB Class #10, distinctive security etiquette, are part of this category. Both of these PMB classes represent basic rules for virtually all computer and electronic device users and are not a surprise. These distinctive security etiquette PMBs include basic computer security principles such as not writing down passwords and leaving a document on the computer desk, not leaving an active computer unattended, and not allowing another insider to use one’s assigned computer.

The eighth and final category for the taxonomy has the following attributes: degree of criticality (low), promotion difficulty (low), and degree of common sense (low). PMBs in this category should be easy to promote and are not critical. The only PMB class to fall in this category was #14, equipment location and storage. It is certainly not a surprise that equipment location and storage is in this category because most organization insiders have little decision power with regard to this PMB class. In fact, in many cases, equipment location and storage was decided before many of the organization insiders were part of the organization. The next section discusses the implications of our findings and areas for future research.

## Discussion

This study is motivated by the assumption that organization insiders can be leveraged to be powerful stewards of organizational information security. However, a key problem is that the range of protective behaviors that can be employed in research and practice are not well known. Without understanding the population of PMBs, it is virtually impossible to make universal predictions about them. Researchers must consequently strive to describe and classify the similarities— and dissimilarities—among objects of interest from which functional studies and subsequent theoretical advancements can emerge (Ulrich and McKelvey 1990). Accordingly, we embraced a systematics approach to understanding protective information security behaviors. We focused on the science of diversity rather than the science of universals or functional science (Lee and Baskerville 2003). For this reason, we created a systematics-based taxonomy that explores the diversity of PMBs. To develop our PMB taxonomy, we followed an innovative six-step process. This classification system brings to light the underlying cognitive dimensions of the PMB construct and the meaningful relationships among the dimensions in the insider’s mind.

Such an understanding of the structure, interrelationships, and commonalities among different PMB classes provides researchers insight on the complex psychological processes surrounding the overall set of linked behaviors. From a theoretical and systematics standpoint, the resulting theory of diversity provides evidence as to why findings from research that focuses on one or only a subset of behaviors typically does not apply to others (i.e., various dimensions exist in the mindset of the insider) (Hanisch and Hulin 1991; Hanisch et al. 1998) and why the science of diversity is a requisite precursor to functional science (McKelvey 1978, 1982).

Our taxonomy provides researchers and practitioners with a comprehensive guide and common nomenclature for PMBs. It details which PMBs are critical for the protection of organizational information, which PMBs are difficult to promote to organization insiders, and which PMBs are basic common sense to organization insiders. The primary value of the taxonomy is that PMBs can be compared and contrasted directly, individually, or in totality rather than the current practice of isolation. In addition, concerned parties can now see how each PMB relates to an organization’s information security efforts to provide a comprehensive, meaningful assessment of strengths and weaknesses that can be talked about, compared to other organizations, and so on, using a common nomenclature. Figure 5 details a hierarchical decision map concerning the taxonomy categories that are vital to both researchers and practitioners.

For researchers, the taxonomy allows a full view of the complete spectrum of PMBs, while at the same time it offers a myriad of possible individual research projects and questions that can be built on a shared foundation. As an illustration, why is PMB Class #4, appropriate data entry and management, so difficult to promote when organization insiders believe that it is highly critical and high in common sense? While organization insiders perceive Class #12, account protection, to be low in common sense, why do they perceive PMB Class #2, protection against unauthorized exposure; PMB Class #7, verbal and electronic sensitiveinformation protection; and PMB, Class #9, widely applicable security etiquette to be high in common sense? Why do organization insiders perceive that PMB Class #1, legitimate e-mail handling, and PMB Class #6, secure software, e-mail, and Internet use, are not highly critical?

For organizations and practitioners, the taxonomy provides a roadmap to developing internal efforts to mold organization insiders into powerful stewards of organizational information security. Importantly, it also creates a common nomenclature and standard by which employees, security experts, and managers can speak the same language about organizational security efforts. It provides security education, training, and awareness (SETA) program developers with knowledge about each PMB class with regard to criticality, promotion difficulty, and common sense. With this information, developers can customize their SETA programs and more efficiently utilize resources to train organization insiders to perform PMBs within the organization. The taxonomy details which PMB classes should be priorities for SETA program trainers.

In addition, the taxonomy can serve as a guide as to where SETA program resources should be applied. For example, PMB Class #3, policy-driven awareness and action, should generally have a higher priority over PMB Class #1, legitimate e-mail handling, because of fundamental differences in how insiders perceive the criticality. Moreover, organizations should apply more effort and resources to the PMB Class #12, account protection, than the PMB Class #2, protection against unauthorized exposure, because it is more difficult to promote Class #12 to organization insiders.

Additionally, our research underscores the behavioral complexity involved with insiders and PMBs; behavioral complexity is composed of behavioral repertoire and differentiation. Recall that behavioral repertoire is the portfolio of roles that individuals perform and their ability to perform multiple roles and behaviors within their organizational context (Wu et al. 2010); and behavioral differentiation is the ability of individuals to switch from role to role at appropriate times to handle paradoxes and contradictions mandated by their jobs (Hooijberg 1996). This knowledge should assist organizations in their training efforts to expand individual organization insiders’ behavioral repertoires and behavioral differentiation in relation to information security threats. Moreover, our taxonomic efforts highlight the potential difficulties experienced by organizations as they attempt to get insiders to perform PMBs of a single class, or when they move from one PMB class to another within the PMB structure.

Our taxonomy also creates the foundation upon which a psychometric measure for PMBs can be created, again, based on a common nomenclature and logical basis. Such measures must be established from and strongly linked to the underlying concept of interest to yield valid empirical results (Churchill 1979; Kerlinger and Lee 2000; Straub et al. 2004). As Pedhazur and Schmelkin (1991) note: “How can one conceive of a construct and proceed to develop a measure of it in the absence of a domain of content?” (p. 79). The taxonomy and homogeneous classes discovered in this research serve as the requisite foundation for this future work. Once completed, researchers can use this measure to assess insiders’ PMB activity empirically within their organizations while also determining the nomological network of each homogeneous class of PMBs.

In closing, it is important to acknowledge that we were not able to study the evolution of PMBs, which study is an important part of the field of systematics (McKelvey 1978, 1982). To study this evolution, researchers must understand that as technologies, organizational environments, and information security threats change, the theory of diversity might need to integrate new PMBs. Just as alterations have been made to important theories of diversity regarding plant pathogens (Shoemaker 1981), bird naming conventions (Sibley and Monroe 1990), the periodic table (Scerri 2007), accounting standards, and human thinking (Anderson and Krathwohl 2001), we anticipate that other researchers can help evaluate the evolution and progression of PMBs over time and enrich the taxonomy. This evolution is important because behavioral information security is a dynamic field that may never be static. We expect new PMBs to develop over time and older behaviors to possibly become obsolete in the future; these types of changes are not unusual for most taxonomic systems and classifications. It will also be important to assess the taxonomy and its evolution in cross-cultural settings.

Clay Posey is an assistant professor of Management Information Systems in the Culverhouse College of Commerce and Business Administration at the University of Alabama. His research has been presented at various national and international conferences and has been published in several academic journals including MIS Quarterly, European Journal of Information Systems, and Computers & Security. Tom L. Roberts is the Clifford R. King Professor of Information Systems and Director of the Center for Information Assurance at Louisiana Tech University. He received his Ph.D. in Information Systems from Auburn University. He developed Louisiana Tech’s Certificate in Information Assurance programs. His research interests include insider computer abuse, information security and assurance, computer privacy and online self-disclosure, information quality, and collaborative systems. His research has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, European Journal of Information Systems, Computers & Security, and others.

Paul Benjamin Lowry is an associate professor of Information Systems at the City University of Hong Kong. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona. He has published articles in numerous journals including MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Information Systems Journal, European Journal of Information Systems, Computers & Security, and Communications of the ACM. He serves as an guest associate editor at MIS Quarterly, and as an associate editor for European Journal of Information Systems, Information and Management, Electronic Commerce Research and Aplications, and Communications of the AIS. His research interests include behavioral information security (e.g., protection motivation, accountability, whistle-blowing, compliance, deception, privacy), human–computer interaction (e.g., trust, culture, intrinsic motivations), e-commerce, and scientometrics of IS research.

Rebecca (Becky) J. Bennett is the Herbert McElveen Professor of Management at Louisiana Tech University in Ruston, Louisiana.

Her research has appeared in Academy of Management Journal, Journal of Applied Psychology, Journal of Organizational Behavior, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and others. She serves on the editorial moards of Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes and Journal of Organizational Behavior.

James (Jim) F. Courtney holds the Humana–McCallister Eminent Scholar Chair in Computer Information Systems at Louisiana Tech

University. His academic experience includes positions at the University of Central Florida, Texas A&M, Georgia Tech, Texas Tech, Lincoln University (New Zealand), and SUNY – Buffalo. ## Summary of Theory of PMB Diversity

## Summary of Theory of PMB Diversity

The key result of our taxonomic analysis was that we discovered that organization insiders think of PMBs in three key dimensions. We labeled the three discovered dimensions of insiders’ perceptual map of PMBs specifically as promotion difficulty (first dimension), degree of criticality (second dimension), and degree of common sense (third dimension). In this section, we discuss why we labeled these dimensions as such.

In terms of the first dimension, insiders separated PMBs on whether these activities were burdensome, whether they were always an issue within the individuals’ organizations, and whether the activities required training. We chose to label this dimension as promotion difficulty because of the underlying, ongoing burden, focus, and effort required with these PMBs. Namely, PMBs classified as high in promotion difficulty are behaviors that insiders believe should remain a steady focus of the organization because the behaviors place more of a burden on the insiders to perform effectively. Additionally, these PMBs require more formal training to ensure the behaviors’ efficacy. These PMBs also require continual emphasis within organizations and include the behaviors of double-checking work completed to ensure accuracy and backing up data on a regular basis.

Conversely, PMBs residing closer to the low end of the promotion difficulty dimension are those that require little-to-no formal training or continual awareness programs because the behaviors either (1) do not encumber the insider when performed and are more easily performed than those behaviors in the high classification or (2) are already being performed to such a degree that organizations need only minimal awareness efforts. Example behaviors include locking workstations before the insiders physically leave the workspace or immediately informing the proper authorities after thieves steal computing equipment.

Degree of criticality is the second dimension within the classification scheme identified by the ProFit procedures. We labeled this dimension relative to the criticalness of the PMBs according to the collective insider mindset. The dimension varies according to whether the activities are commonly accepted protocols or standards, should be performed by everyone within organizations regardless of position, should always be performed, and whether there is an obvious need for such behaviors. Insiders should perform PMBs that have a high degree of criticality—regardless of occupation, status, or organization. These behaviors are included as part of the adopted company protocol and are generally accepted by all insiders. Example behaviors include discussing sensitive information with authorized individuals only, logging out of a computer system as soon as an individual has completed his/her task with it, and changing passwords according to organizational guidelines.

Insiders consider PMBs with a low degree of criticality as having a limited scope and are less obvious than those behaviors with a high criticality. According to insiders, not everyone needs to perform these behaviors and not at all times within the organization. These characteristics are due in part to insiders’ views that many of these behaviors have not been formally adopted within their organizations as protocol or as every individual’s responsibility to perform. Example behaviors placed in this low classification include setting the permissions of computer files to prevent unauthorized access, keeping the electronic devices assigned to insiders by the organization with the individuals at all times, and adequately documenting any changes the individual makes in the computer system are placed within this low classification.

One notable difference in the mindset of insiders regarding behaviors in the high and low degree of criticalness types is the perceptual distance between two behaviors: high—reporting a coworker who deviates from those guidelines to the proper authorities; and low—reminding a coworker of information security guidelines. Interestingly, insiders as a collective unit believe it is critical for everyone to report an internal deviant, while only few believe that it is their responsibility to issue friendly reminders about security matters to their peers.

We best represent the third and final general dimension in the formal taxonomy as degree of common sense. Insiders consider behaviors that are high common sense as having clear logic and rationale. Many insiders referred to these activities as “knowledge commonly held by everyone.” Examples of such commonsensical behaviors are working at a steady but cautious pace or immediately reporting a lost physical access card to management.

Low common sense PMBs refer to actions whose founding logic may be unclear to insiders and whose foundations go beyond norma requirements. For example, many insiders did not consider the behavior of not opening e-mails that “just do not look right” as commonsensica because they cannot assess what constitutes “legitimate business communication” adequately. Insiders thus do not believe it to be reasonable to ask them to determine what looks “right” or what looks suspicious. Insiders also collectively believe that disallowing access to the Internet for nonwork-related material and disallowing use of corporate e-mail for personal matters are irrational requests. This is a particularly salient finding as extant corporate security practice often clash with this collective mindset.

## Summary of Homogeneous PMB Classes

In addition to the discovery of the cognitive taxonomy, we found that various homogeneous classes of PMBs exist. Cluster analyses identified 14 unique clusters within the MDS structure; however, a few of the clusters had only one or two members. Although all of the behaviors are PMBs, not all are similar enough for grouping with others. For example, the behaviors of gaining approval before setting up a wireless network within the organization and the act of keeping the physical electronic equipment assigned to insiders by the organization with them at all times when away from the organization are single-item clusters. Likewise, due to the wide array of behaviors in the perceptual space, some PMBs belong to a cluster whose other behaviors might not appear similar. In these cases, we defined clusters according to the majority of behaviors making up the composition of the clusters. Table C1 (in Appendix C) lists the individual behaviors comprising each of the clusters. We describe the 14 clusters in detail, as follows.

1. Legitimate e-mail handling refers to those PMBs dealing specifically with insiders’ use and handling of corporate e-mail. As a communication method, insiders must handle e-mail securely to limit the detrimental effects posed by organizational security threats. Insiders’ activities such as only responding to e-mails that have legitimate business requests and only opening e-mail attachments originating from known individuals belong in this cluster. Respondents continually mentioned that they have difficulty determining what specifies “legitimate” electronic communication. Some respondents mentioned “all e-mails deserve a response” due to their inability to make this determination. Other comments indicate that many insiders believe that their follow-up to an inadvertent e-mail is necessary only if that e-mail contained sensitive information, whereas other individuals believe that they “don’t send information that is THAT private. [They] would just send it again to the correct address” (emphasis in original). Accordingly, legitimate e-mail handling activities apply to everyone and require more of an organizational focus to assist insiders in making appropriate determinations of “legitimate” communication attempts.

2. Protection against unauthorized exposure is composed of PMBs that specifically limit the exposure of sensitive information to unauthorized internal and external sources. PMBs in this cluster describe how insiders manipulate their personal and/or shared workstations to accomplish this goal. Behaviors such as setting a workstation’s screen saver to password protect, locking a workstation before leaving one’s workspace, and logging other individuals out of a shared workstation before using it belong to this cluster. To prevent unauthorized individuals from receiving sensitive organizational information, insiders must be careful to not discuss sensitive information verbally in proximity to unauthorized persons and to disallow others from doing work for which insiders are responsible. Respondents referred to these activities as “most obvious,” “following good business practice,” and are “always the best thing to do for everyone.”

3. Policy-driven awareness and action behaviors are rooted in formal corporate policy. This set of PMBs includes storing information and changing passwords according to accepted internal security protocol. Further, this behavioral group also includes an insider not using system shortcuts (this involves choosing to skip steps in a protocol so that insiders’ perceived level of performance or customer servic supersedes level of protection; for example, allowing an unauthorized subordinate access to data to cut through red tape to “get a job done”). Despite being against corporate policy, individuals responded that “as long as [the shortcut] does not compromise the integrity of my work or the computer system, I will use [it],” “sometimes you need to meet a deadline,” and “it depends on how effective [the shortcut] is.” Other behaviors in this cluster include not e-mailing spam to coworkers and not bringing a laptop from home and attaching it to the corporate network without prior authorization.

4. Appropriate data entry and management is composed of PMBs related to insiders’ care for data entry and data management. These behaviors include the proper disposal and destruction of all unneeded sensitive documents and the regular backing up of data and documents. Other PMBs assigned to this cluster deal directly with the accuracy of data-entry activities (e.g., double-checking one’s work and working at a cautious but steady pace). Respondents mentioned that they “take pride in doing [their] job right” and that it is “always wise to have a backup in case something happens to the original documents.”

5. Document conversion refers to insiders’ converting sensitive, corporate documents to other formats to reduce potential alterations from their original content by security threats. For example, “tax-related filings,” “legal documents whose wording cannot be changed,” and “official corporate documents to be placed on a website” would need an added measure of protection. Most insiders perform this activity by converting a document to a PDF format. The activity of document conversion is different from many of the other behaviors because “PDFs are made to be sent to others.”

6. Secure software, e-mail, and Internet use refers to a general set of PMBs that highlight insiders’ secure use of computer software, electronic mail, and the Internet. For example, insiders should apply software updates immediately to their workstations upon receipt of notification of important updates. Insiders must also refrain from using corporate e-mail and the Internet for personal reasons while at work and must install software on workstations only if they received prior authorization to do so. However, some respondents believe “these [behaviors to be] important but often not followed” and see it as an impossibility for them to wholly conform to such restrictions. Insiders justify their actions with statements like “some personal e-mail on a limited basis can be acceptable,” “everyone inevitably gets some personal e-mail once in a while,” “there are some times for an organization insider to get personal business done,” and the behaviors should be “allowed as long as they don’t distract from getting business tasks done quickly.” Employees should apply software updates “as soon as is reasonably possible but not if someone is in the middle of a project.”

7. Verbal and electronic sensitive-information protection is the set of PMBs dealing with insiders’ control of their verbal and electroni communication to limit unneeded release of sensitive information. Verbally discussing sensitive information with authorized individuals only and not discussing sensitive corporate information with the media without prior approval reside in this cluster. Individuals within organizations must also attempt to limit the exposure of electronic communication and documentation. PMBs stipulating that insiders not allow anyone to look over their shoulder while working on sensitive documents (e.g., using a laptop in a crowded area such as an airport or airplane) and double-checking all potential recipients of an e-mail before sending it decrease the chance that sensitive electronic documentation falls into the wrong hands.

8. Wireless installation is composed of the PMB that refers to an insider’s seeking permission before setting up a wireless network access point within the organization. Overall, insiders responded that this activity would be “grounds for dismissal” if prior authorization was not given. However, most respondents stated that they would “have no idea how to set up a wireless access point” and such a request would require additional training. Others mentioned that the installation of wireless access points within the organization is the responsibility of managers or the IT group, and thus to place this responsibility on all insiders would be unreasonable because they “don’t need to worry about this” along with everything else for which they are responsible

## 9. Widely applicable security etiquette and

10. Distinctive security etiquette contain behaviors that are general rules of professional conduct within organizations in terms of protecting information assets. The key difference between these two sets of behavioral groups is that insiders believe that one set is critical to more positions and industries, whereas the other should be more restricted to a smaller body of individuals and/or organizations. For example, logging in and out of systems immediately upon completing job tasks and fully reading and paying attention to organizational security newsletters or other forms of communication is necessary for everyone. However, setting file permissions to restrict unauthorized access—although a good, general PMB—does not appear to be the responsibility of many insiders. Further, not all organizations issue access cards or give everyone access to important information-security information

11. Coworker reliance contains PMBs related to insiders relying on each other for important information-security information and activitie within organizations. These behaviors state that insiders have the ability to remind their fellow coworkers of information-security guidelines and policies or inform coworkers when they are violating organization rules

Despite this capability, insiders sometimes feel uneasy or hesitant about approaching one of their fellow employees if there is a chance that they could be incorrect. Some insiders mentioned that these activities require them “to take a leadership role to ensure that others adopt [policy],” which “keeps others out of trouble” even if a simple reminder of the guidelines can avert disaster; meanwhile, others leave this responsibility to management or the IT security group. Realistically, insiders must use caution in using these approaches because they do not want to appear to upset office politics and relationships.

12. Account protection is the set of PMBs referring to insiders’ protection of their system account information, as well as the resources the individuals are able to access under their individual accounts. These behaviors include not allowing anyone else to use the insider’s personal account or workstation or an insider not using another coworker’s account. Moreover, insiders should be concerned with the information resources accessed when logged on under their account. However, some respondents mentioned that “if it saves time and won’t affect anything, [they] will use another account.” This act largely “depends on the situation” and occurs “only under certain circumstances.” Others stated that “it depends on the [insider’s] ethics, but if [the insider] wants to be treated the same way, [the insider] respects the coworker’s privacy” and will do it if s/he receives the coworker’s permission to access the system under the coworker’s credentials.

13. Immediate reporting of suspicious behavior is the set of PMBs that specify the importance of insiders in swiftly reporting potential security dangers to appropriate authorities. Organizations rely on insiders to report suspicious physical or electronic activity to minimize potential security threats as a major line of defense. An example behavior is immediately notifying the proper internal authorities about a coworker’s negligent security-related behavior.

14. Secure equipment location and storage is composed of PMBs specifying that insiders should always keep electronic devices (e.g., laptops, personal digital assistants) issued to them by their organization with them at all times. Although considered a worthy expectation, many insiders stated that always keeping these devices with them “just isn’t sensible” because it “can be under [their] control but not with [them] at all times.” This control is accomplished by devices being “locked up at home or hotel room, but they can’t be beside you all of the time.” Whereas all insiders are not issued portable electronic devices by their organizations, respondents mentioned individuals who make sure to carry these devices all of the time when away from their office were “obsessing with following rules.”
