Abst ract : This paper develops a process model of how and why complementarity and substitution form over time between contractual and relational governance in the context of information systems outsourcing. Our analysis identifies four distinct process patterns that explain this formation as the outcome of interaction processes between key elements of both contractual and relational governance. These patterns unveil the dynamic nature of complementarity and substitution. In particular, we show that the relationship between contractual and relational governance oscillates between complementarity and substitution. Those oscillations are triggered mainly by three types of contextual events (goal fuzziness, goal conflict, and goal misalignment). Surprisingly, substitution of informal control did not occur as an immediate reaction to external events but emerged as a consequence of preceding complementarity. Thus, our study challenges the prevailing view of an either/or dichotomy of complementarity and substitution by showing that they are causally connected over time.

Bot h cont ract ual and rel at ional governance are vital for successful information systems (IS) outsourcing [16, 29, 42]. While contractual governance emphasizes the importance of contracts and their exercise as formal control [13, 14, 36], relational governance relies on trust and informal control [14, 28, 39]. Both types of governance have their unique strengths [2, 14, 39, 47]. Contractual governance prescribes expected outcomes and behaviors for the client and vendor, and thus is an effective safeguard against opportunism. Relational governance is particularly suitable to cope with unforeseen events as it allows flexible reactions to issues not covered by the contract. Since the strength of one is the weakness of the other, it appears plausible that contractual and relational governance complement each other [14, 39]. This complementary view, however, is contrasted by the substitutional view, which argues that pursuing one reduces the need for the other, or even destroys the strengths of the other [39], thus, it would be inefficient, or even counterproductive, to pursue contractual and relational governance simultaneously.

These opposing views have led to a debate on the relationship between contractual and relational governance [5, 14, 33, 39, 48]. Conceptually, this debate has parsed the relationship into a polarized complements versus substitutes dichotomy [31]. However, empirical research has found evidence for both complementarity and substitution [5, 14, 39, 40, 48]. In light of the complements versus substitutes dichotomy, this coexistence seems paradoxical, indicating a gap in the current theoretical and empirical understanding of the relationship between contractual and relational governance. Starting from the premise that the polarized either/or distinction is a simplification of reality concealing complex interrelationships, our study seeks to reconcile this paradox by answering the following research question:

## RQ: How and why do complementarity and substitution form over time?

To address this question, we adopt two promising strategies for studying paradox [31]. First, we integrate different conceptualizations of contractual and relational governance used by prior research. Second, we explicitly recognize the role of time in studying the formation of complementarity and substitution. Drawing on four IS outsourcing projects, we inductively develop a process model consisting of four distinct process patterns. The process patterns show that complementarity and substitution form as an outcome of interactions in the governance adaptation process. Based on the process model, we are able to show that the relationship between contractual and relational governance oscillates between complementarity and substitution. Such oscillations are mainly the consequence of the occurrence of three different types of contextual triggers (goal fuzziness, goal conflict, and goal misalignment), but as an important exception substitution of informal control is not explained by a contextual event, instead, it is the consequence of preceding complementarity. Our process view moves beyond the prior either/or dichotomy and provides an advanced theoretical understanding of complementarity and substitution that accounts for their dynamic nature and even their causal connectedness. The value of this advanced understanding is that the seemingly paradoxical coexistence of complementarity and substitution in reality can be explained and contradictory results of prior research resolved.

## The Dichotomous View of Complementarity and Substitution

The idea of complementarity and substitution originated in economics [35] and was soon taken up by both management and IS scholars [37, 48]. In the governance context, complementarity and substitution have been treated as a dichotomous, mutually exclusive phenomena in terms of what they are, why they form, and how they are related (see Table 1).

## What Complementarity and Substitution Are

Prior research has referred to complementarity as doing more of one thing (e.g., contractual governance) increases the benefits of doing more of the other (e.g., relational governance); substitution referred to the opposite, that is, doing more of one thing decreases the benefits of the other [48]. Variance-theoretical studies invoked these definitions to conceptualize complementarity and substitution as statistical effects. Complementarity (substitution) has been conceptualized as a positive (negative) relationship between contractual and relational governance [39] or as a positive (negative) interaction effect between the two on a dependent variable [14, 48].

## Why Complementarity and Substitution Form

Variance research invoked competing causal mechanisms [3] to explain the occurrence of complementarity and substitution. These causal mechanisms included temporal cause-and-effect relationships and attributed the occurrence of complementarity and substitution to interactions between contractual and relational governance in the process of adapting governance. For substitution, it has been argued that contractual and relational governance replace each other because they are functional equivalents (replacing), that is, since contractual and relational governance do the same thing (e.g., reduce uncertainty), there is no need for the one if the other is in place [10, 14, 39]. In contrast, proponents of a complementary view argue that contractual and relational governance have unique—that is, dissimilar but jointly valuable—strengths such that the one compensates for the weaknesses of the other (compensating) [18]. For instance, relational governance would compensate for the adaptive limits of contractual governance, whereas contractual governance would compensate for the safeguarding limits of relational governance [2, 14, 39, 47]. Another line of reasoning to justify substitution contended that contractual governance would dampen the formation of relational governance (dampening) [14, 17, 39]. For instance, a detailed contract may be perceived as a signal of distrust, and consequently have a pernicious effect on relational governance [39]. Again, proponents of the complementary view argued the opposite. For them, the two governance mechanisms do not negate but rather enable each other (enabling) because each creates favorable conditions that facilitate the other [19]. In particular, effective contractual governance may be enabled by relational governance since it “changes the inputs and therefore facilitates achieving” the goals of contractual governance and vice versa [19, p. 417]. As an example, in the process of crafting contracts, trusted relationships evolve that enable the exercise of relational governance, while the exercise of relational governance facilitates acquiring knowledge that subsequently can be used to refine and exercise contracts [14, 39, 47].

## How Complementarity and Substitution Are Related

Prior static research treated complementarity and substitution as logically isolated polarities [31]. This polarization entailed two core properties. First, complementarity and substitution were separated into either/or abstractions, reducing the debate on the relationship between contractual and relational governance to the question, “Do they ‘function as substitutes or complements?’” [39, p. 707]. Therefore, prior static research implicitly assumed the relationship between contractual and relational governance to be either completely stable over time or at least stable given a particular context; the relationship could not change over time nor could contractual and relational governance simultaneously be complements and substitutes. Second, complementarity and substitution were assumed to be not related to each other, that is, they were treated as logical opposites that mutually excluded each other. Informed by these assumptions, prior research assumed that the relationship between governance mechanisms can be adequately assessed at a single point in time with a static research design. Table 1 summarizes the dichotomous view of complementarity and substitution.

In recent years, however, empirical research has questioned the either/or dichotomy of complementarity and substitution by providing strong evidence for a “bewildering array of possible relationships” [40, p. 11]. Whereas some studies supported the substitutional view [27, 49], others substantiated the complementary view [7, 18, 39]. Other studies took a more nuanced perspective by demonstrating that some forms of contractual governance (e.g., certain contract characteristics) act as substitutes to relational governance while others act as complements [14, 46, 48] or by insinuating that the relationship between governance mechanisms may be contingent upon contextual variables [5]. Thus, even though theory had framed complementarity and substitution as incompatible with one another, empirical research has found support for both, which suggests that they may coexist in reality. This, however, appears paradoxical if indeed complementarity and substitution are logically isolated polarities [31].

## Toward a Process View of Complementarity and Substitution

To elucidate the seemingly paradoxical coexistence of complementarity and substitution and thereby clarify the complements versus substitutes debate, we follow Lewis’s [31] strategies for studying paradox by adopting a multidimensional conceptualization and by explicitly considering the role of time. Accordingly, our primary objective is to provide an empirically grounded process understanding of how and why complementarity and substitution form. Such a process view, however, does not neccesarily stand in opposition to prior variance research; instead, it can fruitfully draw on what research has achieved and at the same time provide additional insights into the formation process of complementarity and substitution. For that purpose, our process view should be able to (1) capture events triggering the emergence of complementarity and substitution; (2) capture the causal mechanisms that bring about complementarity and substitution, that is, interaction processes between contractual and relational governance; and (3) relate these interaction patterns to the well-established notions of complementarity and substitution. In the following, we outline these three elements in detail to build a preliminary process model.

## Adaptation Trigger: Exploring Triggering Events

Process research has established the notion of triggers to signify events that trigger particular processes [26]. Such events may reflect the immediate demands of the situation such as changes in contingency variables [43]. Since there is initial evidence that complementarity and substitution may be linked to the value of contingency variables [5, 25, 40], we will explore whether different types of events trigger different types of governance adaptation processes that can be classified as complementarity or substitution.

## Adaptation Process: Capturing Governance Parameter Interactions

To explore untested causal mechanisms that attribute the formation of complementarity and substitution to interactions in the process of governance adaptation, we develop the notion of governance parameter interactions. In this study, the phrase governance parameter refers to the most commonly applied concepts of prior research used to assess contractual and relational governance. Prior conceptualizations of contractual governance have highlighted design characteristics of the contract (e.g., complexity or level of detail) [14, 15, 39] or behavior aspects such as the use or the exercise of the contract as formal control [6, 41, 48]. Similarly, prior conceptualizations of relational governance have primarily highlighted trust [8, 14] or behaviors based on trust such as informal control actions [7, 48]. Given our goal to better understand the seemingly paradoxical coexistence of complementarity and substitution, we develop a multidimensional conceptualization [31]. Thus, we will conceptualize contractual governance as encompassing contract and formal control and relational governance as encompassing trust and informal control. Each of these parameters can be subject to change that can be described in manifold ways [38]. For instance, trust may grow, a contract may be refined, or the use of formal and informal control may be intensified. Alternatively, trust may diminish, a contract clause may be dropped, and so forth [4, 6, 23]. Two governance parameters are said to interact over time if change in one parameter depends on change in another [30, 44], for instance, if a greater use of formal control leads to a drop in trust.

## Adaptation Outcome: A Dynamic Conceptualization of Complementarity and Substitution

To relate governance parameter interactions to the formation process of complementarity and substitution, we conceptualize them in terms of the as yet untested causal mechanisms described above. The idea is that replacing and dampening are building blocks for substitution. Thus, substitution refers to an interaction pattern in which more of one parameter leads to less of another because the other is mitigated (dampening) or not needed anymore (replacing) (see Figure 1). Likewise, compensating and enabling are the building blocks for complementarity. Since complementarity builds on the concept of positive feedback loops, a complementarity pattern is therefore characterized by two successive reinforcing interactions [37], that is, either two successive enabling interactions, two successive compensating interactions, or a combination of both such as an enabling followed by a compensating interaction (see Figure 1).

In sum, our preliminary process model locates the emergence of complementarity and substitution in interactions between governance parameters in the process of governance adaptation while different adaptation processes may be triggered by different types of events (see Figure 2).

## Case Selection

We chose four out of 90 outsourcing projects from the project database of the human resources department of a German bank (BANK) to study the development of complementarity and substitution over time. Our primary case selection criterion was project duration, that is, each selected project had either already started at least one year ago or was planned to continue for at least two more years. This ensured that the time frame of each project covered several years, and therefore increased the likelihood of pattern variation over time [50]. While our primary objective was to study changes in the constructs of interest (triggers, processes, and outcomes) within each case, we aimed at meaningful comparisons between the cases selected. For that purpose, we made sure that the cases were similar in those dimensions that were not of direct theoretical interest for our research question but were identified as relevant for governance of IS outsourcing projects by prior research. Therefore, all of the projects needed to be similar in size [6, 23], type of outsourced task (i.e., all projects involved both software development and maintenance) [23], and use the same development methodology (i.e., an adapted waterfall approach) [32]. ## Data Collection

Data were collected retrospectively in two phases [21]. Phase 1 took place from September to October 2009 and phase 2 from June 2011 to December 2012. In both phases, interviews and documents were the primary data sources. As IS outsourcing governance is exercised mainly by the client [41], we primarily selected interviewees from the bank who were responsible for managing the partnership with the outsourcing vendor (i.e., senior managers [SMs], project managers [PMs], and application owners [AOs]). However, to capture complex relational processes between the client and vendor, we also interviewed vendor managers (VMs). In addition, we conducted interviews with higher-level managers from BANK (i.e., a project portfolio manager, the head of centralized client management, and the head of human resources application management) who provided us with information about the technological and organizational context across the projects. The interviews were based on a semistructured interview guide, took about one hour, and were mostly conducted face-to-face. Document collection included contracts, operating-level agreements (OLAs), service-level agreements (SLAs), project plans, and audit documentation. Retrospective data collection allowed us to capture relevant project developments and governance changes over several years while reducing the risk of data overload compared to a continuous longitudinal data gathering [13]. To alleviate the disadvantages of retrospective data collection, we applied three strategies [13]. First, cognitive bias and impression management was tackled by asking the interviewees about particular events instead of their general view of the project. Second, to address respondent bias, we triangulated the interviews with documents [50]. If there were discrepancies between interview statements and documents, we contacted the interviewees for clarification. Third, we used multiple sources of evidence to establish construct validity [23].

## Data Analysis

Each IS outsourcing project represents a case with governance adaptations as our unit of analysis. Informed by our theoretically derived preliminary process model, each case was split into episodes that begin with a triggering event and end with a particular outcome (complementarity/substitution) brought about by a governance adaptation process. We followed an iterative four-stage process of data analysis and theory building in which the findings of earlier stages informed later stages and vice versa (see [13, 21]).

## Stage 1: Understanding Case Story and Context

Our first analysis was based on initial information about the projects and their organizational and technological context. We then analyzed the information provided by the project manager of each project to understand the project history. At the end of Stage 1 we had a basic understanding of the chronological flow of events, which was documented in a graphical timeline and a first case write-up.

## Stage 2: Narrowing Down Governance Changes

The second stage built primarily on documents and interviews from the project level to draw a rich picture of governance changes within each case. During this stage, both the documents and the graphical timeline for each case helped us to verify the consistency of subjective accounts. This information was summarized in case study reports (second write-up) in which we extensively used citations from interviews and documents to stay close to the original data and thus achieve high levels of accuracy [13, 26]. To further improve external validity [50], we then conducted a feedback presentation with the bank. This afforded us a detailed and verified understanding of the reasons for and consequences of governance changes within each case.

## Stage 3: Analyzing Trigger-Adaptation-Outcome Episodes

In the third stage we coded the data using NVIVO 9.2. Each piece of evidence was mapped to the elements of our preliminary process model. First, we analyzed the adaptation process by identifying text passages in the interview transcripts and documents that reflected changes in trust, contract, formal control, and informal control [34]. We analyzed whether each change was linked to a related change in another governance parameter to uncover governance interactions. These interactions were coded according to the causal mechanisms captured (see Tables 1 and 4). Second, we examined events that triggered each adaptation process and thus reflect the beginning of an episode. To link these triggers to interactions in the governance adaptation processes, we developed a visual map for each case [26, 34]. Visual maps were compared and discussed between the authors, and any discrepancies were clarified by revisiting the data. Third, we examined the outcome of these governance adaptations. For this purpose, empirically observed governance adaptation processes were matched against theoretically derived complementarity and substitution patterns [50]. The occurrence of those patterns was also used to determine the end of an episode. Thus, by the end of the third stage of data analysis, we had identified trigger-adaptation-outcome episodes for each individual case.

## Stage 4: Developing a Process Model

In the fourth stage we moved from the episodes level via the case level to the cross-case level. We inductively constructed a process model of complementarity and substitution for each individual case. In doing so, we ordered the episodes chronologically and sought to identify connections between them. These models were then compared across the four cases to uncover recurring patterns. This phase of repeatedly comparing and integrating findings from the individual cases allowed us to arrive at our final process model. During the fourth stage we started contrasting our empirical findings with the dichotomous view on complementarity and substitution to reveal new theoretical insights.

## Within-Case Analysis

Case 1: Candidate

In 2006, BANK purchased Gamma’s software solution to support its recruitment process. The outsourcing included the provision of the software package, customization, software maintenance, and application hosting.

Episode 1. When the project started, the parties had not yet elicited the detailed requirements needed to customize the software to the peculiarities of BANK’s payroll process. This triggered a governance adaptation process with the overarching objective of overcoming this uncertainty. Contractually prescribed “onsite [configuration] workshops” (SM) were carried out (formal control). The social interaction during these sessions led to trusted relationships. This enabled subsequent informal information exchange (informal control), which facilitated the clarification of the bank’s requirements, which were formalized as deliverables in the contract. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other in a recursive-enabling interaction; contractual governance facilitated the development of relational governance and relational governance allowed for the sophistication of the contract (contractual governance) (see Figure 3).

Episode 2. The subsequent customization of the system led to a goal conflict, since Gamma’s developers not only customized the system through parameterization but also “had changed the codebase [of BANK’s software instance] an awful lot” (PM). Those code-based changes accumulated to the degree to which new releases of the standard software could not be deployed to BANK anymore; they were simply too different from the standard. This goal conflict triggered a process of governance adaptation. Both parties realized that sticking to the contract and forcing Gamma to keep with the regular release cycle to make the updates work was not an option. Instead of enforcing formal control, both parties sought to resolve the conflict informally. In informal meetings, they agreed to replace the contractual obligation with an informal compromise (informal control), that is, within each release cycle, the bank was allowed to select 30 adaptations of the software that could be either custom-made or predefined functionalities offered by new releases. Consequently, the bank stopped monitoring the respective contract clause. Thus, relational governance first compensated for the weakness of contractual governance in resolving the goal conflict (compensating), then the informally achieved compromise substituted for the contractually prescribed goal of BANK’s participation in the regular release cycle (replacing) (see Figure 4).

Episode 3. At the end of 2008, Gamma was taken over by Delta. ## Case 2: Highpot

In 2009, BANK decided to support performance evaluation of their employees using Beta’s Web-based software-as-a-service (SaaS) solution. The contract with Beta included the parameterization and provision of the software. The bank used contractual documents from previous SaaS partnerships as templates. The objective was to provide this project with a high degree of formalization right from the beginning and to guide the initial parameterization.

Episode 1. Soon after project kickoff, the project manager realized that the contract included “bad documentation of processes and requirements [that were] too abstract and ill-defined” (PM). This triggered a governance adaptation process to resolve this ambiguity. The project team initially attended the contractually defined configuration sessions to gain clarity about the requirements (formal control). However, during these sessions, it was discovered that the software was lacking an important functionality. The software did not allow for role-based access privileges (i.e., a “superior’s view” that allowed superiors to confidentially document their true opinion about a given employee [AO]). At this point both parties began to resolve the issue in an informal way. They engaged in informal dialogues (informal control) that allowed the elicitation of detailed functional requirements for the missing feature. Subsequently, these requirements were formalized as deliverables in the contract. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; first, relational governance compensated for the limits of contractual governance in eliciting requirements (compensating), then, this knowledge facilitated both parties to refine the contract (enabling).

Episode 2. Further configuration sessions brought to light that the “administration [of the software was] inconvenient and time-consuming” (PM), putting the global rollout at risk. A goal conflict arose. While Beta was obliged to provide BANK with about 50 country-specific configurations, the contract did not specify how much work these configurations would involve. Once it became clear that many of the customizations required “by hand” administration (PM), Beta raised serious doubts about the economic value of its relationship with BANK. To solve this problem, the client and vendor engaged in informal dialogues (informal control). BANK agreed to not enforce the contractually defined global rollout, instead agreeing to a less demanding performance norm (“postponing global rollout,” “in only a few countries” [PM]). The norm became the basis for subsequent informal control. Hence, this episode shows how relational governance was used initially to compensate for the weakness of contractual governance in resolving a goal conflict (compensating) and then replaced the formal exercise of the contract’s rollout clause (contractual governance) by introducing an informal norm (replacing).

Episode 3. Subsequently, the focus moved to tailoring the contract templates to meet the specific project demands. This took place in four consecutive but overlapping steps.

Episode 3-1. First, the parties turned their attention to the as yet loosely defined committees and lines of communication. For that purpose, BANK and Beta initially conducted formal meetings (formal control). However, both parties first needed a shared understanding of how to interact, and this was achieved through a process of informal knowledge exchange (informal control). Based on this, they redefined the governance model (e.g., new roles) that was subsequently formalized in the OLA (addendum to the contract). Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; relational governance first compensated for the knowledge-sharing limits of contractual governance (compensating), then, the informally accessed knowledge enabled a contract extension (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 3-2. The second adaptation process was triggered because it was undefined how Beta could meet BANK’s security standards. In a formal meeting (formal control), BANK detected that Beta was not compliant with its data center security standards. Again, both parties entered into an informal knowledge exchange (informal control) that resulted in a list of specific measures to improve data center security (e.g., installation of observation cameras), which became an addendum to the contract. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; relational governance first compensated for the knowledge-sharing limits of contractual governance (compensating), then, the informally accessed knowledge enabled a contract extension (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 3-3. The third adaptation process was triggered by the fact that the information technology (IT) policy acceptance clauses had been deliberately left open in the contract since BANK knew how challenging it is for SaaS vendors to comply with all the policies. When BANK and Beta held a governance meeting (formal control), the parties detected that Beta’s solution was not compliant with BANK’s group-wide IT policies. To reach mutual agreement, both parties informally discussed which policies Beta was willing to accept and which BANK was willing to discard (informal control). The result was a list specifying the policies with which Beta had to comply. Subsequently, the policy acceptance clause of the contract was modified accordingly (contract). Hence, contractual and relational governance first compensated for the limits of contractual governance in finding an agreement (compensating) and then the informally precipitated agreement enabled a contract extension (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 3-4. As a consequence of the preceding three incremental refinements of contractual documents, the nature of the interaction between the parties started to shift: When the refined contracts were exercised as formal control (contractual governance) they incrementally substituted the until then frequent informal mutual adjustments (relational governance) (replacing) (see Figure 7).

Episode 4. In 2011, Beta published a new release of it’s software that overwrote a number of code-based customizations of BANK’s instance of the software. The resulting loss of customizations was “a tremendous problem” (AO) for BANK. Most importantly, the “superior’s view” was deleted; thus, employees had “access to [hidden] comments of their superiors” (AO) on their performance. Once realized, Beta had to shut down the solution for five weeks. This triggered an extensive governance adaptation process. The problems resulted in a sudden drop in “[BANK’s] ability to trust the system” (PM). This led BANK to immediately extend the contract substantially. In particular, BANK introduced and formally exercised a new key operating procedure, a new quality assurance and deployment process, a new scorecard, and more demanding service levels. In addition, BANK increasingly relied on existing contractual clauses (“giving more weight to meetings” [AO]) (formal control) while significantly reducing informal mutual adjustments (informal control). Hence, because of the drastic decrease in trust, relational governance was substituted by contractual governance (replacing).

## Case 3: Alumni

In 2000, BANK purchased a software package provided by Epsilon supporting the global graduate recruiting process. Epsilon, a relatively small start-up company, reengineered its solution fundamentally to comply with BANK’s business processes.

Episode 1-1. In 2007, Epsilon decided to “platformize” the software, that is, separate reusable core components from customer-specific components. BANK had to migrate to this new platform. When the migration procedure started (formal control), BANK discovered that the procedure lacked many details, such as who to contact in case of problems and guidelines on how to test the system. To overcome these problems, the parties engaged in intense informal mutual adjustments (informal control). This enabled the parties to develop an understanding about who was the right contact for a given problem and a suitable approach to testing the system. For some time the parties relied on this rather informal approach for the migration, then they later formalized this knowledge in a “communication plan” (SM) and in schedules for “the testing session” (SM). Both the communication plan and the schedules refined the migration procedure (contract). Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; relational governance first compensated for the knowledge-sharing limits of contractual governance (compensating), then, the informally accessed knowledge enabled a contract refinement (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 1-2. Once BANK and Epsilon followed the refined procedure (formal control), it replaced the former informal approach. Thus, formal control substituted for informal control.

Episode 2. Earlier in the project, the parties had agreed on a formal change request process. Whenever the change process started, the parties typically exercised the prescribed process steps (formal control). However, to further clarify the contents of each change, the parties relied on informal knowledge exchange (informal control). In particular, the client and vendor had “an informal discussion” (VM) to reach a shared understanding about the specific contents of each change (informal control). The specification was then added to the contract as a new deliverable for Epsilon (contract). Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; first, relational governance compensated for the limits of contractual governance to clarify the content of each change request (compensating), then, the informal discussions led to a shared understanding of appropriate specifications that enabled a contract addendum (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 3. The last governance adaptation process was triggered when BANK simulated an attack on Epsilon’s system to assess whether it had security loopholes (formal control). When this test revealed a number of security vulnerabilities, the parties were not sure “how these problems should be resolved” (PM). The client and vendor then started to informally interact (informal control) in order to come to an agreement on how and by whom the issues were to be resolved. This was formalized in an “action plan” (PM) that became part of the contract and a basis for subsequent formal control. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; first, relational governance compensated for the knowledge sharing limits of contractual governance (compensating), then, the informally achieved agreement was formalized in a contract addendum (contractual governance) (enabling).

## Case 4: Payslip

In 2008, BANK mandated Alpha to take over the Payslip processing for their Indian branch as soon as possible. The rush was because BANK’s previous Indian payroll vendor, Pre-Alpha, had been convicted of embezzling the payments of several customers.

Episode 1-1. Because of the rapid vendor switch, the outsourcing relationship started with a note-of-understanding as the sole contractual basis. It defined only one governance committee, while the subtleties of BANK’s payroll process remained unclear. This triggered a governance adaptation process aimed at resolving this uncertainty. However, in the committee meeting (formal control) the parties struggled with the “formalities to be clarified” (VM) due to the “unpredictabilities” (PM) and “complexities” (VM) of the project. Thus, they began to share knowledge informally (informal control). This allowed the definition of additional committees with detailed responsibilities and an elaborate description of the payroll process. The committees and the process were then documented in a first “official” contract. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other; relational governance first compensated for the knowledge-sharing limits of contractual governance (compensating) and then the informally accessed knowledge enabled a contract refinement (contractual governance) (enabling).

Episode 1-2. Once the parties exercised the refined contract, that is, the client and vendor increasingly relied on formal control, which replaced the informal mutual adjustments and knowledge exchange (replacing). Thus, formal control substituted for informal control.

Episode 2-1. In 2009, Alpha’s payroll processing software was customized and became operational. Because of the “project’s hectic nature” (PM) at the beginning, the parties had failed to set in place adequate governance mechanisms. This triggered a governance adaptation process. Committee meetings were held (formal control), leading to intense social interaction such that interpersonal trust grew. This reached a point where the client and vendor employees felt that “it is something beyond a client–vendor relationship” (VM). As a consequence of this trusted relationship, the defined formal meetings went more smoothly because “we understand what the other person actually wants” (VM). This facilitated an agreement on more appropriate key performance indicators and service levels, which were then formalized in the contract. Hence, contractual and relational governance complemented each other in a recursive enabling interaction; contractual governance first facilitated the development of relational governance (enabling), which then allowed for the sophistication of the contract (enabling) (contractual governance).

Episode 2-2. As a second result of the increased trust, it was easier for the parties to come to an informal compromise. This compromise became necessary when BANK’s auditors detected that Alpha did not comply with some of BANK’s security policies. The noncompliance pointed to an underlying goal conflict. For Alpha, a provider of standardized services, compliance with all the BANK-specific rules was simply too resource consuming. To solve this problem, BANK’s manager “spontaneously called [his counterpart] late at night” (SM). The fact that they worked together “very, very well” (VM) made it easy for both to work out a compromise (informal control): Alpha’s manager promised to resolve most of the compliance issues, while BANK’s manager accommodated Alpha’s wishes by promising to not control compliance with some very BANK-specific policies (formal control). Hence, the exercise of contractual governance enabled the development of relational governance (enabling), then the parties found an informal compromise (relational governance), which was a substitute for contract enforcement (replacing) (contractual governance) (see Figure 8).

## Cross-Case Analysis: Building a Process Model

Our within-case analysis showed how triggers led to governance adaptation processes. As an outcome of those processes, complementarity and substitution formed. Each case exhibited a unique temporal sequence of complementarity and substitution episodes. We next examine whether there were any repeated patterns across cases and episodes. Our analysis revealed four distinct patterns (see Table 5). Each pattern has a distinct trigger (goal fuzziness, goal conflict, goal misalignment, or preceding complementarity) and a distinct outcome (complementarity, substitution of formal control, combined substitution, or substitution of informal control). The adaptation processes that link triggers to outcomes reflect the causal mechanisms “enabling,” “compensating,” and “replacing.” Together, the four patterns form a process model that explains the development of complementarity and substitution (see Figure 9).

## Pattern 1: Goal Fuzziness Leads to Complementarity

The first pattern was found across all four cases. It explains the formation of complementarity as a process of reciprocal interaction between contractual and relational governance in that the one was enabled or compensated by the other (and vice versa). This process was triggered by goal fuzziness, which refers to uncertainty regarding either the project’s output (i.e., the attributes of the software application to be provided by the vendor) or the process through which the output should be achieved. In the majority of the cases, the causal mechanisms that brought about complementarity matched a “compensating” mechanism followed by an “enabling” mechanism, that is, the exercise of formal control led to the detection of goal fuzziness. Then, in trying to cope with goal fuzziness, the client and vendor employees struggled with the knowledge-sharing limits of contractual governance. As a consequence, the client and vendor also relied on relational governance to informally exchange knowledge, which was then used to refine the contract. Irrespective of the specific causal mechanisms at work, the outcome of the refinement process was a reduction of goal fuzziness resulting from a contract adjustment that either clarified the output or the process. In two episodes (Payslip Episode 2-1 and Candidate Episode 1), two successive “enabling” mechanisms were observed. The exercise of formal meetings to address goal fuzziness led to trusted relationships. Trust then facilitated an informal agreement between the client and vendor, which was subsequently anchored in the contract.

## Pattern 2: Complementarity Leads to Substitution of Informal Control

The second pattern explains the formation of substitution of informal control in favor of formal control in response to preceding complementarity. This pattern deviates strongly from Pattern 1 (as well as from the other patterns) in that governance interactions were not triggered by a contextual event. Instead, substitution of informal control was triggered by the preceding governance adaptation processes. In particular, this pattern occurred when contractual and relational governance mutually reinforced each other in complementary interactions in such a way that the parties were able to design formal mechanisms that were functionally equivalent to some existing informal mechanism (i.e., Pattern 1). This caused a replacement of informal control with the newly specified formal control mechanisms. For instance, exercising contractually prescribed meetings (e.g., Payslip Episode 1-2) or monitoring contractually prescribed outputs (e.g., Highpot Episode 3-4) replaced informal ad hoc meetings or the reliance on shared norms. Thus, the outcome of this adaptation process was a selective substitution of formal control at the expense of informal control; trust and contract were not affected.

## Pattern 3: Goal Conflict Leads to Substitution of Formal Control

The third pattern explains the formation of substitution as a process in which formal control is replaced by informal control in response to a goal conflict. This substitution pattern was triggered when previously undetected goal conflicts between the client and vendor occurred or became problematic. In all cases, this goal conflict was linked to the customization versus standardization trade-off. The acceptance of all BANKspecific requirements would have undermined the vendor’s business model (i.e., providing standardized services for all clients). Notably, the goal conflict was resolved in a governance adaptation process in which the rationales for complementarity and substitution meshed. In particular, either the exercise of formal control called attention to the goal conflict (Highpot Episode 2, Candidate Episode 2) (compensating) or during formal meetings trust developed between the parties despite their conflicting goals, which then facilitated those parties to come to a compromise (Payslip Episode 2-2). (enabling). In either case, the final conflict resolution was reached when the parties agreed there would no longer be formal monitoring of outcomes such as policy compliance (Payslip) or contractually prescribed goals (Highpot) and instead to evaluate performance based on implicit and nonformalized shared norms about less demanding outcomes (replacing). Thus, the outcome of this adaptation process was a selective substitution where formal control was substituted with an informal compromise; trust and contract were not affected.

## Pattern 4: Major Goal Misalignment Leads to Combined Substitution

The fourth pattern explains the formation of substitution as a process in which both relational governance parameters are substituted by the two contractual governance parameters. Thus, in contrast to Patterns 2 and 3, this type of substitution involves all the governance parameters—not just formal and informal control. This substitution pattern was triggered when a major goal misalignment occurred (Highpot Episode 4, Candidate Episode 3). This triggered an adaptation process, since the goal misalignment caused a sudden drop in trust. As a direct consequence, informal control was no longer deemed to be sufficient for governing the project. Thus, BANK reduced informal control and instead put more emphasis on existing formal control. Moreover, BANK designed new controls by expanding the contract (e.g., a new quality assurance process at Highpot) or by refining existing contractual regulations (e.g., a revised governance structure at Candidate). Hence, goal misalignment was addressed in a governance adaptation process in which contract and formal control were used after trust was diminished (replacing). Thus, the outcome of this adaptation process was substitution, where trust and informal control were replaced by contract and formal control.

## Process Model of the Formation of Complementarity and Substitution

Together the four patterns of governance adaption form a process model in which the formation of complementarity and substitution and oscillations between them are triggered by context or preceding complementarity. This process model is not deterministic in that change progresses through a necessary or fixed sequence of actions [49]; rather, each case consists of a unique temporal sequence of complementarity and substitution patterns (see Table 6). This process model suggests that predictable patterns of complementarity and substitution will occur if distinct necessary conditions (triggers) are met: goal fuzziness leads to complementarity (Pattern 1), goal conflict leads to substitution of formal control (Pattern 3), and major goal misalignment leads to combined substitution. In addition to these contextual triggers, substitution of informal control (Pattern 2) occurs only as a consequence of preceding complementarity (Pattern 1).

## How Complementarity and Substitution Are Related

Prior research has treated complementarity and substitution as logically isolated polarities [31], invoking an either/or notion of the relationship between contractual and relational governance. Considering the temporal context allows us to challenge this view. First, across all of our cases, the relationship between contractual and relational governance oscillated episodically between complementarity and substitution (see Table 6). This goes beyond prior research that has not acknowledged such dynamics in the relationship between contractual and relational governance [5, 14, 39]. Second, in our study, changes in this relationship were triggered by contextual events in many cases. This suggests that the change from complementarity to substitution, and vice versa, may vary systematically with the value of a contextual variable. This goes beyond prior IS research that has not accounted for such contingent relationships between contractual and relational governance in its research design [14, 39]. In addition, our findings contribute to the management literature, which has argued that the relationship between governance mechanisms is contingent. Carson et al. [5] found contractual governance to be more effective than relational governance in ambiguous situations, whereas relational governance is more effective in volatile situations. Thus, contractual and relational governance are not “simply substitutes” [5, p. 1058]. By stressing that contractual and relational governance are effective in different situations [48], Carson advocates a substitutes position—one that is very nuanced: in the case of ambiguity, contractual governance substitutes for relational governance; in the case of volatility, relational governance substitutes for contractual governance [5]. This is confirmed and refined by our findings. The temporal connectedness between Pattern 1 and Pattern 2 confirms that in face of ambiguity (i.e., goal fuzziness), contractual governance indeed substitutes for relational governance (end of Pattern 2). Our findings refine this contingent view on the relationship between contractual and relational governance by showing that fluctuations in the relationship are not fully deterministic in a contingency-theoretic sense but are also subject to self-dynamics. In particular, the superior effectiveness of contracts in the face of ambiguity is not inherent to contracts; rather, it emerges over time through complementary interactions with relational governance. In particular, when faced with ambiguity, complementary interactions enable the parties to design the specific contractual clauses needed to mitigate ambiguity (Pattern 1). Formal control derived from those contractual clauses seemed to be functionally equivalent to previously exercised informal controls (Pattern 2). Thus, substitutability is a property that can be created through complementary interactions in the process of governance adaptation. Hence, complementarity and substitution are causally connected—complementarity is a necessary condition for substitution of informal control.

## How and Why Complementarity and Substitution Form

This study is the first to explicitly investigate the untested causal mechanisms invoked by prior research. Thus, it contributes to the theoretical understanding of how and why complementarity and substitution form over time in three respects. First, our results show which of those untested causal mechanisms work in reality (i.e., compensating, enabling, and replacing) and which do not (i.e., dampening). Therefore, our results can be understood as an ex post justification of invoking the former (compensating, enabling, replacing) (see [14, 39, 48]) but not the latter (dampening) (see [9, 45]). The second contribution regards how those different causal mechanisms work together in the formation of complementarity and substitution. In the formation of complementarity, the different causal mechanisms proposed by prior research (i.e., enabling and compensating) are intertwined. This suggests that complementarity is brought about by several interacting causal mechanisms—a novel phenomenon that prior research has not investigated. Third, for the formation of substitution, our results suggest that even the causal mechanisms for complementarity and substitution mesh together.

In particular, a preceding enabling interaction creates the favorable conditions that facilitate the subsequent replacement of formal control in favor of informal control (see Pattern 3). This is a counterintuitive finding in light of prior research that has proposed unique and mutually exclusive causal mechanisms for substitution (replacing, dampening) and complementarity (compensating, enabling) [5, 14, 39]. This implies that substitution is not the opposite of complementarity, it is rather closely connected in two respects. For the substitution of formal control, causal mechanisms mesh together; for the substitution of informal control, complementarity is a necessary precondition.

While the elucidation of this more complex process view advances the theoretical understanding of complementarity and substitution, it also offers a starting point to explain the contradictory results of prior research. This is discussed next.

## How Contradictory Results of Prior Research Can Be Resolved

The process view suggests at least two potential causes for the seemingly paradoxical coexistence of complementarity and substitution found in prior research. First, prior research has not considered the temporal context and therefore may have failed to recognize the dynamic nature of the relationship between contractual and relational governance. In particular, our results suggest that complementarity occurs when new mechanisms are specified or existing mechanisms are refined (Pattern 1). In contrast, substitution occurs subsequently when those mechanisms are exercised (Pattern 2). This suggests that prior contradictions of static research may be due to their differences concerning the time of measurement. A second potential cause of prior contradictions is differences in underlying conceptualizations. This study adopted a multidimensional conceptualization of contractual and relational governance. The coexistence of complementarity and substitution is not necessarily paradoxical if we consider their distinct dimensions. In particular, we identified three different kinds of substitution. While in the formation of combined substitution (Pattern 4) all four governance parameters were involved, substitution can also exclusively manifest on the control dimension, that is, it only involves formal and informal control but not trust and contract (Patterns 2 and 3). However, in no case were trust and contract exclusively involved in a substitution. Thus, our multidimensional conceptualization of contractual and relational governance is able to capture different types of substitution that manifest exclusively between formal and informal control. Those types would be overlooked if contractual and relational governance were conceptualized solely in terms of contract attributes and trust. Hence, in light of our findings, one would expect studies that focus exclusively on formal and informal control to be biased toward substitution and studies that focus on trust and contract to be biased toward complementarity. This is the pattern in prior research: Only weak support was found for a substitutional relationship between contractual and relational governance, if contractual governance was conceptualized as contract (e.g., [14, 39]), whereas solid support for substitution between contractual and relational governance was found when they were conceptualized as formal and informal control (e.g., [20]). Moreover, thi pattern reinforces the argument that the time of measurement is a potential cause for prior contradictions. Studies inspired by control theory are typically concerned with how control is exercised [22, 24, 41, 48] while the preceding design of mechanisms is not usually taken into account. In contrast, studies inspired by transaction cost and agency theory are typically concerned with the design attributes of the contract [14, 39, 40]. Therefore, those studies implicitly focus on the early stages of an outsourcing relationship in which the contract is designed [11], while the contract’s subsequent exercise as formal control is not taken into consideration [41]. Thus, the differences in conceptualizations may affect the measured effect because they mirror different phases of an outsourcing project that differ systematically with respect to the complementary and substitution relationship of contractual and relational governance.

## Future Research

Future research may further refine our process view of how complementarity and substitution form. A longitudinal event-time-series study could test our process model while bypassing the shortcomings of retrospective data collection [38]. This could be achieved by translating the adaptation triggers identified in this study into contingency variables and the adaptation processes into event chains. Then those event chains could be predicted by contingency variables or preceding complementarity. In addition, such a study may also further substantiate a phenomenon that is already indicated by one of our findings—the same governance parameters may simultaneously be complements and substitutes. For example, at Payslip (Episodes 2-1 and 2-2) some formal controls were complements to informal control while others were simultaneously substitutes to informal control.

Another promising direction for future research would be to elaborate on the link between triggering events and adaptation processes. Since the focus of the current study was on the formation of complementarity and substitution, other constructs were not investigated. Future research could build on the triggers and processes identified in this paper to examine why a particular trigger invokes a particular governance adaptation but not another. Thus, there may be hidden mechanisms such as learning, the desire to balance power, or framing effects that may explain why a certain trigger leads to complementarity but not to substitution.

## Managerial Implications

Managers have been given conflicting advice on how to effectively adapt governance in IS outsourcing relationships. If contractual and relational governance complement each other, it has been recommended to emphasize relational governance to reach higher levels of contractual governance. If contractual and relational governance substitute each other, it has been recommended to decrease relational governance in case of high levels of contractual completeness. In light of our results, managers should follow both pieces of advice at different points in time. Thus, they should purposefully make use of the causal mechanisms that create complementarity and substitution.

As an example, if managers are faced with the adaptive limits of contractual governance, they should actively try to compensate this weakness by utilizing relational governance. However, this does not need to be done immediately in a substitutional fashion. Instead, managers should try to utilize the unique knowledge-sharing strength of relational governance to actively design contractual governance mechanisms that are tailored to their situation-specific needs and therefore fulfill the same function as relational governance. As exercise of governance is costly and resource consuming, it is inefficient to pursue several functionally equivalent governance mechanisms simultaneously. Thus, once managers have created functionally equivalent contractual governance mechanisms, they could then avoid costly redundancy by replacing relational governance with contractual governance.

## Limitations

There are several limitations of our study. Our findings are based on data gathered by retrospective interviews in four IS outsourcing projects within one company. This allowed us to compare differences in governance adaptation between the four projects by keeping other conditions stable. However, even with a stable firm context, there may be drivers of complementarity and substitution that were not considered in this study. As an example, our findings highlight the importance of within-project learning for the formation of complementarity and substitution (Patterns 1 and 2). However, there might also have been learning effects across the four projects that were not considered in this study. Future research should see this as an opportunity to investigate the impact of cross-project dynamics on the formation of complementarity and substitution. Another limitation is our conceptualization of complementarity and substitution in terms of formative interaction patterns. While this conceptualization was important to capture the formation process of complementarity and substitution, it is a departure from their original economic notion, as the costs and benefits of governance are ignored. While our study shares this limitation with important work on the relationship between contractual and relational governance [39], future research should follow recent work that considered the costs and benefits of governance by measuring complementarity and substitution in terms of the interaction effect between contractual and relational governance on performance (e.g., [48]).

## Conclusion

In IS out sourcing proj ect s, one key question is whether to govern a vendor either through contractual or relational governance, or through both in combination. Previous research has explored this question against the backdrop of their complementary versus substitutional relationship, but has not reached a consensus. Indeed, from the current theoretical understanding, one would expect to find them to be either complements or substitutes, however, empirical support was found for both. To understand this paradox, we investigated how complementarity and substitution form through continual interactions between the constituent elements of contractual and relational governance. The key finding of our study is that complementarity and substitution are better understood as temporal states than as stable properties; in fact, they may even be causally connected. Across our cases, the emergence of complementarity was always triggered by a contextual event, while the emergence of different types of substitution was triggered by either a contextual event or preceding complementarity. These findings reveal the dynamic and interwoven nature of the relationship between contractual and relational governance. It is dynamic since over the course of a project, the relationship fluctuates between phases of complementarity and substitution. It is interwoven since complementarity can be a necessary precondition for substitution. Our process view refines the prevalent dichotomous either/or notion of complementarity and substitution. This new perspective helped to resolve the seemingly contradictory results of prior research and move us toward a new scientific consensus.
