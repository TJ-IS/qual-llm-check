# THE INTERPLAY BETWEEN HEALTHCARE INFORMATION TECHNOLOGIES AND DENIED CLAIMS<sup>1</sup>

Sezgin Ayabakan Management Information Systems Department, Fox School of Business, Temple University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A {ayabakan@temple.edu}

Hilal Atasoy Accounting and Information Systems Department, Rutgers Business School, Rutgers University New Brunswick, NJ, U.S.A {hilal.atasoy@rutgers.edu}

Department of Operations and Information Management, Wisconsin School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Madison Madison, WI, U.S.A {mpang9@wisc.edu}

This study investigates the role of health information technology (HIT) in reducing claim denials, which are a significant burden for healthcare providers in the U.S. We theorize the impacts of electronic health records (EHRs) on claim denials, starting with an examination of EHR adoption and followed by a deeper assessment of how EHRs are sourced both within a hospital and across hospitals in the same health system. We propose that while EHR adoption reduces the likelihood of claim denials by improving the accuracy and completeness of information processing, it can also increase claim denials if EHR applications are sourced from multiple vendors within a hospital or different vendors across hospitals. Using a large-scale dataset of claim records from the state of Maryland from 2012-2016, we found that the greater the EHR adoption by care providers, the less likely a claim is denied. In addition, our findings suggest that EHRs are more effective in preventing denials when a hospital sources EHR applications from a single vendor and when a group of hospitals in the same health system sources EHRs from the same vendor. Additionally, we observed a decrease in claim denials when physicians previously worked in hospitals utilizing EHR applications from the same vendor. This study provides significant theoretical insights into the information systems literature on HIT and offers practical implications for healthcare providers by uncovering the multifaceted roles of EHRs in information processing and compliance.

Keywords: Denied claims, health information technology, electronic health records, sourcing decisions, single-sourcing

## Introduction

In the U.S. healthcare industry, approximately \$745 billion of care was provided without compensation in 2000-2020 (AHA, 2022). A significant contributor to this uncompensated care is claim denials. In 2016, out of the \$3 trillion in claims submitted to payers, \$262 billion in claims were denied. Claims may be denied due to claim errors, administrative issues, and difficulty in meeting complex payment requirements imposed by payers (Carroll, 2020). While care providers can appeal and resubmit such denied claims to payers, success rates average 53%-60% (Advisory Board, 2021), resulting in significant financial losses to the providers. Additionally, the appeal process is costly and time-consuming for providers, constraining their cash flow and profitability. Thus, denied claims are a significant challenge for providers and policymakers that cannot be overlooked. If left unaddressed, claim denials will continue to contribute to rising healthcare costs in the U.S. (Rama, 2022).

While claim denials remain a critical challenge in the healthcare industry, to the best of our knowledge, the information systems (IS) literature on health information technology (HIT) has paid little attention to this important matter. A large body of the HIT literature documents a wealth of value created by HIT in terms of improved quality of care, decreased patient mortality, and reduced cost of care (Atasoy et al., 2019; Ayabakan et al., 2017; Jones et al., 2012). Our study differs from this research stream in that while prior literature focuses on how EHRs affect care delivery and costs incurred during care provision, our study looks at the processes after care provision, which are equally significant concerns for care providers.

The impact of EHRs on claims denials is a new question for the HIT literature and the IS literature in general. Prior research on enterprise systems uncovers a range of benefits from digitizing business processes, such as cost savings, efficiency improvement, and increased revenues (Barua et al., 1995; Kohli & Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004). However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies in IS have examined the role of enterprise systems (EHRs in our case) in compliance with data and information requirements (Lee & Choi, 2019).

While intuition might suggest that implementing enterprise systems would always improve compliance, this might not always be the case in the healthcare setting, as different clinical tasks are often operationalized through complex EHR applications sourced from different vendors. The heterogeneity in EHRs among different vendors can hinder the effectiveness of EHRs (Ratwani et al., 2018). EHR applications sourced from different vendors could engender redundant, inconsistent, or complex workflow routines for end users, which can hurt the completeness and accuracy of clinical data (Meehan et al., 2016). Specifically, clinicians who use these applications often encounter difficulties in navigating different interfaces and functionalities. This often leads to issues with data collected and compliance with payers after care provision (Dranove et al., 2014; Ganju et al., 2022; Greenwood et al., 2019). Furthermore, although hospitals have a certain degree of control over their internal EHR vendor selection, utilizing different EHR systems can be burdensome for clinicians working at multiple hospitals (Ratwani et al., 2018). As a result, uncoordinated EHR sourcing within and across hospitals can increase errors at the user level, which, in turn, can impede the accuracy of claim processing and data compliance.

Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, the HIT literature has not extensively examined the implications of EHR sourcing decisions within and across hospitals, with a few exceptions (e.g., Angst et al., 2017; Srivastava et al., 2022; Wowak et al., 2022). This represents a significant gap in the literature, given that such decisions are likely to influence inter-hospital coordination and the sharing of data essential for effective patient care. Our study aims to address this gap by examining the effect of intra- and inter-hospital EHR sourcing configurations on claim processing. We thus pose our research questions as follows:

RQ1: Does EHR adoption lead to a decrease in claim denials?

RQ2: Does the extent to which a hospital adopts EHR applications from the same EHR vendor, within and across hospitals in the same health system, lead to a decrease in claim denials compared to EHR adoption from multiple EHR vendors?

Our empirical investigation utilized a large-scale dataset from the Maryland Health Services Cost Review Commission (HSCRC). This dataset contained individual claim records in 2012-2016 on all hospital visits in the State of Maryland, offering detailed clinical and demographic information. Each patient was provided with a unique identifier number and tracked over time and across 48 hospitals in the state, allowing us to obtain patients’ entire visit and diagnosis histories. The dataset consisted of over 19 million outpatient records of insured patients. Information on EHR adoption and sourcing was retrieved from the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics database. Using a claim-level dataset, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the relationship between EHR sourcing and denied claims.

Our analyses produced a range of intriguing findings. We first found that greater adoption of EHR applications is associated with a significant decrease in denied claims. We also found that the sourcing decisions of EHRs significantly impact claim denials. Specifically, EHR applications sourced from a single vendor within a hospital are associated with a substantial reduction in claim denials. For instance, when a hospital’s intra-hospital EHR single-sourcing rate increases from 80% to 90%, claim denial probability is reduced from 1.18% to 1.03%, representing a decrease of 0.15 percentage points. Similarly, when a hospital’s interhospital EHR similarity increases from 80% to 90%, its claim denial probability is reduced from 0.87% to 0.85%. Given that hospitals lose 1.5% of their patient revenues because of claim denial write-offs (Advisory Board, 2021), these reductions in claim denials due to increased intra- and inter-hospital sourcing similarity would substantially improve hospitals’ financials. To provide context, based on our sample, if intra-hospital single-sourcing and interhospital similarities were to increase by 10% at a hospitalyear level, approximately 28,766 and 3,835 claim denials could be avoided, translating to a total charge of \$31.7 million and \$4.2 million in the State of Maryland over 5 years, respectively.

We also observed a decrease in claim denials when EHR applications exhibit a higher degree of similarity within the hospitals where a physician previously worked, regardless of whether these hospitals belonged to the same health system. This result demonstrates an underlying mechanism: Congruence among EHR applications alleviates challenges for physicians who navigate multiple EHR systems across various hospitals. This alignment improves EHR effectiveness in ensuring data accuracy, ultimately reducing claim denials.

This study expands the body of IS knowledge on HIT by exploring another critical role of EHRs in tackling the pressing challenges in the U.S. healthcare system and offers meaningful implications for practitioners and policymakers. Our study provides an encouraging message that EHRs can potentially address claim denials. Thus, EHR sourcing decisions beyond adoption should be carefully planned by healthcare organizations and policymakers. We also contribute to the broader IS literature by demonstrating the role of enterprise system deployment in data and information compliance with external stakeholders (Wowak et al., 2022).

## Conceptual Development

## Why Are Claims Denied?

Medical claims contain a range of information, such as patient demographics, medical histories, current or past medications, diagnoses, and procedures performed. Claims are submitted by care providers to payers (e.g., insurance companies, government payers) with the expectation of receiving payment. A denial occurs if a claim includes missing or erroneous information, noncovered services, medical services that a payer deems unnecessary, or inconsistencies with its reporting requirements. According to Advisory Board (2021), there are four major reasons for claim denials—(1) demographic/technical errors in claim data, (2) ineligibility of provided care in plans, (3) insufficient justification of medical necessity, and (4) lack of prior authorizations.

While providers can resubmit denied claims to payers for appeal and redetermination, this resubmission and appeal process is costly and time-consuming (WPS, 2020). Although some of the denied claims are approved and paid eventually after appeal, a provider incurs substantial resources such as time, personnel, and expenses in this corrective process, deteriorating its financials (Casalino et al., 2009). According to a recent survey (Advisory Board, 2021), it takes an average of 44-54 days for complete claim collection, and providers spend 2.2%-4.1% of net patient revenues to correct and appeal claim denials during this time, amounting to \$4.2-\$8 million annually (Tieché, 2024). However, appeal success rates range from 53% to 60%, suggesting that many claim denials are never resolved. Each denied claim requires an additional rework cost of \$118 (Sanborn, 2017). Thus, claim denials significantly undermine providers’ financials due to administrative costs and elongated revenue collection times.

Our interviews with three hospital administrators provided an account consistent with the above figures. They indicated that denied claims could take 6-8 weeks for final resolution, significantly restricting hospitals’ cash flows. They also mentioned that providers often struggle to learn ambiguous and ever-changing payer reimbursement requirements. Sometimes, providers conduct one-on-one meetings with payers to justify treatment decisions, further delaying revenue collection (Casalino et al., 2009). When patients cannot pay, denied claims often end up as “bad debts” on providers’ balance sheets (Shoemaker, 2019), and as one of our interviewees explained, this is particularly burdensome for hospitals serving low-income and minority patients. Another interviewee noted that EHR incongruence across hospitals is a significant reason for claim denials, and our subject matter expert explained that data standardization issues across hospitals also contribute to claim denials.

## EHR Adoption and Claim Denials

EHRs have attracted policymakers’ attention as a potential remedy to bend the curve of healthcare costs in the U.S. (e.g., Kohli & Tan, 2016). A vast body of literature has analyzed the impact of EHR adoption on healthcare costs, quality, and patient outcomes (Adjerid et al., 2018; Angst et al., 2011; Atasoy et al., 2019; Dranove et al., 2014; Menon & Kohli, 2013). Prior work investigates the effect of HIT that is mainly observed during a consultation process or patient’s stay, such as length of stay (Angst et al., 2011), duplicate tests (Ayabakan et al., 2017), Medicare spending (Adjerid et al., 2018), average patient cost (Angst et al., 2011), patient referral patterns (Eftekhari et al., 2022), and overall hospital costs (Dranove et al., 2014). On the other hand, our study focuses on claim reporting and processing that takes place after care provision.

As elaborated above, claim processing is a complex, information-intensive task. There is a cornucopia of medical conditions, procedures, coverages, and rules for various payers, care providers, and patient groups (Sullivan, 2011). Therefore, the collection, organization, integration, and reconciliation of comprehensive medical information are essential for sound claim processing. Providers can avoid denials by aligning the information on claims with payer requirements. This involves collecting accurate data, providing proper documentation and medical necessity justification, and obtaining prior authorization (Reiner, 2021).

The literature on the business value of IT indicates that enterprise systems improve the accuracy, quality, and completeness of data and streamline information exchange within and between organizations, leading to greater efficiency and accuracy in business processes (Barua et al., 1995; Kohli & Grover, 2008; Melville et al., 2004). In the healthcare setting, EHRs offer similar benefits by recording accurate data, ensuring legible documentation, and facilitating seamless information sharing among medical personnel (Adjerid et al., 2018; Mishra et al., 2012), preventing denials due to information errors (Advisory Board, 2021). Care providers can leverage EHRs to swiftly generate information and documentation for prior authorizations or to justify treatments based on payer requirements. Therefore, EHR adoption can improve the accuracy and completeness of claim processing, thereby preventing claim denials.

## EHR Sourcing and Claim Denials

EHRs are multi-application systems with several distinct but interrelated applications (Wowak et al., 2022). These applications include clinical documentation, decision support, order entry, and data repository. As multiapplication systems require more maintenance, integration, and management than stand-alone applications, it is challenging for care providers to tackle numerous configuration decisions, such as selecting vendors for each application and integrating them with each other (Sodero et al., 2013; Wowak et al., 2022).

Hospitals consider multiple factors when making sourcing decisions, including requirement assessment, application functionality, compatibility with existing systems, user experience, and interoperability for data exchange within and outside the organization (Hodgkins, 2015). While some hospitals opt to source their suite of EHR applications from a single vendor, others take a best-of-breed approach by deploying individual applications from different vendors. Since applications from different vendors use different data standards, vary in the extent of information sharing, and require distinct interfaces to execute cross-functional business processes, EHR sourcing decisions significantly affect the accuracy and quality of information processing (Ratwani et al., 2018). For this reason, how EHRs are sourced within and across hospitals constitutes a vital decision that has material impacts on clinical processes and tasks, including compliance with externally imposed information and reporting requirements.

EHR sourcing is a function of EHR adoption that depicts the vendor configuration of adopted applications. In the IS literature, a seminal work by Angst et al. (2011) examined the performance implications of EHR sequencing, while Angst et al. (2017) studied the antecedents of EHR sourcing strategies. The research on the consequences of EHR sourcing decisions within and across hospitals is a new and emerging area in IS literature. Recently, Wowak et al. (2022) studied the relationship between single-sourcing and conformance quality, and Srivastava et al. (2022) looked at the impact of sourcing strategies on clinician satisfaction. Bardhan et al. (2023) investigated the impact on the value of care through information sharing. Our work contributes to this evolving stream by examining how the sourcing strategies affect claim processing.

In addition, we go beyond this body of prior work on intrahospital EHR sourcing by examining the inter-hospital similarity in EHR sourcing. This represents a major shift, as patient data often needs to be shared across hospitals, which must use information from various EHRs for adequate care. Physicians working across multiple hospitals face challenges due to different EHR vendors, impacting their system navigation and usage. Furthermore, while a hospital can manage its internal EHR sourcing, aligning this with other hospitals within its network is often beyond its control. Given hospitals’ varying EHR sourcing strategies, it is critical to understand how intra- and inter-hospital sourcing decisions affect claim processing.

## Intra-Hospital EHR Sourcing and Claim Denials

Hospitals with EHRs from multiple vendors must integrate medical data across diverse applications to meet payer requirements. Given the sheer number of required interfaces and the need to standardize data across disparate applications, more errors can occur in claim processing (Salvador, 2007). These hospitals often resort to paper-based workarounds (Walker et al., 2005), which can further cause information inaccuracy and incompleteness. Hence, hospitals with multiple EHR vendors can face more claim denials due to data-sharing barriers and incompatibility across EHR applications.

EHR applications from multiple vendors can make data retrieval and reconciliation difficult (Horsky et al., 2018; Salanitro et al., 2013). For example, when integrating a radiology report onto a patient’s progress note, inconsistent information may be incorporated if the report is generated by a different vendor (Guo et al., 2017), triggering a denial by a payer (Ganju et al., 2022). Different information processing logic and data structures embedded in different EHR vendors can hinder seamless information exchange, causing more errors in claim information. On the other hand, when EHR applications are sourced from a single vendor, care providers can achieve more effective data sharing and fewer workflow interruptions (Bardhan et al., 2023). Thus, we expect a higher level of intra-hospital EHR vendor similarity to be associated with fewer denials.

## Inter-Hospital EHR Sourcing and Claim Denials

Hospitals often need to share patient data with external care providers such as other hospitals, clinics, and laboratories. In these cases, it is necessary to exchange and integrate data from multiple care providers, a task that requires application congruence across these providers (Raghupathi & Tan, 2002). Similar EHR applications across hospitals can enable more streamlined data exchange for accurate claim processing. Enterprise systems that share data across organizational boundaries exercise embedded control, enabling them to exchange with multiple partners and reduce search, monitoring, and enforcement costs (Sanchez & Mahoney, 1996). Similar EHR applications can improve patient data sharing across hospitals in the inter-hospital setting. For instance, patients who visit multiple hospitals need a complete picture of past diagnoses and procedures (Lim et al., 2015). If these hospitals lack close data coordination and exchange due to EHR dissimilarity, their claim processing accuracy will be undermined.

The diversity in EHR vendors across hospitals poses challenges for clinicians working at multiple hospitals (Srivastava et al., 2022), as they must adapt to different layouts, interfaces, workflows, and functionalities. This can lead to information errors such as duplicated procedures or inaccurate records, increasing claim denials. Accordingly, we expect greater similarity among EHR vendors across hospitals to be associated with fewer claim denials.

## Data and Methodology

## Data Sources and Measures

We utilized a large-scale dataset from the Maryland Health Services Cost Review Commission (HSCRC) outpatient records. Since we focused on claim denials, we only considered claims with available payer information (i.e., Medicare, Medicaid, or private insurance). Hence, we excluded claims for patients who paid for care by themselves or did not have insurance. Our dataset contained visit-level claim records (N = 19,177,286) from 2012-2016 on all outpatient visits by insured patients at 48 non-federal hospitals in the State of Maryland, with detailed clinical and demographic records. In this, each patient was provided with a unique identifier number and tracked over time and across all hospitals in Maryland. Our unit of analysis was the patient-visit. Our dependent variable was DeniedClaim, which equaled 1 if a claim was denied. We identified denied claims with a denial flag generated by 3M EAPG software, which processed medical claims and determined whether a claim was to be paid prior to submission to payers.<sup>2</sup>

We retrieved information on EHR adoption from the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) Analytics database. We considered the following EHR applications—clinical data repository (CDR), clinical decision support systems (CDSS), computerized physician order entry (CPOE), order entry (OE), and physician documentation (PD) (Institute of Medicine, 2012, p. 38). These five major EHR systems are widely used in the prior HIT literature (Angst et al., 2017; Atasoy et al., 2018; Dranove et al., 2014). We averaged five EHR applications for each hospital-year and normalized it between 0 and 1, representing the proportion of EHR applications adopted in each hospital-year. A value of 0 indicated no EHR adoption, while 1 indicated the adoption of all five applications. Our main specifications used a one-year-lagged EHR adoption variable to avoid simultaneity issues.

Additionally, we created two EHR sourcing variables: (1) EHR single-sourcing within a hospital (EHR\_SSadj) and (2) EHR similarity within a health system (EHR\_Sim). EHR\_SSadj measured the level of single sourcing across the five EHR applications within a hospital, whereas EHR\_Sim measured the similarity of EHR vendors between the focal hospital and other hospitals within the same health system.

We constructed the adjusted EHR single-sourcing variable (EHR\_SSadj) following the approach of Angst et al. (2017) and Wowak et al. (2022), which can be summarized as follows: Suppose that a hospital’s sourcing configuration for its five applications is A-A-B-C-NA, where A, B, and C are three EHR vendors and NA means no implementation. First, we determined the dominant vendor that supplied the majority of EHR applications for each hospital-year observation. We constructed a prototype single-sourcing (SS) sequence where all applications were adopted from this dominant vendor. In the example above, the dominant vendor is A, and the prototype single-sourcing configuration is A-A-A-A-NA with no adoption for the last one. Then, we compared the hospital’s actual sourcing configuration with the SS prototype sequence using Levenshtein distance, which measured the number of edits (e.g., insertions, deletions, and substitutions) required to transform the actual sourcing configuration into the prototype SS sequence. The Levenshtein distance in the above example was 2 as it took two substitutions to transform the focal configuration into the SS prototype. Finally, we normalized this distance by the number of applications adopted for the focal hospital-year and subtracted it from 1 to obtain a value between 0 and 1, where a higher value represented a higher level of SS configuration. In our example, EHR\_SSadj was 0.5 after normalizing the Levenshtein distance and subtracting it from 1 (= 1 - 2/4). We excluded hospitals that adopted less than two applications (Angst et al., 2017). In our robustness check in Section 4.3, we calculated the unadjusted EHR single-sourcing (EHR\_SSunadj), where the Levenshtein distance was divided by the total number of available applications (in this case, five), irrespective of the actual number of applications adopted by the focal hospital.

To measure EHR\_Sim, we assessed the level of similarity of EHR vendors between a focal hospital and other hospitals in the same health system. For each application of the focal hospital, we matched the EHR vendors with another hospital in the same health system and counted the matched vendors.<sup>3</sup> We normalized the count of matched vendors by the number of EHR applications adopted at the focal hospital. Thus, this measure was adjusted by the adoption level as well. We repeated this process for each hospital within the health system. Finally, we took the average of the similarity measures and obtained a value between 0 and 1 for each hospital. Suppose that the focal hospital has the sourcing configuration of A-A-B-C-NA and two other hospitals in its health system have A-A-A-A-A and A-A-B-C-A configurations, respectively. In this case, the focal hospital would have a vendor matching rate of 0.5 (i.e., the first two applications’ vendors matched out of the four adopted) with the first hospital and a vendor matching rate of 1.0 with the second (i.e., the first four applications’ vendors matched out of the four adopted). This would result in 0.75 for the focal hospital’s EHR\_Sim. This calculation requires the health system to have at least two affiliated hospitals; thus, health systems with less than two hospitals were dropped in our analyses with EHR\_Sim.<sup>4</sup>

We accounted for several control variables measuring timevariant patient and visit characteristics. We controlled for whether a claim was for a revisit, defined as a patient returning to the same hospital with the same principal diagnosis code within 30 days, as revisit claims can be subject to more complex eligibility criteria. We included the work relative value unit (RVU) metric, which is used by Medicare to determine the amount of payments to providers and serves as a proxy for physician productivity (BDO, 2020). Finally, we included measures for perceived medical necessity (Blackwell et al., 2009a), such as a chronic condition indicator, the total number of diagnoses and procedures, and visit charges. Table 1 reports the descriptive statistics of our variables. There were 13 EHR vendors in our sample. The average market share across EHR vendors in the data was 7.1%.

## Results

## EHR and Denied Claims

Our main estimation results are in Table 2. Column 1 shows that the greater adoption of EHRs led to a decrease in the likelihood of claim denials. The coefficient of EHR adoption indicates that when all five EHR applications were adopted, the likelihood of claim denial decreased by 1.5%-point (β<sub>1</sub> = -0.015), translating into a 1.5% reduction in the likelihood of claim denial. Next, the models with the EHR sourcing variables are presented in Columns 2-4. ## Endogeneity and Robustness Tests

In our econometric models, since we accounted for timeinvariant unobserved patient and hospital heterogeneity, the identification of the impact of EHRs is driven by withinhospital changes in EHR adoption and sourcing and within patient-visit changes in claim denials. Despite our thorough econometric design to estimate EHR effects, other identification threats may remain. We conducted multiple tests to ensure the robustness of our findings.

First, to account for time-varying confounding factors, we adopted an instrumental variable (IV) approach. An IV should be associated with our EHR variables but not codetermined with a focal hospital’s claim denial propensity (Greene, 2003). One such possible IV is EHR sourcing decisions by other hospitals in the same health system (Angst et al., 2010; Ganju et al., 2022). A hospital’s EHR sourcing decisions can be affected by hospitals in the same health system (Angst et al., 2017). However, its claim denial should not be directly affected by hospitals’ EHR sourcing decisions, especially if they are not in the same healthcare market. Accordingly, for EHR\_SSadj, we created an IV that measured the average level of EHR single-sourcing in the other hospitals that were affiliated with the same health system of the focal hospital but located in different hospital referral regions (HRR) (Bardhan et al., 2023).<sup>6</sup> Similarly, we created an IV that measured the average EHR similarity of other hospitals in the focal hospital’s health system in different HRRs. These two IVs passed the relevance criterion tests. A weak identification test using the Kleibergen-Paap Wald rank resulted in an F statistic above the maximal Stock-Yogo critical value of 16.38. Our results are robust to the 2SLS estimations with these IVs (Table 3, Columns 1 and 2).<sup>7,8</sup>

Second, our results could have been affected by systematic differences among hospitals that chose different degrees of EHR sourcing. To account for this, we executed a coarsened exact matching (CEM) at the hospital level (Iacus et al., 2012). In this approach, we matched hospitals with a higher value of EHR\_SSadj to hospitals with a lower value of EHR\_SSadj. We followed a similar approach for EHR\_Sim. The statistics of the matching variables before and after CEM are balanced.<sup>9</sup> Table 3, Column 3 presents the CEM results, which are similar to the main results. Next, in addition to CEM at the hospital level, we adopted propensity score matching (PSM) at the patient-visit level to address potential bias due to visit-specific unobserved confounding factors (Blackwell et al., 2009b). We used patient marital status, gender, race, age, charge, work RVU, total diagnoses, and year-quarter indicators as our matching covariates and performed an exact matched sampling based on the nearest-neighbor method.<sup>10</sup> The results with CEM and PSM in Table 3, Column 4 show that our main findings are not due to systematic observable differences across the hospitals.

Third, hospitals might have different trends in claim denials before EHR adoption due to unobservable factors. This could be due to the implementation of various policy changes and new regulations over time that influence the EHR sourcing decisions and claim denials of different hospitals at different times. To examine whether any time-varying unobserved heterogeneity could have caused bias in our results, we included hospitalspecific linear time trends in our estimation in Table 3, Column 5. The main results remain consistent under this more demanding specification.

Fourth, prior research suggests that patients may prefer hospitals with advanced HIT capabilities (Crossnohere et al., 2021), as HIT can facilitate better-managed care and more effective provider coordination (Rudin et al., 2017). If patients with complex health conditions select hospitals with EHR sourcing that can better support seamless care coordination and accurate claim processing, their claim denial rates could be simultaneously affected. To address this potential selection issue, we adopted a two-step Heckman selection procedure (Heckman, 1979). We estimated an inverse Mills ratio (IMR) from a first-stage Heckman selection probit model. To break the potential collinearity between IMR and standard errors in the second stage (Leung & Yu, 1996), we included the average level of EHR\_SSadj from other hospitals in the same health system but different HRRs. We found that the inclusion of IMR in the second stage estimation accounts for patient self-selection into hospitals with higher EHR\_SSadj (Wooldridge, 2010). We repeated the same process for EHR\_Sim and observed consistent results, as reported in Table 4, Column 1.

Next, one might argue that patients who visit the same hospital tend to be more similar to each other than patients randomly sampled from the entire population. Patients who visit the same hospital might share certain medical and socioeconomic characteristics, potentially complicating the independence assumption of observations (Hofmann, 1997). We conducted a hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) analysis to control for the dependence of patients on hospital factors (Woltman et al., 2012). Our HLM approach specified patient-visit factors as a Level 1 effect and hospital factors as a Level 2 effect and followed a mixed effects estimation with a random intercept at the hospital level. We report this HLM estimation in Table 4, Column 2, revealing consistent results. Although the random intercept at Level 1 is statistically significant (0.0001, p-value < 0.001), implying that hospitals differ in their claim denials and there are variations among patient visits within hospitals, our main results remain robust in this setting.

For Table 4, Column 3, we used an unadjusted EHR singlesourcing variable as an alternative measure of within-hospital EHR sourcing similarity. For Column 4, in calculating the EHR\_Sim of a focal hospital, we alternatively considered the hospitals in the focal hospital’s hospital referral region (HRR) because they might refer or transfer patients to other hospitals within the same HRR. This provided an alternative definition of a hospital network besides the health system. For Column 5, we performed logistic regressions by randomly selecting 10% of the patients in our sample and including additional patient covariates to provide robustness to nonlinear specifications.<sup>11</sup> Column 6 reflects a variable control of whether the dominant vendor of a hospital was the market leader vendor in the entire sample. This approach addressed idiosyncratic effects that might be specific to the top vendor in the market. For Column 7, we used all three EHR variables in one estimation to estimate the EHR sourcing coefficients accounting for the EHR adoption level. We observed consistent results in all these additional tests. We also conducted further robustness tests, which are summarized in Table 5.<sup>12</sup>

## Similarity of EHRs Used by Physicians

To further buttress our main findings, we investigated whether physicians’ familiarity with EHR applications from different vendors explained claim denials. Our analysis involved tracking physicians using their national provider identifiers (NPI) in our dataset and examining the claims associated with these physician NPIs. We computed an adjusted similarity of EHR vendors between a physician’s focal hospital and other hospitals at which the physician had previously worked in the previous quarter or year, regardless of whether these hospitals belong to the focal hospital’s health system. This calculation provided an adjusted physician-based EHR similarity measure. As most claims did not have physician NPI information and most physicians did not work in multiple hospitals within a quarter (or year), our visit sample size was reduced to 301,328 observations.<sup>13</sup>

Table 6, Column 1 indicates that when a physician’s focal hospital had higher EHR similarity with all other hospitals the physician worked at in the previous quarter, the visits attended by this physician at the focal hospital were less likely to be denied. It appears that when EHR applications are similar across multiple hospitals that the physician has worked at, the physician encounters fewer challenges in using EHR systems with different layouts, workflows, functionalities, or business logics. Our findings suggest that this alignment improves clinical data collection and adherence to payer requirements, ultimately reducing claim denials. We also considered hospitals where a physician worked in the previous year, with consistent results shown in Table 6, Column 2.

We performed two robustness checks to address the concern of reduced sample size due to missing physician NPI values. While we are not aware of any systematic reason for the absence of physician IDs for some visits, potential differences may exist across visits with and without physician IDs. First, we employed a two-step Heckman selection approach to address the potential selection into the sample with available physician IDs. In the first stage, we estimated the presence of physician ID based on relevant attributes controlled for in Equation 1 and an exclusion restriction variable.<sup>14</sup> In the second stage, we estimated the claim denial probability based on the selection in the first step. The results of the second-stage Heckman selection approach using quarterly Phys\_EHR\_Sim are presented in Table 6, Column 3, revealing consistent findings.

Second, as our reduced-size sample could potentially exhibit bias in observed covariates, we utilized a sample reweighting (entropy balancing) technique to balance the covariates in our quarterly Phys\_EHR\_Sim sample (Hainmueller, 2012). This approach adjusted weights so that the covariate distribution in our Phys\_EHR\_Sim sample matched the covariate moments of the sample used in the main analysis. This adjustment ensured that the smaller sample closely mirrored the main sample. After balancing the sample, we obtained similar results, presented in Table 6, Column 4.

## Discussion and Conclusion

The skyrocketing healthcare cost is among the most challenging “wicked” problems in the U.S. healthcare system (CMS, 2023). One of the contributing factors to this problem is uncompensated care due to claim denials (AHA, 2022). It has become one of the most significant burdens for care providers, both financially and administratively (Carroll, 2020). It is also challenging for care providers to comply with complex and dynamically changing payer requirements, which occur due to advances in medicine, healthcare policy changes, an aging population, and a growing insured patient population (Jacob, 2014). Many fear that the financial burden due to denied claims could lead to the demise of care providers serving rural areas or underprivileged populations, aggravating the disparity in healthcare access (Lent et al., 2022).

We contribute to the IS literature on health information technology (HIT) by identifying the consequences of EHR deployment decisions (adoption and sourcing) and their data and information compliance with claim processing requirements. Using a large-scale dataset of outpatient claim records from Maryland hospitals, we found that hospital EHRs are associated with a significant decline in denied claims. In addition, we revealed that the sourcing decisions of EHRs play a significant role, which has not been extensively studied in the prior HIT literature for processes after the provision of care. We found that EHRs sourced from a single vendor within a hospital and similar vendors across hospitals within a health system are associated with a significant decrease in claim denials. Our results suggest that EHRs with fewer complex interfaces and seamless information flow can better assist care providers in meeting data accuracy requirements. Additionally, our analyses reveal a mechanism for how EHR sourcing plays a role in claim denials. We show that claim denials become less likely when multiple hospitals in which a physician has previously worked use EHR applications from the same vendor. This finding implies that when there is congruence among EHR applications, physicians encounter fewer obstacles due to using different EHR systems, leading to fewer information errors and claim denials.

Our findings provide meaningful implications for healthcare providers and policymakers. Complex payer rules and bureaucratic compliance requirements lead to inefficiencies in care delivery and a substantial revenue loss. Ultimately, governments and patients have to pay for such costs, leading to a cost-shifting phenomenon (Dobson et al., 2006; Frakt, 2011). Simply put, somebody has to pick up the tab; if care is not paid for due to claim denials, providers make up for lost revenues by raising prices on everyone (e.g., patients, private/government payers, taxpayers) (Dobson et al., 2006; Frakt, 2011). Our study illustrates that well-implemented EHRs have great potential to reduce one of healthcare’s most persistent and unnecessary costs. We suggest that it is important to improve vendor interoperability among EHR applications within and across hospitals because while a within-hospital vendor choice is a strategic decision made by a focal hospital, inter-hospital sourcing similarity is often beyond the focal hospital’s control (Raghupathi and Tan, 2002; Atasoy et al., 2018). Our results demonstrate that physicians may face challenges when using EHR applications from different vendors at different hospitals, making claim denials more likely.

At the same time, we note that utilizing multiple EHR vendors, each specializing in specific functions, may be more effective for specific clinical processes. This best-of-breed approach offers flexibility and greater operational functionality for each application (Bapna et al., 2010), and it could potentially improve competition in the HIT market and lower overall adoption costs (Bardhan et al., 2023). Therefore, policymakers might consider introducing policies for greater interoperability and data/interface standardization of EHR applications from different vendors, which could prevent claim denials. In this way, care providers can strike the right balance between single-sourcing and best-of-breed sourcing. Our findings also support enhancing the federal government’s EHR vendor certification program and minimizing EHR variation among vendors (Holmgren et al., 2018) because EHR vendor certification programs have been criticized for having a narrowly constrained set of technical assessments. Accordingly, placing greater emphasis on the usability and standardization of user interfaces and layouts will improve the accuracy of information flow across disparate applications (Holmgren et al., 2018), suggesting a potential to reduce the extent of errors in claim processing.

This study has a few limitations. While our conversations with the HSCRC tell us that denial flags generated by the 3M software indicate claim denials, we do not know why individual claims are denied. Also unknown to us is whether each denied claim is reprocessed for an appeal or whether it remains unpaid. Additionally, the State of Maryland used a rate-setting model that establishes uniform rates for hospital services for all different payers (Altarum, 2020). Although in 2014-2018, Maryland switched its model to the global budget model, where rates are established based on historical revenues for each hospital, the central premise of its payment model remained the same. We acknowledge this unique context of Maryland as a limitation potentially impacting generalizability. Additionally, certain patients may receive treatment from multiple hospitals, and the EHR similarity measure should ideally include those specific hospitals at the visit level. We acknowledge that our measure at the hospitalyear level, which involved all hospitals within the health system of a focal hospital, may not capture such patientspecific heterogeneity.

There are numerous future research opportunities on the role of digital technologies in healthcare and other industries. Design science researchers could develop an interorganizational system or a digital platform that interconnects healthcare providers and payers so that the business processes of both parties are seamlessly integrated, and claims and payments are processed in a more straightforward manner.
