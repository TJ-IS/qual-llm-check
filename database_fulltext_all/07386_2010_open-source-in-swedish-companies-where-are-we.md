# Open source in Swedish companies: where are we?

Björn Lundell,\* Brian Lings<sup>†</sup> & Edvin Lindqvist<sup>‡</sup>

\*University of Skövde, School of Humanities and Informatics, P.O. Box 408, SE-541 28 Skövde, Sweden, email: bjorn.lundell@his.se, <sup>†</sup>University of Skövde, School of Humanities and Informatics, P.O. Box 408, SE-541 28 Skövde, Sweden, email: brian.lings@his.se, and <sup>‡</sup>University of Skövde, School of Humanities and Informatics, P.O. Box 408, SE-541 28 Skövde, Sweden, email: edvin.lindqvist@forsway.com

Abstract. Open Source (OS) is a phenomenon of increasing significance for organizations, offering the prospect of effective alternative business solutions and new business opportunities. A number of surveys have been conducted in various countries with the purpose of understanding the state of practice with respect to OS in companies. In this paper we report on a study of the perceptions of OS and the uptake of OS products and development models in Swedish companies. The study used purposeful sampling of companies that have an expressed interest in OS, and the survey was conducted using a set of pre-prepared questions. Its goal was to investigate the extent to which OS has influenced business thinking, as seen from the standpoint of stakeholders. We found that uptake is much higher than reported in earlier studies, but as with previous studies, activity is stil concentrated in small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). There is increased evidence of interest beyond the simple use of OS components at the infrastructure level. Further, a significant proportion of the companies studied are supporting the OS community as well as benefiting from it. Support includes participation in existing projects and the release of new software under OS licenses.

Keywords: open source in Swedish companies, qualitative survey, open source adoption, perceptions of open source

## INTRODUCTION

It is claimed that ‘Most of today’s innovative products and solutions are developed on the basis of free and open source software’ (Ebert, 2008). Open Source (OS) software is software that is licensed under a recognized OS licence [Open Source Initiative (OSI), 2009]. The ready availability of OS software fundamentally changes the nature of competition in the software sector and has significant implications for business models in that sector (Fitzgerald, 2006).

Ever since the late 1990s, when the term ‘Open Source Software’ was coined (DiBona et al., 1999), the development of software systems using an OS model (Feller & Fitzgerald, 2000)

has attracted researchers and special issues in journals have been devoted to the topic as early as 2001 and 2002 (e.g. Fitzgerald & Feller, 2001; 2002). At that time, researchers investigated specific issues such as the development model used in large OS projects (such as FreeBSD; Jørgensen, 2001), but also more generic aspects of how this way of developing software might impact on the software industry (e.g. Sharma et al., 2002). However, at the time, direct company involvement in OS software development was not as visible as it is today, when OS development in company contexts has become normal.

OS is an issue of increasing significance for organizations today (Ågerfalk et al., 2005; Fitzgerald, 2006; Behlendorf, 2009; van der Linden et al., 2009; Peters, 2009), all the more so given current perceptions that it can offer effective business solutions and new business opportunities (e.g. O’Reilly et al., 2001; Feller & Fitzgerald, 2003; Bonaccorsi & Rossi, 2005; Feller et al., 2006b; Walli et al., 2006). Most companies will be aware at least of Linux, Apache, MySQL and Perl/PHP/Python, the elements of the so-called LAMP suite (Lawton, 2005), even if they are not yet using them. However, as well as involvement in conventional OS component adoption, many companies are getting involved in OS development projects, considering tha in itself this can bring competitive advantage.

Richard Stallman coined the term ‘Free Software’ when he founded the Free Software Foundation (FSF; http://www.fsf.org/) in 1985. The term ‘Open Source Software’ was coined in 1998 to give the phenomenon a more ‘business-friendly’ association. Its definition is controlled by the OSI (2006) and is today much more widely used than ‘Free Software’. The FSF definition states that Free Software is ‘a matter of the users’ freedom to run, copy, distribute, study, change and improve the software. More precisely, it refers to four kinds of freedom, for the users of the software’ (FSF, 2007), namely:

• the freedom to run the program, for any purpose;

• the freedom to study how the program works and adapt it to need;

the freedom to redistribute copies; and

• the freedom to improve the program and release the improvements.

While sharing these tenets and collaborating in initiatives such as the NESSI OS software working group (NESSI, 2008), the two movements are nevertheless distinct. For example, the IFIP working group 2.13 on Open Source Software (IFIP, 2008) considers the wider aspects of OS as a phenomenon, including implications for software processes and business models. As stated by FSF (2008), there is also a fundamental difference in values: ‘For the Open Source movement, the issue of whether software should be open source is a practical question, not an ethical one’. Some authors use ‘FOSS’ when stressing the similarities rather than the differences of the two movements; others have adopted the term ‘Libre Software’ to stress the free nature of the software as in free speech rather than cost-free (see González-Barahona, 2004 for a comment on the origins of this term); yet others use the term Free, Libre and Open Source Software (FLOSS) to refer to the collective phenomenon while trying to avoid an ideological debate. In this paper we consider the wider aspects of the phenomenon of OS and note that this is the term most widely used among practitioners in Swedish organizations.

The goal of the research reported here was to investigate the state of practice with respect to OS in Swedish companies across the SME and large company sectors that have adopted OS software. In particular, it was designed to get an insight into the views of key individuals influencing OS practice and policy within the companies.

## RELATED WORK

There is a particularly strong European interest in OS, with a report from the Information Technology for European Advancement initiative suggesting that a majority of OS software developers live within the European Union (EU) (ITEA, 2004). Further, there are several EU-funded projects investigating the phenomenon, including FLOSS (2002), FLOSSPOLS (2006), Calibre (2006), FLOSSWorld (2007), COSI (2008), FLOSSMetrics (2008) and QualiPSo (2008).

A number of surveys have considered the OS phenomenon from a variety of perspectives including awareness, development and use of OS software in Canada (e-Cology, 2003); opinions of FLOSS developers in the USA (David et al., 2003); company preparedness for OS software (Forrester, 2004); opinions of FLOSS developers in Japan (FLOSS-JP, 2004); opinions of FLOSS developers in Asia (FLOSS-ASIA, 2004); attitudes to FLOSS within European governments (FLOSSPOLS, 2005); obstacles to maximizing the use of OS software in the South Pacific (Schütz et al., 2005); level of interest in and use of OS software in South-East Finland (Nikula & Jantunen, 2005); use of OS software in higher and further education in the UK (Helsper, 2006); OS software use in USA and European enterprises from the perspective of decision makers (Forrester. 2007): the OS software community and OS software industrial sector in Australia (Waugh, 2008); factors and trends in OS software adoption, a quantitative survey (Skok, 2008); adoption of OS software in the Norwegian software industry (Hauge et al., 2008); and the role of firms in commercial OS projects (Capra et al., 2009). There have been few previous studies of the OS software phenomenon in a Swedish context. One notable exception is the study by Dahlander & Magnusson (2005). Case studies were undertaken in three Swedish companies, investigating the relationship between companies and the OS community.

The FLOSS (2002) project included a telephone survey of companies and public organizations in Germany, Sweden and the UK with at least 100 employees. The survey focused on such aspects as organizational dependence on OS software, licensing, advantages and disadvantages of OS software, and the impact of policies and regulations on OS software. Those identified as using OS software (in total 395 for the three countries) were furthe surveyed, using a quantitative questionnaire. The lowest represented sector in this survey was found to be large Swedish companies (at least 500 employees).

## RESEARCH METHOD

We report on a telephone survey of companies selected via purposeful sampling. We have earlier reported initial findings from this survey (<Ref-to-paper-published-in-a-conference-is-tobe-included-in-final-version>). In this paper we report on an extended data collection and a more comprehensive analysis of the results. The survey is a qualitative study of Swedish practitioners within companies known to be active users of OS software. Company size was not a selection factor. The qualitative techniques used are designed to lead to richer information on the phenomenon studied but do not allow any claim that the results are representative of organizations generally. However, the FLOSS project suggests that Swedish companies lag somewhat behind those in the UK and Germany in their uptake of OS, so the level of OS perceptions reported here may somewhat under-represent those held more generally within the EU. The FLOSS project used a two-stage process within the survey: the first to establish which companies were actively using OS software; only those that were doing so were interviewed in detail.

Our approach was to identify individuals within organizations who were actively interested in OS software. Our reasoning was that such people would be able to give an informed view of their own organization’s attitude to, reliance on and experience with OS software. Sampling was conducted starting from an initial shortlist of practitioners known by the researchers to be interested in OS. Prior knowledge of OS-related activities informed further searches in IT magazines and on the WWW for company involvement in OS and for practitioner involvemen in OS-related seminars and conferences within Sweden.

The purposeful sampling led to 58 telephone interviews, conducted in Swedish (the native language of all interviewees and the interviewer) and transcribed. Of these, six interviews resulted in conversations with practitioners whose companies had no current involvement with OS. Any quotes from these non-adopters are clearly noted in the text. All quotations used in the analysis phase and reported in the paper are translations into English.

The interviews were conducted over a 2-month period in 2006, and based on a number of open questions – a sub-set of which had one or more follow-up questions depending on the initial response. Specifically, there were three lead questions.

The first question related to the concept of OS. Respondents were asked, ‘What is your immediate reaction when I say “Open Source?”

The second question related to company use of OS products. Respondents were asked, ‘Does your company use any Open Source products?’ If not, then the reason was probed. If so, as was mostly the case with the chosen individuals, further questions probed the experience of using OS products and which products were most important to the company.

The third question related to company participation in OS projects. Respondents were asked, ‘Does your company participate in any Open Source projects?’ If not, then the reason was probed. If so, then respondents were asked for details.

## RESULTS

## Perceptions of OS

Question: What is your immediate reaction when I say ‘Open Source?

Immediate reaction was requested as we were seeking what respondents associated with OS rather than probing for a definition of it. On several occasions this caused respondents to hesitate before replying. Perhaps not surprisingly, the more understanding a person had of the OS phenomenon the more hesitant they tended to be in responding to this question. As put by one consultant, ‘is this a context where I must explain from the start what is meant by Open Source code, or can I take for granted that these people know what the term means? I ask myself whether or not I need to explain’.

From this question we were able to gain insight into the respondent’s level of exposure to reasoning about the OS phenomenon, which proved important in interpreting statements made in response to later questions. One aspect revealed was the subset of dimensions of the OS phenomenon that dominated their thinking; for example, collaborative development, cost or control.

We found a great variation in perceptions of the OS phenomenon. For some, OS was immediately associated with the business or process levels, or an ideological view. Respon dents typically stressed one or other of these views, although some hesitated to give a shor answer – in particular those heavily involved in OS projects (‘that is a good question . . .’; ‘I don’t know, but . . .’). However, their deep knowledge of the concept became evident during the course of the interview.

Those emphasizing a process view associate the concept with, for example, ‘a development model’, ‘collaboration and an ability to influence’ or ‘that you develop the software together with the users’. Some were more elaborate in their responses, for example, ‘Freely available source code to tools or software where vou try to use a large community over the whole world . . . with Linux as the prime example’ and ‘. . . Eclipse and such things . . . a way of working in which you are contributing and use code in different ways’.

Those taking a business view tend to associate the concept with their own business: ‘We build our entire business on it, so [the company] is my first thought’.

The ideological view primarily stressed the OS community concept, placing it above the idea of OS products. For example, ‘I think of community, and as a second thought of tools’, in particular implying a ‘free-basis community’ with ‘non-ownership’. Another respondent went further, describing it as a ‘social expression’ and ‘software which has evolved from the collaboration community and the politics of lack of national boundaries and non-commercialism that exists on the internet’. Only one respondent in the study used the term ‘libre’, which is common in the South of Europe.

A smaller group primarily associated OS with free access to the source code – a rather pragmatic view. Several respondents stressed the ability to modify source codes: ‘literally that you get access to the source code and are allowed to modify the source code’; ‘I am allowed to play with it myself, if I want’. This can be contrasted with the view from a non-OS user whose immediate association in this regard was rather different: ‘you are expected to modify [the code]’.

On the product side, the major association was with the LAMP suite – for example ‘My firs thought is Linux’. However, some were non-specific ‘application servers and testing tools, and similar things’ and others went beyond LAMP in including desktop products also: ‘Linux, MySQL, Apache, OpenOffice, etc.’.

On the properties side, although many placed ‘no cost’ uppermost in their minds, several referred to the quality of OS products: ‘I really think about very competent software’ or even both: ‘software at no cost that often is just as good as commercial’.

However, other views were represented. For example, a respondent from one company, only involved in Sweden in sales and marketing, saw OS straightforwardly as a ‘growing market for the future’. Another thought of ‘a large number of hackers who sit and innovate whilst dealing with a lot of issues’.

## Company use of OS products

## Question: Does your company use any OS products?

Unsurprisingly given the purposeful sampling used, most responded in the affirmative. Two went so far as to say that their companies only used OS products. After this confirming question, the interviewer asked about the most important OS products for the company and the general experience in using these products within the company.

Overall, in citing the most important OS products for their company, 75% cited elements of the LAMP stack, 50% cited other infrastructure products, 12% cited Open Office and 10% cited OS tools for application development. Perhaps not surprisingly, with one exception, all companies mentioning Open Office also mentioned Linux. In terms of quality, no negative comments were made about any OS product actually adopted. Some of the larger companies not heavily into OS development show higher scepticism, but more involved respondents did not share this scepticism. For example, ‘our experience is that [OS products] are very secure, and have become more stable over time; if you follow the distributions, you can see that it has improved over time’.

The attitude of the respondents towards OS usage depended largely on the level of company involvement in OS. For example, a developer from a large company that ships products with OS components commented, ‘we have really tried to explore and asses the quality, so we typically don’t read about them – instead we want to dig into the source code and assess the quality’. Others differentiate between products that they ship and those that they use internally. For example, for one SME, ‘as a consultancy company, we have adopted some OS products which we deploy at customer sites . . . but Open Office is a product we use on a daily basis ourselves’.

There is a perception that it takes longer to become productive using the LAMP stack, with developers able to more quickly use proprietary development tools ‘out of the box’. However, the view of experienced LAMP developers is that this is not an ongoing problem.

An experienced OS developer, commenting on the quality of OS software, states that in his view, it is ‘always good’. Further, ‘functionality has increased significantly but above all the number of areas in which you can use OS’. The Office package, for example, ‘wasn’t available in a usable form five years ago but this is not at all a problem today’. A company that uses Linux on both proprietary and standard hardware platforms commented that where existing platforms are used, OS tool chains and libraries have proven ‘good enough, even for us’.

Another advantage seen with OS is that using OS products there is a better response to development questions. In particular, questions posed of an OS community via community web sites have often been found to deliver quick, high-quality responses contrasting with negative experiences with proprietary software vendors. In the words of one developer: ‘Open Source is interesting because there is a potential for quickly developing code and quickly getting responses to questions in a way which you do not get in a commercial environment’. Dual licensing for an OS product can alter this relationship. With dual licensing, a user has the option to adopt the product under an OS license or under a traditional, commercial (non-OS software) license, the latter with a direct support agreement with the commercial developer of an OS product which retains the copyright. However, the community around the OS license of a dual-licensed product will be of a different nature, lacking direct input from the large organizations using the commercial license and potentially having less influence on the direction of development.

Alongside this, astute developers are aware of the broader costs of adopting software: ‘It is absolutely not for free, because you have to invest time in order to understand the software, and there is no possibility of writing formal complaints if it doesn’t perform adequately. There is a certain risk associated with it’. However, in his experience, ‘the support in itself is easily as good as for commercial products’. One respondent struck a note of caution about relying on an infrastructure OS product (Debian Linux in this case) in a commercial application: ‘one may find that the OS component does not fully support’ the application. This can result in unexpected costs, and possibly the adoption of less well trusted plug-ins or library components. A respondent in another company saw an advantage in becoming a paying customer for an OS engine that they already incorporated into their own product. This was to be able to make direct demands on the company offering the dual licence, allowing them to better safeguard thei product.

Some company experience is with OS products not so commonly adopted among Swedish companies: ‘We use ObjectWeb, from a European OS consortium, which I would call a hidden pearl. We have used this in a large project at a customer site, and it runs on like a Swiss clock’.

Many companies are aware of the licensing advantages of OS: ‘What I like most with OS is the licence model. It gives a freedom and control over your IT investment, and that I think is a very important factor’.

## Company participation in OS projects

## Question: Does your company participate in any OS projects?

After a positive response to the question about participation in OS projects, the interviewer clarified which ones and in what way the company was involved. Those not participating were asked whether there was a specific reason.

Of the companies actually using OS software within a development environment, 75% actively contribute to OS projects in one way or another. That is, 75% can be classified as having a mutually beneficial relationship with the OS community, the remaining 25% having a relationship in which one party (the user) gains advantage but not at the expense of the other. Over 50% of all the companies in the survey were in one of these categories.

A number of companies proved to be highly active in the OS community. In the view of one developer, ‘life is too short not to get involved in OS development’. In some cases, particularly in small companies, involvement was in an organized network of partners, with participation concentrated in certain partners: ‘participation is in a different part of the network’. Some companies are so heavily involved in OS within their business model that they take it as a firm responsibility to be aware of, and actively encourage successful projects: ‘If you look on SourceForge there are 1000 000 projects 950 000 of which consist of a piece of code which is going nowhere. There are a number which win in the longer term, and it is part of our job to identify those, and become active in them’.

The level of activity varied widely. Some companies started OS projects by releasing source code under an OS license; for example, one company took a strategic decision to release a proprietary commodity product into a saturated market. They reasoned that it was not financially beneficial to compete with other vendors, and releasing the product would increase their user base and undermine the market share of those competitors. Others were strategically involved in OS projects as ‘committers’, ‘reviewers’, and in other leading roles, including responsibility for language translation and publicity (including ‘trying to create an interest from the business world and public sector to encourage Linux solutions’). In other cases there was a lower level of interaction, including bug reporting and submitting bug fixes for consideration. Companies distinguished between software which was essentially commodity, and which they saw advantages in releasing as OS, and software which offered competitive advantage, which typically they kept as proprietary. For example, an internally developed, highly specialized software system, not seen as core to the company’s competitive advantage, was released as OS to encourage universities to train students with the system and to encourage further development – not simply by suggesting (and submitting code for) new functionality but also by performing additional testing.

A number of companies use OS components in their products and are naturally active in the OS projects associated with those components. A common attitude was that it was natural to give feedback about improvements to components, not least to influence the future direction of the project: having their own changes adopted eases later maintenance for their own products. In one case, the company estimates that only 5–10% of a product’s code is developed in house; the rest is from OS components with their code acting as a ‘roof over these that glue them together’. Another company noted that gluing OS components in this way, each of which has modest value, can result in significant added value.

An alternative and not uncommon situation occurs when a company develops software as a consultant for a client. In this case, direct contributions to an OS project may be made where the OS project’s software forms a component within the software system being developed – but major developments for the customer are outside their control. It is a matter for the customer, not the company, to decide to release the extensions developed.

Interestingly, most companies had no policy on activity in OS projects, leaving it to individua enthusiasms. There was a general expectation that developers would submit bug reports bu no stated policy even at this limited level of engagement. Of those that do participate at a company level, an interesting dichotomy is exposed. In such companies, individuals participate in OS projects either on behalf of the company or as interested individuals. In the former role, there is no resentment at demands being made on their skills and level of involvement by bug reports and requests for fixes. However, if they are acting entirely as individuals, it is not reasonable for others to make demands of them. A person reporting a bug has no reasonable call on a contributor’s time and as an individual is in a similar position to offer a fix. If contributing is a voluntary activity, undertaken for stimulation and personal interest, then it must be without a sense of expected responsibility. Having said this, highly motivated developers involved in an OS project often feel loyal to ‘their’ project and to other developers in that project.

## ANALYSIS

In the previous section we have illuminated perceptions among stakeholders in Swedish companies adopting OS; that is, what they most readily associate with the phenomenon of OS. In this section we analyse the findings of the study and relate them to established principles of the OS phenomenon.

Stakeholder perceptions seem to graduate from OS perceived as specific tools and products to something that can revolutionize business models and development processes. Overall, a number of distinct views were encountered emphasizing OS products, no cost, free access to code, system quality, process, business and ideology.

As expressed by one interviewee, ‘Our company wouldn’t be where it is today if not for Open Source’. One important factor seems to be the level of company commitment to OS, in that practitioners in companies contributing to OS projects or modifying OS code seem much more aware of the broader issues related to OS.

The relationship between companies and the OS community has been characterized by relating it to symbiosis in biology, and in particular the three primary categories of symbiosis: parasitic (in which the commercial interest is indifferent to its effect on OS software) – of great concern to the OS software community as over-exploitation can threaten the ‘OS software ecosystem’, mutualistic (in which each gains advantage), and commensalistic (referring to a commercial interest not harming the OS software project). The issue of how to deal with the relationship between company and community values has been a recurring concern raised by practitioners during the EU FP6 Calibre (2006) series of industrial conferences/workshops. Within our study we found evidence of mutualistic and commensalistic relationships. The fact that no examples of parasitic relationships were found could be a result of the sampling strategy used for the survey; such situations are likely to be under-represented among individuals visibly associated in some way with OS.

Developer involvement with OS projects is apparent in half of the companies approached, and in the majority of these the relationship can be said to be mutualistic.

When considering adoption of an OS project, the level of risk is important. The observa tion from one respondent about the very small percentage of OS projects that are likely to be successful may partly relate to the fact that most OS projects are developed by a tiny number of developers (Krishnamurthy, 2002; Zhao & Elbaum, 2003). Within the projects adopted in Swedish companies, the vast majority are large, well-known projects with significant diffusion. They can therefore be considered low risk, in that they will have been well tested in many industrial strength applications and are unlikely to lose their active communities.

Assessing the quality of OS software is being seen as increasingly important for the spread of OS, for example in critical systems areas. A consortium led by the Athens University of Economics and Business, the Software Quality Observatory for Open Source Software (Samoladas et al., 2008), which includes the company KDAB in Sweden, has received EU funding to develop software metrics and a quality assessment platform. Its aim is to ‘enable software companies and open source projects to benchmark the quality of their application’s source code and prove its suitability for enterprise deployment’ (Savvas, 2006). From our study, it is clear that there is a general perception of quality in OS software among Swedish practitioners, strongly supporting the view expressed by Michlmayr et al. (2005) that ‘the quality of some free software projects is comparable to, if not better than, that of closed source software’. However, there was also evidence of an appreciation that ‘not all free software projects are successfu and of high quality’ (Michlmayr et al., 2005).

It is also worthy of note that large companies (over 250 employees) within the sample are more conservative in their uptake than SMEs, being primarily involved only at the level of adopting products from the LAMP suite. However, there are exceptions, including both a large IT and a non-IT company, the latter with very specific requirements. Further, we found only one example of inner-source development. Inner source refers to the development of software within a company using OS processes and principles (Jilderda, 2004; Gurbani et al., 2005; 2006; Fitzgerald, 2006; Melian & Mähring, 2008). We note that the ‘application of Open Source concepts, perspectives, and methodologies within’ a corporate environment has also been referred to as ‘Corporate Source’ (Dinkelacker & Garg, 2001).

One question worth posing is why profit-oriented companies enter the OS field beyond using OS software (Rossi & Bonaccorsi, 2005; Bonaccorsi & Rossi, 2006). There are a number of business models around OS software, some associated with different licensing models (Onett & Capobianco, 2005). Several possible revenue models for OS have been identified and elaborated (e.g. Hecker, 1999; Feller et al., 2006b; Fitzgerald, 2006; Rajala et al., 2006). We have observed a number of these revenue models within the companies taking part in the interviews:

• support sold, including consulting, distribution and training;

• OS software distributed to position the organization for proprietary software-based revenue models;

revenue generated through hardware with OS software adding value;

• software initially sold under a proprietary license-made OS;

complementary accessories to OS software sold, such as books and reports; and

OS software distributed to enable revenue-generating online services.

This suggests that a rich OS business culture is developing in Sweden. However, it should be noted that some companies are multinationals and not all associated activities take place within the Swedish subsidiary. In our study we observed all four revenue models described by OSI (2007) as ‘Four Ways to Win’. All six revenue models observed are identified and described by Rajala et al. (2006) and Feller et al. (2006a).

A number of Swedish companies now see a business in repackaging OS components and offering added value. Some go so far as to ‘brand’ their company by its association with an OS product. Many adopt OS products and components within their own development activities, for competitive advantage. Some go further, releasing the products they have developed unde OS licenses. A major motivation for releasing code as open source is to gain benefit from a larger user and developer community. Of course, a prerequisite for obtaining such benefit is that the community can be built and sustained – something that has been shown to be complex (West & O’Mahony, 2005).

We have also seen evidence of network-based collaboration among Swedish organizations similar to those reported in other countries (Feller et al., 2006a). Such networks comprise ‘interested parties with complementary capabilities’ (Fitzgerald, 2006) who are ‘cooperating to deliver the ‘whole product’ (Feller et al., 2006a). Such collaboration is typically an informal outsourcing of some elements of the business model to the network.

Apart from benefiting by improving an OS software product on which a company relies, a mutualistic relationship may result from less obvious benefits. For example, Lussier (2004) also details an instance of process enhancement in a company brought about through the experience of its developers in an OS software development project. In this survey a number of such benefits were expressed. Firstly, a company can benefit from participation in OS projects as this is ‘very good for recruitment’ as expressed by one interviewee. Secondly, a number of respondents commented on the personal satisfaction found in getting involved in and influencing an OS community. Thirdly, and in more pragmatic terms, almost all participating in OS projects cited skills development as an important outcome of participating. Several also elaborated their experiences of being able to influence and expressed a sense of fun. Skill development happened through both detailed scrutiny of other people’s contributions and the rigours of writing and exposing their own contributions to scrutiny. As expressed by one developer active in a large number of OS projects, ‘apart from being fun, you learn a lot and have good opportunities to influence so that things become as you want yourself’.

## OTHER OBSERVATIONS

Within our study we found evidence of mutualistic and commensalistic relationships, with several interviewees commenting more deeply about the tensions experienced between cor porate and community values. Some interviewees were concerned that company pressure on OS projects could negatively impact on individual motivations for participating in those projects, whereas other amplified the benefit of introducing increased company involvement, stressing the advantages of growing company sponsorship of OS projects. Several practitio ners commented on the importance for a company to devote developer time to allow their employees to engage in OS projects as a mechanism to understanding what it is all about.

We also found clear evidence of a perception of increased ‘professionalism’ in OS projects, considered on the whole to be very positive. In this respect this study confirms many of the observations made in other research on contemporary OS, for which various terms have been coined: ‘Open Source 2.0’ (Fitzgerald, 2006), ‘professional Open Source’ (Watson et al., 2005) and ‘3rd generation of Open Source’ (Letellier, 2005). However, our study has also identified issues that cause people to be more wary of potentially dangerous negative side effects.

In particular, developers active in different OS projects clearly perceive a different collaboration climate (or atmosphere) in projects that have a strong company involvement. They perceived a certain risk with a project that is too strongly goal driven: the fun and enthusiasm for those participating on a voluntary basis can disappear. As a consequence the project may lose an important force for innovation.

This is clearly supported by responses from several interviewees, with experience from a number of OS software projects both in a professional role and as a private enthusiast. They noted a difference in the collaboration climate between projects that have a strong commercia involvement and those that are more ‘traditional’, community-driven OS projects.

This relates to the concern raised in the OS community about a potential tension between corporate and community values (e.g. Lin, 2006). It is therefore fundamentally important for any commercial actor to thoroughly understand how each community works and act according to its ‘informal rules'. From a corporate perspective. it is clear that the big challenge is to properly understand this and handle the difficult balance between the shorter-term corporate goal and the longer-term goal of establishing a mutualistic relationship with OS communities.

Related to this is the potential risk associated with the adoption of OS components from OS projects in which a company has no involvement. This can be a significant risk for the adopting company. For example, the OS company MySQL AB used the OS storage engine ‘InnoDB’ (a key software component for their product) from another software developer (‘Innobase Oy’). When a large competitor (Oracle) bought the component, it was unclear how MySQL should respond (Kirk, 2005; Martens, 2006). Relying on a component the development of which was in the control of a competitor would add risk to the business, but the component was a market leader and popular with customers.

An interesting observation from our study relates to perceptions of role and identity for a developer. We noted that in responses by interviewees in organizations fully committed to OS (especially among SMEs) our results show that people participating in OS projects sometimes cannot clearly differentiate between their own personal motivations for participating and those of their organization.

One may conjecture that the long history and tradition of flat organizational structures in Swedish companies has had an influence here. Characterized as environments in which a significant amount of responsibility has been pushed down to junior staff, these are advantageous environments for accommodating the way of working implied by OS practices. Such environments may also make it easier for OS ‘champions’ within organizations to take the initiative and act accordingly.

## CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our study provides a rich characterization of company involvement in open source and we have analysed its implications from a number of perspectives, including business motivations and rationale, individual and organizational motivations, and tensions concerning different values (community vs. corporate values).

Overall, the survey suggests that organizational involvement in OS development is a promoter of change: of perceptions, development processes and ultimately business models. This is a phenomenon worthy not only of monitoring but also of studying to understand the key tools, techniques, architectures, development methods and licensing for promoting mutualistic relationships. Such alignment is a challenge both for OS communities and commercial soft ware development organizations.

It is perhaps worth conjecturing how such alignment might be supported. OS champions within larger organizations might help this process by promoting inner source projects within their organization. By doing this, the OS development model will be taken in-house, and organizational learning will follow. OS communities could make it easie for organizations to assess the maturity and quality of products, thereby benefiting from wider uptake and increased interest and subsequent contributions. Responsiveness to contributions from commercial organizations is an issue that has been raised; it is some times difficult for organizations with substantive contributions to find an OS developer with upload rights who is willing to accept the contribution by committing the code to the OS project.

The FLOSS project included a quantitative analysis of OS, including medium and large Swedish companies and public organizations. This study complements the findings of the FLOSS survey for Swedish companies, concentrating on qualitative issues in order to understand the underlying dynamics behind the OS phenomenon in companies adopting OS.

What is most striking about the study is the level to which companies are represented which have a business model strongly influenced by OS. In particular, there are examples of companies whose business model is based entirely on offering complete solutions based on OS software products. There is also evidence of the development of networks for offering ‘whole product’ expertise.

Although only examples of mutualistic and commensalistic relationships were found in the survey, we accept that the purposeful sampling used may well have affected the lack of representation of parasitic relationships. What is perhaps more interesting is that mutualistic relationships are well represented in the sample. An ongoing goal of the community of people active in OS is to encourage mutualistic relationships that acknowledge OS, in the words of one interviewee, as ‘a technical discipline in a specia social context’.

We would recommend the conduct of similar studies to this one to build up a broader, European-wide picture of OS activity and encourage further reflection and debate.
