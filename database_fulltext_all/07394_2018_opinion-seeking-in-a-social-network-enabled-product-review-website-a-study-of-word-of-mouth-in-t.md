# Opinion seeking in a social network-enabled product review website: a study of word-of-mouth in the era of digital social networks

Camille Grange<sup>a</sup> and Izak Benbasat<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>HEC Montréal, Montreal, Canada; <sup>b</sup>Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Vancouver, Canada

## ABSTRACT

This paper challenges a long-held assumption of prior research – that online word-of-mouth is anonymous and impersonal – by examining shoppers’ opinion seeking in the context of using a product review website connected to a digital social network (SN). Motivated by the need to account for the idiosyncratic resources available in each shopper’s SN, we distinguish between two types of opinion-seeking behaviours: (i) , seeking peers’ opinions in <sup>exploiting</sup>one’s core network (ie, online friends), and (ii) , seeking peers’ opinions from one’s <sup>exploring</sup>extended network (ie, strangers). Using SN theory, we specify what drives these behaviours, and how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence shoppers’ utilitarian and hedonic experiences in an online product selection task. A free-simulation study, conducted with a restaurant review website connected to the Facebook SN, provided three key insights: (1) Exploitation has a stronger bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ect on shoppers’ utilitarian and hedonic experiences than exploration, (2) there is a strong positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence of social capital (both structural and relational) on shoppers’ propensity to engage in exploitation, and (3) high social capital does not induce the expected extensive substitution of exploration with exploitation. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of online shoppers’ opinion seeking in the increasingly prevalent SN-enabled settings.

## 1. Introduction

Electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) communication involves consumers sharing their opinions about products or services via digital channels, such as online stores or review websites. Researchers have extensively documented the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ects of eWOM on sales and consumer decision-making (Benlian, Titah, & Hess, 2012; Forman, Ghose, & Wiesenfeld, 2008; Schlosser, 2011). Yet they have also started to raise important concerns. Fake reviews are on the rise (eg, now 10–15%) according to Sussin & Thompson (2012), harming consumers’ trust in the content on online platforms (Jindal, Liu, & Lim, 2010; Mayzlin, Dover, & Chevalier, 2012; Sher & Lee, 2009). Online reviews are such powerful catalysts of online transactions that uncertainty about their legitimacy is highly detrimental to platform owners who invest substantial resources to prevent people from gaming their system. This uncertainty is harmful to brands, whose reputation relies on online consumer feedback, and to online shoppers, who risk experiencing less satisfying product search experiences (Kim, Ferrin, & Rao, 2009).

To address these concerns, researchers have suggested designing fraud detection algorithms (Akoglu, Chandy, & Faloutsos, 2013), <sup>fl</sup>agging fake reviews (Ananthakrishnan, Li, & Smith, 2015), and o<sup>f</sup>ering review writing guidelines such as writing more complex, more two-sided, and less a<sup>f</sup>ect-intense reviews (Jensen, Averbeck, Zhang, & Wright, 2013). Little attention has been paid to solutions that rely on a popular feature of today’s Internet: the ability to record and reveal social ties among users (Kane, Alavi, Labianca, & Borgatti, 2014). It is both timely and important to do so because eWOM increasingly relies on digital networks to be shared and accessed (King, Racherla, & Bush, 2014), and individuals tend to consider others to whom they are socially connected to be credible. Being able to identify “online friends” amongst reviewers may also help to create more reliable product search and evaluation environments. The present research focuses on eWOM environments that incorporate social network (SN) features (eg, user pro<sup>fi</sup>les, friends list). One could also study SN platforms that a<sup>f</sup>ord the sharing of product opinions, but such environments seem less suitable to product search and evaluation (Morris, Teevan, & Panovich, 2010; Mulpuru, 2011).

To focus our study, we concentrate on opinion seeking, a consumer behaviour that consists in looking for the consumption-related experiences and evaluations that fellow shoppers report. This focus was motivated by the fact that opinion seeking is critical to shoppers’ decision-making process and their shopping experience, for it can help increase product knowledge and reduce informational gaps (Cheung & Thadani, 2012; Hung & Li, 2007; King et al., 2014). Opinion seeking is also an informative unit of analysis for studying the value-generating mechanisms of SN-enabled eWOM environments, because while social ties can carry or help reach valuable resources, their value potential for network members is heterogeneous (Bampo, Ewing, Mather, Stewart, & Wallace, 2008; Gray, Parise, & Iyer, 2011; Lin, 2001; Liu, Brass, & Chen, 2014; Oestreicher-Singer & Zalmanson, 2013). Such heterogeneity is inherent in a SN context, in which each network member is embedded in a unique set of social ties. This calls for the use of a di<sup>f</sup>erent perspective than the one associated with the cognitive theories dominating eWOM research like the dual theories of information processing (Cheung, Lee, & Rabjohn, 2008), language expectancy theory (Jensen et al., 2013), or technology acceptance theory (Benlian et al., 2012). These theories do not consider the social embeddedness of users, and thus, they are limited in their ability to account for what makes SN-enabled eWOM environments unique. In contrast, since SN theory focuses on social embeddedness (Borgatti & Halgin, 2011), we drew on it to develop a context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c conceptualisation of opinion seeking.

Users of a SN-enabled eWOM website are part of a site-wide network composed of user pro<sup>fi</sup>les and social ties. The notion of online friends is commonly used to represent social ties online (Matook, Brown, & Rolf, 2015), and it suggests two types of opinionseeking behaviours. The <sup>fi</sup>rst, exploitation, involves seeking for opinions authored by online friends, who make up what we refer to as the core network. The second, exploration, involves seeking opinions outside of one’s core network, which we refer to as the extended network. Based on this conceptualisation, we examine: (1) what drives these two opinionseeking behaviours, and (2) what are the e<sup>f</sup>ects of opinion seeking on shoppers’ product search experience. In addressing (1), we consider heterogeneity in the value of friendship ties (ie, social capital), leading to hypotheses about the e<sup>f</sup>ects of these ties on the likelihood of shoppers engaging in exploitation and exploration. In addressing (2), we focus on two proximal outcomes in the form of shoppers’ perceptions of usefulness and enjoyment, which are known to induce other important outcomes such as satisfaction, decision-making quality, and loyalty (Ingham, Cadieux, & Mekki Berrada, 2015; Koufaris, 2002; Xu, Benbasat, & Cenfetelli, 2013, 2014).

To meet these research goals, we conducted a freesimulation study (Fromkin & Streufert, 1976; Liu & Karahanna, 2017) wherein participants completed a preferential choice task by browsing through a restaurant review website that we designed and integrated with the Facebook SN. The empirical strategy involved the measurement of naturally occurring network e<sup>f</sup>ects and opinion-seeking behaviours in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c shopping task context, which has rarely been done in existing research. The study revealed the following key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, exploitation had a stronger bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ect on usefulness and on enjoyment than exploration, which demonstrates the added bene<sup>fi</sup>t of online friends in an eWOM setting. Second, there was a strong e<sup>f</sup>ect of shoppers’ social capital on exploitation: the likelihood of shoppers engaging in exploitation increased with an increase in the extent of relational and informational assets created by, embedded in, and accessed through friendships. Third, social capital did not provoke a strong substitution e<sup>f</sup>ect between exploitation and exploration: the level of exploration remained fairly high even at high levels of social capital.

In summary, this study o<sup>f</sup>ers a new look at the long-standing eWOM phenomenon. It is novel in that it challenges a long-held assumption of prior research that eWOM is “anonymous and impersonal” (King et al., 2014, p. 172), and that “all eWOM messages are equal in regard to consumer impact” (King et al., 2014, p. 176). As such, it extends the stream of eWOM research that has studied the propensity of shoppers to seek peer opinions by focusing on a uniquely di<sup>f</sup>erent type of eWOM context (Chatterjee, Gilly, & Meyers-Levy, 2001). In addition, the use of SN theory improves our understanding of the role of social embeddedness in the opinion-seeking behaviours of shoppers and of the outcomes of these behaviours. Prior research has primarily focused on the adoption of friend-generated information by information seekers. In that regard, scholars have looked at the acceptance of the opinions that friends advocate (Fang, 2014), the self-reported susceptibility to leverage friends for opinions (Chu & Kim, 2011), and the intention to act on recommendations from friends (Matook et al., 2015). Our contribution is to complement this body of work by explaining how and why social capital factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence online shopper’ opinion-seeking behaviours, and how and why the latter can generate utilitarian and hedonic value.

## 2. Background literature

Our review of empirical studies on eWOM during the past decade has revealed three main research streams (Appendix 1). In stream A, the focus has been on eWOM generation. Studies in this stream examine what motivates consumers to generate and share their opinions on products (Alexandrov, Lilly, & Babakus, 2013; Goes, Lin, & Au Yeung, 2014; Hennig-Thurau, Gwinner, Walsh, & Gremler, 2004), how self-selection bias emerges in product reviews (Hu, Pavlou, & Zhang, 2017), and what impact early reviews have on subsequently generated ones (Li & Hitt, 2008; Ma, Khansa, Deng, & Kim, 2013). In stream B, researchers examine the efects of eWOM on market-related outcomes such as product sales (Chen, Wang, & Xie, 2011; Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Chintagunta, Gopinath, & Venkataraman, 2010; Hu, Liu, & Zhang, 2008; Jabr & Zhiqiang, 2014) and success in product launch (Clemons, Gao, & Hitt, 2006). In stream C, in which our study is situated, the focus is on eWOM from a shopper/ decision-maker’s perspective, and the research questions address the impacts of reviews on the beliefs, judgments, and choices of consumers (Benlian et al., 2012; Cheung et al., 2008; Kumar & Benbasat, 2006; Liu & Karahanna, 2017; Yin, Bond, & Zhang, 2014).

Two characteristics of this literature o<sup>f</sup>er an opportunity for further contribution. First, the literature is relatively silent about the characteristics and e<sup>f</sup>ects of opinion seeking (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Yang, Hu, et al., 2012) even though opinion seeking is pervasive and potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uential (Cheung & Thadani, 2012; King et al., 2014). This is a key limitation because knowing how much and whose content is being sought and consumed can help account for the types of information-acquisition strategies shoppers adopt (Berning & Jacoby, 1974; Jacoby, Troutman, Kuss, & Mazursky, 1986) as well as the outcomes of such strategies and the design e<sup>f</sup>orts that could induce the best performing ones.

Second, our current understanding of the role of SNs in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing information-acquisition behaviours such as seeking the opinion of peers is limited. One reason for this limitation is that the current paradigm in eWOM research relies on viewing eWOM content as being produced and used by independent and anonymous shoppers. It considers eWOM to be impersonal, and it assumes that the informational potential of eWOM messages is equal across di<sup>f</sup>erent types of consumers (King et al., 2014). Another reason is that the three key building-blocks of the eWOM phenomenon of interest here, (1) opinion seeking, (2) conducted in a SN-enabled context, and (3) for a shopping purpose, have been examined in isolation instead of together. Two pieces of evidence support this observation. First, there has been some research on opinion seeking in the context of information acquisition for shopping, but either o<sup>fl</sup>ine (Yang, Hu, et al., 2012) or without considering the social embeddedness of shoppers (Chatterjee et al., 2001; Goldenberg, Oestreicher-Singer, & Reichman, 2012). Second, a small stream of research has studied the context of online SN sites like Facebook by surveying users acting as surrogates for shoppers (Chu & Kim, 2011; Fang, 2014; Matook et al., 2015). Yet, such studies su<sup>f</sup>er from two limitations. First, they rely on the survey method for data collection. Thus, the opinion-seeking data that is obtained is general and based on memory recall instead of being objective and contextualised to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c shopping task. Second, they capture the perceptions that participants have about their SN instead of using actual, objective network data. In sum, research situated at the intersection of eWOM and digital SNs needs more contextualisation both in theory and in measurement, an avenue taken in the present study.

## 3. Theory

## 3.1. Key concepts and premises

## 3.1.1. SNs and online friends

SNs are sets of people connected by social ties. Several digital platforms a<sup>f</sup>ord users the possibility to generate and exploit SNs by enabling them to “create a pro<sup>fi</sup>le” and “to friend or follow” other users (Kane et al., 2014). Among these platforms, the most well-known are social networking sites (eg, Facebook), whose core purpose is the maintenance and development of social relationships. Apart from social networking sites, many other sites also o<sup>f</sup>er social functionalities relying on data from an existing SN site such as Facebook (eg, TripAdvisor, Net<sup>fl</sup>ix, to name a few). In practice and in research, the most commonly adopted concept used to capture and represent social relationships in online settings is online friends (Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Choi, Jiang, Xiao, & Kim, 2015; Krasnova, Widjaja, Buxmann, Wenninger, & Benbasat, 2015; Matook et al., 2015). Online friends are individuals with whom users are acquainted (Manago, Taylor, & Green<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2012; Tsai & Reis, 2009). Although a few websites and a few studies have considered nuances in online friendships by distinguishing, for example, between family, friends, and colleagues (eg, Liu et al., 2014), the most common application of the notion of online friendship has been dichotomous: another user is either my friend or not. We drew on this distinction and on the spatial terminology typically associated with network analysis to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the notions of core network and extended network. The former refers to a network composed of users who are one’s online friends while the latter refers to a network composed of users who are not one’s online friends. Figure 1 illustrates this conceptualisation and applies it to the SN-enabled eWOM context.

3.1.2. Opinion seeking as exploiting one’s core network and exploring one’s extended network Opinion seeking consists in looking for the opinions that fellow shoppers have shared about products and services. The distinction made between a core and an extended network suggests two types of opinion-seeking behaviours that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to a SN-enabled eWOM context. First, exploitation involves seeking for opinions authored by online friends (ie, exploiting one’s core network). Second, exploration involves seeking for opinions from strangers (ie, exploring one’s extended network). The notions of exploitation and exploration echo March’s (1991) speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of two classes of organisational actions: those that aim to improve e<sup>fi</sup>- ciencies (ie, exploitation) and those that aim to discover new ways of creating value (ie, exploration). This typology has been adapted liberally in the Information Systems <sup>fi</sup>eld. In the context of supply chain management, Subramani (2004) distinguished between the re<sup>fi</sup>nement of existing capabilities, that is, using the system for exploitation, and the creation of new capabilities, that is, using the system for exploration. In the context of knowledge management, Durcikova, Fadel, Butler, and Galletta (2011) analysed how organisational climate and the access to a knowledge management system in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the exploitation of existing knowledge assets and the exploration of new knowledge resources. In our own conceptualisation of exploitation and exploration, we distinguish opinion seeking within shoppers’ core network from opinion seeking in their extended network.

![](/api/attachments/5TJRYQMX/fulltext/images/e8eb8fc8dcef257f8332a553a2c8cfef5438710a9ae04e222796d0f37142488a.jpg)  
A network-based view of a SN-enabled eWOM environment.

Compared to traditional eWOM settings, user-generated content in a SN-enabled context can be channelled among peers. Yet, users must take actions to reap bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from this content, and they can do so by exploiting their core network and exploring their extended network. One might expect exploitation to be more prevalent and valuable than exploration as individuals are likely to consider their direct ties to be more credible and share more common knowledge and interests compared to their more distant connections (Mcpherson, Smith-Lovin, & Cook, 2001). However, one could develop an opposite argument based on the idea that one’s extended network can embed informational value over and beyond the “core” by including more novelty or reducing overlap in existing knowledge (Gray et al., 2011). In sum, the prevalence and value of exploration and exploitation are not obvious a priori, and they will be the subject of detailed analysis in the hypothesis section.

## 3.1.3. The social capital of friendship ties

As highlighted in the Introduction, a key characteristic of SNs is that they generate heterogeneity among members. In other words, shoppers in a SN-enabled eWOM context bene<sup>fi</sup>t from varying levels of social capital due to their idiosyncratic social relationships. Social capital refers to the potential value that is embedded in or enabled by social ties (Lin, 2001). Some people are “better connected” than others when their social ties provide them access to more valuable resources like emotional support, information, and power. The literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two key dimensions of social capital (structural and relational) that can be used as a basis to analyse the potential value created by social ties (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Granovetter, 1992; Moran, 2005)

Structural capital refers to the potential value associated with the structure of SNs, that is, with the “con<sup>fi</sup>guration of linkages between people or units” (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 244). In the context of the present study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne structural capital as the extent of informational assets that are created by, embedded in, and accessed through online friendships. Any social structure can be characterised in terms of quantity and con<sup>fi</sup>guration (Gray et al., 2011). With respect to quantity, the more social ties shoppers have the potential to activate (ie, the more online friends they have), the larger the amount of informational resources they can mobilise, and to some extent, the more novelty they can be exposed to given the broader range of informants. In other words, quantity is thought to a<sup>f</sup>ord access to more resources and to more diverse ones (Borgatti, 2005; Freeman, 1979). With respect to con<sup>fi</sup>guration, there are two contrasting views. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one, which is conveyed in the theory of closure, proposes the concept of a closed network to represent networks with high redundancy (see network 1 in Figure 2). In such networks, all friends are connected to each other. Networks with high redundancy are particularly valuable when actors need to collaborate, because redundancy favours the sharing of social norms, which in turns bene<sup>fi</sup>ts e<sup>f</sup>ective collaboration (Coleman, 1988). The second view, developed in the theory of structural holes, proposes that social capital is higher when a network structure is more sparse, that is, when it exhibits low redundancy (Burt, 1992). Networks with high sparseness (see network 2 in Figure 2) are particularly valuable from an informational standpoint because when friends are not connected to each other, the information that each holds should be more unique and, as a result, the total informational value available in a sparse network should be greater than that in a closed network (Simmel, 1950). The applicability of these two views is contingent on context (Borgatti & Kidwell, 2010). In the present study, network sparseness better captures the potential value associated with the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of shoppers’ friendship network, for opinion seeking does not require collaboration nor does it rely on past benevolent exchanges. Moreover, shoppers browse through content without peers observing them; thus they are not subject to social pressures or to feelings of mutual obligation (Gray et al., 2011).

![](/api/attachments/5TJRYQMX/fulltext/images/bc17c6aa5b13641d36b73721f8b23e23ee0d035e3ba554ec0560f5fe71447783.jpg)  
Closed versus sparse networks: illustration with A’s ego network.

In sum, shoppers have more structural capital when the size of their friendship network is large and there is high sparseness in the network because in such conditions they are embedded in a network structure that is highly e<sup>f</sup>ective from an informational standpoint (network 2 in Figure 2).

Relational capital refers to the potential value associated with the quality of social relationships (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Moran, 2005). This dimension of social capital takes into account the idea that social ties exhibit nuances because they represent relationships that people have developed with each other over time through a history of interactions (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). In the context of this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne relational capital as the extent of relational assets that are created by, embedded in, and accessed through online friendships. A large variety of social attributes have been considered to be markers of relational capital, including homophily, trust, expertise, power, and closeness (Borgatti & Cross,

2003; Brown & Reingen, 1987; Marsden & Campbell, 1984; Mesch & Talmud, 2006; Moran, 2005; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). While friends in an online network are likely to be familiar with each other and to have developed a certain level of understanding of their interests and ways of thinking, some variance is likely to exist in how meaningful and instrumental certain friendships are likely to be in certain circumstances. This is also true o<sup>fl</sup>ine. For instance, a colleague who may not be the most relevant informant for assisting restaurant choice could be very helpful for giving career advice. In other words, the characteristics of a context can be used to identify the relational attributes that are likely to be most in<sup>fl</sup>uential to certain actors in certain situations. Similarity may be key in a support network for people <sup>fi</sup>ghting addictions, but expertise may be more meaningful in knowledge networks. In a product choice context, the literature has emphasised three relational assets: product expertise, social closeness, and taste similarity. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of these criteria may vary across situations but prior studies strongly suggest that they are all likely to help make information sources more valuable in the context of a product selection process (Al-Natour, Benbasat, & Cenfetelli, 2011; Bansal & Voyer, 2000; Chang, Chen, & Tan, 2012; Gefen, 2000; Gilly, Graham, Wol<sup>fi</sup>nbarger, & Yale, 1998; Matook et al., 2015; Reichelt, Sievert, & Jacob, 2014; Smith, Menon, Sivakumar, Brown, & Naperville, 2005).

## 3.2. Hypotheses

We develop two sets of hypotheses. First, we explain the relationships between shoppers’ social capital and their opinion-seeking behaviours. Second, we explain the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the latter on shoppers’ evaluation of their product search experience in terms of usefulness and enjoyment. Two related premises regarding consumer information search behaviour underlie our reasoning. First, opinion seeking is limited, since decision-makers have constrained internal (eg, mental processing) and external (eg, time) resources (Simon, 1956). Thus, they rarely, if ever, make fully informed decisions. Instead, they tend to satis<sup>fi</sup>ce, terminating search when they have just enough information to make a reasonable decision or when they are not learning anything new or interesting (Browne, Pitts, & Wetherbe, 2007; Schwartz et al., 2002; Simon, 1971). Second, because opinion seeking is limited, shoppers need to be selective. In discriminating between information sources and information content, shoppers strive to maximise the value they can get while minimising search e<sup>f</sup>ort (Bettman, Luce, & Payne, 1998; Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011; Shugan, 1980). In other words, shoppers select information items based on their prospective value-to-cost ratio (Payne, 1982). We explain next the implications of these premises in the context of the proposed hypotheses.

## 3.2.1. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of online friendships on opinion seeking

We expect a positive relationship between network size and exploitation because of two complementary factors: the transparency of digital networks and the tendency of opinion-seekers to value interacting with familiar peers. Di<sup>f</sup>erent from o<sup>fl</sup>ine networks, digital networks are highly formalised and transparent (Erickson & Kellogg, 2000; Kane et al., 2014). This unique attribute of digital networks helps users to discriminate between peers with whom they are familiar (ie, online friends) and peers with whom there is no meaningful history of past interactions and thus very little to no familiarity (ie, strangers). The transparency of digital networks also a<sup>f</sup>ords observing and assessing the value potential of friendships. Two key functional capabilities of SNenabled word-of-mouth (WOM) websites facilitate such transparency, and thus contribute to make shoppers’ core network and its informational potential more salient and accessible. The <sup>fi</sup>rst capability involves being able to consult the list of one’s online friends, and the second involves being able to read the product reviews that these friends have produced. By exploiting these capabilities, shoppers can get exposed to familiar and in<sup>fl</sup>uential informants and the content they have shared on the site (Lee, Hosanagar, & Tan, 2015). Sourcing opinions from familiar peers is common and highly valued by information seekers (Jack, 2005). This preference is especially salient when it comes to information seeking for product choice because this context is prone to information asymmetry and uncertainty, and familiarity can facilitate understanding and reduce uncertainty (Gefen, 2000). Moreover, familiar sources are likely to be valued by information seekers because of their potential to provide information that is diagnostic and that requires low cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort to process. Therefore, shoppers can expect the sourcing of opinions from friends to facilitate an e<sup>fi</sup>cient product evaluation process. As a result, we propose H1:

H1. The size of a shopper’s friendship network positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences exploitation.

Similar to H1, the mechanism motivating the pro posed relationship between network sparseness and exploitation is driven by characteristics of shoppers as decision-makers and by the a<sup>f</sup>ordances of SNenabled WOM environments. First, shoppers value novel information because it adds value to their product selection and evaluation process. Decision-mak ing relies on an adaptive information search process in which information seekers engage in information gathering and sense-making activities that lead them from one piece of information to another. This process, often referred as information foraging (Pirolli, 2006, 2007), is adaptive as it depends on the expected value (the scent) of the information that is encountered. In other words, the information-seeking process dynamically unfolds based on the available environmental cues. Because of this adaptive search process and of the valuing of non-redundant information, decision-makers are expected to keep search ing as long as they can acquire information that has a marginal degree of novelty and can be useful to informing the decision. In contrast, they will truncate search when the information becomes redundant, that is, when the piece of information being acquired yields a decreasing marginal degree of novelty (Browne & Pitts, 2004; Browne et al., 2007). In par allel, SNs that are sparse are known to decrease the likelihood of information redundancy because peers who share few mutual contacts are more likely to hold and share unique information (Burt, 1992; Csikszentmihalyi & Sawyer, 2014; Gray et al., 2011). The opposite of a sparse network structure is a dense network, in which a shoppers’ friends are connected to each other. A dense network returns less diverse information; thus it is considered to be less e<sup>fi</sup>cient. In sum, we expect a positive relationship between network sparseness and exploitation because of the desirability of information that appears new to an information seeker and because network structures that are sparse a<sup>f</sup>ord more novelty bene<sup>fi</sup>ts than those that are dense. These ideas lead us to propose H2:

H2. Greater sparseness in a shopper’s friendship network increases exploitation.

We also expect that relational capital will encourage exploitation. We noted earlier that digital networks have the unique capability of making social ties and socially embedded content more transparent and more accessible. Another important capability of digital networks is that they feature user pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Thus, they emphasise personal identities (Kane et al., 2014), which enables shoppers in SN-enabled eWOM settings to identify and evaluate the quality of their online friends. Previous research has shown that the signalling in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information items in a decision-making environment is typically higher when these items are created by more highly relevant others (Wilson & Sherrell, 1993). This is in line with the idea that information sources that manifest more relevance tend to activate personal schemas in one’s mind and to encourage an information seeker’s attention to the opinions of such sources (Tam & Ho, 2006). Moreover, decision-makers value and seek homophilous and credible informants to assist product search and evaluation (Brown & Reingen, 1987; Reichelt et al., 2014), and relational quality, which manifests here via aspects of closeness, product expertise, and taste similarity, is a strong enabler of credibility. Thus we expect relational capital to motivate the seeking of friends’ opinions, which leads us to propose H3:

H3. Greater relational capital in a shopper’s friendship network increases exploitation.

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, shoppers seek to optimise informational e<sup>fi</sup>ciency by seeking information that seems valuable yet economical to get (Bettman et al., 1998; Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011; Shugan, 1980). The three <sup>fi</sup>rst hypotheses have drawn on this premise to posit that shoppers will be increasingly motivated to exploit their core network when their social capital increases because more social capital generates more accessibility to familiar sources of information (H1), to novel information (H2), and to informants that are more credible and relevant (H3). Yet because shoppers have limited resources, they are likely to split their e<sup>f</sup>orts between exploitation and exploration (Hertwig & Herzog, 2009). In this information selection process, shoppers are likely to engage in exploration when their social capital is insu<sup>fi</sup>cient and to favour exploitation whenever possible, that is, when their social capital is high. The former case results from a compensatory mechanism whereby exploration is used by shoppers to make up for the fact that exploitation appears to be unavailable or have little appeal. The latter manifests a substitution e<sup>f</sup>ect whereby exploitation increases with social capital while exploration decreases. When shoppers increase their reliance on their core network as their social capital grows, we expect that they will perceive a low marginal value of consulting additional content created by strangers, and thus that their motivation to explore their extended network will weaken. Altogether, these ideas lead us to propose H4:

H4. Greater network size (H4a), greater network sparseness (H4b), and greater relational capital in a shopper’s friendship network (H4c) decrease exploration.

## 3.2.2. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of opinion seeking on shoppers’ product selection experience

Perceived usefulness (PU) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as shoppers’ perceived value of using an eWOM platform to evaluate products during a selection task. Usefulness is induced by environments that help consumers to better deal with uncertainty and to make more educated product choices by e<sup>f</sup>ectively informing them (Smith, Johnston, & Howard, 2011) and encouraging product understanding (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007). Prior eWOM research suggests a positive association between consumer-generated content and usefulness (Forman et al., 2008; Kumar & Benbasat, 2006; Schlosser, 2011; Yin et al., 2014). We extend this work by considering two main reasons why shoppers are likely to derive more usefulness when they engage in exploitation as part of their opinion-seeking process, that is, when they consult informational content produced by their online friends.

First, a familiar source like an online friend is more credible than a foreign source, which helps to increase message believability (Ananthakrishnan et al., 2015), trustworthiness (Kubiszewski, Noordewier, & Costanza, 2011), and in general, usefulness (Sussman & Siegal, 2003). Prior research has shown that online shopping environments that appear more familiar and personalised decrease uncertainty and provide better answers to one’s informational needs, and that people prefer receiving recommendations from others they know and trust, such as friends and family members (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). In more general terms, the content created by familiar peers should be particularly useful because familiarity facilitates trust (Gefen, 2000) and trust is a strong predictor of usefulness (Gefen, Karahanna, & Straub, 2003). Second, familiarity between the sender and the receiver of a message implies that the two parties are likely to share some common knowledge, which can facilitate the assessment of whether a product might satisfy one’s expectations and preferences. It is not easy to interpret recommendations from peers when one has to make a choice among a large set of product alternatives. This is because shoppers need to take into consideration their needs and tastes together with opinion providers’ own preferences, such as whether the latter appreciate the same types of features in a product or a service. Such information can be di<sup>fi</sup>cult to codify and acquire, but it is easier to obtain from familiar peers within a SN (Reagans & Mcevily, 2003). Thus exploitation should facilitate interpretation and it should be more diagnostic than exploration. In summary, we expect exploitation to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence PU more than exploration because messages written by familiar others tend to be perceived as more trustworthy and interpretable, in brief more informative, than those written by unknown sources.

While time spent searching for opinions through extended networks could still be useful, we expect that, compared to opinions from friends, opinions from strangers will be less reliable and less diagnostic, thus less useful, which lead us to propose H5:

H5. Exploitation has a stronger positive e<sup>f</sup>ect than exploration on a shopper’s perceived usefulness.

Perceived enjoyment (PE) refers to the intrinsic pleasure experienced by online shoppers during a product selection task using an eWOM platform. It is induced in informational environments that are engaging (Mathwick & Rigdon, 2004) and trustworthy (Komiak & Benbasat, 2006). We posit two main reasons why shoppers should derive more enjoyment when they conduct exploitation compared to exploration.

First, messages from familiar others are expected to stimulate more enjoyment by being more engaging, ie, by being perceived as more fun and compelling (Shneiderman, 2004). This is supported by a large stream of research showing that social cues in digital environments trigger positive a<sup>f</sup>ective responses on behalf of users. For example, users’ awareness of whom they interact with stimulates cognitive absorption (Goel, Johnson, Junglas, & Ives, 2011). In addition, websites that include social elements such as those promoting familiarity yield more arousal and enjoyment (Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, Baker, & Wang, 2011; Wang, Baker, Wagner, & Wake<sup>fi</sup>eld, 2007). Moreover, socially relevant content facilitates a gratifying state of mind (Novak, Ho<sup>f</sup>man, & Yung, 2000) and promotes focus and <sup>fl</sup>ow, which together with cognitive absorption are well-known markers of intrinsically gratifying usage experiences (Mahnke, Benlian, & Hess, 2015; Webster, Trevino, & Ryan, 1993). In a similar vein, content created by familiar peers like online friends should convey a strong social <sup>fl</sup>avour and, therefore, we expect that it will surpass content from the extended network with respect to the level of engagement it can generate.

Second, messages from familiar others are expected to stimulate more enjoyment by being more trustworthy. As explained earlier, exploitation involves accessing product opinions that are deemed more reliable than opinions created by shoppers outside of one’s core network. This should contribute positively to shoppers’ enjoyment because trust reduces information evaluation and processing e<sup>f</sup>orts, breeding positive a<sup>f</sup>ect. The link between e<sup>f</sup>ort and enjoyment is evident in theories of intrinsic motivation, which explain that a reduction in e<sup>f</sup>ort encourages a greater sense of selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Bandura, 1982), with subsequent positive consequences on a<sup>f</sup>ect (Compeau, Higgins, & Hu<sup>f</sup>, 1999). In contrast, systems that are more di<sup>fi</sup>cult to use are less likely to be enjoyable (Van Der Heijden, 2004). In other words, messages whose source is familiar, and thus credible, should be more certain and easier to process, yielding more enjoyment.

In sum, exploitation should in<sup>fl</sup>uence PE more than exploration because opinions from familiar people tend to be more engaging and trustworthy, and thus more pleasing. Therefore, we propose:

H6. Exploitation has a stronger positive e<sup>f</sup>ect than exploration on a shopper’s perceived enjoyment.

## 3.3. Method

## 3.3.1. Study design and context

We explored the drivers and outcomes of opinion seeking using a free-simulation design (Fromkin & Streufert, 1976; Liu & Karahanna, 2017). This approach was based on the need to (1) create an environment that would o<sup>f</sup>er typical features of a SN-enabled eWOM website while (2) making it as natural and realistic as possible by inducing natural variance in the independent variables instead of controlling them. We customdeveloped an online restaurant review website and integrated it with the Facebook SN. Facebook was chosen as the underlying SN because it is the largest existing SN platform (2 billion monthly active users as of June 2017). Restaurants were chosen as the context for two main reasons. First, looking for information online about restaurants is a common task (Rainie, Purcell, Mitchell, & Rosenstiel, 2011), so it enhances the study’s ecological validity. And second, people like to spend time searching for information about restaurants (Gu, Park, & Konana, 2011); thus, this product is likely to be relevant to a broad cross-section of the population (across many di<sup>f</sup>erent categories of gender, ages, etc.), thereby facilitating the recruiting of subjects. The website included social features, such as the possibility to access restaurants reviewed by friends, as well as regular search features, such as the possibility to <sup>fi</sup>lter restaurants by location or cuisine (see Appendix 2).

As an initial step, we recruited a user base for the website by advertising the study in a large North American city of 2 million inhabitants, using o<sup>fl</sup>ine means (eg, postings at our university) as well as the Facebook advertising platform. We sought individuals who had an interest in restaurants. Moreover, the test of our research model necessitated some degree of social interdependency between subjects. To realise this condition, we prompted explicit referral by asking participants to share the study with their friends on Facebook. We also relied on implicit friend referrals by advertising the study to the Facebook friends of the existing user base. Once recruited, subjects were asked to visit our website and to review restaurants they knew of among the 287 that were included on the list created based on the city’s most popular dining festival. On average, they rated 15 restaurants, recommended 7.5, provided 73 tags, and wrote 1.9 open comments. All types of restaurants obtained reviews, irrespective of neighbourhood, price bracket, and cuisine, indicating that our list was relevant to subjects in the sample. Of the 404 people who took part in the review task, 386 completed it. Next, they answered an online survey that captured demographics and other variables (eg, product knowledge). One month later, we drew a sample of individuals from this user base to invite them via email to proceed with the restaurant selection task. We explain next our sampling approach and data collection method.

## 3.3.2. Participants

The website user base was composed of 352 people (386 minus 34 that quit the study). Because this study was part of a larger research programme, we did not contact all users. To ensure the sampling of a representative set of participants among the user base, we used strati<sup>fi</sup>cation, which involves splitting a population into nonoverlapping homogenous subgroups before sampling. One key way in which the users were systematically di<sup>f</sup>erent from one another was the number of Facebook friends they were connected to on our website; thus, we divided the user base into six strata of similar size: one with all isolates (ie, unconnected users); three strata with subjects having1, 2, or 3 friends, respectively; a fourth stratum with subjects having 4–5 friends; a <sup>fi</sup>fth stratum with subjects having 6–11 friends; and a <sup>fi</sup>nal stratum with subjects having ≥12 friends. Each user was assigned into one particular stratum. The random sampling mechanism applied to each stratum yielded a sample of 82 subjects (42% males, 58% females; 50% students, 50% non-students) that was su<sup>fi</sup>cient to allow hypotheses tests of medium e<sup>f</sup>ect size (Cohen, 1992).

## 3.3.3. Tasks and measurement

As part of the study, participants could use any desktop or laptop computer from the location they wished to complete the following steps: (1) logging in to our website using their Facebook credentials; (2) completing a tutorial about the site’s key features; (3) browsing the site to identify new restaurants to try out and <sup>fi</sup>lling out a wish list to validate their choice (note that the website prevented subjects from selecting restaurants they had reviewed); and (4) completing a survey about their experience using the site. Vouchers usable at restaurants chosen in the search task were used as incentives for participants. With respect to (3) above, which pertains to the restaurant search and selection task per se, it is worth mentioning that subjects could freely engage in both exploitation and exploration.

The measurement items for PU were adapted from Davis (1989), and those for the PE scale were adapted from Van Der Heijden (2004). Exploitation was operationalised via a computer-generated count of the number of restaurant reviews generated by friends that subjects had consulted during their restaurant search task. The same approach was used to measure exploration, using reviews authored by strangers in the network. We could obtain this measurement by implementing a “see review” button, which users had to click on to make a review appear (see Appendix 2). Relational capital was operationalised via a self-report scale, similar to previous research (Kale, Singh, & Perlmutter, 2000; Moran, 2005; Sun, Fang, Lim, & Straub, 2012). We developed three items to re<sup>fl</sup>ect relational quality in a product choice context, using closeness (Marsden & Campbell, 2012), expertise (Borgatti & Cross, 2003), and similarity (Mesch & Talmud, 2006) as relevant markers. Further, network size was measured by counting the number of Facebook friends that users had on our website. Network sparseness was computed by dividing network e<sup>f</sup>ective size by network size (Ahuja, 2000; Burt, 1992; Gray et al., 2011; Hanneman & Riddle, 2005). Network e<sup>f</sup>ective size is a measure of network size minus social redundancy, that is, minus the average number of ties friends have among them. At one extreme, a score of 1 in network sparseness re<sup>fl</sup>ects a perfectly sparse network (ie, a core network with no friend connected to one another), and at the other extreme, a score of 0 characterises a network with all friends connected to one another. Finally, we controlled for product knowledge, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as consumers’ selfreported knowledge of a product category. We operationalised it with three items measuring the extent to which subjects were familiar with, well-informed about, and knowledgeable about restaurants in the city the study took place. Existing research suggests that a lack of product knowledge increases the necessity and motivation for information search (Anderson, Engledow, & Becker, 1979; Punj & Staelin, 1983; Jacoby et al., 1986; Yang, Hu, et al., 2012). When consumers have little product knowledge, they face more uncertainty and rely more on external information sources. In contrast, consumers with a high level of product knowledge require less external information and opinions to make a product decision. Measurement items are listed in Table 1.

We tested H5 and H6 by running two independent multiple regression analyses on PU and on PE with exploitation and exploration as the predictors. Thus, the results suggest that the e<sup>f</sup>ects of exploration were, in practice, either not present or present to only a small degree. Either way, the regression results and the dominance analyses provide complementary indicators of the relative importance of both opinion-seeking approaches. ## 4.3. Supplementary analyses

We conducted two supplementary analyses. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one aimed at gaining further insights on the hypothesised substitution mechanism whereby social capital induces a decrease in exploration in parallel to an increase in exploitation (ie, in relation to H4, which was not corroborated by the empirical results). The second one further investigated subjects’ patterns of opinion seeking and the relationships between these patterns and PU and PE (ie, in relation to H5 and H6).

First, we had argued that, as network size, network sparseness, and relational capital increase, exploitation would rise (H1, H2, H3) while exploration would diminish (H4a, H4b, H4c). However, we found no support for the three latter hypotheses, thus we deepened our examination of the e<sup>f</sup>ects of shoppers social capital on opinion seeking. For each of the three network variables, we split the sample into two subsets: high (above median value) and low (below median value). We then reported the average levels of exploitation and exploration observed in each subset (Table 4). As expected, given the results of testing H1, H2, and H3, exploitation increased substantially (by a three to six-fold surge) between low and high levels of the three social capital variables. However, the average levels of exploration were stable or exhibited fairly small variations, the biggest change being a 30% drop between low and high relational capital. These numbers suggest that exploration is not just a compensatory opinion-seeking approach used by shoppers when their SN lacks value, and that the expected substitution did not happen either at all or as widely as we had anticipated. ## 5. Discussion

This study drew on SN theory to analyse the nature, drivers, and e<sup>f</sup>ects of shoppers’ opinion seeking in a digital SN-enabled eWOM setting. In this section, we synthesise the key contributions of the study, acknowledge its limitations, and discuss avenues for future work.

SNs have increasingly been integrated into product or service-oriented websites or applications; thus they have become prevalent means for channelling eWOM. This has revealed an important and interesting new context, and with it an opportunity to extend eWOM research. This context is important because eWOM increasingly relies on digital SNs to be created, spread, and used, and prior research has shown that these networks can bene<sup>fi</sup>t users (eg, Bampo et al., 2008; Gray et al., 2011).

Moreover, it is interesting because the social embeddedness of users and of content calls for the adoption of a SN perspective, which has the potential to reveal dynamics that the traditional views used to study eWOM are not <sup>fi</sup>t to explain. Thus, by adopting a SN paradigm, the present study extends the body of work on eWOM to match this new context where content is identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by its authorship and is socially embedded. In particular, considering SN-enabled eWOM with a SN lens enables conceptualising opinion seeking in terms of two distinct behaviours (exploiting one’s core network, and exploring one’s extended network) and identifying antecedent variables that are speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the context by capturing the heterogeneous value potential of online friendships. In addition to the theoretical lens, another novel aspect of this research is its empirical approach. The free-simulation study was designed in the spirit of previous research on digital networks, which demonstrated the utility of developing and testing theories in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts and with actual SN data (Gray et al., 2011; Lin, Prabhala, & Viswanathan, 2013; Liu et al., 2014; Susarla, Oh, & Tan, 2012). Our empirical setup has enabled the capture of naturally occurring network e<sup>f</sup>ects, and the objective measurement of opinionseeking behaviours in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c shopping task context, a strategy that has rarely been implemented in prior research.

## 5.1. Research <sup>fi</sup>ndings and contributions

This research yields a number of new <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Numerous studies in the literature have highlighted that looking for fellow shoppers’ experiences and evaluations of products or services is essential to shoppers decision-making process (Cheung & Thadani, 2012; Hung & Li, 2007; King et al., 2014). We extend this work by demonstrating that social heterogeneity a<sup>f</sup>ects opinion seeking, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences important outcomes of successful product search experiences (ie, shoppers’ usefulness and enjoyment perceptions). We found that online shoppers engage more with their core network of online friends when they are “better connected”, both at a structural and a relational level. SN theory suggests that friendship networks that are large and sparse are highly valuable from an information perspective. Overall, our study substantiates the notion that online shoppers are more strongly motivated to exploit their core network when it a<sup>f</sup>ords more access to less (socially) redundant views on products. In parallel, we found that relational capital had a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on exploitation, which brings support to the view that, together with structural con<sup>fi</sup>gurations, the quality of social ties also matters in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing shoppers’ information search on SN-enabled eWOM platforms (Adler & Kwon, 2002; Moran, 2005).

Another interesting discovery is that although we had hypothesised that exploration would diminish with increasing levels of social capital, we observed that it remained fairly high. In other words, although we thought that shoppers with high social capital would be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the informational resources from their core network, we observed that they tended to extend their search outside of their network of friends as well. This was a counterintuitive <sup>fi</sup>nding in light of the large body of literature highlighting consumers’ tendency to seek e<sup>fi</sup>ciency-maximising decision-making tactics (Todd & Gigerenzer, 2003; Todd, 2007; eg, Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, 2011). The results of a cluster analysis corroborated our initial observation as they identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a group of subjects exhibiting a pattern of high exploitation and medium exploration. In addition, exploitation and exploration were negatively correlated in this cluster, suggesting two insights. First, a small portion of subjects (those with high social capital) partially substituted exploration for exploitation, a phenomenon that we had hypothesised (see H4) but that we did not observe when analysing the full sample. Second, it is possible that exploration remained high because highly connected shoppers are intrinsically more inclined to explore outside of their core network. The mere fact that they bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a high level of social capital is a sign that they invested in social relationships, a practice that often requires a certain level of socialising and information seeking outside of one’s core social circle (Ellison, Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld, & Lampe, 2007). Finally, in terms of outcomes, the study corroborates the idea that online friends matter to online shopping. Their opinions better help shoppers learn about and evaluate products (thus increasing their perceptions of usefulness), and generate stronger enjoyment compared to opinions from peers outside of their friendship network.

Finally, this research is also connected to the conversation on the nature and e<sup>f</sup>ects of digital networks (Agarwal et al., 2008; Kane et al., 2014; Sundararajan, Provost, Oestreicher-Singer, & Aral, 2013). This literature has documented how and why digital SNs are important to di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders. For example, they can increase funding probability and reduce default rates in <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets (Lin et al., 2013), and they can raise online users’ subscription to premium online content services (Oestreicher-Singer & Zalmanson,

2013). We extend this body of work by explaining how and why digital SNs in<sup>fl</sup>uence shoppers in the domain of eWOM and product decision-making.

## 5.2. Practical implications

Even though an increasing number of online platforms incorporate consumer-generated content and make it possible for their users to connect to their existing SN (eg, Facebook), the understanding of the role of SNs in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing consumers remains limited. Online retailers who wish to improve the quality of their online presence by launching a SN initiative should enable users to easily connect with and access reviews in their core, friendship network. The Facebook SN is a good candidate to serve as the SN vehicle of an eWOM website because of its potential to foster exploitation. Facebook is actively used by more than 2 billion people, who are each connected to more than 300 friends on average (Smith, 2014), including those they share o<sup>fl</sup>ine interests and experiences with (Ross et al., 2009). Given the size and scope of this network, and the fact that it tends to connect people who know each other in real life, an integration with the Facebook SN platform can help shoppers to “import” into online shopping settings the social capital they have developed on Facebook and to promote a sense of relational relevance that can induce exploitation. In parallel, vendors should not discount the value of allowing users to explore the content created by strangers in their extended network. Even though exploration did not o<sup>f</sup>er direct meaningful bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in terms of usefulness or enjoyment, our results show that a fair proportion of users did nonetheless invest some search time and e<sup>f</sup>ort in this opinion-seeking behaviour. Because time spent searching for and accessing content on a digital platform can o<sup>f</sup>er secondary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to platform owners (eg, in terms of advertising exposure, see Sy, Bodo<sup>f</sup>, & Tam, 2010), a<sup>f</sup>ording exploration could still be useful to online vendors.

## 5.3. Limitations and future work

This research is subject to a number of limitations that suggest opportunities for future research. From a methodological standpoint, instead of manipulating the independent variables we designed an environment in which they freely varied. This enabled examining whether and how opinion seeking manifests in the focal SN-enabled eWOM context and how naturally occurring variations in social capital relate to their opinion seeking and ultimately to their instrumental and hedonic evaluations. A drawback of this design is that it makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to disentangle the roles of social capital and opinion seeking in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing shoppers’ evaluations. This is because there is a natural dependency between the variables capturing a value potential (ie, social capital) and those representing how (and how much of) this potential is mobilised (ie, opinion seeking). An alternative empirical design to help separate these e<sup>f</sup>ects would consist in a framed experiment in which subjects would be randomly assigned to di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions where the presence/absence of social cues and the a<sup>f</sup>ordance/ inhibition of opinion seeking would be manipulated (Harrison & List, 2004). This could help better understand how and why social capital may act as a trigger of either or both opinion seeking and user evaluations. Another method-related limitation concerns the operationalisation of relational capital via an omnibus score calculated by averaging the scores obtained on the three measurement items. Although this construct exhibited adequate levels of measurement validity and internal consistency, its indicators tapped into the notions of closeness, similarity, and expertise, which can be studied and measured independently. Future research could generate richer insights by examining the disaggregated e<sup>f</sup>ect of these distinct dimensions.

The study has a limited scope. First, it considered usefulness and enjoyment as the focal outcome variables, but other variables could be examined. Decision quality and purchase decisions, for example, are interesting candidates because the former is another desired marker of successful product search experiences while the former is of strong interest to online vendors. Second, the study does not account for the e<sup>f</sup>ect of review valence (ie, whether the reviews obtained by subjects re<sup>fl</sup>ected positive or negative opinions), but future research could examine the conjoint e<sup>f</sup>ect of this factor on shoppers’ product search experience. Because restaurant reviews were obtained before the task and reviewers had no prior knowledge of their SN or the experimental task, valence is not likely to have biased our results. A recent meta-analysis suggests that valence has a lower e<sup>f</sup>ect on decision-making than volume of reviews (Carrillat, Legoux, & Hadida, 2017). Still, combining heterogeneous characteristics of the product review’s source (eg, friends vs. non-friends) together with its valence is a promising area for future work because factors complementary to valence might interact to drive consumer attitudes and behaviours (Yang, Kim et al., 2012). Third, while this study considered social capital variables that capture a value potential associated with direct connections (ie, the core network), it does not account for social capital associated with indirect connections such as friends of friends or friends of friends of friends. Thus a worthwhile extension of this local view of social capital would be to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of variables such as closeness centrality (Freeman, 1979), which measures a node’s average distance to all other nodes in the network, or eigenvector centrality, which takes into account the idea that not all ties in a network are equivalent and that those that link to more important nodes should count more (Borgatti, 2005). Fourth, the study is limited to restaurants, which are experience goods. We intently selected this context because eWOM is more important in the context of shopping for experiential products compared to that of search products. Indeed, consumers are highly likely to rely on extrinsic attributes such as others’ opinions to assess the quality of experiential products (Huang, Lurie, & Mitra, 2009). Moreover, choosing a restaurant can involve a certain level of social risk when eating out involves peers from social referent groups such as family, friends, or colleagues. This could contribute to explain the non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative e<sup>f</sup>ect of social capital on exploration because although consumers are likely to satis<sup>fi</sup>ce in information search, the extent of satis<sup>fi</sup>cing could depend on the level of risk incurred by the decision-maker. On that matter, there are two other plausible reasons why we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any direct bene<sup>fi</sup>t from exploration. One reason is related to the use of self-reports to measure value outcomes. Because exploration tends to be perceived as costlier than exploitation (when the latter is permitted), shoppers may incorporate this cost in their evaluation and minimise the scores they report. Thus in future research focusing on the e<sup>f</sup>ects of exploration, we advise researchers to incorporate the measurement of objective outcomes (eg, product knowledge, product discovery, sales). Moreover, because the value of exploration may take substantially more time to manifest than the value of exploitation, outcomes that take longer to emerge (eg, product knowledge) may be more relevant to exploration and could also be considered in future studies. Another reason is that the value of exploration may be contingent on user traits like innovativeness and novelty seeking, for shoppers are likely to have heterogeneous preferences with respect to the appeal and risk of going beyond what is familiar to them (Hirschman, 1980)

Finally, more research is needed to further examine the distinct and possibly complementary roles of exploitation and exploration and how these two opinion-seeking behaviours <sup>fi</sup>t into the more general strategies used by socially embedded shoppers to conduct product selection task. This direction involves explaining how online shoppers initiate, engage in, and cease activating their SN to reach and consume information about products. This inquiry would require analysing whether and when socially embedded online shoppers act upon the visibility of their SN. It would also involve examining the sequences of shoppers’ information-acquisition behaviours that are more likely to be used under certain circumstances.

## 6. Conclusions

An important shift is under way with anonymous or quasi-anonymous eWOM platforms being replaced by SN-enabled ones where consumers engage with online content while being embedded in their SN. In this research, we drew on SN theory to develop a better understanding of online shoppers’ opinion seeking in the context of using a product review website that a<sup>f</sup>ords access to a digital SN. We found that the more shoppers sought for (exploited) their online friends opinions, the more they bene<sup>fi</sup>ted in terms of perceiving their product selection experience as useful and enjoyable. We further demonstrated that shoppers who bene-<sup>fi</sup>ted from higher levels of social capital had a stronger propensity to engage in this opinion-seeking behaviour, but that they kept exploring informational resources outside of their friendship network even when they were “well connected”. Overall, this study highlights the unique attributes of modern (SN-enabled) eWOM settings and lays some useful foundations for continuing the study of consumer behaviour in socially infused online settings.

## Appendix 3. Measurement validity

We estimated the constructs’ internal consistency as well as convergent and discriminant validity. We found that the typica validity indices were all above recommended thresholds. Cronbach’s alpha (CA) (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994) was above 0.70, composite reliability (CR) was above 0.80, the average variance extracted (AVE) was above 0.50, and the square root of their AVE was superior to the inter-construct correlations (Table A1). Those results provided supporting evidence for measurement validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). In addition, the item standardised loadings (Table A2) all exceeded the recommended minimum of 0.70 and were higher for the constructs they were supposed to measure than for any other (Barclay, Higgins, & Thompson, 1995). ## Appendix 4. Cluster Analysis

A cluster analysis was conducted to explore subjects’ opinion-seeking patterns and to investigate whether their perceptions of usefulness and enjoyment varied across these patterns. Cluster analysis relies on a prede<sup>fi</sup>ned set of variables used by a clustering algorithm to arrange cases into homogeneous groups. Cases within each of these groups, called clusters, are highly similar in terms of the aforementioned variables while cases across the groups are highly dissimilar. In this Appendix, we describe how we conducted the cluster analysis using SPSS (version 21).

Step #1. Determining the variables used to create the clusters: exploitation and exploration were used, for they represent the two opinion-seeking behaviours speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study.

Step #2. Identifying the number of clusters: we conducted a hierarchical cluster analysis, using Ward’s minimum variance method as the clustering algorithm and squared Euclidian distance as the similarity measure. The amalgamation coe<sup>fi</sup>cients produced by the algorithm at each of its iterations were examined to determine the number of clusters. A large leap between two iterations indicates that two relatively dissimilar clusters have been merged. The recommended number of clusters equals the sample size (72 in our case) minus the iteration stage where the abovementioned leap happened (69 in our case). Descriptive statistics of the three-cluster solution (Table A4) revealed meaningful patterns (cluster 1: low exploitation and high exploration; cluster 2: both low exploitation and exploration; cluster 3: high exploitation and medium exploration). A four cluster solution was considered but one group only had two cases, making it inappropriate for analysis Step #3. Testing if exploitation and exploration vary across the three clusters: we conducted a non-hierarchical k-means cluster analysis, which requires the a priori speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the number of clusters. Based on Step #2, a K-value of 3 was used. The ANOVA results showed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences among the three clusters along the two dimensions of exploitation and exploration (Table A5). Moreover, Bonferroni contrast tests indicated how the three clusters di<sup>f</sup>ered on each opinion-seeking behaviour. With respect to exploitation, cluster 1 (M = 3.9) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>erent from cluster 3 (M = 27) but not from cluster 2 (M = 2.1), and the di<sup>f</sup>erence between clusters 2 and 3 was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. With respect to exploration, all clusters signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly di<sup>f</sup>ered from each other (cluster 1, 2, and 3 had means of 43, 2.1 and 15.4, respectively).

Step #4. Naming the clusters: each cluster was named based on the values obtained on the clustering variables (Table A6). We named cluster 1 “exploration focus” to indicate that shoppers in this cluster are likely to rely mostly on their extended network in conducting their product search. Finally, we referred to cluster 3 as “balanced exploitation”, to indicate that this is the only cluster where exploitation was high (and yet exploration was medium, and hence more balanced overall).
