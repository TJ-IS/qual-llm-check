## A B S T R A C T

Aligning staf with changing customer and store needs is key to store operations management. There is considerable research on personnel scheduling, but little on the common phenomenon of Real-Time Labour Allocation (RTLA), where mismatches between workforce supply and demand are addressed by allocating potentially cross-trained emplovees in real time. Our interviews with retail practitioners confirm that RTLA de cisions lack analytical justification. In view of this, we design a generalisable stylised Retail Store Simulator (RSS) and instantiate the RSS using data from a gourmet supermarket. Simulation results show substantial longterm benefits to store performance from RTLA – a potential 6.6% increase in market share compared with No-RTLA. We further discuss RTLA's benefits under various employee cross-training configurations, answering a question from the collaborating retailer – “given the benefits of RTLA, how should we manage workforce flexibility?” We conduct extensive “what-if” analysis and find that broadening employee skill range and deepening employee proficiency increase the benefits of RTLA. This research helps understand workforce management at the execution stage.

## 1. Introduction

Matching customer demand with workforce capacity is critical to store operations. While stafing and scheduling decisions are widely studied, fine-grained decisions balancing labour supply and demand over short time intervals have received little attention. Previous literature has identified several strategies, e.g., on-call employees and overtime, to mitigate such imbalances [1,2]. We investigate Real-Time Labour Allocation (RTLA): the common practice of allocating crosstrained employees to diferent jobs in real time. For instance, store managers may allocate an employee working on shelf replenishment to open another checkout lane to alleviate a long queue.

Despite RTLA being widely observed in practice, our discussions with a global retail software provider (Reflexis Systems, Inc.), retail executives, and store managers reveal no specific guidelines for RTLA decisions and the impacts of RTLA are yet to be estimated. To our knowledge, the only practical RTLA decision support system (DSS) is that of Kroger Co., which implemented a queueing management system to recommend, in real time, how many checkout tills should be open 30 min ahead, enabling managers to allocate cash registers proactively [3]. This system reportedly helped decrease average checkout wait times across Kroger stores from more than 4 min to about 30 s [4].

A number of factors make designing an appropriate RTLA policy complex. Most RTLA decisions happen without full knowledge of evolving store situations and must normally be made quickly (often within a few minutes at most). Moreover, RTLA decisions may entail conflicting objectives [5], and estimating the long-term efects is dificult. The above factors render analytic models intractable. Simulation ofers a natural tool for us to understand and evaluate RTLA practice. We design a generalisable stylised Retail Store Simulator (RSS), mimicking typical grocery store operations: in-store shopping, checkouts, shelf inventory management, demand dynamics, and RTLA decisions. In particular, the RSS traces customers' repeat purchase behaviour to assess RTLA's long-term impact. Working with a grocery retailer, we instantiate the RSS using data from a gourmet store. Simulation results show the impact of RTLA on market share can be substantial. We also illustrate how the RSS informed the collaborating retailer's decisionmaking on the design of an in-house cross-training programme. Specifically, to answer their question – “given the benefits of RTLA, how should we manage workforce flexibility?”, we conduct extensive “whatif” analysis on various cross-training configurations.

The paper's structure is as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on personnel scheduling, dynamic task assignment, and cross-training. In Section 3. we discuss the design of the stylised RSS. We demonstrate its instantiation with the real-world case of the gourmet store in Section 4 and conduct extensive simulation experiments based on that store, with a discussion of the results in Section 5. Section 6 discusses the generalisability of the RSS, summarises the contributions and managerial implications, and suggests future re search directions.

## 2. Literature review

Personnel schedules rely on forecasting workforce requirements weeks or even months in advance. Despite significant eforts to develop good demand forecasts and robust scheduling systems ([6,7]), imbalances between planned and required labour capacity persist for many reasons, including forecast inaccuracy and employee absenteeism. Overstafing wastes labour, while labour shortages undermine service. Consequently, managers often need to make adjustment at short-notice, e.g., redeploying employees to higher-trafic areas [8,9]. Most workforce management research addresses the planning stage – stafing and scheduling decisions; few articles consider operational execution, in which original schedules are adjusted for realised demand. As van den Bergh, et al. [6] have extensively reviewed articles on workforce planning, we concentrate on studies at the execution stage.

Several articles discuss the one-of allocation decision at the begin ning of a shift. For instance, Campbell and Diaby [10] develop an assignment heuristic to reallocate available cross-trained employees to multiple departments, evaluating it against a classic Lagrangian heur istic and a greedy approach. However, that study and others do not account for RTLA decisions being made over time. While a stream of dynamic scheduling research exists, the time basis of dynamic scheduling (typically an hour or more [11,12]) ofers limited applicability to RTLA, whose much shorter decision-making intervals prevent managers revising schedules comprehensively. Another literature stream addresses queueing-based task-switching problems. To our knowledge, Berman and Larson [13] are the first to discuss task-switching between frontline checkouts and backroom operations in the retail context. Using a tandem queueing model, their threshold-based task-switch heuristic factors in the number of cross-trained employees assigned to checkouts, queue length at checkouts, and the number of customers yet to join a queue. Some articles extend this, employing a similar queue-based framework [14–18]. Overall, the literature shows that RTLA is yet to be adequately addressed – previous studies either exclude the dynamics of RTLA or simplify complicated store operations in a queue-based model for the ease of mathematical analysis. We instead propose a simulationbased framework, capturing the dynamics of RTLA and considering store operations with finer granularity.

Multiskilling is commonly regarded as a flexible way to address mismatches between labour supply and demand [19,20]. A cross trained workforce is characterised by three dimensions: extent (the proportion of employees cross-trained in a department), breadth (the number of additional departments cross-trained emplovees are trained for), and depth (the employee proficiency in the additional departments). Most studies consider how cross-training benefits stafing and scheduling decisions at the planning stage, stressing that an optimal staf mix combines both flexible and specialised workers [21–23]. However, only a few studies investigate using cross-trained workers to meet short term demand uncertainty when implementing workforce schedules. Campbell [24] discusses the trade-of between breadth and depth for one-of workforce allocation decisions. Computational experiments show that cross-trained workers need not be fully qualified (i.e., as “specialists”) for organisations to benefit. A team with a narrower skill range (i.e., qualified for fewer departments) but higher proficiency may achieve the same benefits as a team with more skills but lower proficiency. Easton [25], using extensive simulation experiments, evaluates various cross-training configurations for planning and one-of allocation decisions, considering the proficiency of cross-trained workers and specialists. He found that optimal workforce size (determined at the planning stage) decreases with cross-training depth, but that capacity shortages (relating to employee absenteeism and demand volatility) are relatively insensitive to cross-training depth. Henao et al. [26] discuss a chaining strategy, in which the combinations of cross-trained workers and departments are linked in a chain [27] which has been found to be an efective cross-training architecture providing flexibility [28–32] in a Chilean retailer, focusing on extent and breadth. Simulation experiments show the optimal number of cross-trained employees per department (extent) increases with demand variability and there is no need to cross-train employees for all departments. Indeed, their most cost-efective stafing mix contains specialists and employees crosstrained for just one other department. In summary, despite extensive studies on cross-training, the depth of understanding of the value of cross-training at the execution stage is limited.

## 3. A stylised retail store simulator (RSS)

Aiming to address the above research gaps, we design a stylised RSS that mimics typical grocery store operations. We model a closed customer catchment area with a constant population, assuming two stores: the first being the “focus” store, and the second – the “rival” store – representing other retailers and/or other branches of the same chain. Each customer decides which store to visit at the beginning of each period (typically one week) based on his/her own Store Choice Probability (SCP) – the likelihood of visiting the “focus” store. At the end of each period, the SCP for each customer visiting the focus store is updated based on his/her shopping experience in the focus store. The SCP remains unchanged for any customer visiting the rival store – as we assume the competitor's policy is unchanged over the simulation hor izon. This assumption follows from our interviews with store managers in the case retailer and previous literature [11] which indicate that RTLA decisions depend heavily on the discretion of store managers and/or employees – RTLA policy is dificult to mimic. Furthermore, the “closed-system” market design of consistent competitor practice focuses attention on the store-switching behaviours of the existing population over multiple periods, allowing us to investigate the long-term impact of RTLA. We calculate the average value of steady-state hourly store traffic (a surrogate for market share) to capture the long-term benefits of RTLA. In what follows, we specify the design from the perspectives of customers, products, and employees respectively. The notation is listed in Table A1 of Appendix A.

## 3.2. Modelling product availability

In most store areas (all except service counters/stations<sup>3</sup>), customers select items without staf engagement. Modelling on-shelf availability (OSA) of hundreds of stock keeping units (SKUs) in an area is an onerous task. However, we may create a sample of representative SKUs. ## 3.3. Simulating employee management

Fig. 3 illustrates the logic of workforce management and typical instore jobs. At the beginning of every period, the RSS initialises the stafing profile composed of (1) a cross-training matrix and (2) an “employee-area” matrix indicating the “home” area where each employee is scheduled to work and stays – barring any RTLA decision that allocates him/her to a new area.<sup>4</sup> Note that there may be multiple employees working in one area.

Employee proficiency in any area may difer in both work speed and service quality. Our discussions with retail practitioners and previous literature (e.g., [25,26]) suggest the relationship between work speed and service quality defies easy description. We define an employee with standard service quality and work speed as a “specialist”. We let p ∈ [0,1] denote employee e<sup>′</sup>s proficiency – his/her work speed for jobs in area j, relative to a specialist $( p _ { e j } = 1 )$ , with the same service quality as a specialist. A fractional value, e.g., p = 0.5, implies he/she would take twice as long to complete a task to the same quality as that of a specialist.

## 3.4. RTLA decisions

Within a period a store manager may observe particular perfor mance metrics, e.g., checkout queue length, or receive requests from frontline employees, and then allocate on-hand employees in real time. This practice – RTLA – resembles “threshold-based” allocation policies described in the literature [13,14]. We choose easily-observable service metrics W that may vary for diferent types of jobs (see Table 1). Most grocery stores feature three main types of jobs: shelf replenishment, checkout operations, and serving customers at service stations. We have already described the first two types. For the third, we consider both customer engagement that resembles checkout operations and “back ground” work associated with that service station, e.g., cleaning its floors, organising display areas, disposing of trash, pre-cutting meats for the butchery station, etc. Delaying background work may lower customer service at the station, directly and/or indirectly. The RSS traces the percentage of background work for a service station undertaken at a certain time as a percentage of the retailer's requirement.

Let W denote the threshold value of W in an area, above which we deem there to be a labour shortage. As per Table 1, depending on the job type, W could be “2 customers waiting at the butchery station”, “30% less than required achievement in deli background operations”, or “45 minutes since someone has checked shelf inventory”. Note that service stations have two thresholds, denoting both queue length and background achievement.

In practice, whether assessing store situations from front-line employees' reports or by walking around, managers must make RTLA decisions quickly, e.g., within several minutes, as delay or latency may reduce the value of information [48]. Thus, it is important to find a satisfactory solution in a timely fashion. Given the relatively short time for making decisions and natural cognitive limitations, managers may struggle to precisely transform observed service metrics to the exact number of employees short (“capacity gap”). Therefore, the RSS transforms the problem of explicitly calculating capacity gaps to a binary variable: an area is either understafed or not. This relaxation may significantly impair the efectiveness of RTLA, especially when there is a sudden demand fluctuation. To mitigate the efect of thi relaxation the RSS reviews store situations frequently.

Within each RTLA cycle, the RSS firstly addresses the understafed area with the highest priority, allocating one of the in-store workers who meet specific criteria (e.g., is currently not engaged with a customer) to it. The RSS then works through successively lower priority understafed areas. Total movements within each RTLA cycle are limited by the number of cross-trained workers, and the number of understafed areas.

## 4. Instantiating the RSS in a case retailer

Having described the design of the stylised RSS in Section 3, we turn in this Section to its representation in our case retailer. Our RSS<sup>5</sup> utilises Arena – a widely used discrete-event simulation package [49–51]. We collaborated with a New Zealand gourmet food retailer, “Alpha”, and ran the RSS based on data from one of its high-end boutique supermarkets. Other retailers may follow a procedure for instantiating the RSS similar to that described below. We present two sets of simulation studies. The first set (“base model”) in Section 4.2 seeks to validate the design of the RSS and evaluate the current RTLA practice. The second set in Section 4.2 provides a what-if analysis to quantify the efective ness of RTLA in various cross-training configurations.

## 4.1. Case description and simulation setups

Fig. 4 provides a plan of the floor of our case store. From our in terviews with managers and analysis of product characteristics, we divide it into 14 areas of interest: 13 shopping areas, and one checkout area (with eight checkouts). Four of the shopping areas have service stations, namely cofee, deli, seafood, and butchery, and are highlighted in italics and shaded in Fig. 4.

As accurate pre-existing records of customer trafic were not avail able, we utilised data from the in-store camera and Point of Sale (POS) checkouts transactions to approximate in-store flows. We analysed POS data over eight weeks (February 12 – April 8 2017), in which there were no public holidays. See Table A2 of Appendix A for the statistical summary of POS data. To isolate the impact of personnel schedules, we focus on the three-hour peak period (11 am – 2 pm) on a Friday – when no shifts begin/end and workforce size (30) remains constant. During peak hours, an employee, or a group of employees, is assigned to a particular area – their “home” – unless allocated elsewhere. The 14 areas fit into five departments: Produce, Deli, Chilled, Grocery, and Checkout (as will be shown later, in Table 3). Each employee thus has a home department as well as a home area.

Selecting a “representative” SKU in an area is challenging. POS data from the case store reveals a “long tail” phenomenon in the sales distribution of SKUs in an area. Selecting a top-selling “superstar”, an item closest to the median or the average value, or a lower-selling “niche” SKU may skew the simulated fill rate. To overcome this, we ranked SKUs in an area based on the number of units sold and then chose the one whose units sold was closest to half that of the top-selling item. Next, we observed shelf replenishment activities as mystery shoppers and interviewed department managers to estimate inventory control parameters for the representative SKU. Furthermore, from interviews with department managers, we assume a 5% possibility of OOS in the backroom for each representative SKU.

When several candidates are available, the manager allocates the worker with the least absolute diference between current and destination area proficiencies, aiming to balance gain and loss of productivities in current and destination areas.

Overall, the current RTLA policy addresses higher-priority jobs urgently while minimising the negative impact on low-priority jobs. In the case store, employees and/or department managers can report store situations (e.g., queue length) personally or via internal calls to the store manager, who also occasionally manages by walking around. On the one hand, with a larger RTLA review interval, some realtime information may not be captured by the RSS due to its active information collection mechanism. On the other hand, a smaller RTLA review interval requires a lot more computational efort with only marginal benefit in revenue.

## 5. Scenario analysis

In this section, we demonstrate how the RSS can be used for improving decision-making. We address a question the case retailer asked: “Given the current RTLA policy, how should we manage workforce flexibility?” To answer this and to understand the value of cross training at the execution stage, we simulate various cross-training configurations while keeping the RTLA policy in the case store (see Fig. 5) and the ratio of customer demand to workforce size unchanged. This assumption holds because grocery customers' shopping lists are relatively stable in the short term.

## 5.1. Experimental design

As previously described, cross-training may be characterised by extent, breadth, and depth. The RSS can examine any instance from these dimensions. Here we restrict ourselves to considering the benefits of cross-training at two treatment levels (high and low) in each of the three dimensions.

The cross-training extent focuses on a department's proportion of cross-trained employees. We consider (1) one-sixth of the employees in each department, i.e., the number of cross-trained employees is one (Produce), two (Deli), one (Chilled), one (Grocery), and one (Checkouts) and (2) two-thirds of employees in each department, $\mathrm { i . e . , }$ the number of cross-trained employees in each department is three (Produce), six (Deli), five (Chilled), two (Grocery), and three (Checkouts). In the event of fractional values, we round to the nearest integer.

The treatment levels for cross-training breadth are (1) two depart ments and (2) five departments in which employees are cross-trained. We specifically considered D-skill chaining (DSC), whereby a worker is cross-trained for his/her home station and for the next (D − 1) stations [57]. Note that the five store departments yield 120 combinations of chain sequences. We selected only the chain sequence: “Produce-Deli-Chilled-Grocery-Checkout”, because this fits most customers' in-store shopping routes. We explored 2SC and 5SC structures, where a crosstrained worker from the Produce department is capable of jobs in Deli for the 2SC structure and capable of all five departments for the 5SC structure.

The two treatment levels for cross-training depth are 0.5 and 1.0, denoting two proficiency levels in the secondary skills. For the 0.5 proficiency level, employees only learn the basic skills via on-site training or learning by doing, with low training cost. Cross-training programmes to produce the 1.0 proficiency level are well-structured and more expensive.

Combining the three dimensions at two levels yields eight scenario (see Table 5) and a No-RTLA model for comparison.<sup>10</sup> For each scenario, we ran 50 replications. We represent each scenario utilising the notation in Table 5. For instance, scenario $A _ { 1 } B _ { 2 } C _ { 1 }$ denotes that 1/6 of employees within each department are cross-trained and each crosstrained employee is capable of jobs in the “next” department at the proficiency of “specialists”.

## 6. Results and discussion

We collected store performance data (average hourly trafic over steady-state periods) from each replication of the nine scenarios and analysed it with IBM SPSS Statistics 25. The following observations are conditional on the utilisation level in the case store (see Fig. 6) as well as the RTLA policy (see Fig. 5). Using One-Way ANOVA, we compared the mean values to answer our first key question: whether or not RTLA improved store performance across various cross-training scenarios. The simulated data conforms to the three main assumptions of ANOVA: normally distributed dependent variable (as evidenced from the Shapiro-Wilk Test – the significance values of all nine scenarios exceed 0.05), homogeneity of variances (using Levene's Test), and Independence of Observations (as shown in the experimental design).

Table 6 shows the results of multiple comparisons of means (among nine scenarios). Store performance in all scenarios under the current RTLA policy – while we cannot guarantee the optimality – is statistically better than under No-RTLA, highlighting the benefits of timely matching of capacity with demand.

To attain the highest performance level of the eight cross-training configurations (A2B2C2 in Table 6), the case retailer could change values of extent, breadth, and depth. Some cross-training configurations may lead to the same efect size (as evidenced in Table 6 some scenarios share the same letter). For instance, A1B2C1 and A2B1C1 scenarios were categorised in the same group, implying that the investment in broadening skill range (breadth) may, to some extent, be substituted by improving skill proficiency of cross-trained employees (depth).

Fig. 7 presents the long-term impacts (measured by store trafic) and the intermediate impacts (characterised by employee utilisation) of breadth, depth, and extent. The case store can increase market share by increasing workforce flexibility in any of the three dimensions, even at low treatment levels. Comparing results at high and low treatment levels of three dimensions, we may make several additional interesting observations from Fig. 7. Firstly, employee utilisation and store trafic increase with breadth. RTLA promotes the utilisation of cross-trained employees with wider skill ranges. Secondly, store trafic increases, but employee utilisation declines, with depth. This is because higher proficiency leads to shorter service and processing times, and thus improved service levels. Lastly but counterintuitively, having more cross-trained employees per department (extent) does not necessarily result in improved store performance. In particular, Fig. 7 (b) shows a slight drop in employee utilisation from the low to the high treatment of extent. The current RTLA policy (see Fig. 5) that takes no account of future demand may “over-utilise” crosstrained employees, resulting in excess non-value added changeovers.

To compare impacts from the three cross-training dimensions, we ran a Factorial ANOVA using IBM SPSS Statistics 25, retaining the three main ANOVA assumptions, but excluding the No-RTLA data. The results of the factorial ANOVA test are reported in Table 7. It shows that the main efects of breadth are statistically significant (at the 0.05 level) and there is no interaction effect between breadth and the other two dimensions. Consistent with the results in Fig. 7 (a), broadening a crosstrained employee's skill range is beneficial for RTLA.

Since the interaction efect between extent and depth is significant $( p = 0 . 0 2 7 )$ , interpreting the main efects of extent and depth directly from Table 7 can be misleading. We split the dataset into two groups based on extent (1/6 or 2/3) and ran the factorial ANOVA separately for each group (see Table 8). The efect of Depth is statistically significant (at the 0.05 level) in both groups. Our results demonstrate that providing employees (the current workforce) with higher proficiency is beneficial to store performance.

We further split the dataset into another two groups based on depth (see Table 9). The efect of extent is statistically significant (at the 0.05 level) when skill proficiency for any additional departments is 50%, but not statistically significant (at 0.05 level) when skill proficiency is 100%. This finding suggests that when implementing an in-house crosstraining programme that improves workers' additional skill proficiency to the half that of a specialist, increasing the number of cross-trained workers leads to better store performance. Nevertheless, when crosstrained employees are of the same proficiency as a specialist, statistically there is no benefit in increasing the number of cross-trained workers per department, implying that the “low-level” workforce mix (1/6 crosstrained employees and 5/6 specialists in a department) is suficient to deal with real-time capacity mismatches. The case retailer confirmed this finding but stressed that the benefits of in-house cross-training extend to flexibility in handling absenteeism, and improve morale, workforce retention and even recruitment.

## 7. Concluding remarks

## 7.1. Generalisability of The RSS

Our research provides a stylised RSS that captures the essential elements in most grocery store operations, namely customer-facing operations at checkouts and in-store service stations, and shelf replen ishment. The main characteristics and behaviours of customers, employees, products, and the store manager have been considered in the framework. As such, the stylised RSS can be adapted to the operations of other retailers (e.g., click and collect, and advising customers at the entrance), in-store technology innovation (e.g., smart shelves that allow retailers to track on-shelf availability in real time [4]), or specific RTLA considerations (e.g., individual preferences relating to allocations). Furthermore, other retail formats, $\mathbf { e . g . }$ , fashion stores and luxury retailers, can also benefit from instantiating the stylised RSS. While the observed efect sizes may vary when the RSS is applied in another store, the approaches for eliciting parameters are generalisable, and our instance can serve as the base version for further software customisa tion. In summary, we believe that our stylised RSS is sound and reasonably generalisable to other retailers.

## 7.2. Research contributions

This research expands theoretical knowledge of retail workforce management. Firstly, our findings help fill research gaps relating to the execution stage of workforce management. Previous OM studies focus mainly on stafing and scheduling decisions at the planning stage, leaving the execution decisions to store managers and/or employees. We find that the benefits of RTLA are significant – about 6% in market share in our case of a high-end boutique supermarket. Retailers with higher utilisa tion would intuitively give better results. Secondly, we utilise a real world case to discuss the benefits of workforce flexibility derived from cross-training extent, breadth, and depth at the execution stage. Thi complements extensive studies showing the importance of cross-training flexibility for stafing and scheduling decisions ([25,58,59]). In our illustrative case, broadening store associates' skill ranges and providing indepth cross-training increase market share. However, increasing the number of cross-trained employees does not necessarily improve store performance. Thirdly, we demonstrate how a simulation model can be used to evaluate the long-term efects of RTLA practice. Researchers can use this simulator to address other store operational problems, e.g., layout design, or assessing the value of RTLA on waiting time. For instance, an additional question from the case retailer was how to develop better rules of thumb for when to open/close checkouts.

## 7.3. Implications for practitioners

Our research also provides important implications for retail practi tioners. Firstly, our results suggest that the benefits of RTLA can be substantial, highlighting the value of workforce management at the execution stage. The case store's RTLA is a form of practice of Management-By-Walking-Around (MBWA) (or “GEMBA walks” in the Toyota Production System) that relies on managers to make frequent visits (or observations) to front lines and solve problems on the site [60]. Secondly, the stylised RSS can be used to evaluate a retailer's current RTLA practice. For store managers, it should help to evaluate their RTLA policies and/or quantify the efect of their management activities. For general managers of retail chains, it may assist in summarising guidelines at the execution stage (e.g., when to open a new checkout lane) and evaluating diferent cross training configurations, based on extensive simulation experiments.

## 7.4. Limitations and future studies

We realise that the case study is only an initial attempt towards a

comprehensive understanding of RTLA practice. This paper has several limitations, and accordingly many fruitful research directions remain. Firstly, our study lacks data on cross-training costs. Future research could look at both the cost and benefits of cross-training. Secondly, as the literature [61–63] observes, behavioural considerations, such as interruption, performance feedback, and proactivity, may influence (positively or negatively) the process flexibility gained from cross training. Taken these factors into account may prove useful in facil itating retail store decisions including stafing [64] and queue management [65]. Thirdly, this research simulates a priority-based policy that may be sub-optimal. An attractive direction for future research is proposing improved policies or heuristics to facilitate RTLA practice. Fourthly, it may be worthwhile to explore the efect of RTLA with heterogeneous customer groups (e.g., which groups benefit the most) and how the RTLA policy is adjusted accordingly. Lastly, and en couragingly, recent DSS studies have taken advantage of emerging re tail technology to solve particular issues, such as detecting product missing on shelves based on machine learning [66], designing personalised coupons utilising location-based services [67], and dynamic pricing using item-level RFID [68]. We believe an appropriate storelevel DSS with high computational capacity and real-time information analysis has great potential to improve retail store execution and relieve administrative workload.
