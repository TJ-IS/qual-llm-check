# A critical realist script for creative theorising in information systems

Clay K. Williams<sup>a</sup> and Donald E. Wynn Jr.<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Computer Management and Information Systems Department, School of Business, Southern Illinois University Edwardsville, Edwardsville, IL, USA; <sup>b</sup>School of Business Administration, University of Dayton, Dayton, OH, USA.

## ABSTRACT

Research in information systems (IS) has been dominated by an epistemic script that leads to a variety of negative consequences related to the novelty, creativity, and nature of theory produced. While having matured as a field, theory and theorising in IS tend to lack diversity, struggle in balancing the role of reference theory and empirics, inadequately internalise the information technology (IT) artefact into theories developed, and lack clear relationships to what practitioners actually experience. Our premise is that critical realism (CR), based on specific ontological and epistemological foundations, afords the potential to establish alternative epistemic scripts that can enhance the theory product in IS research. We discuss the basic concepts of theory and theorising and relate these to the current state of theory developed in IS research. Next, we review the fundamental principles of CR and describe how a research script based on this philosophy of science addresses the negative consequences posed by the current dominant script. Lastly, we demonstrate the potential value of CR in terms of generating novel and substantive theory as manifest in two examples of published research that adopt the CR script.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, there have been several articles written by senior scholars criticising the state of theorising in the IS field (Alter, 2015; Avison & Malaurent, 2014; Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). The general tenor of these papers is that our research is inadequate for developing theories that adequately and richly explain the IT artefact. They also result in a wide range of disparate and incommensurable theories which make it dificult to conduct research which is progressive and coordinated. While raising a number of issues, these articles have only provided a limited range of solutions to the negative efects of our current mode of theorising.

This essay puts forth the suggestion that critical realist-based research, while not a prescription for all of the ills associated with the dominant epistemic script, ofers one potentially fruitful way forward that can substantively enhance the substance and creativity of IS theorising. The intent is not to address all the points raised by Grover and Lyytinen (2015), but rather to address certain points of their assessment, echoed by others, which help to more precisely articulate the value in CR-based theorising. This paper is organised as follows. First, we discuss the basic concepts of theory and theorising and relate these to the current state of theory developed in

IS research. Next, we review the fundamental principles of CR and describe how a research script based on this philosophy of science addresses the negative consequences posed by the current dominant script. Lastly, we demonstrate the potential value of CR in terms of generating novel and substantive theory as manifest in two examples of published research that adopt the CR script.

## 2. Theorising in information systems

As with most management domains, IS researchers have long prized theory as the core product of our studies. Scholars have viewed theory as “king” (Straub, 2009a), the essential component of the research enterprise (Compeau & Olivera, 2015), and similarly important descriptions. Each of us likely learned the primacy and focal importance of both theories and theory building as doctoral students. In recent years, several scholars have begun to reassess the value of our “religious fervour” with respect to theories (cf. Hambrick, 2007). In this section, we take a brief look at theories, theorising, and several concerns that have been identified with regard to the state of theorising in IS.

Arriving at a precise concept of theory can be dificult due to the many divergent definitions that have been published over the years in each domain of knowledge.

Theory can be loosely defined as “a formulation of the relationships among the core elements of a system of variables” (Shapira, 2011). A more formal definition is “a set of interrelated universal statements, some of which are definitions and some of which are relationships assumed to be true, together with a syntax, a set of rules for manipulating the statements to arrive at new statements” (Cohen, 1980, p. 171). For our purposes, agreement on a precise definition of theory is unnecessary as we will address several distinct, incommensurate forms of theory as part of our discussion.

Theories can be classified in several ways (cf. Gregor, 2006). One typology seeks to classify theories according to their scope and level of abstraction. At one extreme, “[g]rand theories are all embracing, unified theories in which observation about every aspect of the phenomena find their preordained place” (Hassan & Lowry, 2015, p. 7). Grand theories attempt to characterise the phenomena into a formalised set of concepts and relationships suficient to analyse all events, without resorting to concrete empirical data. On the other extreme are studies which are based primarily on the empirical data associated with a particularly interesting phenomenon. Studies of this type begin with the application of statistical and econometric tools to analyse large data-sets in order to identify any regularities and anomalies which would indicate interesting areas for further analysis (Helfat, 2007). The end result is often a proposition summarising the relationship between the empirical variables.

As Merton (1996) points out, an emphasis on theorising from top-down often results in hypotheses that are scattered and unimpressive (p. 49). Merton further notes that as a result of this emphasis on these hypotheses, “[w]e have many concepts but fewer confirmed theories; many points of view, but few theorems; many ‘approaches’ but few arrivals. Perhaps some further changes in emphasis would be all to the good” (p. 49). Instead, he proposed an emphasis on developing ever-evolving theories from relatively idiographic “special theories”, which are used to develop empirically testable hypotheses, into “a progressively more general conceptual scheme that is adequate to consolidate groups of special theories” (Merton, 1996, p. 48).

Grover and Lyytinen (2015) identify the existence of a dominant script that is responsible for over 70% of the published research in information systems journals. The stages in this script are (1) identify a research question related to an IS phenomenon (often, but not always, from a gap identified in another study that follows the dominant research script); (2) identify a reference theory that addresses some of the constructs in the research question (often, but not always, that were used in the previous study); (3) develop a research model based on the reference theory along with several IT artefact-based constructs; and (4) test the theory by collecting and analysing empirical data (often, but not always, using previously established instruments) (p. 275). One benefit of this script is that the resulting manuscripts conform to the status quo, making it more likely that they will ultimately be published. Furthermore, the level of theoretical and empirical rigour is higher due to researchers’ eforts to conform to a set of paradigmatic rules for producing and evaluating knowledge. The downside of this normal science is that our theorising often shows a lack of novelty and creativity and an inability to develop theories from within the IS domain that are specific to IS. Grover and Lyytinen (2015) explicitly identify three sets of negative consequences and imply a fourth resulting from the dominant script: a tendency to dissuade diverse theoretical insights in favour of “incremental and imitative theorising”; inadequate theoretical development of the IT artefact through externalising the IT as a construct or through a proxy while relying on shallow adaptations of reference theories; inadequate usage of theory and empirical data for research, based on a tension between high and low levels of abstraction; and finally, an implied inability to relate theories to practitioners efectively given incommensurable perspectives of abstraction and relevance. We will examine these consequences in more detail later in the paper.

Other scholars have noted similar concerns regarding the overly conventional manner of theorising in IS research (Gregor, 2006; Grover, 2012; Rowe, 2012). Avison and Malaurent (2014) classify a number of concerns, such as a tendency to use theory in a rigid way, the questionable application of reference theories from other disciplines, and the lost opportunity of making other contributions in favour of following the dominant script. Similarly, Gregor (2006) and Taylor, Dillon, and Van Wingen (2010) call for greater diversity with respect to the types of theory to be developed and applied to IS research. Together, these point to a deficiency in IS theorising as a result of an adherence to the dominant script.

## 3. Introduction to theorising with critical realism

Critical Realism has been advocated as a way to improve theorising in IS researchers for over a decade (Dobson, 2001; Mingers, 2004a; Mutch, 2002; Smith, 2006) As a result, a growing body of CR-based empirical research exists that ofers mechanism-based causal explanations for a variety of IS phenomenon at levels of analysis from the individual to an industry (Bygstad, Munkvold, & Volkof, 2016; Dobson, Jackson, & Gengatharen, 2013; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Mingers, 2015a; Volkof & Strong, 2013; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). In this section, we present the process of theorising from a critical realist perspective as a new way to explore real IS phenomenon in inherently creative ways that ofers new conceptualisations of theory. As such, it represents one possible way to address the issues raised with theorising from the dominant script.

In general, theorising follows a pattern flowing from problem identification, to fact/data gathering, to theory generation and development, and finally (in many cases) to empirical justification or falsification. At its most restrictive, a given discipline may impose a script on its researchers which dictates the specific assump tions, theories, and methodologies which are accepted as the standard tools for progress. In such “normal science” (Kuhn, 1970), the main thrust of research in a given field is constrained largely to extending familiar lines of inquiry while discouraging many researchers from pursuing outright novelties, particularly in the face of “publish or perish” doctrines. The result is focusing on the use, extension, and modification of existing theoretical concepts and relationships in the field to explain new phenomena.

Theorising in CR is aimed at identifying the set of mechanisms that explain a given phenomenon. In order to arrive at this explanation, a researcher must begin with the knowledge of a given phenomenon, which is then used to develop a theoretical description of the existence and outcome of a given set of mechanisms which would result in the given phenomenon as described. As stated in Bhaskar and Lawson (1998, p. 5, emphasis added):

Briefly put, explanatory science … seeks to account for some phenomenon of interest – typically an experimentally produced event pattern – in terms of a (set) of [sic] mechanism(s) most directly responsible. Producing this explanation will involve drawing upon existing cognitive material, and operating under the control of something like a logic of analogy and metaphor, to construct a theory of a mechanism that, if it were to work in the postulated way, could account for the phenomenon in question. The reality of the mechanism so retroduced is subsequently subjected to empirical scrutiny, and the empirical adequacy of the hypothesis maintained compared to that of competing explanations. Following this any explanation that is (tentatively) accepted must itself be explained, and so forth, a move which, in itself, presupposes a certain stratification of reality. On the transcendental realist view of science, then, its essence lies in the movement at one level from knowledge of manifest phenomena to knowledge, produced by means of antecedent knowledge, of the structures that generate them.

In other words, CR seeks to develop a theoretical explanation by (a) identifying the observable changes and actions (i.e., experiences) which constitute a given phenomenon of interest, (b) using existing cognitive material (antecedent knowledge) to frame these experiences ontologically in terms of the abstracted structures and events, (c) hypothesising (using analogy and metaphor) the existence and enactment of a set of mechanisms that would have accounted for the phenomenon (and events) in question, followed by (d) empirically corroborating the existence and operation of the hypothesised mechanisms and their efects.

It is important to note that the ontology of scientific knowledge in CR consists of both transitive and intransitive dimensions. The intransitive dimension of knowledge includes those things which exist in the “real world” that we seek to understand, while the transitive dimension consists of our ever-evolving theories and observations about the real world. As the intransitive dimension is largely independent of the transitive dimension, so too are the phenomena we seek to understand independent of our theories about them. Our transitive knowledge about the relatively stable independent world is influenced by not only our existing knowledge, sensory observations, and conceptual interpretations, but also the social structures to which we belong. In many respects, this follows Kuhn (1970), who argues that science proceeds through ever-evolving theoretical interpretations of physical phenomena, with occasional revolutions that establish new theoretical systems upon which to build subsequent scientific interpretations and explanations.

As such, CR does not make absolute claims about the infallibility of knowledge regarding a given phenomenon. In fact, CR makes exactly the opposite claim in that it prescribes our knowledge of the independent reality (including physical and social objects) as subject to shifting interpretations which are formed from the evolving knowledge of scientists and theorists in a given domain, social practices and standards for theories and methodologies within the domain of study. This highlights two key aspects of theorising in CR: manifest and antecedent knowledge, along with retroduction/retrodiction.

Manifest knowledge is any observable or abstracted knowledge about a given phenomenon. For example, we may watch someone flip a given coin 30 times, resulting in it coming up “heads” every time. This manifest knowledge typically comes from data collection, and the redescription, interpretation, or abstraction of this data in theoretical terms. Antecedent knowledge consists of our existing understanding of the world, including theories, facts, and previous observations. To continue our coin flip example, most of us are aware that there is only a 50% chance of a normal coin flip coming up as heads, regardless of prior flips. In the research context, antecedent knowledge consists of the theories and frameworks that a given researcher already knows or has access to via a search or absorptive capacity process.

Theorising depends upon these sets of knowledge from which we can draw references. With this knowledge, researchers can use a form of metaphor to identify a set of concepts and relationships as a way to develop and communicate new theories (in the transitive dimension of knowledge) about a phenomenon existing in the intransitive domain. This is also known as conversational referring in critical realism (Bhaskar, 2011), as opposed to physical referring, which denotes things such as measurements or perceptions. In other words, the researcher seeks to provide a theoretical description of the hypothesised entity which enables an audience to make epistemological links to a known theoretical entity (Cohen, 1980).

In CR, this is typically called retroduction or retrodiction. Both terms refer to the abductive process of proposing a set of theoretical propositions regarding the causal forces (i.e., mechanisms) that would have to exist in order to explain a given phenomenon. However, retroduction refers to the development of new mechanistic explanations for the current phenomenon, typically with no existing theory to act as a guide (Fleetwood & Hesketh, 2010). We “use what we do know (in the form of theories, observations and knowledge, possibly from related areas or other disciplines that inform our conjectures, analogies, and metaphors) to explain what we do not know” (Fleetwood & Hesketh, 2010, p. 245, emphasis in original). This in many ways is consistent with Merton’s advice to move from our assumptions and empirical observations to develop specific, delimited theoretical systems from which we can develop and test a set of hypotheses.

Retrodiction refers to the application of existing mechanisms and theories to explain a diferent phenomenon. Retrodiction assumes that we have transitive knowledge about the mechanisms underlying a given phenomenon, which we will use to apply to a new context. The resultant theory is likely to be prescriptive in telling us how to achieve a desired outcome in a specific context (Grover, 2012). This is also consistent with Merton (1996) in that both suggest that the resultant dominant script theories may not have been derived from a reference theory, “though once developed they may be consistent with one” (p. 43).

In either case, the resultant theoretical output of CR-based research is a set of mechanisms. A mechanism “is an integral part of an explanation which (1) adheres to the four core principles … [of action, precision, abstraction, and reduction], and (2) is such that on the occurrence of the input, I, it generates the efect or outcome, $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { O } } ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ (Hedström & Swedberg, 1998, p. 25). The mechanism explains how one event is linked to another by opening the black box of the relationship (Hedström & Swedberg, 1998). Specifically, a mechanism consists of the causal force emerging from the interaction of structural entities and their inherent properties, along with the activities or tendencies in which these entities engage.

Causal mechanisms, which form the statement of theory in CR, can be described in the following terms (Volkof & Strong, 2013). Mechanisms have an ontologically real existence that may or may not be observable. These mechanisms may be physical, social, or conceptual and are relatively enduring with respect to the events they generate. Mechanisms are the powers and tendencies that emerge from, but are irreducible to, inherent properties of structure elements. Social mechanisms are similar to physical mechanisms but have diferent characteristics in that they are unobservable and exist only in the practices of social activities, and while relatively enduring they depend on the self-awareness of actors, they are localised in time and space, and they are unpredictable (Archer, 1995; Wynn & Williams, 2012). The power of a mechanism to generate change in a system depends on the context in which it operates and other operant mechanisms in that context. In combination with contextual factors, the precise result of generative mechanisms is dificult to predict due to multifinality of potential outcomes.

The resulting set of mechanisms is then subjected to empirical testing to corroborate the claims that they were indeed responsible for the outcomes as experienced and explicated. As such, the mechanisms gain epistemic value even though they remain subject to later falsification or modification, much the same as more conventional theories. Over time, the collection of mechanisms would provide a library of theories to use for subsequent retrodiction as a means of applying our theories to new contexts and phenomena in a practical setting. The result of this accumulated explanation is the establishment of a collection of theoretical material which is useful for both research and practice in IS and other fields.

## 4. CR-based theorising to overcome the challenges of the dominant script

In their Issues and Opinions article, Grover and Lyytinen (2015) addressed a number of challenges associated with the dominant epistemic script in information systems research that adversely impact the value of its theoretical contributions. As outlined above, a number of other researchers have also expressed concerns regarding the state of IS theorising resulting from the dominant epistemic script. Given the fundamental philosophical tenets discussed previously, we highlight the ways by which CR-based theorising can alter the script to address four limitations attributed to the current approaches. To simplify our presentation of the benefits to be derived from a CR-based script, we adopt the labels used by Grover and Lyytinen (2015) to identify the negative consequences of the dominant script. Critical realism poses its own dificulties for researchers in terms of generating sound and rigorous theoretical contributions, and will not address all the challenges to theorising in IS research. However, the CR-based approach provides specific relief to impacts of dominant script that lead to homogenised and incremental theoretical outputs, the relative absence of data-driven and blue ocean theorising in IS, unsatisfying approaches to including the IT artefact in research, and severe challenges in relating theory to practitioners. How CR impacts each of these challenges is discussed next.

## 5. Inadequate diversity in theorising

The dominant script in place within our field encourages a modest degree of incremental theorising rather than supporting the creation and dissemination of innovative constructs and relationships associated with a given phenomenon. This incrementalism results in research which minimises novel theories and outputs. Instead, CR supports the creation of theories based on mechanisms, whether existing from previous research or developed via retroductive thinking to explain the study at hand.

CR research, adhering to the growing body of methodological principals and guidance (e.g., Danermark, Ekstrom, et al., 2002; Sayer, 2010; Wynn & Williams, 2012), will produce novel theories in the form of causal mechanisms. These theories provide context-specific explanations of focal events, derived from the retroductive process and that have been empirically corroborated. Moving to a new context (e.g., organisational setting, industry, type of IS), allows the researcher to refine the understanding of specific causal mechanisms expected to emerge from the relevant structures and the various factors impacting the activation and operation of causal mechanisms in that new environment. This is likely to involve identification of new elements of the socio-technical structures along with refinements to explications of recognised structural components relevant to that setting, deeper levels of causal structures and their relationships, new conditions related to mechanism activation, or additional mechanisms altering the generative forces impacting observed outcomes. It is true that each event to be explained represents a unique occurrence and that the tenets of CR focus research eforts on explaining how the mechanisms emerge from the structures in that unique setting to produce the observed events.

Yet each retroduced and empirically corroborated explanation presents a novel statement of theory based on the spatial–temporal uniqueness of the research setting. Every efort to produce a causal explanation through retroductive reasoning is a demanding, creative exercise in theory making (Dobson et al., 2013; Smith, 2010). While the conduct of this creative exercise is beyond the scope of this paper, there are several texts that ofer advice on how to do so (cf. Swedberg, 2014; Weick, 1989). Ultimately, retroduction requires the use of analogy and metaphor (Lewis, 1996) in order to identify and describe existing or newly identified causal mechanisms in a given phenomenon. Yet these mechanistic statements of causal theory ofer the potential to establish a body of knowledge based on explanations of demi-regularities (Lawson, 1997), and represent a practical and relevant alternative to the incrementalist and conformist approach of the dominant script. Eforts to generate mechanism-based theories that explain the causal logic of events associated with the application of IS technologies respond to calls for greater diversity in IS research based on relevant solutions to real-world problems (Taylor et al., 2010). This also ofers a fecund opportunity for knowledge production by providing focus to new research eforts based on exploration of the contextual influences and web of causal mechanisms that underlie the event regularities.

An early yet demonstrative example of mechanism-based theorising comes from the work of Volkof, Strong, and Elmes (2007) to understand how the implementation of an enterprise system alters the organisational environment. Embedding of organisation elements (routines, roles, and data) into the enterprise system was identified as the key mechanism to explain organisational change. The authors leveraged concepts from morphogenesis (Archer, 1995) to develop a theory of technology-mediated changed based on the embedding mechanism that overcomes a number of issues with structuration and actor–network theory-based approaches.

## 6. Inadequate freedom in relating theory and empirics

The expectations for instantiating reference theory into the IT context embedded in the dominant script tend to result in common “gravity in the kitchen” theories (based on “as is” adaptations of the reference theory to “new” contexts in which little diference is expected) or a multitude of irreconcilable theories (resulting from flexible adaptations of abstract reference theory). The implication is that as a result of the dominant script, IS misses out on opportunities for data-driven research and blue ocean theorising (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). CR ofers the opportunity to address both data and theory (in the form of mechanisms) with nearly equal importance and attention.

As described previously, CR-based research relies on abductive/retroductive reasoning applied to observations in the empirical domain to develop theoretical explanations of the causal mechanisms generating those events. Changes to the structures and systems in which agents and technology interact, in other words the events that constitute the organisational, IS, and individual phenomenon that we want to understand, are the bases for developing explanatory theory based on explication of causal mechanisms (Mingers, 2015a). For example, Williams and Karahanna (2013) conducted detailed analyses of structural changes associated with multiple key events in coordinating eforts to expose the inherent properties within the structures giving rise to causal powers. Retroductive analyses generated explanations based on two causal mechanisms (consensus making and unit aligning) that explained the observed coordinating outcomes in a federated IT governance environment.

An exploration of causal explanations must be founded on a variety of data types drawn from the research context that have the potential to expose participant experiences and actions, significant events, relevant structural entities and other influencing factors. The empirical observations of key events and relevant socio-technical structures are theoretically redescribed to expose deeper causal relationships (Danermark et al., 2002) and is very likely to reference existing theory to identify elements of structure that are relevant and potential explanations (Dobson et al., 2013). The relationships established in existing theory may ofer key inputs to retroductive theorising of the causal logic of potential mechanisms that could explain empirical observations. At the same time, existing theory may ofer little to inform causal explanations of the phenomenon of interest. For example, Dobson et al. (2013) initiated a study to understand broadband adoption in Australia as part of a national efort to establish universal access. A search of extant research failed to identify supportive explanatory theory (p. 971). In such situations, the retroductive theorising will rely on data derived from the study as the basis for identifying and describing potential causal mechanisms. In the case of Dobson et al. (2013), the authors entered into the case organisation after 6 months of interviews and group meetings with industry representatives and government agencies. This preliminary work had introduced the potential to adapt Archer (1995)’s concepts of reflexivity to identify individual reflexivity as a causal mechanism to explain broadband adoption decisions. As part of the research process, CR evaluates several possible theoretical explanations for a given phenomenon, seeking to determine which perspective ofers the best explanation.

Critical realist research leverages both data and the ory, tying them together, without giving primacy to either. Once mechanisms have been identified, through additional observations – new and diferent data drawn from the specific context – we seek corroborating empirical support for these explanations. A crucial aspect of critical realism-based theorising is that it is, in fact, datadriven in the sense that mechanistic explanations must leverage data as an integral part of theorising and in the evaluation of the proposed theories. For example, Williams and Karahanna (2013) demonstrate the full causal logic of the unit-aligning and consensus-making mechanisms across levels (macro-micro-macro impacts) based on observed and expected outcomes within the two comparative cases. This analysis was extended to demonstrate how the mechanism-based explanation compliments existing theories relevant to coordinating in IT governance.

Citing Helfat (2007) and Gregor (2006), Grover and Lyytinen (2015) suggest that empirical regularities lacking theoretical support ofer a potential for new types of theorising. Interesting, paradoxical, inconsistent and unexplained event regularities and construct correlations experienced across time and organisational environments represent an ideal starting point for explorations of the physical, social, and psychological structures that give rise to the causal powers that generated them. In essence, theoretical conceptualisations generated from the dominant script that lack satisfactory meaning and practical application or that lead to inconsistent empirical results shine a spotlight on opportunities to adopt the CR-based approach in order to enhance theorising.

The second opportunity to enhance IS theorising is to adopt an alternative script that promotes so called blue ocean theorising. While recent discussions of blue ocean theorising (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Straub, 2009a, 2009b) lack precise descriptions for this type of theory, we interpret blue ocean thinking as focusing on novelty in theory and theorising – theory that taps into truly new concepts or relationships and presents new ways of applying existing theory or theoretical concepts. Blue ocean theorising does not have to mean overarching grand theory. Rather, it remains likely that our theorising will result in the development of middle-range theory which accounts for both theoretical concepts and empirical data. In our field, blue ocean theory represents a valuable contribution in the sense that it can open new doors to substantive explorations of how and why “IS can … be efectively deployed in the human enterprise” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 272).

From the CR perspective, each time an information technology is deployed in an organisation we may reasonably expect certain patterns in socio-technical structures and tendencies for likely actions and outcomes to exist. But the ultimate outcomes are indeterminate based on prediction and theory. Thus, every efort to explain context-specific outcomes through theories of causal mechanisms is novel, and will involve “… armchair speculation, thought experiments, and the novel formulation of ideas that could generate alternative ways of interpreting what we know …” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 286). Interesting data and findings can be the basis for quality theorising (Hambrick, 2007; Shepherd & Suddaby, 2017). This is not to say that every CR theory based on mechanisms is blue ocean. However, the ontological and epistemological foundations of critical realism provide for adopting a new script focused on novel “blue ocean” theories of causation. We suggest that existing CR-based empirical research clearly represents, and shows the potential to create, blue ocean theory. One example, described in detail below, is the work of Volkof and her colleagues to understand IT-based organisational change (Strong & Volkof, 2010; Volkof & Strong, 2013; Volkof et al., 2007). A second example is Bygstad’s (2017) work to explain generativity in digital infrastructures through the manifestation of innovation, adoption and scaling mechanisms and the loosely coupled interaction of heavyweight and lightweight IT systems.

## 7. Inadequate treatment of the IT artefact

We continue to struggle with the role of the IT artefact in our theorising (Hassan & Lowry, 2015; Smith, 2010). In essence, “… if we want to address the fundamental questions of our field, we need to ask how we can treat the IT construct in richer ways” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 283). This requires that we move beyond the black box, or the contextualisation of reference theory to IS, to “conceptualise the IT constructs and their dimensions in theoretically rich ways and how we can relate these to other constructs in other domains – including social facts and structures, or properties and processes related to information and informing” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 282). CR-based theorising, in the form of explanations based on networks of causal mechanisms, seeks to conceptualise the properties and powers emanating from the IT artefact itself, and those that emerge as a product of the artefact’s interaction with these “other constructs” including human actors.

Adopting a CR-based script enables researchers to create theory to explain why the IS phenomena are taking place – the nature of information created, how and why it is used, the potentialities enabled and/or constrained based on the nature of the system, and the actions and decisions taken through and as a result of information in the IS. An excellent example of opening the black box to understand how users engage with information systems is provided in Aaltonen and Tempini’s (2014) exploration of a mobile network operators efort to create advertising audiences based on the analysis of telecommunication network data tokens. Telecommunication networks generate massive amounts of log trafic in the form of call detail records (CDR). In isolation, these CDR lack sub stantive contextual meaning. Using retroductive analysis related to key audience making events, the authors describe three mechanisms (semantic closure, pattern finding, and framing) that explain how a digital audience is defined and maintained through analyses of the CDR.

The process of retroduction, the essence of CR theorising, provides several important benefits in terms of exploring the causal influence of an IT artefact or some portion thereof (Bygstad et al., 2016). First, the search for mechanisms to explain events recognises regularities without deterministic prediction. Next, ontologically we hold structure (including the IT artefact) separate and distinct from action. This provides the opportunity focus specifically on the role of the technology, its inherent powers and tendencies, and the ongoing interactions of actors with the technology. Third, by its very nature, the mechanism helps us to “understand better what is happening in the real world, rather than simply reporting a statistical relationship” (Bygstad et al., 2016, p. 85).

To gain greater insights into the role of the IT artefact in specific applications, an emerging body of CR-based research leverages the concept of afordances as a particular type of causal mechanism. Afordances are defined as “the potential for behaviours associated with achieving an immediate concrete outcome and arising from the relationship between an object (e.g., an IT artefact) and a goal-oriented actor or actors” (Volkof & Strong, 2013, p. 823). This definition emphasises the relationship of the actor(s) with the focal information system as the source of the causal powers. This highlights the central role of, and the absolute necessity to explore the essential nature of, the information system in order to create a causal explanation for empirical events. Two examples of afordance-based research (based on Bygstad et al., 2016; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Volkof & Strong, 2013) are detailed next.

## 8. Inability to relate theory to what practitioners experience

The last issue associated with negative consequences of the dominant script is the opportunity to make our theorising, in general, more applicable to practitioners and their practical experience. This is more than just the rigour and relevance debate. The research questions we ask and the resultant theories we generate could be much more recognisable and useful to practitioners – those creating and deploying information systems in organisations. In this, a critical realist script ofers a way to counter the dominant script that, if not unique, is fundamental to the nature of the theorising endeavour.

We seek causal explanations, as the accepted form of theory, for spatio-temporally constrained, context-specific events. Organisational participants are instrumental in identifying and explicating the events deemed significant and the associated structures. As described in the prior section, particularly relating to identifying afordances, the treatment of the IT artefact will engage practitioners at deeper levels of system capabilities, business processes, and the influences of participant actions. These explanations are tied to emergent properties of relevant structures – interactions of human, social, and physical/technical entities – through the process of retroduction. This is the essence of “theoretical sense making based on practice-based evidence” (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015, p. 287) which represents an opportunity to improve our theorising. Good theory in the form of statements of mechanisms and afordances include clear causal logic of the tendencies associated with specific IT-enabled structures and relevant environmental factors. These statements provide suficient description to allow practitioners to identify relevant causal structures and environmental influences in the same context at a diferent time or a diferent context where there is expectation that the mechanism(s) may be present and activated. While not predictive, the mechanisms can provide guidance to practitioners on how to intervene at the level of the system capabilities, the interrelated social and physical structures, and contextual environment so as to improve outcomes (Volkof & Strong, 2013). From this perspective, a causal mechanism-based theory will, by definition, be both recognisable and applicable to those involved. Two recent empirical studies provide clear links to engage practitioners through CR-based theorising. In the first, Aaltonen and Tempini (2014) demonstrate through their mechanism-based explanation how digital marketers may view the data pool artefact of a telecommunications network as a new resource, how digital audiences are created from this data pool and how the value associated with the new audience is created. The other is Volkof and Strong’s (2013) use of afordances to further explore organisational change. The authors specifically address the relevance to practitioners:

For practitioners, this approach should help them plan for the implementation process and recognize and intervene when there are problems. Specifically, understanding that higher level afordances are emergent properties that typically first require actualization of lower level afordances can help in planning the implementation and managing expectations. Understanding the diferent sources of problems (i.e., those that arise from the inherent nature of the afordance, those that arise from missing afordances either in the form of missing technology features or missing individual abilities, and those that arise from poor or incomplete actualization) can help professionals determine how and whether intervening can produce better outcomes. (p. 832)

## 9. Examples of CR-based theorising

In this essay, we have argued that a research script leveraging the potential of critical realism stands to enhance the quality and novelty of theory in IS research by providing ways for researchers to overcome limitations on theorising imposed by the dominant epistemic script. Here, we ofer a brief discussion of two examples of CR-based IS empirical research that highlight the benefits associated with the alternative CR script. We do not suggest that each of these studies manifests the full range of the benefits that may be derived from CR to address the negative consequences associated with the dominant script, nor do we suggest that these are the only examples that efectively demonstrate the value of CR research. Rather, these examples are indicative of what researchers can achieve in terms of creating novel, useful theory and one avenue available to improve IS theorising more generally.

The first example is a longitudinal study of IT innovation through infrastructure evolution presented into two separate but related articles (Bygstad et al., 2016; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). The study focused on a Scandinavian discount airline that successfully evolved a complex IT infrastructure to enable rapid growth as a passenger air carrier as well as successful entry into a number of internet information-based businesses. In terms of diversity in theorising, the earlier article presents a detailed empirical analysis of data drawn from interviews, observations and other sources to identify and precisely explicate theory as three specific mechanisms (innovation, adoption, and scaling mechanisms) which explain digital infrastructure evolution and are responsible for generating the successful innovation outcomes observed. For example, the authors identified an innovation mechanism described as “a self-reinforcing process by which new products and services are created as infrastructure malleability spawns recombination of resources”, and an adoption mechanisms defined as “a self-reinforcing process by which more users adopt the infrastructure as more resources invested increase the usefulness of the infrastructure” (p. 918). Further, the causal logic presented identifies new services as the outcome of the innovation mechanism which, as “new services ofered”, are the starting point of the adoption mechanism.

This was extended by a configurational analysis applied to a case survey of 41 cases of infrastructure evolution, demonstrating novelty in relating empirical data to the emerging theory, to identify the causal pathways through which these mechanisms combine to generate successful innovation outcomes. In order to explain how the mechanisms actually combined to generate the outcomes observed, the authors identified how the mechanisms interacted in two configurations to produce positive infrastructure evolutions. The patterns identified through the configurational analysis were then demonstrated in the events of the Scandinavian Airline. For example, the authors determined that a single mechanism (innovation) is not suficient to produce a successful outcome and in one configuration of the mechanisms (adoption-innovation-scaling), “the innovation and adoption mechanisms fed on each other, which created fertile ground for the scaling mechanism as combinatorial possibilities (innovation) increased and the provision of more users (adoption) leveraged the scope of the infrastructure” (p. 923).

The latter article reanalysed the study data to identify afordances emerging from the interactions of the technology infrastructure and goal-oriented actors so as to refine the understanding and explanations of successful technology innovation. The authors developed and applied a six-step framework for critical realist data analysis to identify eight afordances deeply tied to the interactions of the primary actors and specific, evolving capabilities of the technology infrastructure that generated the successful outcomes. “An afordance arises from the relation between entities that may produce the functional outcome. The afordance is the potential action involved in getting to that outcome” (Bygstad et al., 2016, p. 91). Examples of the afordances include “developing concepts”, “prototyping” and “developing a service”. Far from externalised, the treatment of the IT artefact was focused on specific information processing capabilities existing or added to the infrastructure associated with key events that enabled the actors to achieve certain outcomes. These detailed afordances were abstracted based on enabling conditions, type and functional outcomes to the more generalised mechanisms identified in the prior study. The three afordances identified above, for example, were abstracted to the innovation mechanism.

These results diversify and extend our understanding by introducing new theoretical concepts (i.e., specific afordances associated to generalised mechanisms) to explain technology innovation in ways that are dependent on the specific technology infrastructure involved. The theory, represented as mechanisms and afordances, ofers a detailed explanation of how and why successful innovation through evolving network infrastructure was achieved in a manner that is profoundly diferent than research conducted adhering to the dominant script. While informed by limited existing research on infrastructure innovation, both articles leverage prior theory and in-depth case data intensely through retroductive analyses to develop and corroborate novel statements of theory. These theories, which one could reasonably label as “blue ocean”, explain the context specific innovation outcomes. These articles also demonstrated how the body of knowledge can be extended through CR-based research in that the original mechanism-based explanation is refined and enhanced through the addition of specific afordances underlying the generalised mechanisms. It is interesting to note that Grover and Lyytinen (2015, p. 285) use an example related to IT innovation in calling for more data-driven theorising in instances where empirical results lack clear theoretical support. These articles demonstrate a useful way to answer this call.

The second example of empirical CR research (Volkof & Strong, 2013) involves the reanalysis of two published longitudinal cases to demonstrate the value of causal mechanisms and afordances in understanding IT-associated organisational change outcomes. One case involved the implementation of a large-scale packaged enterprise system (ES), and the other a custom developed, proprietary automotive vehicle modelling system (MOD). The authors first identified specific concrete outcomes demonstrative of organisational change then retroductively identified afordances through analysis of the case data to explain them.

A set of 10 afordances were identified and corroborated for the ES case along with nine for MOD. In each case, the specific afordances were grouped into more generic afordance categories. For example, standardising and integrating afordances of “standardising process and data” and “integrating processes and data” were identified at ES while similar standardising afordances of “standardising model set-up routines” and “standardising output of post-processors” were identified at MOD. In each instance, the afordances were linked to specific capabilities of the information systems within the context of system use from which the causal influence emerged. The authors demonstrated precedence in activation of afordances, multiple levels from which the afordances are activated and operate, and generalising cross-case (similar afordances producing similar concrete outcomes).

These groupings of afordances and explications of how they operate for each case are explicitly presented as “preliminary afordance-based theories … being technology specific, but also producing some generalizable results” (p. 828). The statements of afordances, as a specific subset of mechanisms, ofer a new, diverse concept of theory as a way to consider how and why the ways actors engage with an IS produce change. The afordances are explicated through the interaction of various structures in the IS (e.g., coded transactions, data structures and user screens) with specific levels of actors (e.g., single role-based vs. group-based actors), thus providing deep exploration of the artefact as part of the change process. The proposed afordance-based theory of organisational change is linked to and extends the concepts and explanatory power of imbrication-based theory of change (Leonardi, 2011). And the preliminary theories of afordances efectively link theory to practice through specific recommendations to practitioners on planning for and intervening in large-scale IS implementations in order to enact efective organisational change.

## 10. Conclusion

The state of theorising in information systems research is advancing in terms of acceptance, quality, and rigour. However, the mainstream of this research is constrained by a dominant epistemic script that is leading to a number of limitations in terms of creating novel theories domestic to our field. Critical realism, as a philosophy of science, is founded on a number of ontological and epistemological precepts which have specific implications for the nature of theory derived and the theorising processes utilised. We have provided a review of IS theory and theorising, and leveraging prior work, outlined the primary challenges to theorising based on the current dominant script. We have presented critical realism as a basis for an alternative script for researchers to adopt that afords the opportunity to generate novel theory, ofers the potential to utilise reference theories in ways that expand opportunities to demonstrate causal logic to explain important system-related phenomena, creates clear links to the real-life experience of practitioners and internalises the IT artefact in deeper and richer ways in our theorising. To demonstrate the potential of a CR-based script, we provided numerous examples from empirical CR research and utilised more in-depth reviews of two recent articles to highlight how this research embodies an approach to research and theoretical outputs that stands in stark contrast to what would be expected from the dominant script.

There have been several criticisms of CR as a research philosophy. However, we contend that many of these criticisms are based either on diferent variations of CR or a comparison to other philosophical orientations. As to the former, CR has developed over three distinct phases: original or basic CR, dialectical CR, and the philosophy of meta-reality. It is likely that there is much to be gained eventually by pursuing research grounded in the subsequent phases (cf. Mingers, 2015b for a brief discussion of the potential benefits of dialectical CR). However, we advocate the use of original CR as a foundation for creative IS research based on its ontological and epistemological capacity for generating causal mechanistic explanations, without the additional philosophical commitments of later phases. As such, this article refers primarily to the original version of CR. As to the latter, CR has been criticised for not being sufficiently diferent from positivism, and lagging behind hermeneutics and interpretivism with regard to socially constructed concepts (Klein, 2004). Others have argued efectively against these criticisms (e.g., Mingers, 2004b; Mutch, 2013). We do not present CR as either the only alternative available, or that a CR-based script is without limitations. Instead, we suggest that in many contexts, CR possesses a suficiently rich ontological and epistemological foundation for developing useful IS theories that difer from those developed under the dominant script as well as other philosophies.

The development of a library of hypothesised and empirically corroborated IS mechanisms has the potential to provide an intra-disciplinary source of reference IS theory for subsequent theoretical and applied research. It also allows us to pursue a deeper understanding of how causal relationships function, particularly those empirical regularities which we may not understand or for which we may not have adequate theoretical sup port. And yet, the specific form of the theoretical statements describing mechanisms is not well established, introducing a social barrier to publication and accept ance within the discipline. We remain confident that as more researchers employ a CR approach and as more CR-based studies make their way through the editorial process, the structure and content of accumulated theoretical knowledge will lead to a wave of original, practical research in the IS field.
