## a b s t r a c t

We develop an analytical model and conduct subsequent empirical analyses using data collected from the online retailers of digital cameras and video games. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that (1) Internet retailers' base prices are positively associated with on-time delivery probability but shipping charges are negatively associated with on-time delivery probability; (2) Internet retailers will increase base prices when they offer free shipping; and (3) Internet retailers' shipping price difference between the standard and expedited shipping modes increases with the shipping time of standard shipping, but decreases with the shipping time of expedited shipping and also the Internet retailer's on-time probability. ## 1. Introduction

The advent of Internet technology has given rise to electronic commerce. A key feature of electronic commerce is the spatial separation between buyers and sellers. As a result, the buyers rely on shipping to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their orders. Hence, how e-tailers should price their shipping services, in addition to their product prices, has invoked a lot of discussion among managers and researchers alike [1,16]. Although economics theory has predicted that prices on the Internet are likely to converge due to reduced search cost [4], especially for homogenous goods, empirical studies have nonetheless found significant price dispersion on the Internet [6–9,12,14,29]. One source of price dispersion comes from the variation in retailers' shipping and handling charges (henceforth referred to as simply shipping charges as in Pan et al. [24]) [3]. Such variation often exists for strategic reasons; that is, how a retailer prices shipping services is a valuable method to differentiate itself from competitors. Appropriate shipping fees are also essential to winning business, given that shipping charges are considered a main reason why online shoppers abandon their shopping carts and discontinue the purchase process [10].

Retailers have several options for designing shipping prices: they can charge nothing (i.e., free-shipping) by subsidizing the shipping cost, they can share some of the costs with customers [12,15], or they can charge shipping fees that are higher than the actual shipping cost in order to make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t (i.e., pro<sup>fi</sup>t-shipping) [27]. Anecdotal evidence has shown that Internet retailers use all of these options. For example, Amazon.com, BN.com, and Buy.com have been practicing a free-shipping policy with a minimum order amount. Even Wal-Mart. com and ebay.com have decided to offer free shipping as their competitive strategies [15,16]. In contrast, CDNow.com exercises a pro<sup>fi</sup>t-shipping policy. At the time of Tedeschi's [30] research, CDNow.com charged a price that was higher than the average cost of shipping a CD: \$3 for the <sup>fi</sup>rst item shipped and \$1 for each additional item. These shipping charges yielded a pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin (15–20%) that was similar to the actual CD sale. There are, of course, other retailers, such as Ashford.com, who charge exactly the cost of shipping in order to gain customers' trust and loyalty [10].

In some cases, when retailers charge shipping fees that are higher than actual shipping costs, they are doing so not to increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts but rather to offset a reduced product price that was designed to help win business. In other words, a retailer may simply transfer part of its pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin from the product price to the shipping charges by charging a shipping fee higher than actual shipping costs [10]. Research has indeed shown that strategically allocating the total price between the product price and its shipping charges can be an effective marketing strategy for Internet retailers [22,23,21].

Therefore, how to determine prices for products and shipping services is a clear challenge for Internet retailers [5,15], especially given that these retailers have limited knowledge about customers' price and time sensitivity. Given this information asymmetry between Internet retailers and customers, whether or not to bundle the pricing of product and shipping together (i.e., the free shipping model), and if it is pro<sup>fi</sup>table not to bundle, how to set prices for product and shipping separately (i.e., the pro<sup>fi</sup>t shipping model)? The objective of this paper is to answer these questions through a game-theoretic model and empirical analysis of data collected from Internet retailers selling digital cameras and video games. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>rst built an analytical model that studied the equilibrium pricing decisions of the base product and shipping schedules of Internet retailers and the purchasing and shipping choices of customers. Considering heterogeneous customers' self-selection in terms of retailers and shipping options as well as the competitor's response, each retailer sets a base price plus a menu price for shipping services of different levels to maximize his own pro<sup>fi</sup>t. We then performed empirical analyses to validate the results from the analytical model, using the data collected from major Internet retailers.

Our major <sup>fi</sup>ndings include: (1) Internet retailers' base prices are positively associated with on-time probability, but shipping charges are negatively associated with on-time probability; (2) Internet retailers will increase base prices when they offer free shipping; and (3) Internet retailers' shipping price premium of expedited shipping over standard shipping increases in the time of the standard shipping mode, but decreases in the time of the expedited shipping mode and the Internet retailer's on-time probability. All of these results are supported by subsequent empirical analysis using data collected from the Internet on the retailers selling digital cameras and video games. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that Internet retailers, to maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, may strategically determine base price and shipping prices, and they also may strategically pace menu pricing for different shipping options.

There is a growing body of literature on Internet retailers' pricing issues [13]. Smith and Brynjolfsson [29] and Brynjolfsson and Smith [9], in analyzing price dispersion online, found that customers are very sensitive to shipping fees and shipping times. Similarly, Lewis et al. [19], using data collected from an online grocery store, showed that shipping fees signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact order incidence as well as basket size. Pan et al. [24] found that the shipping options adopted by retailers contribute to the variation in <sup>fi</sup>rms' prices. Dinlersoz and Li [17], using data collected from the Internet book retailing industry, found that <sup>fi</sup>rms with lower product prices offer higher shipping quality (measured by delay in shipping) and lower shipping fees than <sup>fi</sup>rms with higher product prices. Schindler et al. [27] conducted an experiment to show that customers skeptical of shipping charges prefer a bundled-price format, whereas customers not skeptical of shipping charges prefer a base price and shipping charge format, when an external reference price is available. Also, Kauffman and Lee [22] tracked price changes for 387 books online for 309 days, and found empirical evidence that some Internet retailers use shipping price changes, instead of product price changes, for competitive advantage.

However, the pricing of shipping services remains under-studied. Our paper is different from previous works and makes important contributions in a number of ways. First, we are one of <sup>fi</sup>rst studies that comprehensively capture the dynamics of purchase, shipping, and return between Internet retailers and customers, whereas most of prior paper focused on a segment of the purchase processes. Second, our paper builds a theoretical foundation for Internet retailers' optimal choices of shipping strategy, and provides supporting empirical evidence on how a shipping price scheme can be designed. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from both the analytical model and the empirical analysis reveal that product prices and shipping charges can be strategically manipulated by Internet retailers to their bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Overall, to our best knowledge, our research is one of very few in the literature that studies pricing issues with respect to Internet retailers' product prices and shipping services.

## 2. Analytical model

## 2.1. Modeling framework

We model a business-to-consumer (B2C) setting starting with an Internet retailer interacting with customers. In Section 2.3, we extend the model to a market with two competitive retailers. We consider a single product that is transacted between the retailer and the customers. Due to the nature of Internet retailing, i.e., spatial separation between retailers and customers [19], customers have to wait a certain amount of time before they receive an order placed online. The time to wait depends on the shipping mode customers choose— for example, 3–5 day ground service versus overnight service. Customers, of course, differ in their willingness to wait for product delivery. Hence, we model customers who are heterogeneous in terms of their marginal waiting cost.

Following the Hotelling's [18] location model treatment for heterogeneous customers in the literature (e.g., [26]), we assume that customers differ in their marginal disutility in terms of shipping time $( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ days) θ, which is uniformly distributed on the support of $[ 0 , 1 ] . ^ { 2 }$ The higher the θ, the higher the marginal waiting cost. In other words, customers with higher θ would pay more to have the needed product delivered in a faster fashion than would those with lower θ. The Internet retailer does not know the sensitivity to shipping speed of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c customer, but knows its distribution. We use w to measure the customer's waiting disutility for ordered products to arrive and write w in the following functional form: $w = \theta ( d + f d _ { 0 } )$ , where d is the shipping mode measured by shipping time (i.e., days), f is the probability that the order is delayed, and $d _ { 0 }$ is the expected number of days delayed.<sup>3</sup> We assume there are two modes of shipping, expedited and standard, $d \in \{ d _ { 1 } , d _ { 2 } \}$ , measured in days of delivery. We assume that the expedited mode $\left( d _ { 1 } \right)$ represents faster shipping than standard mode $\left( d _ { 2 } \right)$ plus the expected shipping delay, i.e., $d _ { 1 } + f d _ { 0 } < d _ { 2 } .$ We use $f _ { q } = 1 - f$ to represent the on-time probability for a retailer.

We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally modeled consumers' self-selection behaviors among all possible choices. When an order is delivered later than the estimated shipping window, the customer would have the choice to either keep the product or return it to the retailer. When an order is delayed, the customer will determine whether to accept it when it arrives, or to purchase from another source and return the delayed order when it arrives. When the customer returns it, the customer can purchase from an alternative source (e.g., a physical store). Without loss of generality, we normalize the net utility of this outside option for a customer to zero. The customer will be refunded the base price (i.e., product price) but not the shipping charges, and pay for the return shipping, as Internet retailers typically indicate in their return policies that the retailer is only responsible for defective products. (Note that most Internet retailers have a 30-day return policy. The buyer pays the return shipping fees unless the product is defective. For example, the return policy maintained by Buy.com states, “If you are returning a non-defective product, you must pay return shipping fees.”)

## 2.2. Analysis of the optimal base price and shipping charge menu

In this section, we explore the relationship between optimal base and shipping prices with other factors such as on-time probability, free shipping, and shipping times for expedited and standard shipping modes by providing a number of propositions. These factors potentially drive the Internet retailer's strategic behavior in setting the base price and the menu of shipping prices. We denote on-time probability as $f _ { q } = 1 - f .$

Proposition 1. When $\frac { c _ { 1 } - c _ { 2 } } { d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } } < \frac { \nu + c _ { r } ^ { R } + c _ { r } ^ { C } } { d _ { 0 } } ,$ , the retailer's base price increases with increasing on-time probability, $\begin{array} { r } { . \mathrm { e } . , \frac { \partial p } { \partial f _ { q } } > 0 , } \end{array}$ and vice versa.

Proposition 2. When $\frac { c _ { 1 } - c _ { 2 } } { d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } } < \frac { \nu + c _ { r } ^ { R } + c _ { r } ^ { C } } { d _ { 0 } } ,$ , the retailer's shipping charges decrease with increasing on-time probability, i.e., $\frac { \partial p _ { 1 } } { \partial f _ { q } } = \frac { \partial \hat { p } _ { 2 } } { \partial f _ { q } } { < } 0 ,$ , and vice versa.

Propositions 1 and 2 show that the impacts of on-time probability on retailer's base price and shipping charges depend on the condition about the ratio of the cost difference and shipping days difference, which roughly represents the retailer's marginal cost in reducing shipping time by 1 day. When this ratio is reasonably small, the consumption utility is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large as we assumed earlier in order to ensure nonempty market coverage, and the expected delay in shipping is not too large, the condition $\frac { c _ { 1 } - c _ { 2 } } { d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } } < \frac { v + c _ { r } ^ { R } + c _ { r } ^ { C } } { d _ { 0 } }$ is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Under this condition, Propositions 1 and 2 indicate that a retailer with high on-time probability sets a higher base price and lower shipping charges than one with low on-time probability. They suggest that Internet retailers, dependent on their on-time probability, may choose different pricing strategies: retailers with high on-time probability tend to charge a low shipping price but a high base price, whereas those with low on-time probability tend to attract the customers with a lower base price and then charge a higher shipping price.

Proposition 3. The retailers who offer free shipping will increase base price when $\Psi > 0 ,$ , where $\begin{array} { r } { \Psi { = } ( d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } ) \nu { - } d _ { 0 } c _ { 1 } { - } f \bar { d } _ { 0 } c _ { r } ^ { R } + ( 2 d _ { 2 } - 2 d _ { 1 } + } \end{array}$ $f d _ { 0 } ) c _ { r } ^ { C } - ( d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } - ( 1 - f ) d _ { 0 } ) c _ { 2 } .$

Proposition 3 shows the retailers who offer free shipping may engage in different pricing strategy in setting their base price. When $\Psi > 0 ,$ that is, the consumption utility is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large, the retailer's shipping and restocking costs are low, and customers' returning cost is not too low, the retailers charge a higher base price when they offer free shipping. This result suggests that retailers may use free shipping as a loss leader to attract customers, and in return they use a higher base price to subsidize the free shipping. For example, instead of offering a price combination of \$90 for the product and \$10 for shipping, the retailer might design an alternative combination of prices such as \$100 for the product and free shipping. Doing so would enable the retailer to increase visits from potential buyers, without any loss of pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

Proposition 4. The gap between a retailer's shipping charges for expedited and standard shipping modes increases with the shipping time of standard shipping, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial p _ { s } } { \partial d _ { \gamma } } > 0 , } \end{array}$ but decreases with the shipping time of expedited shipping, i.e., $\frac { \partial p _ { s } } { \partial d _ { 1 } } < 0 .$

Proposition 4 shows how the shipping price gap between expedited and standard shipping modes changes with respect to the shipping times for those modes. In particular, the <sup>fi</sup>rst half of Proposition 4 indicates that with a longer standard shipping time, the retailer may lower the standard shipping price or increase the expedited shipping price. Similarly, the second half of Proposition 4 indicates that with a shorter expedited shipping time, the retailer will charge a higher price premium for expedited shipping over the standard shipping mode.

Proposition 5. When $\frac { c _ { 1 } - c _ { 2 } } { d _ { 2 } - d _ { 1 } } < \frac { \nu + c _ { r } ^ { R } + c _ { r } ^ { C } } { d _ { 0 } } ,$ , the gap between a retailer's shipping charges for expedited and standard shipping modes decreases with on-time probability, i.e., $\frac { \partial p _ { s } } { \partial f _ { q } } < 0$ , and vice versa.

This result suggests that the retailers with high on-time probability offer a smaller gap between shipping options than those with low ontime probability. This result, along with Propositions 1 and 2, describes the different pricing strategies between retailers with varying on-time probability. Retailers with high on-time probability not only tend to charge lower shipping prices, but also a smaller gap between shipping prices of different durations.

## 3.2. Econometric mode

Propositions 1 and 2 unveil the relationship of shipping price and base price versus on-time probability. When the consumption utility is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high, Proposition 1 states that base price increases with on-time probability, whereas Proposition 2 states that shipping price decreases with on-time probability. Models 1 and 2 are constructed to test these propositions, respectively. The base price is de-<sup>fi</sup>ned as the product price offered to the customer, and the retailer's on-time probability is measured by customer ratings regarding “ontime shipping.” Since multiple shipping prices are offered by most Internet retailers for different shipping options, we use all of the shipping options collected, and the shipping price for each individual shipping option collected, as dependent variables, sequentially, in model 2. We further use model 1 to test Proposition 3. Proposition 3 hypothesizes a positive association between base price and free shipping offering.

Model 3 is constructed to test Proposition 4. In line with these propositions, the shipping price gap is expected to show a positive association with the shipping days of the slower shipping option, and a negative association with the shipping days of the faster shipping option. For example, the shipping price gap between shipping option #1 (e.g., overnight service) and shipping option #3 (ground service) is expected to increase with the shipping days of shipping option #3 but to decrease with the shipping days of shipping option #1. We further use model 3 to test Proposition 5, which hypothesizes a negative association between shipping price gaps and on-time probability. The normalized price is therefore the percentage of a particular <sup>fi</sup>rm-product pair price over the average all <sup>fi</sup>rms price the product (i.e., base price) or the shipping service (i.e., shipping price). For example, the base price for product B at <sup>fi</sup>rm A is \$5.5, and the mean price is \$5. The normalized price for product B at <sup>fi</sup>rm A is then 10%, i.e., (5.5 − 5) / 5, meaning that <sup>fi</sup>rm A prices product B 10% higher than the average for all <sup>fi</sup>rms.

At the <sup>fi</sup>rm–product pair level, the variance of base price and shipping price, our variables of interest, may depend on both across-<sup>fi</sup>rm and across-product effects. Hence, the effects from the differences in <sup>fi</sup>rms and in products are carefully controlled. The control variables included in the models include a dummy for physical channel, a dummy for public <sup>fi</sup>rm, and days of operation, for <sup>fi</sup>rm effects, and the category dummy, product rank, and availability for product effects. In particular, a dummy for physical channel vs. pure Internet channel is included because some Internet retailers operate a hybrid distribution (such as Circuit City) whereas others operate a pure Internet storefront (such as Amazon.com). A dummy for public vs. private <sup>fi</sup>rms is included because public <sup>fi</sup>rms may have access to greater <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources and publicity. The number of days of operation for a <sup>fi</sup>rm is included since <sup>fi</sup>rms operating for a longer period of time may achieve a greater scale of economy. The category dummy is included to provide for potential differences between digital cameras and video games. Product rank is included as a control for a product's popularity. Availability is included as a proxy to control whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm exercises drop-shipping for a particular product; previous work has found that longer availability for a product often suggests drop shipment [25]. These control variables are consistent with those used in the literature (e.g., [3,17,24]).

## 3.3. Estimation results

Table 5 presents the results of our estimations for models 1, 2 and 3 using multiple regressions. For model 1, the estimation is performed using the dependent variable of base price. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for on-time probability is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (β=0.21, pb0.05), lending support for Proposition 1, which states that Internet retailers' base price increases with on-time probability. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the free shipping is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (β=0.04, pb0.05), indicating that the offering of free shipping results in a higher base price. This result provides support to Proposition 3.

For model 2, the estimation is performed using the dependent variables of shipping price for shipping option #1, shipping price for shipping option #2, and shipping price for shipping option #3, sequentially. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for on-time probability in all regressions are negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (pb0.001). The negative coef<sup>fi</sup>cients suggest that Internet retailers with higher (lower) on-time probability tend to charge lower (higher) shipping prices. These results are consistent with Proposition 2 which predicts that the relationship should be negative.

For model 3, three multiple regressions were performed using the dependent variables of shipping price gap between shipping options #1 and #2, shipping price gap between shipping options #1 and #3, and shipping price gap between shipping options #2 and #3, respectively. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for shipping time of the faster shipping options (days of shipping for shipping option #1 in the 1st and 2nd regressions, and days of shipping for shipping option #2 in the 3rd regression) are all negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (pb0.001). These results lend support to Proposition 5. Among the 3 coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the slower shipping options, only the one in the estimation of the shipping price gap between shipping options #1 and #2 is positive and significant (pb0.01), lending only partial support to Proposition 4. In addition, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for on-time probability are all negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in model 3 estimation, lending support to Proposition 5.

Some of the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the control variables are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. For example, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for public <sup>fi</sup>rm and for days of operation are positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in the base price equation but negative in the shipping price and shipping gap equations, suggesting that both Internet retailers with longer days of operation and public Internet retailers tend to charge a higher base price but lower shipping prices. They also tend to have lower gaps of shipping prices between various options. The F statistics for all models are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and the adjusted R-squared statistics are between 0.09 and 0.46, indicating a good <sup>fi</sup>t for the regression estimation.

## 4. Discussion

Our analyses shed light on how different Internet retailers determine base price and shipping charges. In particular, we show that Internet retailers with lower on-time probability tend to offer higher shipping charges and lower base prices than Internet retailers with higher on-time probability. This may be due to the fact that those Internet retailers with lower on-time probability are less well known or established (thereby having a smaller advertising budget), so that they have to offer a lower base price to initially attract customers' attention. In the meantime, they are not able to subsidize shipping, therefore they must charge higher shipping prices. This may also represent an attempt by the lower on-time probability Internet retailers to exploit the possibility of pro<sup>fi</sup>ting from charging higher shipping prices that will not be refunded if the product is eventually returned.

Our research also examines the pricing interaction of various shipping options, which has not yet been explored in the literature. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that optimal shipping prices tend to converge (diverge) when the shipping time of these shipping options converges (diverges). The strategic implication of this result is that Internet retailers can manage their bundle of shipping options as a whole to maximize their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. As predicted in Proposition 4, for example, if the Internet retailer decides to increase the shipping time for the slow shipping mode without reducing the corresponding shipping price, the retailer can increase the shipping charges for the fast mode while maintaining the same service level.

Some Internet retailers have been using free shipping as a promotional device to attract Internet traf<sup>fi</sup>c or potential customers [20]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the analytical modeling, which are later supported by the empirical analysis, show that Internet retailers that offer free shipping tend to charge a higher base price. In other words, the <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to use free shipping as a loss leader, to attract more customers and pro<sup>fi</sup>t from selling products. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are in agreement with anecdotes from the trade press. For example, Amazon.com, a leading and reputable Internet retailer, announced that it subsidized over \$30 million in shipping fees due to its promotional shipping policies (e.g., free shipping) in the 4th quarter of 2002, and expected this subsidization to grow to \$100 million in 2003 [31]. The present <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with and extend those from earlier research, showing that Internet retailers may strategically use either free shipping [19] or pro<sup>fi</sup>table shipping [4] to their bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

## 5. Conclusions

Strategically pricing for product and shipping service has become a critical and effective tool for an Internet retailer to differentiate himself from the others, which is necessary due to reduced transaction cost and heightened competition on the Internet [4,11]. In this paper, we present an analytical model and subsequent empirical analyses that shed light on how an Internet retailer can design an optimal combination of base price and shipping price plus an optimal menu scheme of shipping charges and shipping options to gain a competitive edge. We further examine the relationships between optimal shipping charge and various driving factors affecting a retailer's pricing decision for shipping services (e.g., on-time probability, free shipping offering, etc.), as well as the interactions between shipping charges and shipping delays for different shipping options.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that Internet retailers' base prices are positively associated with on-time probability, shipping charges are negatively associated with on-time probability, and that Internet retailers' base prices are positively associated with free shipping offerings. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that Internet retailers' shipping price gap between expedited and standard shipping modes increases with the shipping time of the standard shipping mode, but decreases with the shipping time for expedited shipping. The shipping price gap also decreases with the Internet retailer's on-time probability. All of these results are supported by our empirical analysis using data collected from the Internet retailers of digital camera and video game products. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that Internet retailers, to maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, may strategically choose a combination of base price and shipping prices, and may also strategically pace menu pricing for different shipping options.

Our research contributes to the electronic business literature on Internet pricing in the following ways: (1) by developing an analytical model that comprehensively captures the dynamics of purchase, shipping, and return between Internet retailers and customers, and (2) by empirically validating those results from the analytical model using data collected from Internet retailers selling digital cameras and video games. Our paper builds a theoretical framework for Internet retailers making decisions on the optimal shipping strategy, and provides supporting empirical evidence that Internet retailers strategically design different shipping price schemes. To our best knowledge, our research is one of the few in the literature that study pricing issues with respect to Internet retailers' product prices and shipping services.

The research also provides managerial insights into Internet retailers' strategic behavior on determining different shipping strategies (e.g., free shipping vs. pro<sup>fi</sup>t shipping) and on designing their optimal pricing scheme for shipping. “Good” Internet retailers are more likely to offer a combination of high product price and low shipping charges than are “bad” Internet retailers. When offering free shipping, Internet retailers may be able to subsidize their free shipping service by charging higher prices for the product itself than those when free shipping is not offered. Furthermore, Internet retailers, dependent on their on-time probability, can strategically pace their different menu of shipping time and charges to maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Finally, our research has several limitations that can be addressed in future research. Our model captures the mechanism by which an Internet retailer interacts with heterogeneous customers. Future research can extend our modeling frame to a multi-period dynamic model that can capture the negative impact of shipping delays on future sales. Empirical analysis can also be extended from our cross sectional analysis to longitudinal analysis in order to validate the dynamic model. Another limitation of our research is that the data used to measure Internet retailers' on-time probability comes from customer feedback. More objective variables, such as percentage of ontime shipping, will enrich the analysis. Finally, the shipping practice may be country speci<sup>fi</sup>c. Our study is focused on the practice in the U.S. Future research may expand our models to other countries and examine if our <sup>fi</sup>ndings still hold.
