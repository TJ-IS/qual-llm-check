## Abstract

Software development organizations (SDOs) innovate in diverse ways. In this paper, based on an extensive review of innovation typologies, we develop a theory‐driven typology of SDO innovator classes. We theorize about the driving forces behind these classes, with specific attention given to knowledge‐based dispositions that relate to the SDO's knowledge‐based competencies in technology appropriation, technology generation, and network intensity within ecological niches. Through a survey of 136 SDOs, which adopted Internet technologies during 1995‐2006, we empirically verify that these classes also determine different ways that SDOs innovate around software services they provide to their clients. Our findings suggest that the knowledge‐based dispositions of different SDO innovator classes impact how they innovate. Overall, the study involves theorizing around different types of innovation behaviours and associated knowledge‐based factors that influence the way SDOs innovate.

## KEYWORDS

Internet‐based innovation, IT innovation, latent class clustering, service innovation, software development organizations

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Software development organizations (SDOs)—firms that develop software and provide related services—need to constantly innovate to survive heavy competition in this dynamic industry. The SDO innovation goes beyond the simple adoption of key information technologies, such as Web services, for example, this set of base technologies is widely available to all SDOs. The key locus of competitiveness for SDOs depends not only on the base technology choices they make but also on how they organize processes to leverage these technologies by intensively involving customers in the development process (Rai & Sambamurthy, 2006). Software is a generative, ever‐evolving experience, which is increasingly cocreated with customers—in this regard, software products resemble service offerings as much as they do product offerings (Barrett, Davidson, Prabhu, & Vargo, 2015; Yoo, 2010). Recent research into SDO innovation provides us with new insights into the service dynamics and interdependencies among different forms of innovation (Carlo, Lyytinen, & Rose, 2011; Carlo, Lyytinen, & Rose, 2012; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003a; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003b). Despite multiple calls for research into digital innovation (Li, Su, Zhang, & Mao, 2017; Lyytinen, Yoo, & Boland, 2016; Ojala, 2016; Tilson, Lyytinen, & Sørensen, 2010; Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010), so far, extant studies have not analysed the systematic differences across these innovation classes among sets of SDOs and what might explain these differences.

This gap in existing research is problematic because in most industrial fields, different organizations serve differentiated roles and locate themselves in specific “niches” of an industrial ecosystem (Hannan, Carroll, & Pólos, 2003; Hannan & Freeman, 1977; Zammuto, 1988). Success in those niches is rooted in distinct knowledge‐based capabilitie that influence how organizations are likely to innovate (Kogut & Zander, 1992). These knowledge capabilities are interlinked with organizations and dynamics of their ecological niches resulting in organizational dispositions that recursively reinforce and are reinforced by a firm's position in the ecology (Hannan et al., 2003; Weeks & Galunic, 2003). We refer to these bundles of reproduced capabilities and tendencies as “knowledge dispositions.” Moreover, we argue that different knowledge dispositions will impact how SDOs are likely to innovate. Just because other indus trial fields find diverse niches in their innovative ecosystems, according to their knowledge dispositions, does not necessarily mean that all SDOs will operate in similar niches or just in one niche nor that these niches affect the ways SDOs will innovate over time. They can move from one niche to another. In this paper, we ask 2 questions: (1) Do SDOs generally locate themselves in specific niches, and does the location influence the way these SDOs innovate? (2) When SDOs are located in specific niches, what knowledge dispositions shared across SDOs operating in simila niches influence their innovation behaviours?

To address these 2 questions, we conduct a 2‐step study among 136 SDOs about how they innovated around Internet technologies between 1995 and 2006. To do so, we first classify general forms of innovativeness within a broader notion of organizational innovation. This provides an overview of organizational and IT innovation typologies and helps analyse SDOs regarding to what extent they exhibit similar classes of innovation behaviours. This sets a stage for articulating hypotheses for how different knowledge dispositions explain the innovation variation among identified classes of SDO innovators. By developing and testing hypotheses covering primary knowledge dispositions that explain differing SDO's innovation behaviours within SDO innovation clusters, we begin to formulate ingredient of a theory of knowledge‐based factors that can explain differences in SDO's innovation

## 2 | A TYPOLOGY OF SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT ORGANIZATIONS

The SDOs generally provide software services. They must continually innovate their offerings, because the services underlying these technologies—such as cost, complexity, use settings, and approaches to deliver—perpetually change The SDOs typically provide a range of products such as custom development services, shrink‐wrapped software products (eg, apps), and software‐as‐service offerings (cloud). The SDOs deploy these services using a variety of technologies available in the environment, leveraging their existing capabilities, and honing their competitive strate gies—such as market selection. Over time, these choices result in realized strategies, which largely dictate what sort of software and hardware technologies the SDOs are likely to acquire and use, what types of computational solutions they are likely to identify and seek to deploy, and which types of software processes they are likely to engage. By constraining these innovation choices over time to specific sets or configurations, SDOs can ultimately reduce the nearly limitless variety of innovation strategies available to them down to a set of feasible ones (Miller. 1986). There: fore, it is likely that we can find types or classes of SDOs, which share similar innovation characteristics based on th history of environmental choices and their implementations. However, to date, there is no research specificall classifying SDO innovation behaviours in such a way, although there is an extensive research stream in industrial organizations in general focused on this topic.

Researchers, however, must be careful when seeking to apply findings from nonsoftware industries directly to software development. Software represents a different sort of product, and the dynamics and economics of offering it are quite different from other industries, Some key differences between software and industries focused on tangible products include (1) software is always evolving and emergent—as a result of co‐evolving collaborations between the SDO and its customers; (2) software is intangible with minimal fixed production assets such that software has near zero marginal cost for reproduction; (3) software is now, due to virtualization and “the cloud,” as much a service offer ing as a product. Consequently, research on service innovation has long argued that service firms innovate differentl from firms focused on manufacturing traditional, tangible, industrial products (Barras, 1986; Barrett et al., 2015). Whether there are distinct classes of innovators among SDOs is, however, an empirical question. To investigate whether SDOs cluster to broadly similar classes of innovators as observed in other industries and related organiza tions, we next briefly review the literature on industrial organization and innovation and related typologies of innova tors. We then discuss whether we can find similar phenomena among SDOs.

## 2.1 | Different types of innovators in an industry

Environmental factors in any industry interact with an organization's internal factors, whereby organizations searc and locate themselves within niches within an industrial ecosystem (Miles, Snow, Meyer, & Coleman, 1978). Environmental factors include volatility, expectations and pressure of the marketplace, the firm's size, key relationships within the organization, path dependency (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2010), mutual dependencies between key strategic factors and thrusts (Miller, 1986), and mimetic and normative pressures (DiMaggio & Powell 1983). In general, such factors build up an organization's “selection environment” (Nelson & Winter, 1977). Alternative sets of selection constraints also arise because of internal factors such as imprinting (Marquis & Tilcsik, 2013) and cog nitive inertia that shapes the firm's intrinsic orientation toward innovation. Overall, both environmental factors and internal constraints constantly shape the firm's ongoing innovation decisions, which, down the road, will further limit or enable its future choices (de Jong & Marsili, 2006; Evangelista, 2000; Klevorick, Levin, Nelson, & Winter, 1995; Pavitt, 1984). Firm‐level innovation thus results partially from the current structure, shape, and dynamics of the surrounding industrial ecology and partially from the competencies and choices internal to the organization (Wozniak 1993). Both external factors and internal constraints will vary over time and across contexts because of noise, random events, and individual agency.

In sum, given the variation in their environment, history, and emerging strategic choices and orientations, organi zations are highly unlikely to provide exactly similar innovations to their clients. Ongoing technological or market innovation will also constantly generate seeds of divergence in these sets. Yet, over time, groups of organizations are likely to exhibit higher similarity in the set of innovations offered to their clients, which reflect the potential space for inno vation provided by their current ecological niche. The innovation strategies pursued by these groups are, with high likelihood, relatively similar because of isomorphic environments (Reger & Huff, 1993; Robinson & Pearce, 1988). In any population of organizations, we would expect this population to fall into a few classes that follow generally similar innovation strategies. One approach that researchers have adopted to identify the presence of such systematic strategies has been to create theoretically grounded typologies that cluster organizations into separate innovator classes based on common shared innovation indicators and characteristics

## 3 | TOWARDS AN SDO INNOVATOR TYPOLOGY

## 3.1 | Innovator typologies

Typologies are fundamental to any scientific endeavour. The first grand typology was Linnaeus' systematic classifica tion of plants, which laid the foundations for botanical science and later for Darwinian evolutionary theory. It later on also led to the emergence of modern biology and genetics. Typologies seek to reduce the complexity of the studied phenomenon to a few essential categories of phenomena. This enables explanation and prediction of the phenomenon based on the presence of a select set of systematic indicators for each category established for the phenomenon (de Jong & Marsili, 2006, p. 214). For example, based on certain indicators of plants, we can say how they will repro duce. By their very nature, typologies offer an efficient form of making generalizable statements about the phenom ena found in a selected domain (Bacharach, 1989; Doty & Glick, 1994). Not surprisingly, innovation typologies have been proposed in most fields in management study including organization theory (eg, de Jong & Marsili, 2006 Hollenstein, 2003; Khan & Manopichetwattana, 1989; Rai, Tang, Brown, & Keil, 2006), information systems (Ra et al., 2006), operations (Khan & Manopichetwattana, 1989), entrepreneurship (de Jong & Marsili, 2006), and service science (Hollenstein, 2003). Empirically grounded typologies have also been offered as a way to understand and explain innovation behaviours over time (Bacharach, 1989; de Jong & Marsili, 2006) and to build generalizable innovation theories (de Jong & Marsili, 2006).

To formulate a typology of SDO innovations, we next put forward a relatively simple and generic classification. The classification draws upon and synthesizes a wide array of available typologies of organizational innovation. To develop this typology, we performed a systematic literature search in EBSCO and ABI‐INFORM databases by using keywords like “innovator types,” “innovator classification,” “innovation classification,” “innovation typology,” “innova tion patterns,” and so on. Our goal was to identify articles that have suggested criteria for creating specific innovator typologies and used them for predicting innovation behaviours. The search resulted in 18 articles listed and summa rized in Appendix A. Overall, the literature analysis identified several common distinct identifiers to distinguish between innovating organizations and their behaviours. Using this synthesis as a baseline, we next elicited a typology of SDO innovators that consists of 3 types of SDO innovators we refer to as broad, selective, and minimal innovators We summarize these findings in Table 1. Overall, this typology reflects SDO's typical relationships with their environment given a specific class of service that the SDO provides to its clients. Accordingly, per Table 1, broad, selective, and minimal SDOs will either—based on their dominant innovation strategies—innovate across a wide array of domains, focus on a niche domain, or only a few domains, respectively. At the same time, any determination of innovation types among a given set of SDOs is relativistic—innovators are broad, minimal, or selective only when compared to others in their current industry setting This relative interpretation has its roots in the information systems literature and existing typologies, which are consistent with our typology—although they often come up with different industry‐specific categories. For example, Ra et al. (2006) studied assimilation patterns of procurement systems and identified broad innovators with 4 types of systems (electronic ordering, payment, catalogue, and auction), selective innovators with some but not all 4 systems (focused niche and asymmetric deployment), as well as minimal innovators (nondeployment). In another study, de Jong and Marsili (2006) categorized 1234 small firms according to 6 dimensions of innovation (output, input, sources, management, planning, and orientation) and identified 2 types of broad innovators (resource intensive and science‐based), a class of selective innovator (specialized), and a class of minimal innovators (supplier‐dominated). Thus, the final iden tification of the 3 classes is relative to the potential level of innovation in the field and the specificity of the studied context.

Given a population of SDOs, which face new technological innovations, we expect these 3 SDO classes to inno vate differently in that setting even given relatively similar environmental opportunities. Consequently, each SDO that follows a broad, minimal, or selective innovation strategy can be seen to possess a distinct configuration of innovation capabilities (de Jong & Marsili, 2006; Evangelista, 2000; Pavitt, 1984), which, as a whole, influences the likelihood that the SDO will innovate in ways such that its survival likelihood in its ecological “niche” is increased (Carroll, 1985). In the context of SDOs, such capabilities are primarily knowledge‐based (Carlo et al., 2011), path‐dependent, and driven by the firm's behavioural orientations expressed towards the environment. Per path‐dependency, continued success acquired through innovation within a given ecological niche reinforces the maintenance and selection of current, specific, and invested knowledge‐based capabilities and related knowledge and behavioural commitments (Wozniak 1993). Next, we discuss dominant innovation behaviours associated with each class, which also differentiate the goals and reasons why the SDO innovate when compared to other classes. We refer to these behaviours as knowledge dis positions since they identify key knowledge capabilities and related behavioural orientations that underlie SDO innovation activities.

## 3.2 | Drivers of SDO innovation: knowledge dispositions

Alternative sets of behaviours, capabilities, and cultural modes of thought are associated with different classes o firms; they reflect firms' preferences and dispositions that enable the firms to carve out and succeed through niches within their industrial ecosystems (Hannan et al., 2003). The SDO innovation behaviours can thus be expected to reflect relatively stable accumulated and invested cognitive and decision‐making capabilities, which shape SDO's ongoing responses to environmental variation and change. The SDOs are likely to continue to innovate—in terms of their knowledge‐search patterns, ways of using knowledge, and how it is applied—in ways that cohere with their past innovation activities. One reason for the coherence is that innovation draws primarily upon capabilities and learning garnered through past experience (Koberg, Detienne, & Heppard, 2003). Similar to Nelson and Winter's (1977) and de Jong and Marsili's (2006) observations that organizational adaptive behaviours vary by industry or by domain, variation in SDO innovation behaviours is likely to be influenced by factors that determine the knowledge dispositions of selected SDOs—present cognitive, structural and dynamic decision-related elements that influence the innovation of those SDOs (de Jong & Marsili, 2006; Hollenstein, 2003; Khan & Manopichetwattana, 1989; Rai et al., 2006). We labe this bundle of preferences, habitual and established behaviours, and cognitive modes of thought as SDO's knowledge dispositions.

Although an organization's innovation outcomes may appear to change dramatically over time, we posit that their dominant knowledge dispositions persist over significant durées. At least 2 factors explain the persistence and com plement each other (Mishra & Agarwal, 2010). The first involves the nature of organizational knowledge or capabili ties, and the second involves organizational culture. Organizational knowledge captures the coordination, communication, and learning capabilities of an organization that go beyond the knowledge of any particular individua (Kogut & Zander, 1992; Kogut & Zander, 1996). An innovation always involves a combination of new ways of doing things that need to be integrated with present capabilities. Therefore, an organization's capacity to innovate depend also on its current knowledge capabilities (Garud, Tuertscher, & Van de Ven, 2013; Zahra & George, 2002). Since inno vation is always a recombination (firms evolve through a “knowledge synthesis” of what they currently know and th new capabilities), any innovation is subject to the effects of existing ways of thinking (Garud et al., 2013). Essentially there are significant interpretive barriers to different forms of innovation (Dougherty, 1992). Existing routines and structures limit the degrees of freedom that organizations can pursue in innovating. Thus, knowledge disposition reflect the variety and quality of pre‐existing organizational knowledge, which influences the scope within which a organization is likely to innovate

Knowledge dispositions also reflect “cultural modes of thought,” which are deeply rooted ideas, values, assumptions, interpretations, and tacit knowledge within the organization (Weeks & Galunic. 2003). This view of knowledge dispositions emphasizes the salience of organizational culture in shaping innovation and draws upon the notion of epi stemic stance: “an attitude that organizational actors collectively enact in pursuing knowledge” (Fayard, Gkeredakis, & Levina, 2016, p. 302). An epistemic stance forms one element of an organization's culture and impacts the ways an organization frames and pursues opportunities to innovate. In general, organizations frame innovations they pursue in ways that are consistent with their present and persistent epistemic stance (Fayard et al., 2016). Thus, both existing organizational capabilities and culture contribute to an organization's knowledge dispositions and impact the organization's ability to innovate. As shown in Table 2, we next review 3 salient knowledge dispositions associated SDO innovativeness. Each of them is related to 2 critical innovation factors that characterize the organization's knowl edge capabilities and behaviours (Carlo et al., 2011).

In an innovation context, typical to SDO‐based innovation knowledge dispositions will manifest in a variety of ways. Often, knowledge dispositions relate to the organization's modes of learning. These are typically conceived as dualisms in the pertinent literature. Examples of such dualisms include internal generation of industry‐leading innovation versus response to innovations to keep up with the industry (technology push vs technology pull [Zmud, 1984]); learning associated with local, focused search versus distal, diverse search (exploitation vs exploration [March, 1991]); or pioneering technical knowledge versus adopting well‐established technical knowledge (early vs late adopter [Wozniak, 1993]). We summarize these general ways of thinking about technological knowledge associated with inno vation in terms of 3 categories we call technology generation, technology appropriation, and network intensity dispositions.

Technology generation involves SDOs' knowledge dispositions associated with promoting innovation internall (“push”). Technology generation involves those SDOs that creatively combine internal technologies and knowledge to generate novel and original technical innovations (Arthur, 2009). Two components typically associated with the capability for technology generation are the knowledge depth of the organization within particular technology domains and its experimental practices. Radical organizational innovativeness has long been associated with the dept of an organizations knowledge (Dewar & Dutton, 1986). Knowledge depth and related concentration of knowledg produce a “greenhouse effect for the development and support of new ideas” (Dewar & Dutton, 1986, p. 1424). Such concentration of knowledge is the precondition for the generation of truly novel contributions (Ettlie et al., 1984). Deep knowledge about the specific market of the innovation, in particular, enables SDOs to generate novel innovations, and such deep knowledge is most effective when combined with experimental organizational practices (Zhou & Li, 2012). Experimentation involves applying knowledge to the generation of innovations and then testing them in an iterative fashion (Thomke. 1998). Experimentation, in general, expands SDOs' capacities for generating innova tion (Carlo et al., 2012). Continuous iteration has also been found fundamental to generative forms of software inno vation (Berente & Lyytinen, 2005).

Technology appropriation captures knowledge dispositions associated with externally focused assimilative inno vating (“pull”). Technology appropriation is concerned with the grafting of innovations generated elsewhere, and focused on the assimilation of innovations and related knowledge within the organization (Attewell, 1992). The assim ilation of innovations requires specific types of organizational learning, and those organizations that have less to learn will have better assimilation capabilities and outcomes (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). In other words, the appropriatio of technology calls for a capacity to assimilate, or “absorb,” the innovation knowledge. Such capability comes mostly from a broad‐based knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Zahra & George, 2002). In this regard, knowledge breadt strengthens the SDO's absorptive capacity to appropriate new technologies (Fichman & Kemerer, 1997; Grant, 1996). However, before a firm can appropriate a technology, it must be sensed in the environment (Zahra & George, 2002). Particularly in fast‐paced environments, organizations that successfully innovate can maintain a wider variety of activ ities to sense their environment. In this regard, the greater the variety, the better they innovate (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1998). Thus, SDOs can use a diverse set of tactics to sense their environments and respond to presented opportunities to innovate (Roberts & Grover, 2012).

Network intensity is a knowledge disposition that looks outward from the organization. However, it does not orient to the technology, but rather to the surrounding social network of an organization that helps to garner complementarities necessary to innovate successfully. Although a bulk of research in the past on technology innovation has focused on the modes through which organizations learn about and appropriate focal technologies, in the past, decade innovation research has started to emphasize that innovating organizations need to also garner social competencies to relate to other organizations and stakeholders. This helps coordinate and leverage heterogeneous knowledge residing in an innovation network (Brusoni, Prencipe, & Pavitt, 2001: Ritter & Gemünden, 2003) necessary to innovate successfully. We refer to this dimension of knowledge dispositions as network intensity—the degree to which SDOs can communicate, collaborate, and leverage knowledge within their innovation network. The SDOs with extensive and intense relationships with customers and other stakeholders are likely to innovate more and more suc: cessfully; they can garner more heterogeneous knowledge and also render such knowledge more fluid and appropri ate for mobilization and use. Therefore, much of the recent innovation research has focused on aspects that increase knowledge identification and fluidity within networks including the impact of an organization's position and its forms of social interplay (Ahuja, 2000; Obstfeld, 2005; Tuomi, 2002; Van de Ven, 2005). This pinpoints the constant need for SDOs to find a fit among their stakeholders who are likely to benefit or drive the firm's innovation including, for example, clients (Srinivasan et al., 2002a). Carlo et al. (2011) indicate that a larger number of knowledge linkages with a higher intensity to external organizations form an important dimension of social interplay that drives SDO innovation. In the case of SDOs, customer relationships are particularly important, because of the market‐oriented, service focused nature of many SDOs (Damanpour, 1991; Srinivasan, Lilien, & Rangaswamy, 2002b)

Overall, innovation preferences associated with social learning in addition to modes of learning dictate experi ences around technology. By emphasizing both the technological (technology appropriation and generation) element and social (network) elements in SDOs' knowledge dispositions, we can address both what and who of organizationa learning that influence innovation outcomes. In total. these knowledge dispositions are likely to influence the SDO's decisions to engage in one kind of software innovation and ignore or neglect other kinds. For example, a member of an SDO class that innovates broadly is likely to emphasize interorganizational knowledge coordination more strongly than members in other SDO classes (Brusoni et al., 2001). Broad innovators are also likely to innovate early and broadly and read “weak” technology signals in search for opportunities. To this end, broad innovators have garnered wide‐ranging search capabilities that are less limiting (internally) as to the type of knowledge they will use for innova tion. Broad innovators can take advantage of a broader “menu” (Teece, Pisano, & Shuen, 1997) of environmenta opportunities and will work more intensely with key stakeholders to prioritize and coordinate their innovation activity. Similarly, a selective SDO provided with a limited menu of opportunities because of its limited appropriation capabilities will focus on a problem‐driven search. Selective SDOs are therefore less affected by environmental conditions (including customer pressure) and, in contrast, drive their innovation primarily through active experimentation and by seeking to lead the field in a specific niche (Zammuto, 1988).

## 4 | THEORY DEVELOPMENT

We will next formulate hypotheses across the 3 SDO innovator classes, which theorize about differences of the effects of knowledge dispositions on innovation levels (ie, how much they innovate) across classes.

## 4.1 | H1: Baseline: innovativeness and relative knowledge dispositions

Before we look at the effects of knowledge dispositions for each type of innovator, it is important to first establish a baseline for innovation levels. Overall, we expect those SDOs who are broad innovators to show a greater presence of each knowledge disposition than the other 2 classes. This is primarily because those organizations that have a culture of widespread innovation need to simultaneously emphasize knowledge breadth, knowledge depth, and actively leverage their knowledge network (Garud et al., 2013). This view is consistent with the general innovation literature on classes of innovators. Broad innovators to place greater emphasis on each knowledge disposition, ie they demon strate higher sensitivity to customer pressure and to emerging technologies, have higher knowledge diversity and depth, engage in higher levels of experimentation, and have denser knowledge linkages. As a result, they will respond more strongly to the presence of new technology—resulting in broader deployment of innovations (Garrison, 2009) Accordingly, they will perceive software innovations to have a more strategic role (fit) with their clients' needs—this justifies expenditure on resources to explore broadly (Damanpour, Walker, & Avellaneda, 2009).

Organizations with greater depth and breadth of knowledge—and associated practices including experimentation and active sensing of opportunities—will innovate more than firms with weaker knowledge dispositions (Benner & Tushman, 2003: Zhou & Li 2012). Furthermore, as de Jong and Marsili, (2006) found, high yalues across the board correlate with being highly connected to other innovation sources (such as universities). Those who innovate more are more likely to be disposed to working with their networks (Garud et al., 2013) and to be more responsive to their customers (Zmud, 1984). Those SDOs that innovate selectively will therefore exhibit less of each knowledge disposi tion, and minimal innovators will exhibit the least. This leads to our first baseline hypothesis.

H1 Broad innovators will exhibit higher values in each of the knowledge dispositions (H1a); selective inno vators will exhibit lower values in each of the knowledge dispositions (H1b); and minimal innovators will exhibit the lowest values in each of the knowledge disposition (H1c).

It is important to note what the hypothesis does not imply whether the different dispositions matter more in dif ferent classes of SDO innovators. For example, could one knowledge disposition differentiate broad innovators, whereas another knowledge disposition differentiate selective innovators? ## 4.2 | H2: Differential impact of knowledge dispositions for different SDO classes

Software is a dynamic business. Therefore, all SDOs will innovate in some regards. At the same time, they hold various positions in the industrial ecology and are thus subject to different innovation needs. Therefore, alternative knowl edge dispositions imply different things to different classes of SDOs, and different knowledge dispositions will have a larger impact on the level of innovativeness for some class of SDOs. Figure 1 summarizes the knowledge disposi tions that matter most for each SDO class and their influence on overall innovativeness

Overall, the impact of separate knowledge dispositions on their innovative outcomes should be in relation to the other firms in the same class. Broad innovators should be compared with other broad innovators to understand how knowledge dispositions will drive their relative innovativeness. Since all SDOs are also innovators, the question now becomes as follows: In different niches, specific knowledge dispositions will become a differentiating driver of innovation. In other words, different modes of learning and different knowledge competencies matter in different industria contexts. Technologically oriented modes of learning enable appropriating and generating technological innovations from within, Network competencies help innovate through coordinating knowledge and skills with stakeholders. There: fore, the relationship between knowledge factors and innovation outcomes will be different for each SDO innovato class. Consequently, the effects of knowledge acquisition and deployment behaviours will be different and determined by the SDO's current niche. 4.2 summarizes the overall logic for the hypothesis, which will be detailed next.<sup>2</sup>

Minimal innovators are likely to fill a low‐cost or efficiency‐oriented niche (Zammuto, 1988). Such organizations wait and follow the broader and selective innovators only after the technologies have been established (Zammuto, 1988). Therefore, customer pressure and relations with other stakeholders, although certainly important, will likely receive a more conservative response from minimal innovators. Minimal innovators do not drive innovations; they appropriate them, and therefore, they actively maintain some level of knowledge breadth. Further, since these SDOs operate still in a dynamic environment, they do not want to be left behind with totally outdated technologies. Therefore, minimal innovators will mostly engage in some technology sensing to keep pace with a minimal level of technological innovation and adjust their service offerings to keep abreast with the ongoing technological and market change. The adoption of such technologies, however, is mostly dictated by competitive necessity. Thus, the innovative activities of this SDO innovator class will be most impacted by technology appropriation factors including sensing (Evangelista, 2000). Accordingly, technology appropriation‐related dispositions will have a stronger impact on innovativeness for the minimal innovator SDOs. Those that have broader knowledge and have superior practices for sensing technologies will have an advantage over other minimal innovators in terms of their level of innovation

Selective innovators fill a specialty niche (Hannan et al., 2003). Selective, niche SDOs must acquire deep knowl edge in particular domains and stay ahead of the competition in these domains (Zammuto, 1988). To become or remain a market leader, selective innovators will thus seek to create next generation technologies for their customers, rather than simply trying to respond to customer pressure or follow trends in the technology market (Abernathy & Clark, 1985; Hollenstein, 2003). Therefore, they are likely to innovate through garnering specialized in‐depth knowledge and engage internally in experimentation. Selective innovators that have such highly specialized knowledge and iterative practices will be more successful in generating innovations and will have an advantage in terms of their inno vativeness in their class. These SDOs depend on specific, unique, and invested capabilities, and therefore, they hunt selectively within the market for knowledge, which can strengthen their core competencies (Zammuto, 1988). Selective innovators will thus continually push a particular technological frontier. This entails doing intensive—potentiall trailblazing—innovative work. When there is no other organization to follow or to learn from, trial‐and‐error learning and experimentation take the front seat (Thomke, 1998). Thus, the level of innovation among the selective SDOs can be best predicted by generative factors: knowledge depth and experimentation. Accordingly, these generative factors will be weaker predictors for the broad and minimal innovators.

Broad innovators are known for their expansiveness in innovation, and they often self‐identify their firm wit innovativeness (Arvanitis & Hollenstein, 1998; Leiponen & Drejer, 2007). Miles et al. (1978) refer to broad innovators as “prospectors”—those who like to move early and capitalize on innovation quickly. These firms also do so broadly i that they are in a “continuous state of development”:

the Prospector's prime capability is that of finding and exploiting new product and market opportunities.…

the Prospector's domain is usually broad and in a continuous state of development…. the Prospector must

develop and maintain the capacity to survey a wide range of environmental conditions, trends, and events

… scan the environment for potential opportunities” (p. 551‐552).

As such, broad innovators are likely to respond to customer pressure quickly and will innovate to foster capabilities that draw upon linkages with external knowledge resources (Ritter & Gemünden, 2003; Teece et al., 1997). Becaus they are broad innovators, they do not necessarily limit themselves to leading the field in a select set of technologies (Leiponen & Drejer, 2007). Broad innovators all possess technology generation and appropriation knowledge disposi tions—it is what they must do to innovate all the time. However, the most innovative will be those SDOs who are able to garner wider stakeholder engagement that garners fluid knowledge flows. Broad-innovating SDOs that can acguire routines to effectively respond to new customer pressures, to observe latent customer needs and to capitalize on coor dinating related heterogeneous knowledge, will innovate more effectively (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). To wit, the mos important factors affecting the level of innovation for broad innovators are network intensity factors—customer pres sure and knowledge linkages—given the significant presence of other innovation factors. Accordingly, these factors wil be weaker predictors of innovativeness for the selective and the minimal innovators. We will posit that

H2 The positive relationship between innovation factors and innovation level will be moderated by SDO class such that:

H2a : for minimal innovators, innovation level will be best explained by technology appropriation‐related variables.

H2b : for selective innovators, innovation level will be best explained by technology generation‐related variables.

H2c : for broad innovators, innovation level will be best explained by network intensity‐related variables.

## 5 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

## 5.1 | Survey design and sample population

To validate the proposed model, we used a secondary data set originally collected by Carlo et al. (2012). They sent 2 rounds of paper‐based surveys to people with titles such as CEO, CIO, CTO, president, chairman, owner, principal, or vice president of R&D at software firms and IT departments that were engaged in innovating with Internet‐based software technologies. These respondents were deemed to be the key decision‐makers in technology and market choices and most qualified in their company to provide information about the firm's experience with Internet comput ing. They received 141 completed surveys from the 710 firms, with an acceptable response rate (20%), given that th survey was voluntary and involved top management (Stimpert, 1992). Of the participating companies, 5 provided unusable responses. Thus, the final sample included 136 valid responses

For the 136 SDOs in the dataset, Internet‐based software was either the most or the only kind of software they developed for their clients. Carlo et al. (2012) chose Internet computing for 3 reasons: (1) a transformative innova: tion, (2) analytically and empirically validated to trigger innovations within SDOs across the 3 innovation types (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003a), and (3) a recent enough phenomenon at the time of data collection to minimize hindsight and recall bias. The sampled SDOs included mostly small software firms, which are appropriate for our research for 3 reasons. First, we needed a sufficiently large<sup>3</sup> sample size for testing variations across innovation clusters and to validly identify such clusters. This was more realistic by sampling among smaller SDOs. Second, the theoretica model is more plausible for SDOs that develop software products for external clients, and this is more typical among small software firms. Finally, 2 US‐based software associations offered the researchers access (eg, use of their logos and joint seminars) to their member organizations, which were almost unilaterally small SDOs, which helped boost response rate.

The 6 knowledge disposition‐related factors are selected from Carlo et al. (2012). We deploy all 5 factors from Carl et al. (2012) in addition to their control variable (customer pressure). Thus, we operationalize those constructs as did Carlo et al. (2012), and therefore, we do not repeat what was established in that study with regards to operationalization and validation. Unique to our study, however, is the level of innovation, measured by the number of Web‐based innovations (service, base, and process) developed and/or adopted<sup>4</sup> by the SDOs from 1995 to 2006. Level of innovation consists of a potential of 31 software innovations listed in Carlo et al. (2012)

## 5.3 | Clustering and hypothesis testing approach

To detect the presence broad, minimal, and selective service innovators among the sample, we used latent class cluster analysis (Vermunt & Magidson, 2002). This technique is an advanced Q‐factoring technique, where data points are clustered according to the range of values on a select set of variables, in contrast to grouping the variables as in the typical R‐factoring techniques (Hair, Black, Babin, & Anderson, 2010). Widely used methods for Q‐factoring are dis criminant analysis and cluster analysis (Hair et al., 2010). Latent class clustering—also known as finite mixture model ling—belongs to a class of relatively new statistical methods for forming a latent profile (ie, a class or cluster) based on a set of indicators and their values. Such latent classes are viewed as unobservable (latent) subgroups or segments within a sample. Cases within the same latent class are homogeneous on certain indicators, and cases in different latent classes differ statistically on those indicators. One main difference between latent class clustering and traditional clustering methods, such as K‐means clustering, is that the latent class clustering technique involves postulating an a priori model (specification), and thereby, it assumes a model of probable distributions between clusters based on the set of indicators (Vermunt & Magidson, 2002). In contrast, traditional clustering methods are totally inductive and, therefore, offer no fit statistics to evaluate the quality of the clustering results. In this regard, the latent class clustering resembles confirmatory factor analysis in R‐factoring techniques (Hair et al., 2010). Consequently, the test statistics offered by the technigue can be used to determine the goodness of fit between the postulated model and the data —in essence, this indicates the degree to which the resultant set of clusters are an accurate representation of the existing correlations in the data set. Latent class clustering also overcomes limitations of scaling and distribution inherent in traditional clustering techniques (Vermunt & Magidson, 2002)

For latent class clustering, we used a software package called Latent Gold<sup>5</sup> (Vermunt & Magidson, 2005). This is a relatively recent tool for conducting latent class clustering analyses and has been mainly used in other fields such as psychology (Hardie, Critchley, & Morris, 2006), marketing (Bijmolt, Paas, & Vermunt, 2004), education (DeCarlo, 2005), and medicine (Bernstein, Bradley, & Zarich, 2002). It is particularly useful in determining whether underlying (latent) profiles exist when considering a select set of indicators. For example, in our case, we searched for 3 genera classes of software innovators and we used as indicators the software (services) they produced.

We guided our cluster analysis with the a priori intent to find a 3‐cluster model (broad, selective, and minimal). To identify the optimal set of indicators for a 3‐cluster model, we used the 14 service innovations<sup>6</sup> identified in the survey as our base model in Latent Gold: we then removed one innovation at a time based on the $R ^ { 2 }$ contribution for that innovation (ie, to what extent does this innovation help explain the cluster structure?)—removing the innovation with the lowest $R ^ { 2 } .$ . We repeated this process until we reached a cluster model that maximized the P value for the chisquare fit statistic. Our final cluster model included 6 of the 14 service innovations (see Figure 2) and had excellent fit (P value for the $\mathsf { X } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 9 )$ . In Figure 2, the x‐axis is a list of the 6 service innovations used for clustering. The horizontal lines represent the 3 clusters. The y‐axis is the percent likelihood that an innovation will be adopted/innovated For example, a broad SDO is 100% likely to innovate with intranets, whereas a minimal SDO is very unlikely to do s (around 5%).

These 6 innovations represent the (statistically) optimal subset of innovations for distinguishing between clusters that innovate on services broadly, minimally, and selectively. To validate these extracted clusters, we conducted a post‐hoc analysis of each cluster to determine the percent of cluster members (SDOs) that innovated with each of the 31 innovations. ## 6 | DISCUSSION

In their seminal work, Hannan and Freeman (1977) suggest that organizations unintentionally develop a sort of divi sion of labour within an industry, because the managerial decisions made in the past, in combination with environmen tal factors, place constraints on organizations and their behaviours. Thus, in a given ecology, there will be a limiteo number of classes of organizations, and the organizations within a particular class will be isomorphic (Hannan & Free man, 1977). Our study demonstrates how this insight translates to the study of contemporary SDOs and their innovation behaviours. Using the extant literature on innovator typologies as a starting point, we identify 3 classes amon SDOs—broad, minimal, and selective service innovators. We tested 3 sets of hypotheses regarding the differences in innovation behaviours across these classes using data from 136 SDOs that had actively innovated with Internet tech nologies. Our findings support our primary arguments of our theorizing. Based on our findings, we next draw severa implications for future research on SDOs

In this study, we leverage a generalized typology of SDO service innovators. Our specific contributions involve theorizing around the ways in which different SDO innovator classes appear to innovate differently. In doing so, we contribute to the extant research on innovation types and strategies (Carlo et al., 2011; Damanpour & Evan, 1984; Grover, Fiedler, & Teng, 1997; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003a; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003b; Swanson, 1994), as well as innovating organization typologies (de Jong & Marsili, 2006; Hollenstein, 2003; Khan & Manopichetwattana, 1989; Nelson & Winter, 1977; Rai et al., 2006). More importantly, our findings indicate that not only do classes of software innovation matter (Grover et al., 1997; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003b; Swanson, 1994) but so do classes of software servic innovators and that these 2 are likely to interact in complicated ways

Notably, through our literature search, we observed that no existing study has developed innovator typologies for SDOs. To our knowledge, this article is the first one to do so. Most typologies are too broad and seek to cover multiple industries, rather than focus on a single and specialized industry, such as SDOs, with unigue characteristics. In this regard, our study is also fairly unique. Lastly, only about half of the studies we examined extracted clusters of observations using statistical approaches, and none of these used extracted clusters to test moderated hypotheses between predictor and outcome variables as we have done. Thus, our study paves the way for more rigorous future studies on organizational typologies and increasingly accurate mid‐range theorizing. It provides an example of a feasible approach to use the extracted clusters to better explain specific predictions of organizational innovation. While typologies are useful in their own right (ie, they provide intellectual structure and generalizations to a previously amorphous and random body of things), the proposed typologies do not ultimately live up to their potential if the cannot be further used for better understanding of the underlying phenomena. In our case, the theory of dispositions achieves this.

Overall our study lays a foundation for more nuanced research of typologies of innovating SDOs, Specifically, we found that there are indeed, broad, minimal, and selective SDO innovators, as proposed (implicitly) by virtually every innovator typology in the past (de Jong & Marsili, 2006; Hollenstein, 2003; Khan & Manopichetwattana, 1989; Nelson & Winter, 1977; Rai et al., 2006). Our findings suggest that observed effects in the whole adopter population are not necessarily present within specific classes of SDOs (Carlo et al., 2011). Classes of innovators are thus of critical impor tance if we want to truly understand the impact of specific innovation factors and how they are likely to shape SDC innovation decisions in a specific industrial context. While there may only be one kind of broad and one kind of min imal innovator (by definition, there should not be much variance in these classes), there is likely significant variation among a host of selective innovators, just as there is variation in selective behaviours based on preference and strat egy. Future research may benefit, therefore, by more deeply exploring selective innovators.

Perhaps the most interesting finding from this research involves relating the 3 types of innovators to the 3 genera knowledge dispositions (technology appropriation, technology generation, and network intensity). We find some support for our hypotheses that network‐based dispositions are the most strongly associated with broad innovators, gen erative dispositions are significantly associated with selective innovators, and appropriation dispositions are the most strongly associated with minimal innovators. These findings point to a more nuanced understanding of different roles that technological knowledge and social knowledge play in SDO innovation in different ecological niches. It is not just the quantity of innovations that marks the 3 classes but the unique knowledge dispositions of the innovators, which are qualitatively different depending on their role in the ecology.

As a final note, we also deploy a new methodological approach to analysing moderated hypotheses by applying latent class clustering techniques not applied before in IS research. Latent class clustering remains still a “blue water”

technique in the main stream of IS research and offers significant potential for formulating novel and rigorous data based typologies grounded in emerging or a priori theory. We hope that our use prompts other IS scholars to probe its potential and that the influx of growing interest will contribute to the further development of this still formative methodology.

## 7 | LIMITATIONS

By way of limitations, we intentionally did not include a dependent performance variable (such as profit) in our conceptual development and analysis. We felt that this would distract from explorative aspects of articulating typology of innovating SDOs. Nevertheless, we conducted a post‐hoc ANOVA using 2 such performance variables—profit and annua sales—to verify that the 3 SDO classes did not significantly vary in performance (ie, we are essentially controlling for per formance). The results show that altogether, SDO classes do not significantly differ in their average profit (f = 0.365) or annual sales (0.075) nor do they differ in 2‐way comparisons (all Bonferroni comparisons were non‐significant). Thus, these SDO classes are not in competition as “better” or “worse” performing organizations. The variations, rather, can be accounted within each class with regard to their innovating behaviours. In other words, each organization is located in a specific environmental niche where it either meets the needs of the environment including its clients or it does not

Additionally, we were limited by the small sizes of class clusters (ranging from n = 35 to 62). Thus, we are susceptible to making Type II errors because of low power. We calculated the power for testing H2, and values ranged from 0.52 to 0.99. Thus, we struggled to achieve sufficient statistical power for some of the tested relationships—because primarily of low sample sizes among groups and low $\mathsf { R } ^ { 2 } \mathsf { s }$ for some of the weaker effects. One hundred thirty‐six firms are certainly not representative of all firms, particularly given that all these were collected within the United States, rather than internationally. Accordingly, future research aspiring to use multiple classes is well‐advised to obtain the largest sample sizes feasibly possible for each class. We also only included a small set of software innovations for indicators in our latent class cluster analysis. Although we collected data on 14 distinct software innovations, we only used 6 of them to define latent classes, and all were service innovations (see Carlo et al., 2012. for a complete list). However, using a small list of indicators for cluster analysis is quite common (eg, Rai et al., 2006). As more indi cators are added to a cluster analysis, both the ability to extract good‐fitting models and the ability to meaningfully interpret them become increasingly difficult and unreliable. Additionally, the indicators we chose represent well the different populations of Internet‐based software applications by accounting for customer‐centric, partner‐centric, and administration‐centric services. Future research may benefit, however, from exploring new theory‐based config urations of innovation indicators.

Furthermore, it is important to note that we did not collect data on the frequency of innovation. Future research might explore the overall magnitude of innovativeness, which would involve some combination of the frequency and breadth of innovative activity. Finally, the innovations we are studying and theorizing around in this paper are from the turn of the millennium. This does not, however, diminish the relevance of the theories or findings. At the genera level, we are theorizing around ways different SDO innovator classes adopt and innovate. These sorts of phenomen will cycle endlessly through future history of IS research. A more recent example of this is the way many SDOs hav innovated around mobile applications and social media or cloud. A similar study to ours might easily replicate thi paper in such a context to test the generalizability of these findings.

One of the potential weaknesses with this study is the age of the data and its transferability to current events However, the age of the data is a feature, not a flaw. Since we are studying disruptive cycles, 2006 represents approx imately the latest date one could ask firms who were involved in an IT disruptive cycle and still have memory of th beginning of the wave. By all indications from qualitative field data (in a working paper), Internet Computing as a dis ruptive cycle entered its unique high period in 2001 and settled into it around 2003. So, by 2005, when the data were collected, small SDOs who had waited on the sidelines had a chance to enter the market. The data set we use capture that phenomenon. It is unclear whether the current candidate for a disruptive wave including combinations of mobile data analytics, cloud, and social media has reached a similar level of maturation. The fact that SDOs are still competing within radical base, process, and service innovations testifies to this.<sup>8</sup> Whatever the current disruptive wave is, it is not clearly in the specific phase in the Lambe and Spekman (Lambe & Spekman, 1997) model, but in a point where radica base, services, and process innovations represent a significant marketplace for SDOs. In contrast, we know that in 2004‐2005, the eCommerce wave reached its maturation stage and had worked its way to small SDOs. Our dataset is, in this regard, appropriate. Indeed, it is possible to collect data from the current wave to make similar predictions but it is likely premature because the data needs to be well established and appropriately timed for the type of anal yses we conducted. In this regard, this paper provides insights into the most recent disruptive IT wave. The SDOs can use our analyses to determine how they may want to navigate or surf their way as the current tsunami impacts the industry. Carlo et al. (2011) argue that many small SDOs did not adopt Internet technologies until after the specific period (approximately half of the participants) had adopted. So, for small SDOs, this analysis is essential because small SDOs are more likely to wait before they join a radical innovation cycle. Future research can hopefully triangulate ou findings by replicating the data collection done in the Internet wave within the current technology wave

## 8 | CONCLUSION

Software innovations are generally thought of as just as another innovation. Yet some research has emphasized the rich variety of software innovations and invited revisiting innovation theory to consider a variety of software innovation types (Lyytinen & Rose, 2003b; Swanson, 1994). In this study, we have extended this line of research and taken steps towards a more nuanced theory of software innovators. We extend extant literature by providing empirical evi dence of SDO innovator classes. This cautions IS researchers to be mindful regarding classes of innovators, in addition to types of innovations, as they strive to build a solid body of knowledge about software innovation. Further, our work points out how different knowledge dispositions are emphasized by different classes of innovators—it is not simply a manner of quantity of innovation but also the overall approach each type of organization has towards innovation. Such findings bear implications for practice in SDOs as they seek to more effectively innovate by playing to their strengths and understanding their ecological niche in the innovation landscape. We hope this study will kindle continued interest in fascinating variations among innovators, antecedents, and outcomes.

## APPENDIX A: A REVIEW OF TECHNOLOGY INNOVATION TYPOLOGIES

The studies represented in the table below each developed or used a typology of innovators. We reviewed each set of innovator classes from each study to determine if the underlying classes could be reclassified as broad, selective, or minimal innovators. Because multiple studies used the same label for particular classes that they then treated differently (eg, many studies use “supplier‐dominated” as an innovator class label), we looked beyond the given labels. We focused instead on the definitions, findings, descriptions, and general treatment of each class within each study. Thus, if the treatment of the class seemed to indicate that class innovated broadly, then we reclassified them as “Broad”; if sparsely or only as a reaction to demand, then “Minimal,” else, “Selective.” As a result, even classes with identical labels from their originating studies may have been classified into different meta‐classes. For example, the “supplier‐domi nated” label was used 3 times in Table 1. but we found that one study treated this class as we would classify a selective innovator, while the other 2 studies treated this class as we would classify a minimal innovator. As with any cluste analysis that has both loose and tight clusters (and often has cases that fall somewhere in between clusters), ou meta‐clustering was not perfect but fit fairly well. To leave no ambiguity, we explain the reclassification decision for all matching or similar configuration labels in footnotes below Table A1.

## APPENDIX C: MULTIPLE DISCRIMINANT ANALYSIS

We conducted a multiple discriminant analysis to further support the testing of a to c. This analysis tests whether and to what extent the 6 innovation factors can predict SDO class membership. This would provide support for profiling along these 6 factors. The results of the analysis are largely confirmatory. In Table C1, the “sig” column indicates that the means of the 6 innovation factors are significantly different between SDO classes. In Table C2, the Wilks' Lambd was also significant, indicating that the 6 factors do sufficiently predict group membership. In Table C3, the grey highlighted cells indicate the percent of correctly placed cases based on the 6 factors. The 6 factors were able to correctly predict roughly 50% of the cases' class memberships. The proposed model must be at least 25% better at predicting group membership than a random model (Burns & Burns, 2008). The random model for this set of 6 factors was able to predict 35.6% of the cases accurately. A 25% increase would mean that our model should predict at leas 1.25\*.356 = 44.5%. Our model exceeds this threshold at 49.3%.
