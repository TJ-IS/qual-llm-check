# THE TAKEOFF OF OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE: A SIGNALING PERSPECTIVE BASED ON COMMUNITY ACTIVITIES<sup>1</sup>

Pankaj Setia Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad, Vastrapur, Ahmedabad 380015 Gujarat, INDIA and University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {pankajsetia@iima.ac.in}

Barry L. Bayus Kenan-Flagler Business School, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, Chapel Hill, NC 27599-3490 U.S.A. {Barry\_Bayus@unc.edu}

Balaji Rajagopalan College of Business, Northern Illinois University, DeKalb, IL 60115-2929 U.S.A. {brajagopalan@niu.edu}

A few open source software (OSS) products exhibit an abrupt and significant increase in downloads. However, the majority of OSS products fail to gain much interest. Identifying early success is important for catalyzing growth in OSS markets. However, previous OSS research has not examined early product success dynamics and assumes adoption to be a continuous process. We propose OSS takeoff in adoptions as a measure of eventual product success. Takeoff is a nonlinear inflection point separating the early development from the growth phase in the product lifecycle. Using arguments from the signaling literature, we propose that community activities send signals about product quality and reduce information asymmetry faced by potential adopters of OSS products. Estimating a Cox proportional hazard model using a large sample of OSS products from SourceForge, we find that takeoff times are significantly associated with signals of quality deficiency and improvement. Further, we find that target audience and product innovativeness moderate this relationship.

Keywords: Open source software, takeoff, signaling, product quality

## Introduction

Identifying early success is important for the continued development and growth of the open-source movement that now boasts over 430,000 projects and 3.7 million registered developers.<sup>2</sup> Creating efficiency in such a large OSS market requires mechanisms to determine which products are likely to be successful based on their early market acceptance. For typical OSS products, downloads are very low (if not zero) for several months during the early stages of an OSS project. For successful products, downloads eventually exhibit a sharp takeoff (i.e., the well-known hockey stick pattern; see Figure 1). In the marketing and economics literatures, takeoff is often used to identify early product success (e.g., Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997). However, existing OSS research has ignored the critical role of takeoff in the adoption of typical open source products. Software industry experts largely benchmark on the most prominent examples of successful projects when identifying key OSS success factors. Most previous OSS research largely focuses on the continued and linear growth in adoptions (Daniel et al. 2013; Setia et al. 2012).<sup>3</sup> However, a focus on linear dynamics limits our understanding of OSS product adoptions because it ignores the product lifecycle and role of early success in continued growth of a product.

The occurrence of a takeoff in downloads alleviates uncertainty about the long-term survival of an OSS product and enables successful OSS products to attract resources required for continued growth and development. Because of its focus on only linear adoption dynamics, the current OSS literature doesn’t address the question: Why are some OSS products successful, and others are not? It is widely known that the product lifecycle is not continuously linear. Successful products exhibit a sharp increase in adoptions, indicating the start of the growth phase and end of the early development phase (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997).

Theoretically, it is important to understand predictors of open source products’ early success. Identifying project characteristics that are early predictors of eventual success is important for the efficient allocation of scarce resources across OSS projects (Haefliger et al. 2008). Indeed, it is essential to allocate resources (e.g., developers’ time, organizational support, and public attention) to OSS projects based on their early lifecycle stage and promise of eventual success. However, it is challenging to identify dynamics that trigger takeoff in the OSS context. For proprietary products, it is widely known that early market success depends on product development processes that influence product quality. Often, various signaling mechanisms (such as advertising, price, warranty or return policy, and promotions) are indicative of proprietary products’ quality and influence potential adopters’ decisions (Kirmani and Rao 2000). However, these traditional mechanisms are not meaningful in the OSS markets because typical OSS products lack the brand awareness and recognition to send credible signals about product quality.

Our study contributes by introducing the idea of a takeoff in the OSS product lifecycle and unraveling how OSS products realize early success by understanding key insights from antecedents to takeoff. The open source development process is largely owned and driven by its community of developers and users (Lakhani and von Hippel 2003; Roberts et al. 2006). Therefore, unlike proprietary products, these products lack formal corporate governance mechanisms to send signals of product quality. Because OSS products may not have established mechanisms in place to send traditional signals, we posit that community activities act as signals that can influence takeoff. Specifically, we use an information asymmetry perspective to identify two community signals that may influence OSS product takeoff. We propose that the presence of community activity reduces the information asymmetry between producers and potential adopters. Building on previous research (Setia et al. 2012), we consider the impact of two community activities that send a signal of quality deficiency (SQD) or a signal of quality improvement (SQI).

While SQD sends a negative signal about product quality, SQI sends a positive signal. As suggested by the two OSS products in Figure 1, takeoff is clearly visible for two OSS projects: Radeon Tweaker CDX. Radeon Tweaker has several bug reports and fixes before its relatively quick takeoff, while CDX has several bug reports but few bug fixes before its rather long wait for takeoff. Also, empirical evidence indicates that community support may enhance open source product adoption of OSS products, such as operating systems (e.g., Linux, or Solaris) and applications (e.g., Tk/Tc, Sendmail, GNU Make, or GNU CC) (Ozel et al. 2007; Wang and Wang 2001).

In addition to examining the direct effects of SQD and SQI on takeoff, we study the differences in the effects of these signals on takeoff across OSS products that are (1) targeted to different potential audiences (developers versus end-users) and (2) are different in product innovativeness. A study of these contextual factors is motivated by the emerging evidence that contextual factors play a major role in the adoption of soft ware products (Lyytinen and Yoo 2002). To test our hypotheses, we empirically investigate a sample of 941 OSS products from SourceForge. Estimation results from a duration (survival) model support our core hypotheses: we find differences in the effects of SQD and SQI on time to takeoff, across products with different target audiences and product innovativeness.

## Theoretical Background

Takeoff is an inflection point in the product lifecycle associated with a nonlinear change in new product adoptions. It is well-known that the adoption pattern of successful new products follows an S-shaped sales curve: adoption is very low in the introduction stage, followed by a sudden and sharp takeoff in the growth stage, and finally a flattening of adoptions in the maturity and decline stages (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997). Notably, researchers focus on the first takeoff because it is a measure of early success. In line with previous research, we define OSS product takeoff as the first abrupt change in cumulative downloads that indicates the start of the growth stage.

Takeoff is triggered when a product acquires a critical mass of adopters and leads to sustained product adoption (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997; Gort and Klepper 1982). In OSS contexts, informal word-of-mouth (WOM) plays an important role (Bonaccorssi and Rossi 2003). For example, Dalle and Jullien (1999) argue that local interactions, such as those in closed groups, were more influential for Linux, than for Windows NT. Unlike in the case of Windows NT where the adopters know the firm (Microsoft) producing the product, potential users may know little about the developer group building the OSS product. Owing to this lack of knowledge about the entity creating the product, potential adopters have to deal with significant uncertainty about product quality. In essence, there is significant information asymmetry between potential adopters and producers of the product (developers). When effective mechanisms contribute to reducing this information asymmetry, the WOM effect enables the product to acquire a critical mass of potential adopters.

A signaling perspective is often used to understand how proprietary products alleviate information asymmetry. Often, proprietary products use formal signals (such as advertising and branding) to reduce uncertainty about product quality among potential adopters. The information asymmetry perspective offers a foundation for these effects (Kirmani and Rao 2000). Conceptualized as a mechanism to reduce information asymmetry, signaling theory was initially proposed by Spence (1973) in the domain of information economics. In his seminal work, Spence postulated that signals play an important role for potential employers who face information asymmetry in the labor market. Education credentials act as signals for potential employers, helping them differentiate between high and low-quality candidates. Spence argues that such signals contribute to reducing information asymmetry, as potential employers gain greater knowledge about the potential of candidates. The signaling perspective has been successfully applied to study the adoption of proprietary products (Kirmani and Rao 2000). Specifically, signaling helps potential adopters minimize information asymmetry, reducing uncertainty about products by enabling potential adopters to differentiate between them based on their quality.

## Developer Community Activities as Signals of Product Quality

Previous studies have examined how proprietary firms send signals through various instruments such as advertising, brand names, coupons and warranties (Kirmani and Rao 2000). In the software context, Griggs (2009) reports about signals sent by Microsoft advertising for its launch of Windows. Unlike proprietary software products that often follow a stage-gate approach whereby product development and diffusion (commercialization) are distinct and discrete phases, OSS products typically have a more unstructured development process (Bajaj et al. 2004; Setia et al. 2012). Because not much information about OSS product quality is available in the early lifecycle phases, appropriate signals are required to help potential adopters discern if the product is progressing well to acquire high quality that will make it useful. However, due to their unstructured product development processes, OSS products lack the ability to send signals traditionally used by producers of proprietary products. While OSS products lack the ability to send meaningful and credible signals using traditional means (such as advertising, free return policy, price, or length of warranty), we argue that the community’s role in product development dynamics imply that community activities act as signals of product quality for potential adopters. Because of the lack of formal resources, OSS products rely on the developer community for product development. Therefore, product developer activities by the community send informative signals about unobservable product quality.

These signals emerging from community activities meet the dual criteria for credible signals. For an activity to qualify as a signal, it should be observable and costly to obtain. Because OSS products are developed through shared intellectual contributions of the developer community, product development activities on a project are openly visible and easily observable by potential adopters. In OSS settings, there are only a finite number of skilled and motivated developers who are willing to voluntarily contribute to product development (Dahlander and McKelvey 2005). Hence, attracting community attention is hard for open source products as numerous products compete for developer time and effort, making the signals costly. Because they are observable and costly, community contributions are informative and credible signals that help potential adopters evaluate OSS product quality.

Community participants contribute to OSS products by assessing and enhancing product quality. In particular, Setia et al. (2012) note that the community helps assess deficiencies and enable enhancements in OSS product quality. We conceptualize two community-based signals about OSS product quality: signals of quality deficiency (SQD) and signals of quality improvement (SQI). We assess both technical and functional aspects of OSS product quality because software quality spans technical performance and the ability of software to meet functional needs (Baker and Fisher 2008). These two aspects—technical and functional—have been validated across other domains such as service quality (Tan et al. 2013; Tian 2005). That is, to conceptualize SQD, we argue that a deficiency may be either technical (as evidenced in a bug report) or functional (as indicated by request for a potential feature addition). Similarly, SQI indicates whether a product has been enhanced technically (for example, through a bug fix) or functionally (as indicated by the release of a new version). Based on these signals, we develop our research model and hypotheses.

## The Effects of a Signal of Quality Deficiency

Because little is known about an OSS product in its early lifecycle stage, SQD communicates crucial information about product quality indicating a technical or functional deficiency. The signal’s effect underscores the lack of other quality verification mechanisms. Proprietary software products use various third-party certifications (e.g., CMM 2001). However, there are relatively few formal mechanisms, such as third-party verifications, to assess OSS product quality. Further, most open source products lack formalized work teams for product testing. In this context, SQD is perceived to offer a reliable report about product quality as it is created by community participants who closely engage with OSS product development. Poor quality increases the cost of learning and assimilating software (Fichman and Kemerer 1999; Kemerer 1992). Potential adopters perceive that a product with SQD possesses lower quality, associating greater adoption costs.

Because it indicates technical or functional deficiency, SQD leads to a negative assessment of the product’s readiness for use, delaying its takeoff. Mechanisms of word of mouth and internal community dynamics help spread the perceptions of poor product quality, and as a result, fewer potential adopters engage with the product. As many potential adopters delay their adoption decisions, the product takes longer to acquire a critical mass of adopters and thus the time to takeoff is longer. Therefore, we hypothesize

H1: OSS products with a signal of quality deficiency (SQD) will have longer takeoff times in comparison to products without such a signal.

## The Effects of a Signal of Quality Improvement

As compared to SQD, SQI has the opposite influence on time to takeoff. Such a signal conveys positive information to potential adopters about an OSS product’s ability to continually enhance its quality. An OSS product realizes better product quality if the developer community plays an active role in product development, fixing bugs and making version releases (Setia et al. 2012). OSS products have to coordinate developers’ activities to enable community contributions. Coordinating such activities is challenging because of the lack of structured governance mechanisms. Governance mechanisms are important for product development as this lateral creating lateral linkage to coordinate expertise across activities (Astley and Zajac 1991). Repeated cycles of trial and error involving core project developers and the associated community are essential for the development of high-quality OSS products. Such interactions help combine tacit and explicit knowledge across OSS developers (Setia et al. 2012).

Therefore, SQI indicates that the product has the capabilities that enable interactions within the open source product development environment. The positive perceptions that the product has governance mechanisms reduces uncertainty among potential adopters about the continued evolution of its quality. This advantage spreads with WOM (both electronically and physically), creating a buzz about the product. Therefore, we propose

H2: OSS products with a signal of quality improvement (SQI) will have shorter takeoff times in comparison to products without such a signal.

## The Moderating Effects of OSS Product Characteristics

In this section, we consider how the effects of SQD and SQI vary across different types of OSS products. Previous research finds that the impact of signals depending on the characteristics of the receiver or the entity about which the signal is being sent (Highhouse et al. 2007; Prabhu and Stewart 2001). Further, we expect that product innovativeness is another key moderator since assessments of quality are related to perceptions of innovativeness (Boisvert and Ashill 2011).

## Developer Versus End-User Focused OSS Products

An open source product’s target audience represents the potential adopter group likely to adopt the product, and OSS products targeted to developers—in comparison to those targeted to end-users—take off sooner. The ability of potential adopters to discern the quality of OSS products varies based on adopters’ technical knowledge and capabilities. In this vein, end-users and developers have different technical capabilities. In general, individuals that lack knowledge or experience have limited abilities to comprehend tasks, ideas or entities (Kahneman et al. 1982; Simon 1996). In the context of this study, because they have limited knowledge of software code and programming principles, typical end-users of open source software have less ability to assess product quality by evaluating an OSS product’s architecture, design, or code. Thus, end-users face greater uncertainty about product quality and have larger information asymmetry vis-à-vis the developer community. The larger information asymmetry for typical users who have little or no technical expertise, we argue, results in slowing the time for adoption. Therefore, we argue

H3: OSS products targeted to developers have shorter takeoff times in comparison to OSS products targeted to end-users.

We also propose that the effects of SQD and SQI are more pronounced in OSS products targeted toward end-users as compared to those targeted toward developers. Impact of signals depends on the characteristics of the receiver or the entity about which the signal is being sent (Highhouse et al. 2007; Prabhu and Stewart 2001). In OSS contexts, given their lack of domain expertise, typical end-users have to rely more on signals to assess product quality. In comparison, developers have the capability to discern product quality by evaluating technical and functional features themselves. Therefore, developers rely on their own (private) judgment of the quality of the product, irrespective of the social influence from the community. Indeed, it is known that knowledgeable decision makers place less weight on others’ decisions in making their choices (Duan et al. 2009). Because they have the capability to assess product quality, developers assign less importance to community generated signals about OSS product quality. Hence, the ability of SQD and SQI to resolve information asymmetry and trigger an early takeoff is more pronounced for end-user targeted products. Thus, we hypothesize

H4: A signal of quality deficiency (SQD) leads to longer takeoff times for OSS products targeted to end-users as compared to those targeted to developers.

H5: A signal of quality improvement (SQI) leads to shorter takeoff times for OSS products targeted to end-users as compared to those targeted to developers.

## Innovative Versus Non-Innovative Products

A fundamental premise in the marketing and technology management literature is that innovative products have faster diffusion than products with incremental improvements (Garcia and Calantone 2002; Rogers 2003). Innovative products are thought to offer a greater relative advantage to potential adopters in comparison to products that are not innovative (Garcia and Calantone 2002; Kleinschmidt and Cooper 1991). More innovative products may address a more prominent unmet need, as individuals usually look for products that use newer technologies, or can be used on new platforms, or those that are new applications of existing technologies. Previous research argues that greater perceptions of innovativeness enhance a product’s diffusion through the social system (Garcia and Calantone 2002; Rogers 2003).

Following the existing literature that argues for the importance of product innovativeness, we expect that innovative OSS projects will have shorter takeoff times than products that are not innovative.

H6: Innovative OSS products have shorter takeoff times in comparison to OSS products that are not innovative.

Because they find greater innovativeness to be more advantageous, potential adopters are more tolerable of the product quality. That is, innovativeness gives potential adopter novel ways to use the product, reducing the uncertainty regarding the functional quality of innovative products. Therefore, the perceived information asymmetry is lesser for the innovative products, in comparison to the products that are not innovative. Research suggests that the signals to bridge product quality uncertainty have greater impact when perceptions of information asymmetry are high (Spence 1974). Therefore, community activities that signal product quality have less influence on the takeoff of innovative OSS products. Due to their favorable perceptions of innovative products, potential adopters feel less need to rely on community signals providing information about product quality. Non-innovative products lack such an advantage, making potential adopters pay more attention to SQD and SQI to assess product quality. Hence, we propose

H7: A signal of quality deficiency (SQD) leads to longer takeoff times for OSS products that are not innovative as compared to those that are innovative.

H8: A signal of quality improvement (SQI) leads to shorter takeoff times for OSS products that are not innovative as compared to those that are innovative.

## Data and Variable Definitions

Our data consists of a random sample of OSS projects hosted by SourceForge (SF). In addition to being the largest host for OSS projects, SourceForge is also a rich source of data on the OSS development process (e.g., number of developers, version releases), and hence, is an excellent data source for researchers (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003). Various studies have used this data source, and its potential limitations are well-known (Comino et al. 2007).<sup>4</sup> Data for our study were collected from SF in December 2003. In general, we exercised caution in acquiring, cleaning, and analyzing the data.

We used a web crawler to generate a comprehensive list of projects from the six largest project categories listed on SF in December 2003 (a total of 17,035 projects). From these projects, we randomly selected 1,000 projects to be included in our study using a random number generator. Based on the frequency distribution of project types, our sample is representative of OSS projects in SF. Data on project characteristics and development activities was then collected for these projects with a web crawler. We later supplemented this data with information on product innovativeness. Because 59 projects had to be dropped for lack of information on the main explanatory variables or were duplicates of other projects, the sample size for our study is 941 projects. Summary descriptive statistics of our variables are in Appendix A.

## Download Takeoff

We follow the related literature to determine takeoff times based on monthly OSS product downloads (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997; Gort and Klepper 1982). In line with these studies, the takeoff time for most OSS products is visually clear as it corresponds to the first sharp increase in sales (e.g., the hockey stick pattern). We confirmed the takeoff times from visual inspection with the maximum growth rule (Golder and Tellis 1997): takeoff is associated with the month in which the percentage growth in cumulative downloads is largest. Further, we confirmed the takeoff times with a third systematic approach based on generalized search principles in the discriminant analysis (Gort and Klepper 1982). A detailed example is in Appendix B. Not surprisingly, all three methods generated very similar results (Haapaniemi and Makinen 2007).

The time to takeoff varies considerably across OSS products (see Figure 1 and Appendix D). At the time of our data collection, most of the OSS projects in our sample had exhibited a download takeoff (67%). Projects without a download takeoff by December 2003 were considered to be censored. For the projects with a takeoff in downloads, the mean (median) time to takeoff is 13 (9) months. Average downloads per month for the open source products in our sample are just over 1,220. As expected, the average downloads are considerably higher among OSS projects that exhibit a takeoff (1,461) in comparison to products that have not as yet experienced a takeoff (710). See Table 1 for some descriptive statistics associated with the OSS products that displayed a takeoff.

## OSS Product Quality Signals

The quality signals associated with each OSS project in our sample come from information in SF. Each of these variables is calculated based on cumulative data up to one month before the download takeoff (or last observation if no takeoff is exhibited). To test our hypotheses, we construct binary variables that capture whether or not there is a signal for a particular OSS project. The presence of a bug report or feature request is used to classify a product as having SQD. Bug reports involve specific problems with the current code, while feature requests are desired improvements to the software. Over 30% of the OSS projects in our sample had some activity involved with SQD. Specific development activities signaling quality improvement include fixing bugs or releasing new versions of the software by the project administrator. Projects were categorized as having SQI if they had a bug fix or a version release. In our sample, over 70% of the projects had some activity directed toward SQI. Not surprisingly, over 90% of projects with an SQI exhibited a takeoff and mean time to takeoff for a project without an SQD is lower (11.2 months) compared to that with an SQD (16.1 months) (see Table 1). Here, H1 is supported if SQD has a negative and significant effect on time to takeoff, while H2 is supported if SQI has a positive and significant effect on time to takeoff.

## Control Variables

Using information available from SF, we also include several control variables for product-specific characteristics that might provide an alternative explanation for shorter download takeoff times. These control variables comprise OSS license, product category, development stage, project size, project age, and project activity. We briefly discuss them below.

As noted by Stewart et al. (2006), users without the knowledge or desire to fully evaluate software code often use extrinsic cues such as a license to decide on the usefulness and ease of use of a software program. More restrictive licenses hamper the possible modifications that can be made to the source code, and thus significantly reduce the opportunities for commercial exploitation. We follow the definitions established by Lerner and Tirole (2005): projects with a Restrictive License (1 if yes, 0 otherwise) are those protected by a GNU General Public License (GPL). This license has the copyleft provision that allows anyone to use, share, and modify the original source code and requires that any derived products not have any new restrictions. Thus, software that uses source code with a GPL license cannot become a proprietary product. In our sample, 70% of the OSS projects have a restrictive license. This percentage is very similar to other studies (Lerner and Tirole 2005; Stewart et al. 2006).

We also control for software category by employing the set of dummy variables Education, Game, Communication, and Utilities (here, other applications are the baseline)—education and games have the largest representation in our sample. It is interesting that over 90% of the game products displayed a takeoff, while less than 50% of education and utilities products exhibited a takeoff (see Table 1). Similar to prior studies (Setia et al. 2012), we control for the project stage by including a set of mutually exclusive dummy variables to account for possible differences across projects in development efforts prior to being listed on SF—we expect that projects in later development stages will tend to have shorter takeoff times since more mature projects may be perceived to have greater quality. We also include controls for project size (Number of Developers) and age (Project Age = months on SF before takeoff) to control for any differences in project availability on SF. Finally, we also included Activity Percentage (average project activity percentile before takeoff), to assess the impacts of the activity on takeoff. Also, we included two other measures of project activity to account for any effects that might not be captured by the quality signal variables: Page Views (cumulative number of page views before takeoff), and Commits (cumulative number of developer commits before takeoff).

## Estimation Approach

To model the probability of takeoff and explicitly account for censored observations, we use the statistical approach associated with survival analysis. Survival analysis is appropriate in our case because the assumption of normally distributed residuals in regression models (including Tobit models that handle censoring) does not hold for takeoff times (Cleves et al. 2004). Following the related empirical literature considering takeoff (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Golder and Tellis 1997), we use Cox’s (1972) proportional hazards regression model to study OSS takeoff times. See Cleves et al. (2004) for an excellent discussion of this model and its nuances. As suggested by Allison (1984), we do not include a term for unobserved heterogeneity since we only analyze non-repeated events. To account for the possibility that two projects have the same observed takeoff time, we assume that there is a true but unknown ordering for the tied events times and use the standard Breslow approximation (for details, see Cleves et al.). Finally, we report results using robust (Huber/White sandwich estimators) standard errors for the estimated coefficients.

## Empirical Results

Estimation results are reported in Table 2. As evidenced by the significant overall $\chi ^ { 2 }$ values, the estimation results for these models provide a very good fit to the OSS data. As expected, projects with relatively high activity tend to have short takeoff times. The results in Model 2 provide strong support for H1 and H2. Consistent with our hypotheses, the estimated coefficient for SQD is negative and significant, while the estimated coefficient for SQI is positive and significant. The coefficient value for SQD indicates that the hazard rate for OSS projects with a product quality deficiency signal is 61% of the hazard rate or those without such a signal $( \mathrm { e } ^ { - 0 . 5 0 } = 0 . 6 1 )$ . Similarly, the coefficient value for SQI implies that the hazard rate for OSS products with a product quality improvement signal is more than 600% of that for products without such a signal $( \mathrm { e } ^ { 1 . 9 1 } = 6 . 7 5 )$ . While both types of product quality signals are clearly significant, these results indicate that activities directly related to improving a product have a much greater impact on takeoff times than activities geared toward identifying the gaps with a current product.

The results in Table 2, Model 3 strongly support H4 and H6. However, we find no statistical support for H3 and H5, even though the coefficients are directionally aligned with the hypotheses. The positive and significant coefficient estimates for the interaction terms involving SQI imply that the effects of product quality improvement signal differ by target audience and product innovativeness. Our results indicate that the influence of signals associated with product deficiency efforts does not vary by target market or product innovativeness. We confirmed the robustness of these results with an extensive set of additional analyses (see Appendix C).

## Conclusions

We examine how two community-driven activities trigger takeoff. Using arguments from the signaling literature, we propose that community activities send signals about product quality and reduce information asymmetry faced by potential adopters of OSS products. Two main conclusions come from our research: (1) OSS products with SQD have relatively long takeoff times, and (2) OSS products with SQI have relatively short takeoff times. As hypothesized, our results suggest that potential adopters use product quality signals from the OSS development process to make their download decisions. Building on these two central findings, we also consider how target audience and product innovativeness moderate the effects of signals of quality deficiency and improvement. As hypothesized, the impact of SQI in shortening takeoff times is greater for OSS products targeted to end-users than those for developer-targeted products. Further, we also find that impact of this signal on takeoff times for non-innovative OSS products is greater than those for innovative products.

Together, these findings suggest that a signal indicating improvements in software quality is much more effective for products targeted to non-experts (i.e., end-users) and products developed for an existing market that uses existing technology (i.e., products that are not innovative). We hypothesize that signals are less effective in other cases. Expert developers can directly judge product quality by examining the software code. Similarly, the quality of innovative products is less consequential, as potential adopters associate higher technical and functional performance due to their novelty and rely less on signals for such products.

Contrary to our proposed hypotheses, we find that the effects of SQD do not differ by target audience or product innovativeness. These findings indicate that negative signaling effects of SQD may be stronger and not moderated, as compared to the positive signaling effects of SQI. We tested the assumption by running another model to examine the impacts on takeoff when SQD and SQI occur together. An assessment of the interactive effect between SQD and SQI indicates that SQD has a stronger (negative) influence than SQI. The findings are consistent with prospect theory (i.e., individuals value the prospect of losses more than gains; Kahneman and Tversky 1979). In general, bad events have a stronger effect than the good ones (Baumeister et al. 2001). The negative influence of SQD is stronger, having a similar effect for the experts (developers) and non-experts (endusers)—that is, negative news about non-readiness of the product for adoption is interpreted the same by the two audiences. Indeed, it is widely known that fixes for software bugs can be very challenging (Raymond 2001). Therefore, the negative information conveyed by a signal of quality deficiency that the product is not yet performing as desired or expected has similar effects for both innovative and noninnovative products.

## Contributions

OSS product takeoff is a distinct and unique landmark in the product lifecycle as it differentiates successful products from others that may never be widely adopted. By identifying takeoff, we offer a mechanism for early identification of successful OSS products. The focus on takeoff extends previous OSS research that compares products’ number of users, popularity, or views of information pages (Crowston et al. 2003) as well as downloads (Grewal et al. 2006). Specifically, our study extends this literature by offering a new conceptual and operational mechanism to identify the early success of OSS products.

Takeoff may not suffer from personal reporting biases pertinent to other self-reported measures of product success. For example, the development stage of a project is typically selfreported by project leaders (Crowston and Scozzi 2002). Further, takeoff also helps compare relative development stages across OSS products that serve different needs. Because it is a standardized measure, takeoff allows an accurate comparison across unrelated products (differing in their target market size), based on where they are in their product lifecycle. In general, takeoff may be leveraged to compare adoption dynamics across other software and technology products. Therefore, takeoff extends and enriches the repertoire of previous IS adoption concepts, such as decisionmaking stages in assimilation (Fichman and Kemerer 1999; Meyer and Goes 1988), behavioral intentions of adopters (Davis 1989; Davis et al. 1989), frequency or incidence of adoption (Grover and Goslar 1993; Zmud 1982); and routinization of innovations (Yin 1979; Zmud and Apple 1992).

While takeoff offers a new concept to compare progress along the adoption lifecycle for IS products, our study does not examine the adoption dynamics in its entirety. Indeed, an OSS product may have multiple takeoffs; that is, the growth phase in OSS may be more complex than what we have considered in this study. While we have considered antecedents to the first takeoff, we underline that more research is required to unravel the theoretical and empirical significance of multiple takeoffs. Theoretically, multiple takeoffs may represent a pattern whereby early success (first takeoff) may form the foundation for stable growth and multiple future increases in adoption rates. Some research in marketing on “saddle” patterns may offer other theoretical explanations that might be explored in future research (Goldenberg et al. 2002). Also, more empirical research is required to develop techniques to identify and measure these dynamics associated with multiple takeoffs. In summary, takeoff is a complex phenomenon, and more research is required to unravel the takeoff dynamics across open source projects.

Our study presents a theoretical understanding of the early success and takeoff in OSS product downloads. By unravelling community-triggered adoption dynamics, we extend prior studies of product adoption that have focused on individual-level theories, such as theory of planned behavior and theory of reasoned action (Davis 1989; Davis et al. 1989; Venkatesh and Davis 2000); product antecedents, such as relative advantage, observability and trialability (Rogers 2003); organizational theories, such as the relational view of the firm, institutional theory, and organizational inertial theory (Bala and Venkatesh 2007), institutional isomorphism (Liang et al. 2007), knowledge-based view of the firm and institutional theory (Purvis et al. 2001), organizational learning theories (Fichman and Kemerer 1999), resourcebased view and the technology–organization–environment (TOE) framework (Zhu and Kraemer 2005); and network theories, such as information communication through social systems (Rogers 2003). Notably, we extend this previous OSS research by unraveling signaling perspective as a theoretical basis for predicting early success that manifests as takeoff.

The study also has implications for various other virtual communities—such as Kickstarter and IndieGoGo—that are emerging rapidly. While open source represents a virtual community focused on building software, recent research highlights the growth of other virtual communities that create knowledge or services (Bock et al. 2005; Kankanhalli, et al. 2005), raise finances (Burtch et al. 2013), share knowledge (Chiu et al. 2006), meet identity verification needs, or help acquire social affiliation and build social capital (Ma and Agarwal 2007; Wasko and Faraj 2005). These communities represent complex systems, whereby geographic or cultural differences amongst developers and adopters lead to information asymmetry. Thus, future studies in these domains may benefit from considering signals that might be related to the growth and takeoff in acceptance of these communities.

## Takeoff Measures

Several measures of takeoff appear in the literature. For example, Golder and Tellis (1997) use a threshold rule, whereby they define “the threshold for takeoff as a plot of the percentage increase in sales relative to its base sales” (p. 259). The first time a product’s relative growth crosses a threshold is then identified as the takeoff. Other ways of determining the takeoff have been used, including the first turning point of the logistics sales curve and the maximum growth rate in a three year period (Golder and Tellis 1997). Other well-established methods include discriminant analysis and visual analysis (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Haapaniemi and Mäkinen 2006).

Notably, there is remarkable convergence in the results obtained by these different methods to measure takeoff. Golder and Tellis (1997) find that three rules—the threshold rule, the logistic curve rule, and the maximum growth rule—consistently identify the same takeoff points, with no significant difference. They find that their heuristic rule fits a visual inspection of more than 90% of the product categories they study While takeoff is especially hard to discern in real time, historical analyses of takeoff have been relatively uniform. Different methods (including visual analysis) lead to very similar takeoff times (Golder and Tellis 1997; Haapaniemi and Mäkinen 2006). Based on this literature, we use visual analysis, the maximum growth rule, and discriminant analysis to determine takeoff times for the OSS products in our sample.

To apply these methods, we use cumulative OSS product downloads. While most of the literature uses sales to assess takeoff, we use OSS downloads primarily because sales figures are not widely applicable to OSS products. Among the various OSS performance measures, produc downloads is an often studied and a readily available measure of project “popularity.” This is primarily because open source products are often available free for download. Arguably, downloads are a key measure of product interest and adoption for OSS products and have been used as a surrogate for sales by researchers studying shareware (Chandrashekaran et al. 1999). More recently, downloads have been more comprehensively linked with the product life cycle by Wynn (2004) who examines the cumulative download pattern of OSS products. Following the maximum growth rule, we define the first increase (doubling) of (monthly) downloads as our takeoff point. Also, we use two other methods to ensure the robustness of our takeoff measure.

Using visual analysis, one of the authors and a graduate assistant initially determined the first sharp increase in adoption. Figure 1 exhibits cumulative downloads for four OSS projects in the games category. 1964 is a “popular Nintendo64 emulator for Windows.” Registered on SourceForge /01, 1964 is a “popular Nintendo64 emulator for Windows” that is written in C programming language and allows its users to play many Nintendo64 games on their PC. Targeted at developers, dLibSIMD is a “mathematical library utilizing SIMD features of common processors to accelerate many common-used algorithms” coded using assembly and C language. Written in C/C++, megaZeux is a powerfu game creation system that was initially developed in 1994 by Alexis Janson. RadeonTweaker is a tweaking utility for ATI’s radeon-based graphics card. For most OSS products, the takeoff time is visually clear as it corresponds to the first sharp increase in sales (e.g., the “hockey stick” pattern). As can be visually noted, the time to takeoff varies considerably across OSS products (e.g., the time to a sharp increase in downloads for 1964 and megaZeux is more than twice that of LibSIMD and RadeonTweaker).

We also use discriminant analysis to identify the download takeoff point for each product in our sample<sup>7</sup> (Agarwal and Bayus 2002; Gort and Klepper 1982; Haapaniemi and Mäkinen 2006). ## Robustness and Additional Analyses

## Robustness Analysis

We confirm the robustness of our results in multiple ways. A test of the Schonfeld residuals indicates that our sample meets the proportional hazard assumption. To test the robustness of our results to the specific measures used for controls, we estimated our models with severa alternative variable definitions: (1) project stages assessed as discrete categories (planning, pre-alpha, alpha, beta, stable and mature) and (2) innovation type based on the original categories proposed by Klincewicz (2005). In both these cases, the results are qualitatively similar to those reported in Table 2. Further, we confirmed the robustness of our results by using two different simulation procedures—bootstrapping (with 5,000 replications) and jackknifing. Also, we tested our model after controlling for the effects of cumulative downloads in the analysis Finally, we verified the robustness of our results by conducting parametric analyses using two commonly used survivor functions (Weibull and Gompertz).

## Endogeneity

We also considered the possibility that SQD and SQI are endogenous. As noted by Lancaster (1990), coefficient estimates for any endogenous variables in a Cox model are still consistent. However, the treatment of endogeneity in survival models raises some statistical issues and there is no formal analog to instrumental variables used in linear models (Prieger 2008). Thus, we first explored the potential endogeneity of SQD and SQI using linear OLS and 2SLS models. Based on these linear models,<sup>8</sup> Hausman tests suggest that both $S Q D \left( { \mathrm { F } } = 2 . 4 2 , { \mathrm { p } } > 0 . 1 0 \right)$ and SQI $( \mathrm { F } = 0 . 0 1 , \mathrm { p } > 0 . 1 0 )$ are exogenous. In addition, we also explored an instrumental variables approach with a two stage residual inclusion (2SRI) estimation method to assess the effects of endogeneity. Again, results reported in the appendix suggest endogeneity does not threaten the results in our nonlinear Cox hazard models (Terza et al. 2008).

## Controlling for Alternate Explanations

## Signaling Duration and Frequency

We explored how the timing of signals (such as bug reports or bug fixes) may influence adoption. For example, a more recent signal may have a greater effect and the effect of a signal may decrease with the passage of time (Clarke 1976). Therefore, we assessed if the effects of SQD and SQI are influenced by the total duration for all signals, such as bugs reported. To test this, we created a new control variable based on the timing of the community activity. For all activities in month t before takeoff, we constructed duration by adding the time difference between the month the activity was reported and the takeoff month. Thus, duration is calculated as $\Sigma _ { t = 1 } ^ { n } t ,$ where t is the difference between takeoff month and the month of the activity (if there is activity reported) and 0 otherwise. For bug reports across all projects, the average duration is 0.8 and the maximum duration is 37 months. Similarly, for bug fixes the average and maximum are 0.56 and 22; feature requests, 0.14 and 25; and versions released, 1.5 and 23. Our analysis suggests that controlling for the duration of community activities does not qualitatively change our conclusions, confirming that the effect of SQD and SQI on takeoff is independent of the duration of the event. While we do not find the duration to be relevant across other community activities, with the passage of time the effects of the version released on takeoff decreases.

In addition, repeated occurrences of a signal may increase its influence (Batra and Ray 1986). To model this dynamic, we created a new variable assessing the frequency of signals. If there is an occurrence of a signal (say i) in a month (say t) before takeoff, we set $O c c _ { i , t }$ to 1, else $O c c _ { i , t }$ is set to 0. The new control variable, frequency of signal, is then measured as $\Sigma _ { t = 1 } ^ { n } t O c c _ { i , t } ,$ where n represents all months before the takeoff. In our sample, the average frequency of signals before takeoff for SQD is 0.90, and average for SQI is 1.92. Our results indicate that frequency of signal does not influence the effects of SQD or SQI on takeoff (p < .05).

## Magnitude of Signals

Another threat to the validity of our results arises from the complexity in the individual decision-making process. When deciding to adopt an OSS product, potential adopters may consider the overall magnitude of community activities such as bug fixes and bug reports. Further, this evaluation is likely to be more nuanced and complex than merely assessing the presence (or absence) of a signal. It is known that the effect of signals may depend on the magnitude and may deteriorate with time (Leone 1995). That is, the effect of even a large signal (say, numerous bug reports) will be less over time. In other words, an older bug report may have less impact than the most recent report<sup>9</sup>. Therefore, we assessed whether the magnitude of a signal, discounted over time, may interfere with individual decision-making due to the mere presence or absence of a quality signal. To control for this more nuanced adopter decision-making process, we created a new variable incorporating the time of an activity (bugs reported, bugs fixed, feature requests, and versions released). As a measure of the magnitude of a signal, we use the total number (and not merely the presence of) signals in each time period. Let’s say the magnitude of a signal i is Mag in time t, $M a g _ { i , t - 1 }$ in time (t-1), and so on. Our new variable is then a geometric series:

$$
\frac {M a g _ {i , t}}{t} + \frac {M a g _ {i , t - 1}}{(t - 1)} + \frac {M a g _ {i , t - 2}}{(t - 2)} + \frac {M a g _ {i , t - 3}}{(t - 3)} + \dots
$$

which is the summation of the ratio of magnitude and duration weighing the effect of a signal (say a bug report) for all time periods, 1 through n, where n is the number of periods for which the data has been created and a signal has been observed, that is, $\scriptstyle \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { n } { \frac { M a g _ { i , t } } { t } }$

Using this formulation, a bug report in the most recent month is given twice the weight of a bug reported in the previous month, and three times of the bug reported two months prior. Further, the effect of 2x bugs in a period is two times the effect of x bugs in that period. We controlled for this aspect of the individual decision-making process and find that our results are robust, that is, the presence of SQD and SQI has a significant influence on takeoff, even after controlling for the more complex individual decision-making. In this case, the control variables did not have any significant effect on takeoff in our sample $( \boldsymbol { \mathsf { p } } < . 0 5 )$ 10

## Project Efficiency

We also considered the project’s efficiency by evaluating if the ability to address quality deficiencies may overshadow the effects of signals. To assess the effects of project efficiency, we undertook two tests. First, we tested if the results change with the inclusion of bugs outstanding. We calculated the difference of total bugs reported and total bugs fixed, and used it as a control variable. Second, we tested the difference in the total duration of SQD and SQI. To so this, we compared the difference in time between total bugs reported or features requested and the total duration before takeoff to fix outstanding bugs and release new versions. Again, the results indicate that the effects of SQD and SQ are qualitatively unchanged, even after considering project efficiency

## Developer Reputations

We also considered whether developer reputations influence takeoff. Often, OSS projects gain attention through their developers. As a result, reputations and developer participation on a project often may lend attention and credibility to the project, enabling a takeoff in downloads. To control for this effect, we collected data on the participation and contributions of all individual developers on our projects. Because developer reputations may be due to their tenure in the community (Daniel et al. 2013), we controlled for the average joining time of developers participating on a project (the mean of join time is 1.39 years). Our results are qualitatively unchanged, and we do not find join time to be a significant determinant of takeoff. Further, developer reputation also depends on the contributions they make (Daniel et al. 2013). Thus, we also controlled for average participation across all developers on a project. Specifically, we used the average of bugs released and versions released by developers working on these projects to assess developer reputations. We again find that our main results are qualitatively unchanged.

## Project Inertia

Finally, project inertia might be another factor influencing takeoff. Projects with more downloads may have greater inertia and a quicker takeoff. Therefore, the total cumulative downloads of a project are an important control variable that may explain the takeoff time. Our results continue to be robust even after including cumulative downloads as a control variable.
