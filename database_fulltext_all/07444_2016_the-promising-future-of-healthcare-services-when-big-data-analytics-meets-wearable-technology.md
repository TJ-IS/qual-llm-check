## <ABS-HEAD>Abstract

<ABS-P>As policy-makers and business practitioners across the globe expend extraordinary effort toward the field of e-health, the thriving development of healthcare-wearable technology is creating great opportunities and posing a remarkable future for healthcare services. This paper employs a game theory model to investigate the dynamics of wearable device market. We extend the two-dimensional product differentiation model by incorporating consumer diversity, consumer density, and firms’ big data analytics (BDA) investment strategy. Our model reveals that with differentiated consumer densities firms are more likely to engage in quality competition and the firm that invests in BDA can achieve higher profits. Furthermore, the overall quality of biomedical and healthcare services can be improved under various market conditions. Our findings provide practical guidance to wearable device manufacturers on optimizing competition strategies and offer insights to social planners on potential policy-making to promote better healthcare services.

## <H1>1. Introduction

With the development of advanced technologies for biomedical research and healthcare digitalization, data in today’s biomedical research and practice have been growing exponentially. Recent advancement in wearable technologies and big data technologies provides an accessible way to collect and transform biomedical data in real time, which has great potential to improve healthcare services such as reducing injury-related risks, improving doctor–patient communication, and revealing unseen scanning and sensory features [1].

With the recent \`\`passion'' of several technology giants, who are seizing the opportunity to integrate medical functions into wearable devices, the competition in the wearable device market will be quite interesting. Google is aiming to diagnose cancers, impending heart attacks or strokes, and other diseases at a much earlier stage than what is currently possible.<xps:span class="xps\_endnote">1</xps:span> Apple is making efforts to develop medical sensor-laden devices to monitor blood through the skin and to analyze glucose levels through tears. Intel has launched a wearable-to-analytics devices program called \`\`A-wear'' that directly ties wearable devices with big data analytics (BDA) technology. Samsung has a joint venture with medical professionals at the University of California, San Francisco, to accelerate validation and commercialization of promising new sensors, algorithms, and digital health technologies for preventive health solutions. These initiatives have led to a more healthy competition in the healthcare-wearable device market, and innovations from the existing players and new entrants are constantly being introduced to the market, which are energizing the wearable technology industry.

BDA technology is believed to transform all industries, and it sure can bring new opportunities for healthcare-wearable device providers. First, BDA holds the promise of making effective usage of large quantities of data to support a wide range of healthcare functions, including disease surveillance, clinical decision support systems, and individual healthcare management.<xps:span class="xps\_endnote">2</xps:span> Second, BDA can offer insight to help reduce healthcare cost and improve healthcare quality and efficiency through analysis of the substantial amount of healthcare data [2]. Third, BDA can enhance the firm’s consumer relationship, gain insight into consumer behaviors, conduct accurate market analysis, offer customized services, and optimize production and operation strategies.

However, the implementation of BDA technology in healthcare-wearable devices faces several challenges. The first challenge is that the consumer’s privacy and security concern might be heightened by the BDA capability on a wearable device. As a device with BDA has the tendency to collect personal information beyond the regular healthcare data, users might be concerned with the potential data breach. Even though firms make investment on BDA and can offer better healthcare services, whether consumers will embrace the wearable devices with BDA is still uncertain. The second challenge is the cost associated with BDA. The cost of establishing the initial BDA

#

infrastructure might be substantial for a firm, and the ongoing maintenance cost, storage cost, and data analysis cost are also nontrivial. Whether it is worthwhile to implement BDA should be based on the cost and benefit analysis of a specific firm. The third challenge is whether the firm’s BDA team has the ability to generate the right value/services for consumers that will yield consumer appreciation, further adoption of the device, and subscription for additional services.

As they face the challenges and benefits of BDA, some firms choose to embrace the BDA technology in their devices (e.g., Intel and Jawbone), while others choose to wait (e.g., Coodon, Fitbit, and Garmin). Meanwhile, consumers have different predispositions toward healthcare-wearable devices with BDA capability and different willingness to pay toward various product qualities and prices. BDA in wearable devices serves as a two-edged sword: on the one hand, it offers promises for a better healthcare service for its users; on the other hand, it associates with some resistance factors that cause users to shy away from BDA devices. The most significant resistance factor is the consumer’s heightened concern for privacy [3] and the perception of increased privacy risks [4, 5]. For example, to provide personalized services, some forms of tracking must be employed to build a customized database with personal information. This increases consumers’ privacy concerns and perceived risks. In addition, consumers’ resistance to BDA technology could also stem from the perception of a shorter battery life in BDA devices or the inconvenience associated with downloading apps, uploading data, receiving alerts, etc. Given that the issue of privacy was highlighted every time consumers thought of BDA and personal information [3-5], we have chosen privacy risk as the representative resistance factor for consumers.

In our model, we define two groups of consumers: the efficiency-oriented consumer and the privacy-oriented consumer. The efficiency-oriented consumer is defined as the consumer whose utilities generated from the BDA-enabled healthcare efficiency outweighs the disutility caused by the resistance factors. This group of consumers might be more prone to purchase a wearable device with BDA capability, whereas their counterpart, whom we call the privacy-oriented consumers, might prefer a device without BDA capability. Whether a firm should use a BDA strategy to gain competitive advantage over its rivals and whether the BDA can attract enough efficiency-oriented consumers to be economically advantageous are important questions to investigate.

In this study, we investigate the healthcare-wearable device market from an economic perspective, which differs and complements prior research that focuses on the technology perspective, i.e., design of specific devices [6-8]. We explore the firm’s BDA strategy in the competition of wearable device market in conjunction with their price and quality strategies. Given that various products (i.e., horizontal differentiation) with different quality levels (i.e., vertical differentiation) are in the healthcare-wearable device market and consumers have heterogeneous preferences on both product types (i.e., the efficiency-oriented consumer might be more prone to purchase a wearable device with BDA and the privacy-oriented consumer might prefer a device without BDA) and product qualities, we employ the two-dimensional product differentiation framework [9] to study the competition between the firms with and without BDA capability in the wearable device market. Considering that the fractions of efficiency-oriented consumers and privacy-oriented consumers are

#

distinctly different in the market, we extended the baseline model to incorporate differentiated consumer densities [10].

The model demonstrates how healthcare-wearable device firms should strategically adjust their optimal quality and price levels according to the dynamics of consumers’ predisposed preferences (firms’ consumer density) and BDA efficiency (consumer’s healthcare utility from BDA). In addition, the model extension on the diverse consumer density not only captures better the reality of healthcare-wearable device market but also overcomes some of the criticisms faced by the classical two-dimensional product differentiation model, which has the tendency of overpredicting the firm’s advantage on BDA implementation. For practice, business managers are guided by our results on strategically deciding their optimal BDA strategies as well as pricing and quality strategies. Social planners may also seek guidelines from this research to conduct better policies to promote the adoption of BDA in healthcare. This research has the potential to provide theoretical foundations for the research and practice of applying BDA and wearable technology in healthcare.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on product differentiation and the applications of BDA and wearable technology in healthcare. Sections 3 and 4 present the baseline and extended models, respectively. Section 5 summarizes the effect of consumer density and BDA investment on firms’ optimal strategies and social welfare. Section 6 concludes the paper.

## <H1>2. Literature Review

<H2>2.1 Two-dimensional Product Differentiation Model

#

Lancaster [11] has defined two types (dimensions) of product differentiation: horizontal differentiation reflects the variety of product and vertical differentiation reflects the quality of product. Scholars have since proven the existence of equilibrium in the two-dimensional model and applied it in various cases, as recorded in Table 1. Compared with the one-dimensional model, the two-dimensional product differentiation model can better capture the reality of the market dynamics and provide additional insights that might be otherwise overlooked [9].

However, earlier two-dimensional models assume that consumers are uniformly distributed between firms, which miss to capture the consumer’s distinctive predisposed preferences on healthcare-wearable device. In this paper, we enhance the earlier two-dimensional model by considering differentiated consumer densities [10] to examine the competition between healthcare-wearable device firms with and without BDA investment. We believe that this modification yields more realistic and robust analytical results.

<H2>2.2 BDA and Wearable Devices in Healthcare and Health Information Systems

BDA is proved to be effective in managing and utilizing the tremendous amount of healthcare data in recent years [2, 33]. BDA shows distinctive advantages in reducing healthcare cost and improving the healthcare quality, as it integrates massive healthcare data with advanced technological infrastructures [33, 34]. Researchers have identified the sources of healthcare big data as genomics-driven big data (such as sequencing data and gene expression), payer-provider big data (including electronic health record (EHR), personal health record (PHR), patient feedback and responses, and insurance records), and real-time data generated from wearable devices [35, 36]. Both information systems (IS) and medical researchers have made technological efforts in applying BDA in healthcare, such as clinical relevance identification among different diseases [37], the control and prevention of HIV/AIDS [38], and healthcare personalization [39].

After recognizing that most pioneering studies on big data focus on technology aspects, Wang et al. [40] have proposed five strategies for big data implementation in healthcare, including implementation of big data governance, development of information-sharing organizational culture, training of data usage for key personnel, incorporation of cloud computing to big data, and generation of new business ideas. Heitmueller et al. [41] indicate that clear objectives, alternative examples, discussions of barriers, and proposed levers are required to be considered when making public policies to promote big data adoption in healthcare.

Another advancement of e-health is the application of wearable devices in the healthcare field. Researchers have discussed users’ response to healthcare-wearable devices. Both Steele et al. [42] and Fraile et al. [43] have shown users’ positive attitudes toward the application of wearable devices in healthcare. Hensel et al. [44] indicate that the perceived ease of use plays an important role in the adoption of healthcare-wearable devices. Claes et al. [45] demonstrate that the main reason the elderly should adopt wearable devices is that these devices allow them to live independently, safely, and longer at their own homes. Meanwhile, the benefits of wearable devices in healthcare are widely studied. Berger et al. [46] show that the \`\`real-world'' healthcare data generated from healthcare-wearable devices hold great promise in improving healthcare efficiency and our abilities to develop new cures and treatments. By conducting a discrete event simulation, Radhakrishnan et al.

[47] found that the healthcare-wearable devices yielded beneficial results in reducing patient denials and serving more patients. In addition, healthcare-wearable devices provide doctors with better abilities to monitor and supervise their patients’ wellness [48].

Observing the distinctive advantages of wearable devices in healthcare, pioneer research works have focused on the design of specific wearable devices in the healthcare sector [6-8, 49] and the development of healthcare-wearable technologies [50, 51]. Meanwhile, BDA in wearable devices constantly brings streams of innovative applications in the healthcare sector [36] and offers great promises for future healthcare. However, the research in the field of applying BDA and wearable technology in healthcare is still in its infancy stage. The current studies mainly focus on the conceptual definition of key factors such as BDA and sensor-based healthcare [52, 53], the opportunities and challenges of combining BDA and wearable devices in healthcare applications [54, 55], and the development of technologies for various applications within the specific context [56, 57].

Some of the previous studies have applied economic principles to the health information system (HIS) research. Ozdemir et al. [58] investigated the incentives for healthcare providers to adopt and share EHRs by considering EHR’s benefits on improving healthcare quality and consumer’s switching cost. They found that an independent PHR platform can create incentives for service providers to share health records electronically. Meanwhile, Li and Wu [59] proposed a stylized two-dimensional product differentiation model to measure the effects of network externality on the competition in the healthcare-wearable device market and firm’s optimal product compatibility strategies. Their results indicate that health information technology (IT) providers would provide

incompatible products in horizontal-dominance differentiation and provide compatible products in vertical-dominance differentiation.

In this paper, we extend the previous literature on economics of HIS by modeling the opportunities and challenges of an emerging technology – BDA, and applying the two-dimensional product differentiation models to a specific healthcare-wearable market. Our result can provide guidance to healthcare-wearable device manufacturers to conduct better BDA strategies on how to adjust their optimal quality levels and prices with different market situations in terms of consumer’s predisposed preferences, BDA efficiency, and the unit cost on product quality.

<H1>3. The Baseline Model

We establish a two-dimensional product differentiation model [9] to study the dynamic competition in the healthcare-wearable device market. We refer the healthcare-wearable device market as \`\`market'' in the rest of the paper. We assume the market offers different types of products (horizontal differentiation) and the products are of different qualities (vertical differentiation). Competitions between two types of firms with different BDA strategies are studied: Firm 1 provides wearable devices implementing BDA technology, while Firm 2 does not implement BDA technology. Both firms offer similar wearable devices that are substitutes, and each consumer purchases only one unit of the product. Both types of firms choose optimal quality and price levels to compete. $s _ { 1 }$ and $s _ { 2 }$ denote the quality levels offered by Firms 1 and 2, respectively, and $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ denote the prices charged by Firms 1 and 2, respectively.

Two dimensions of consumer preferences are considered. Horizontally, consumers have different inclinations toward the wearable device with and without BDA capability. On the one hand, the BDA capability in wearable devices could enhance healthcare service efficiency for consumers by recognizing their healthcare conditions, identifying risks for serious health problems in an earlier stage, and providing personalized healthcare services [36, 60, 61]. On the other hand, BDA technology might also generate resistance from consumers because of a variety of potentially unfavorable factors associated with a BDA device. As discussed earlier, the most significant resistance factors are the consumers’ heightened privacy concern [3] and increased perceived privacy risks [4, 5].

A higher level of BDA efficiency might generate more privacy concerns from consumers because the device will collect more detailed personal information [4, 5]. We assume that consumers are uniformly distributed on a Hotelling line depending on their preferences and attitude toward BDA capability. The consumer who values more about healthcare efficiency is located closer to position 0, and the consumer who has more privacy and security concerns is located closer to position 1. In addition, the consumers incur fit costs when purchasing products, which increase the distance between locations of their most-preferred product and the purchased product. The fit cost of the consumer who is located at <sup>\*</sup>l and purchases a product at location l is modeled as $t \left| l ^ { * } - l \right|$ | , where t represents the unit fit cost.

Vertically, consumers have preferences over the quality of a wearable device. According to DeLone and McLean’s model of IS success [62], in the context of healthcare-wearable devices, product quality is specifically regarded as the consumer’s evaluation or perception of the product’s functionality, fashion, comfort, battery duration, informativeness of health conditions, information accuracy, etc. The higher the product quality is, the higher the utility it brings to the consumer. We use  to denote the consumer’s preference on the product quality, where  is uniformly distributed over a unit line, i.e. $\Theta \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . The coordinate of $( x , \theta )$ in the plane represents the consumer’s position toward the product.

We assume that more investments on BDA development yield better BDA-related services and, in turn, enhance the utility of the consumer who purchases a product with BDA capability. Therefore, we use the term BDA efficiency  $( \delta \in ( 0 . 1 ] )$ to denote Firm 1’s effort/investment in improving BDA, which directly increases the consumer’s healthcare utility. The consumer is assumed to have a reservation utility v for wearable devices, and v is sufficiently large so that the market is fully covered. Consumers can obtain an additional utility of $\delta \nu$ when purchasing a wearable device with BDA capability.

Hence, located at $( x , \theta )$ position, a consumer’s utility on purchasing from Firm 1 and Firm 2 is given as follows, while Firm 1 has BDA capability:

$$
\begin{array}{l} u _ {1} = (1 + \delta) v + \theta \cdot s _ {1} - t x - p _ {1} \\ u _ {2} = v + \theta \cdot s _ {2} - t (1 - x) - p _ {2} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

Consumers maximize their net utilities to decide whether to purchase from Firm 1 or Firm 2. The indifference curve/line is defined as the set of consumers who have the same net utilities when purchasing from both firms. By setting the utilities equal, $u _ { 1 } = u _ { 2 }$ , the indifference line is given as the following:

$$
\theta (x) = \frac {p _ {1} - p _ {2} - \delta v - t}{s _ {1} - s _ {2}} + \frac {2 t x}{s _ {1} - s _ {2}}\tag{2}
$$

The indifference line divides the market into two parts, and each part represents the market share from a firm. Firm 1’s market share is composed of consumers whose utility $u _ { 1 }$ is higher than $u _ { 2 }$ and vice versa. s represents the quality differentiation between the firms. Quality-fit (Q-F) ratio, $\lambda = \Delta s / t$ , is introduced to denote the dimension that is more preferred by consumers [9]. The slope of the indifference line depends on the Q-F ratio. Four possible values of the Q-F ratio are depicted in Fig. 1.

In the cases when the indifference line intersects both θ = 0 and θ =1, each firm captures those consumers located near it for all values of  . The consumer’s preference on the product fit dominates the utility. We denote this situation as the horizontal dominance. However, if the indifference line intersects both $x = 0$ and $x = 1$ , one firm captures consumers with higher preference on quality and the other firm captures consumers with lower preference on quality; we denote this situation as vertical dominance. There are other cases when the indifference lines intersect with both vertical and horizontal lines; however, in these cases, no Nash equilibrium is found. Therefore, we focus on the four cases in the rest of the paper.

We assume that the two types of firms have a similar cost structure. The firm’s costs include the marginal cost of producing the device and the additional cost associated with providing a higher-quality product. We assume a constant marginal cost of c and a quadratic cost structure for quality $k \cdot s ^ { 2 }$ , where k is the unit cost on quality and s is the product quality. Generally speaking, to implement BDA technology, the firms need to hire talents with BDA skills (i.e., NoSQL, MapReduce,

## <H1>6. Conclusion and Discussions

Using the healthcare-wearable device market as an example, we investigate the effects of the firm’s BDA efficiency and the consumer’s different predisposed preferences (consumer density) on the dynamics of competition between firms with and without BDA capability. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to investigate the firm’s BDA strategy in healthcare sector from an economic perspective, and we believe that it contributes and complements to the current mainstream studies on BDA, which focus on the technological perspectives. A two-dimensional product differentiation model is developed to examine how the firm’s BDA efficiency and consumer density interact with firms’ optimal strategies on price and quality decisions. Both horizontal and vertical differentiations are investigated to study a market with differentiated products and diverse product quality levels. Our model demonstrates how firms’ optimal quality levels, prices, market demands, and profits are affected by the alterations in consumer density and BDA efficiency under various scenarios and market conditions.

From our analytical results, we have two main streams of findings. First, we summarize the effects of BDA efficiency and consumer density on the firms’ quality and price differentiation. We find that the consumer density of the firm with BDA capability (Firm 1) is more likely to intensify the quality differentiation between the firms since it increases the higher-quality firm’s optimal quality level while decreasing the lower-quality firm’s optimal quality level under both horizontal and

vertical-dominance differentiation. However, Firm 1’s consumer density does not significantly affect the firms’ price differentiation because it has negative (positive) effects on both firms’ optimal prices in Case 1 (Case 2). Meanwhile, the firm’s BDA efficiency always increases both quality differentiation and price differentiation between firms as it always positively (negatively) affects the higher-quality (lower-quality) firm’s optimal quality and price levels in each scenario under both baseline and extended models.

Second, we identify various conditions under which the qualities of biomedical and healthcare services can be promoted through the firm’s investment in BDA technology. Under the same assumption of consumer densities (baseline model), the quality of biomedical and healthcare services can be promoted when the firm’s net cost associated with BDA efficiency is low and the multiple of the firms’ unit cost on product quality and the consumer’s unit fit cost reach a certain level ( 36kt 1). When relaxing the same assumption of consumer densities (extended model), we find that when the firm’s unit net cost on BDA efficiency is low, the quality of the biomedical and healthcare service can also be promoted under two scenarios: (1) firm with BDA capability loses some of its natural consumers to the rival or (2) firm with BDA capability gains some of its rival’s natural consumers, and the consumer density of its rival is lower than a certain threshold $\left( 1 - \mu < 3 6 k t \right)$ ).

Our extension of the two-dimensional differentiation model with diverse consumer density not only captures well the reality of BDA’s effect on the e-health product market but also overcomes some of the criticisms faced by the classical two-dimensional product differentiation model, which has the tendency of overpredicting a firm’s advantage on BDA implementation. We believe our enhanced and extended product differentiation models can provide a theoretical framework for future research. <H2>6.1 Implications for Practice

This research also provides several practical implications for healthcare-wearable devices manufacturers and social planners. First, our findings offer guidelines for the firm with BDA capability on how to balance its investment on BDA technology and its product quality and price levels. For example, under the same consumer density assumption, if its current quality level is higher than its rival, the firm with BDA capability should consider raising both its optimal quality and price levels after increasing its investment in BDA. Otherwise, it should decrease the optimal quality and price levels after increasing the BDA investment. Similar strategies can be applied under certain conditions when the firms’ consumer densities are different. For example, in horizontal-dominance differentiation, if the firm with BDA strategy gains some natural consumers from its rival and its consumer density is relatively small $\left( \mu < 1 - 3 6 k t \right)$ , it should decrease its optimal quality and price levels after investing more in BDA technology; in vertical-dominance differentiation, if the firm with BDA strategy offers higher quality with a relatively small consumer density $\left( \mu < \left( t - 2 \delta \nu \right) / ( 2 t ) \right)$ ) or it offers lower quality but with a large consumer density $( \mu > ( t - 2 \delta \nu ) / ( 2 t ) ) ,$ ), it also should decrease the optimal quality level after investing more in BDA technology.

Second, our results provide insights to the firm without BDA capability on how to adjust its optimal quality and price levels to compete with the firm with BDA technology. Under the same consumer density assumption, the firm without BDA capability responds to the increase in rival’s

BDA investment by increasing its optimal quality and price levels if its current quality level is higher than its rival and decreasing its quality and price levels if its current quality level is lower than the rival. The firm without the BDA technology also should choose the same strategies as above when consumer densities are different and certain conditions are met. The various scenarios are similar to the ones faced by the firm with BDA capability, and the findings are not repeated here.

Third, our results offer advice for both firms on how to adjust optimal quality levels and prices after observing the different consumer densities. In horizontal-dominance differentiation, when a firm’s (Firm 1 or Firm 2) own consumer density is increased it should increase the quality level if (1) the firm with BDA capability loses some natural consumers or (2) if the firm without BDA capability loses some of its natural consumers and its consumer density is lower than a threshold (1 –μ < 36kt). In addition, when a firm loses (gains) some natural consumers, the firm should reduce (increase) its price to compete if it has a higher consumer density. However, in vertical-dominance differentiation, when a firm’s (Firm 1 or Firm 2) consumer density is increased, it should increase the quality level if Firm 1’s BDA efficiency is higher than a certain threshold (δ > (1 – 2μ)t /(2v)).

Furthermore, our results provide insights for social planners on potential policy intervention to promote better quality in healthcare services. Regardless of market conditions, the first thing social planners can do is to utilize the policy to help reduce the firm’s unit net cost on BDA efficiency, i.e., tax incentives, etc. Once the unit net cost on BDA efficiency has been lowered, the healthcare service quality may be increased; that is, the firm with BDA capability has incentive to invest more in BDA efficiency and provide higher quality products, and consumers can enjoy the higher quality products or

#

services with BDA functions. Below are some suggestions for policy interventions for social planners to promote better healthcare and biomedical services after reducing the unit net cost on BDA efficiency: (1) when both firms have the same consumer densities, social planners can find ways to help fulfill a certain condition ( 36kt 1) with respect to the costs associated with consumers and firms; (2) when consumers have different predisposed preferences on BDA technology (a) social planners can take measures (i.e., improving wearable device’s healthcare efficiency and enhancing consumer’s privacy awareness associated with BDA, etc.) to enhance the competitive advantages of the firm without BDA strategy or (b) social planners can promote the devices with BDA capability to attract more consumers to enjoy BDA-enhanced services when the consumer density of Firm 2 is lower than a certain threshold.

To summarize, we have developed a two-dimensional differentiation model with different consumer densities to study wearable devices market competition. We provide useful insights for wearable devices manufacturers, including suggestions on how firms should adjust quality investment and price levels with respect to a firm’s BDA efficiency and how they should adjust the quality and price levels and BDA investments with respect to changes in consumer densities. In addition, the suggestions for social planners on how to conduct better policies to promote better healthcare service are proposed on the basis of our results.

<H2>6.2 Limitations and Future Research

Although the extended two-dimensional differentiation model captures some more market reality, as this is the novel study to explore the BDA effect on wearable device market competition,

#

there are some limitations in this study. First, we assume that the firms simultaneously choose the quality levels and prices in the two-stage game. However, the firms might enter the market sequentially. As most of the firms in the market have not invested in BDA, the firms with BDA capability could be regarded as potential entrants. Therefore, future research can extend this work to incorporate a sequential entry into the model, and the dynamics of the competition might be more interesting. Second, we have assumed an exogenous BDA efficiency and a nonzero unit net cost associated with it, and ideally, we should solve for the optimal equilibrium BDA efficiency level for Firm 1. In addition, although we could analyze the effects of BDA efficiency and consumer density on firms’ optimal quality, we did not obtain the closed-form solution for optimal quality levels in vertical-dominance differentiation because of model complexity. However, given the multiple dimensions of differentiation and various factors of influences we want to capture, it is mathematically impossible to solve the equilibrium with the BDA efficiency and get the optimal quality in the vertical-dominance case. Future research can decrease the dimensions of differentiation and employ numerical simulation methods to seek this optimization. Third, we assume consumer’s reservation utility is sufficiently large so that the market is fully covered. Future research can extend the model to study the firms’ behavior under partially covered market assumption. As the wearable healthcare device market is maturing, a partially covered market might soon be a reality. Incorporating intensified competition in the partially covered market might promise more thorough and profound results.

## C. Other Cases

In addition to the horizontal- and vertical-dominance cases that were investigated in our model, there are also some other cases when the indifference line interacts with horizontal (i.e., $x = 0 \mathrm { o r } x = 1 )$ and vertical lines (i.e., $\textstyle \theta = 0 \ { \mathrm { ~ o r ~ } } \theta = 1 $ ). All these other possible scenarios are shown in Fig. 5.

After solving all these scenarios, we find that there are no Nash equilibria existing in these cases. Here, we take the example of the first scenario to show how we make the adjustment, because other scenarios have the same process and reason to indicate the inexistence of Nash equilibrium. In the first scenario, the indifference line intersects both x = 0 and θ =1 at $\theta _ { \imath }$ and $x _ { _ 2 }$ , respectively. The demand functions are given as

$$
q _ {1} = \frac {\left(1 - \theta_ {1}\right) x _ {2}}{2} = \frac {\left(p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right)}
$$

$$
q _ {2} = 1 - q _ {1} = \frac {4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) - \left(p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right)}
$$

The FOCs of profits on prices in the second stage require that

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {1}}{\partial p _ {1}} = \frac {(p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2}) (p _ {2} - 3 p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2} + c)}{4 t (s _ {1} - s _ {2})} = 0
$$

$$
\frac {\partial \pi_ {2}}{\partial p _ {2}} = \frac {4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) - \left(p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) \left(3 p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2} + c\right)}{4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right)} = 0
$$

To simply the FOCs, we obtain that

$$
p _ {2} - 3 p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2} + c = 0\tag{1}
$$

$$
4 t \left(s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) - \left(p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2}\right) \left(3 p _ {2} - p _ {1} + \delta v + t + s _ {1} - s _ {2} + c\right) = 0\tag{2}

From (1), we get that $p _ { { _ 2 } } = 3 p _ { { _ 1 } } - \delta \nu - t - s _ { { _ 1 } } + s _ { { _ 2 } } - c$ . Then, we substitute it into (2). We obtain that $4 t ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - [ 2 p _ { 1 } - 2 \delta \nu - 2 t - 2 ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - c ] [ 1 0 p _ { 1 } - 4 \delta \nu - 4 t - 4 ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - 4 c ] = 0$ . In this case, the

$$

$$

Therefore, $4 t ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - [ 2 p _ { 1 } - 2 \delta \nu - 2 t - 2 ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - c ] [ 1 0 p _ { 1 } - 4 \delta \nu - 4 t - 4 ( s _ { 1 } - s _ { 2 } ) - 4 c ] > 0$ always hold.

In other words, there is no equilibrium in this case. For the same reason, all the other scenarios as shown in Fig. 5 also prove the inexistence of Nash equilibrium.

## E. Other Cases

In addition to the cases that were investigated in the extended model, there are also some other possible scenarios as shown in Fig. 6.

The first two figures in Fig. 6 show all the possible scenarios in which the indifference line interacts with horizontal (i.e., x = 0 or x = 1) and vertical lines (i.e., θ = 0 or θ =1). Each blue line in the figure represents a certain scenario. The reason and calculation are the same as those for the baseline model shown in Appendix EC; we can conclude that there is no Nash equilibrium in the scenarios as shown in the first two figures in Fig. 6.

In addition to these cases, there is also a special case: each firm loses some natural consumers in horizontal dominance as shown in the third and fourth figures in Fig. 6. The complexity of the model makes it impossible to obtain the analytical results in this special case. However, the results and insights of this case can be derived from Case 1 and Case 2 in the extended model. The reason is that when each firm loses some natural consumers, one firm must get more consumers than the number of its lost consumers. Therefore, after considering the tradeoff between new gained consumers and lost consumers, the market situation is that either Firm 1 loses more natural consumers (Case 1) or Firm 1 gains more natural consumers from Firm 2 (Case 2). Hence, in essence, the case (i.e., each firm loses some natural consumers) that has not been analyzed would not challenge our final results.
