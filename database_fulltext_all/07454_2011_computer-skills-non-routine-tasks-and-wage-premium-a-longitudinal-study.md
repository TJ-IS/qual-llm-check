# Computer skills, non-routine tasks, and wage premium: A longitudinal study

Gang Peng ⇑, Rangamohan V. Eunni <sup>1</sup>

Department of Management, Williamson College of Business Administration, Youngstown State University, Youngstown, OH 44555, United States

a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 10 February 2009 Received in revised form 28 January 2011 Accepted 13 September 2011 Available online 22 October 2011

Keywords: Computer skills Wage premium Labor economics Non-routine tasks

## a b s t r a c t

In this study, we investigate whether and how employees’ computer skills are rewarded in the workplace applying the theoretical lens of labor economics. Analyzing data from the Current Population Surveys for the period 1984–2003, we found strong evidence that employees are rewarded both for the depth as well as the breadth of their computer skills, and that the computer skills wage premium follows an inverted-U shape over time. We also found that the complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks is a major factor contributing to the wage premium, and that such contribution increases over time.

## 1. Introduction

The past three decades have witnessed increased penetration of information technology (IT) into the workplace. While there are numerous studies delineating the impact of IT on firms, organizations, and even industries (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1996), there is a paucity of research that investigated how IT has affected employees in the workplace, particularly in non-IT industries and in relation to the general (i.e., non-IT) workforce. Our study contributes to this important literature by exploring whether employees using computers at workplace in general are compensated higher as compared to those not using computers, and if so, what factors drive the wage premium, and whether there is a pattern in changes of such premium over time.

The impact of IT skills on employee compensation is an important theoretical and managerial issue. Employee compensation is a critical factor in attracting and retaining professionals with necessary skills, particularly IT skills, which are increasingly required by the modern work practices (Bresnahan et al., 2002; Levina and Xin, 2007). A compensation schedule reflecting the levels of IT skills could potentially motivate employees to acquire those skills, thus influencing employees decisions for developing IT-related human capital (Ang et al., 2002). Moreover, a computer skill-based wage premium could be expected to increase the commitment and level of effort of employees, and thereby enhance worker productivity (Mortensen, 2003). Consequently, investigating the potential impact of IT skills on employee compensation would affect education, training, and career choices of individuals, and would have profound implications for educational policies, future supply of a qualified workforce, and, even for the overall competitiveness of a nation.

In this study, we draw upon the data from the Current Population Survey for the years 1984, 1989, 1993, 1997, 2001, and 2003, and apply insights from labor economics to derive and test hypotheses relating employee computer skills to wage premium. The longitudinal nature of the study enabled us to take a closer look inside the black box of the computer skill-based wage return, and better understand how computers are used in the workplace, the potential impact of the breadth and the depth of computer skills, and the competitive dynamics generating the wage premium.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypotheses

## 2.1. Return on investment in computer skills

The question why employees possessing differing types and levels of computer skill sets might be paid differently has long attracted the attention of researchers. Several theories from the labor economics perspective were proposed to explain the wage differentials (Mortensen, 2003). The most well-known among them arguably is the human capital theory, which postulates that earnings are a payoff to human capital, and that education and work experiences are the two major determinants of wage differentials (Mincer, 1974).

While deriving the causal relationship between education and wages, Mincer (1974) argued that enhancing the quality of one’s human capital by improving educational qualifications is a time consuming process. Each additional period of schooling or job training not only involves direct cost outlays but also reduces the working life span of employees and defers their future earnings. Therefore, the level of the deferred income stream should more than offset the total cost of the current investment before an individual decides to opt for more education. The human capital theory also provides a cogent argument for the causal relationship between computer skills and wage return. Employee skills entail capital investment, and acquisition of computer skills involves significant investment in human capital. In order for employees to invest in acquiring such skills they should expect to be compensated adequately for mastering such skills. Thus, only if the investment can be justified by increased wage returns in future would employees be motivated to develop the computer skills.

Another important factor that might contribute to computer skills wage premium is the skill-biased technological change (SBTC). Studies have documented a wide adoption and diffusion of computers and related technologies over the past three decades. This has brought about further changes in the relative productivity of various types of workers and has shifted the demand toward more skilled workers (Bound and Johnson, 1992; Bresnahan et al., 2002; Murphy and Welch, 1992). In addition, increased use of computers at workplace has triggered complementary organizational redesign and changes in products and services, which in turn have further increased the demand for computer equipment (Bresnahan et al., 2002). For example, expanded Internet connectivity and developments in web-based technologies in the late 1990s significantly spurred organizations’ demand for Internet-based computer skills (Ang et al., 2002), and new organizational structures featuring decentralized decision making and improved information flows have increased investment in and installation of computer equipment (Brynjolfsson, 2003). Indeed, several empirical studies have provided evidence of a robust correlation between the use of computer-based technologies and increased demand for skilled workforce during the period (e.g., Bresnahan et al., 2002; Goldin and Karz, 1998). However, the supply of such skills might not be able to catch up with the demand and therefore, the return to computer skills tends to increase (Levina and Xin, 2007). Based on these arguments, it would be reasonable to formulate the following hypothesis:

H1. Ceteris paribus, employees who possess computer skills at work are more likely to earn higher wages than those without such skills.

Along with the technological advances, in recent decades, the labor market itself was undergoing important changes as well. One such change was that employees are required to possess a broader variety as well as a higher level of skills (Osterman, 1995), among which computer skills occupy a prominent place (Bresnahan et al., 2002). The computer skills of employees are not uniform, but vary significantly. Both the variety and the sophistication of the skills possessed would have a direct bearing on employee compensation. Skill variety focuses on the scope or breadth of the skills, while skill sophistication refers to the depth or levels of these skills. Possessing a broader skill set enables an employee to work on a greater variety of tasks that need multiple and wider skills, thereby improving their job autonomy and flexibility, reducing labor cost, and enhancing job quality and worker productivity (Murray and Gerhart, 1998). The depth of the skill set implies greater knowledge and stronger competencies, which would allow employees to tackle more complex problems, improve job quality, and speed up work flows and processes (Shaw et al., 2005). From the perspective of human capital theory (Mincer, 1974; Mortensen, 2003), employees would be compensated based on both the breadth as well as depth of their skill sets. Therefore, we propose:

H2a. The wider the variety of a worker’s computer skills, the greater would be the wage premium.

H2b. The higher the level of a worker’s computer skills, the greater would be the wage premium.

Another important objective of this study is to reveal the pattern of computer skills wage premium over time. To this end, we believe the dynamics in the labor market over time are particularly relevant (Ang et al., 2002; Levina and Xin, 2007). Recognizing that wage is the price for labor (Mortensen, 2003), the determination of return to computer skills has to take into account both the supply and demand for such skills in the labor market.

As discussed earlier, one of the important dynamics in recent years is the SBTC, which has increased the demand for computer skills and further brought wage premium to computer skills (Bound and Johnson, 1992). This is particularly true when the gap between the supply and demand of these skills is large, which was exactly the case back in the 1990s (Ang et al., 2002). Nevertheless, we expect that the increase in computer skills wage premium will not be able to sustain over a long period of time. Several factors could contribute to the potential downturn in this wage premium. First, a promise of higher pay for computer skills due to the SBTC tends to induce a large number of workers to acquire such skills thus increasing their supply, and thereby altering the dynamics in the labor market. Second, the dot-com bubble burst had also eased the demand for computer skills in the early 2000s, as evidenced by the statistics of US Department of Labor (Levina and Xin, 2007). Third, in order to meet the increasing demand for computer-related talents, many employers are known to off-shore jobs to foreign workers outside the US (Blinder, 2007; Friedman, 2005). All these factors could potentially put a downward pressure on the computer skills wage premium over time. As a result, one would expect to see the wage premium for computer skills spike towards the end of the 1990s and early 2000, and then start to decline thereafter. Hence, the following hypothesis:

H3. Overall, the wage premium attributable to the possession of computer skills would increase initially and then decrease gradually, such that the computer skills wage premium follows an inverted-U shape over time.

## 2.2. Computer skills and non-routine tasks

Studies suggest that the impact of IT in the workplace is not uniform (Cappelli, 1993). For instance, self-service kiosks are now replacing staffed check-in counters at airports to dispense boarding passes. Radiologists employ computerized imaging to help insert stents into arteries to diagnose aneurism in the human brain (Levy and Murnane, 2006). While computers serve as a substitute for human skills in the former case, they complement such skills in the later. Thus, the relationship between computer skills and wage premium might also depend upon how they substitute or complement the tasks that employees perform in the workplace.

To explore the variable roles that computers play in the workplace, following Autor et al. (2003) we propose that work could be conceptualized as a series of tasks that may consist of routine and non-routine components. Routine tasks include manual as well as cognitive tasks which follow well-defined and explicit rules, thereby making them easily programmable. Non-routine tasks, on the other hand, are not well-defined, and hence are not readily programmable. Since computers are capable of performing repetitive activities more efficiently, routine tasks are more amenable to substitution by computers. However, IT, at least in its current state of the art, is a relatively poor substitute for the mental capabilities of people deployed in carrying out non-routine tasks (Brynjolfsson, 2003; Mandel, 2002). Rather than replace the human brain, computers aid and complement it in performing non-routine tasks such as complex problemsolving, cognitive analysis, and decision-making. Therefore, those workers who could apply technologies to support non-routine job tasks could be expected to earn more by becoming more productive and valuable in the workplace. Thus we have:

## H4. The computer skills wage premium would be higher for employees who perform non-routine tasks.

According to Autor et al. (2003), though the functionalities of computing machinery remained relatively stable over the past 200 years, the prices of computer technologies have fallen significantly. The exogenous price decline of computer equipment has a major impact on capital investment and task composition; this further changes the pattern of complementarity between computers and non-routine tasks over time. Declining computer prices and the increasing adoption of computing equipment allow more routine tasks to be computerized and for workers to specialize in non-routine tasks and thereby become more productive with them. Thus, we are likely to see an increase in return to the use of computer skills in performing non-routine tasks over time.

Furthermore, as suggested by Milgrom and Roberts (1992), when complementary factors are used in the production process, an increase in any one factor will increase the marginal productivity of the other. Thus, as computer prices continue to decline over time and computers are increasingly adopted at workplace, workers who perform non-routine tasks tend to gain increasing amount of benefits by using computers to perform such tasks (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992). Therefore, we would expect the following:

H5. The complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks would increase over time.

## 3. Data sources and methodology

The data used in this study is derived from two sources: (i) the Current Population Survey, and (ii) the Dictionary of Occupational Titles.

## 3.1. Current Population Survey

The Current Population Survey (CPS) is conducted jointly by the US Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The CPS includes a basic monthly survey and supplementary surveys, which are designed to meet a variety of additional needs.<sup>2</sup> In the years 1984, 1989, 1993, 1997, 2001, and 2003, six supplementary surveys were conducted focusing on computer and Internet use. The data for the present study are sourced from these six surveys spanning a total of 19 years. The items included in the supplementary surveys were: ‘‘Does (the person) use a computer at work?’’ and ‘‘Does (the person) use a computer at home?’’ Except in the year 1984, the surveys also included additional questions on seven forms of computer usage at work: (i) Word processing or desktop publishing, (ii) internet or e-mail, (iii) calendar or scheduling, (iv) spreadsheets or databases, (v) graphics or design, (vi) programming, and (vii) other computer tools. Based on the responses to these questions on computer usage, we defined the variable number of computer skills to measure the breadth of the computer skills an employee possesses. It is simply given by the total number of such forms of computer usage by an employee.

We further classified computer usage into three categories evidencing different levels of sophistication of computer skills required to perform various tasks in the workplace: (i) Elementary skills: Include the use of Word processing, Internet, email, calendar, and/or scheduling tools, but not other tools; (ii) intermediate skills: Refer to the use of spreadsheets, databases, graphics, and/or design, but not programming; (iii) advanced skills: Use of programming applications at work. Following this approach, each employee was placed into one of the following mutually exclusive categories based on the level of computer skills possessed: No skills, elementary skills, intermediate skills, and advanced skills. This classification is consistent with prior literature on the subject (e.g., Borghans and ter Weel, 2006; Dickerson and Green, 2004; Green et al., 2001).

The dependent variable in our study is the log of the hourly wage rate (Mincer, 1974), deflated by the GDP deflator.<sup>3</sup> Density estimation of the wage levels revealed outliers at both ends, and so we restricted our sample of observations to hourly rates ranging between \$1.50 and \$100. Further, since our study focused on non-executive workforce, we excluded from our sample employees holding managerial positions.<sup>4</sup>

## 3.2. Dictionary of Occupational Titles

The Dictionary of Occupational Titles (DOT) was originally created by the US Department of Labor to measure occupational skills and match job applicants to jobs (Cain and Treiman, 1981). The most recent version of the DOT released in 1991 contains more than 12,000 detailed occupations, which are scored on 44 criteria, including dimensions such as training time, aptitude, temperament, interest, physical demands, and environmental conditions. Typically, one or two trained occupational analysts observe and interview workers, write their job descriptions and rate the characteristics of the occupation. The rigor of the procedures and metrics make the DOT scores objective, accurate, and therefore more reliable as sources of data for research as compared to self-reported scores. Not surprisingly, the DOT scores were extensively used by researchers in sociology and economics (e.g., Autor et al., 2003). These scores were aggregated using the census occupation codes to further match the CPS survey data (Autor et al., 2003; England and Kilbourne, 1988). Since the CPS occupation codes changed substantially in 2003 as compared to earlier years, we did not attempt to match the DOT scores with the CPS survey data for this year.<sup>5</sup>

In order to quantify the level of ‘‘non-routineness’’ of the tasks required to perform a job, we adopted the non-routine task percentage method developed by Autor et al. (2003). Five different DOT variables were used to measure the non-routine content or inputs in an occupation: DCP, MATH, STS, FINGDEX, and EYEHAND. DCP stands for direction, control, and planning, and measures the non-routine interactive inputs. MATH measures quantitative reasoning required to perform a job and thus represents non-routine cognitive inputs. STS measures routine cognitive inputs at work requiring set limits, tolerances, or standards. FINGDEX stands for finger dexterity, and measures routine manual inputs. Finally, EYEHAND stands for the eye-hand-foot coordination which measures the non-routine manual inputs required to perform a task. Using these five variables, non-routine task is defined in terms of the percentage of the non-routine task component in an occupation as follows:

$$
\text { Non - routine   task } = \frac {\text { Non - routine   score }}{\text { Non - routine } + \text { Routine   score }} \times 100 \%\tag{1}
$$

where Routine score = STS + FINGDEX, and Non-routine score = DCP + MATH + EYEHAND. Table 1 provides a summary of the definitions of all the variables used in our study.

## 3.3. Methodology

We employed the ordinary least squares (OLS) model for our analyses. It is possible that employees who use computers at work might possess superior individual abilities and so they might be able to earn more, even without using computers at work. In other words, computer use might capture the unobserved individual abilities. If this is the case, then the estimated wage premium will be biased upward (Greene, 2003). To mitigate this problem, following Krueger (1993), we controlled for computer use at home as a proxy for individual abilities. In addition, we had also controlled for geographical region and industry sector in the estimation model.

## 5. Discussion

The impact of IT on individual workers has not received enough attention from information systems researchers. It has been suggested that more research should be devoted to examining how IT and computer skills have influenced employees in terms of their income, competency, employability, and career development. Our study contributes to this literature by examining whether and how computer skills affect employee wage premium. We invoked the human capital theory and the SBTC from labor economics to argue for the existence of a computer skills wage premium (Mortensen, 2003). We investigated the impact of the depth as well as breadth of computer skills, and explored possible mechanisms underlying the computer skills wage premium. Our study makes some theoretical contributions and also has practical and strategic implications.

## 5.1. Theoretical contributions

We were cognizant of the fact that computer skills come in different varieties and at various levels (Borghans and ter Weel, 2006; Dickerson and Green, 2004; Krueger, 1993), and therefore argued that the wage premium should reflect both the breadth and the depth of employees’ computer skills. The empirical evidence from the computer and Internet use supplementary surveys of the CPS 1984–2003 database supports our arguments. Thus, rather than examining computer skills as a solitary set, we extend prior literature by taking into account the variety and proficiency of such skills, and delineate a more nuanced view of the computer skills wage premium. The finding that the magnitude of returns to computers skills hinges on both their breadth and depth vindicates the arguments of both the human capital theory and the SBTC. In modern workplaces where computers and technologies have become ubiquitous, computer skills represent an important form of human capital. Employees possessing higher and broader sets of computer skills would be more valuable and productive, and therefore would be compensated more for these skills. This result also confirms the SBTC argument that increased investment in IT equipment at workplace due to exogenous computer price decline, coupled with organizational redesign and changes in products and services, has shifted the demand to more skilled and educated employees, particularly those commanding relevant computer skills. As a result, IT-skilled employees are compensated higher in the labor market.

Our study also adds to the understanding of the mechanisms that underlay the computer skills wage return. Prior studies on the return to computer skills tended to focus on the skills per se (e.g., Krueger, 1993). In this study, we argued that computer skills wage return not only depends on the breadth and depth of the skills, but also on the tasks to which computer skills are applied. To this end, we extend recent findings from labor economics (e.g., Autor et al., 2003) and propose that the complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks may well be the underlying mechanism driving the computer skills wage premium. To test this proposal empirically, we applied an objective and innovative approach to quantify non-routine tasks using the DOT scores (Autor et al., 2003). There are several advantages to this approach: First, DOT scores are directly linked to census job categories, thus making this approach particularly helpful for job analysis and compensation research. Second, the scores are based on experts’ observations using a large sample, and thus are less prone to biases identified in self-reported surveys. Finally, the DOT scores are publicly available, and therefore allow for easy replication and validation of studies based on them.

We observed that while computers could substitute routine tasks, they also complement non-routine tasks such as complex communication, problem solving, and expert thinking. Consequently, our results suggest that examining the returns to computer skills in isolation, ignoring the tasks to which these skills are applied, is not a satisfactory approach. More importantly, we take advantage of the longitudinal nature of the CPS survey to show how the computer skills wage premium and the complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks change over time. Our results reveal that the computer skills wage premium followed an inverted-U shape over the past two decades. This finding is consistent with the SBTC and confirms results from prior literature that the dynamics in the labor market is crucial in determining the wage returns to IT skills (Levina and Xin, 2007). In addition, we also find that the complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks has been increasing monotonically over time. The pattern of complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks is different from that of return to computer skills. We argue that increased computerization of routine tasks and the resultant requirement for performing more non-routine tasks at work (Autor et al., 2003) coupled with the complementary mechanism (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992) is the underlying cause of these two differing patterns.

It is important to note that returns to computer skills are not static but dynamic. Computer skills that were new and in short supply in the labor market might become obsolete or widely diffused over time, and new computer skills that are required by new applications, new technologies, and new equipment may emerge. Consequently, it is possible that some computer skills relevant in the early 2000s may no longer afford a wage return, and computer skills that are new and in short supply now are more likely to command a wage premium. This phenomenon notwithstanding, the mechanisms and patterns of computer skills wage premium revealed in this study should continue to hold.

## 5.2. Practical and strategic implications

Our findings bear important practical and strategic implications too. First, to further their overall strategic goals, firms should design effective compensation schedules that encourage development of required computers skill sets. In today’s highly competitive business environment, it is imperative for firms to develop a multi-skilled workforce that is at once flexible, competent, and also enables leaner staffing. Therefore, companies need to encourage development of such skills by designing appropriate compensation schedules that reflect both the breadth and the depth of employee computer skills. A broader skill set enables employees to engage in a greater variety of tasks that need multiple and wider skills, while the depth of the skill set affords greater knowledge and stronger competencies, which would allow employee to tackle more complex problems at workplace. By designing a compensation schedule that reflects both the breadth and the depth of computer skills, businesses can increase workforce versatility and productivity, enhance employee satisfaction, induce organizational learning and skill acquisition, and thereby gain a competitive advantage in the marketplace.

Second, our findings also have important implications for job and task design at workplace. Our results suggest that employees performing non-routine tasks are paid higher. More importantly, computers complement performing non-routine tasks and substitute routine tasks. In today’s competitive business landscape, few firms are capable of carrying out all the activities required to deliver products and services to customers. Therefore, increasingly firms are entering into strategic alliances with their business partners so as to leverage their core competencies. Our results suggest that, to get the most out of such alliances, when firms design their work flows and processes it is to their benefit to focus on the tasks that are typically non-routine, and outsource those that are routine and less critical. At the same time, firms should attempt to adopt various computer tools for performing non-routine tasks, and thereby enhance the effectiveness and efficiency of their employees in performing them.

Finally, our results have important implications for public policy. A highly skilled workforce is more productive and more competitive in the global market. Understanding what computer skills contribute to wage premium as well as the underlying mechanisms is crucial for governments to plan and implement intervention programs to enhance the skills required by the general workforce (Dickerson and Green, 2004). Our study suggests that both the breadth and the depth of computer skills are rewarded in the labor market, and therefore, governments need to provide incentives for individuals and businesses to invest in developing them. They should also encourage and promote IT education and training opportunities for the workforce so as to enhance workers’ employability and career development. Moreover, given the complementarity between computer skills and non-routine tasks, policy makers should support greater use of technologies and computing equipment in such jobs and tasks in the workplace. As IT-skilled workers will be more productive and valuable for performing non-routine tasks, they will be motivated to further upgrade their computer skills, and this in turn will increase the input of non-routine tasks due to the complementarity mechanism. Through this interactive process, both worker skills and job content could be qualitatively enhanced, further contributing to creativity and innovation in the workplace, which is the very foundation of the vitality of the US economy.

## 6. Conclusion

Despite the important findings that have emerged from this study, we are mindful of its limitations. First, we have used a proxy variable, computer use at home, to alleviate the endogeneity problem while estimating the return to computer skills. Future studies may use other methods, such as instrumental variable approach or panel analyses, to validate the results of this study. Second, we categorized the levels of employees’ computer skills based on the computer tools they use. Although the usage of various tools is reflective of computer skills, they are not the same, i.e., the usage of tools may not accurately capture the level of computer skills. Future studies may address this issue by controlling for both the variety of computer tools and skill levels. Third, it is quite possible that the ways in which computer skills complement job tasks exhibit heterogeneity for different types of workers. Thus, it would be interesting to investigate the robustness of this complementarity across different demographic segments, occupational groups, and industry sectors. Finally, our study was conducted using data from the US. Considering that wage structures and labor market contexts vary across countries, future studies may explore these issues and validate our findings using data obtained from other countries. Notwithstanding these limitations we believe that our study will succeed in drawing the attention of strategic IT scholarship on the need to further explore the wage premium implications of the use of IT skills in the workplace.
