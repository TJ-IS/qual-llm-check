# Protecting a whale in a sea of phish

Daniel Pienta<sup>1</sup> , Jason Bennett Thatcher<sup>2</sup> and Allen Johnston<sup>2</sup>

Journal of Information Technology 1–18 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2020 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839622091859DOI: 10.1177/0268396220918594 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

Whaling is one of the most financially damaging, well-known, effective cyberattacks employed by sophisticated cybercriminals. Although whaling largely consists of sending a simplistic email message to a whale (i.e. a high-value target in an organization), it can result in large payoffs for cybercriminals, in terms of money or data stolen from organizations. While a legitimate cybersecurity threat, little information security research has directed attention toward whaling. In this study, we begin to provide an initial understanding of what makes whaling such a pernicious problem for organizations, executives, or celebrities (e.g. whales), and those charged with protecting them. We do this by defining whaling, delineating it from general phishing and spear phishing, presenting real-world cases of whaling, and provide guidance on future information security research on whaling. We find that whaling is far more complex than general phishing and spear phishing, spans multiple domains (e.g. work and personal), and potentially results in spillover effects that ripple across the organization. We conclude with a discussion of promising future directions for whaling and information security research.

## Keywords

Whaling, phishing, spear phishing, hypercontextualization, business email compromise, cybersecurity

## Introduction

Whaling refers to a form of phishing that is specifically aimed at wealthy, powerful, or prominent individuals, known as whales (e.g. C-Suite Executives, celebrities, politicians) (Trend Micro USA, 2019; Rapid7, 2019), and the cybercriminals who conduct such an attack are referred to as whalers. To conduct their attacks, whalers rely on social engineering to compromise legitimate business email accounts or impersonate trusted co-workers, superiors, or business partners to coerce the whales into engaging in detrimental actions, such as approving fraudulent wire transfers, divulging intellectual property, forwarding sensitive employee information, or giving away control of digital branding (e.g. username and passwords, payroll information, confidential databases, Instagram account hijacking) (CIO Review, 2015; Rapid7, 2019).

Because whales are high-social-status individuals, typically of great importance to their respective organizations (e.g. a CEO who is the face of the company) or to those around them (e.g. a celebrity who is managed by a team of people) (CIO Review, 2015), whaling can lead to devastating economic losses. In 2017 alone, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) reported losses of \$1.4 billion USD due to business email compromise (BEC) scams, with whaling attacks attributed to \$675 million USD of these losses. In addition, on 12 July 2018, the FBI issued a warning regarding the increase of BEC scams. The warning indicated that between October 2013 and May 2018, there were 78,617 reported incidents, with exposed dollar losses of over \$12 billion USD across more than 150 countries. On 10 September 2019, the FBI updated these numbers showing total reported incidents of 166,349 and exposed dollar losses of \$26.2 billion USD from October 2013 to July 2019. Given these losses, it is surprising that security firms, especially those that specialize in protecting high-value targets, have not done more to secure their whales. Perhaps the reason is that it’s not all that clear how they can protect whales from whaling attacks.

Unfortunately, we possess little understanding of what makes whaling unique from other forms of phishing, making it difficult to develop security protocols, controls, and training programs specific to whales and whaling. Absent a richer understanding of whaling, it will be difficult to help information security (ISec) professionals combat these attacks because we believe that they unfold differently than general phishing or spear phishing. For instance, we do not know if training whales differs from training other individuals (e.g. employees), if technologies designed to protect individuals from phishing and spear phishing are equally effective at detecting whaling messages, or if individuals respond differently to emails sent by cybercriminals impersonating whales. As this article explains, the whaling problem is complex, and before we can devise effective security solutions to the problem, as a community, we need to pursue a systematic research agenda in order to realize a much better understanding of it.

In the following sections of this article, we (1) summarize relevant phishing literature, (2) elaborate on our definition of whaling, (3) differentiate whaling from phishing and spear phishing, (4) provide real-world cases of whaling, and (5) provide an agenda for future whaling research and ISec research, in general.

## A school of phish, but no whales

The ISec research community has directed substantial attention toward understanding phishing (Abbasi et al., 2010; Jensen et al., 2017b; Wang et al., 2016, 2017; Wright et al., 2014). Phishing in ISec research refers to a form of social engineering in which cybercriminals obtain confidential information via electronic communication from an individual through the imitation of a trustworthy source (Hong, 2012; Wright et al., 2014). Typically, phishing attacks use psychological manipulations of trust to elicit detrimental behavior from a broad population (e.g. all Gmail users), a group (e.g. all individuals in the financial department of an organization), or an individual (e.g. the CEO of an organization). The ISec community has studied phishing and spear phishing, which are more targeted, context-specific attacks directed at certain groups of individuals in an organization (Hong, 2012; Schuetz et al., 2016), and has provided guidance to practice on how to defend against these attacks (Table 1).

ISec research employs different theoretical perspectives to understand phishing and spear phishing (Pienta et al., 2018). For example, some studies have devoted attention to how a message’s contextualization (Jagatic et al., 2007) and content (Wright et al., 2014) increase an individual’s susceptibility to falling victim to a phishing attack through deception and persuasion theoretical lenses. Jagatic et al. (2007) showed that deceptively embedding social context in malicious emails is a key element of successful spearphishing messages (i.e. persuading the user to click and give up credentials), while Wright et al. (2014) demon strated that not all persuasion tactics (i.e. influence techniques) are equally effective for spear phishers.

ISec studies have also applied the psychological theories of mindfulness and influence tactics to understand how to more effectively design antiphishing countermeasures from individual and technological perspectives. For example, Jensen et al. (2017b) illustrated how mindfulness training can mitigate a phishing email’s effectiveness, while Abbasi et al. (2015) developed a novel technological countermeasure that improves the detection of phishing websites by individuals. Wright and Marett (2010; Wright et al., 2014) also looked at the role of influence tactics in persuading individuals to fall victim to a phishing message, click on a malicious link, or disclose login credentials.

ISec research directs attention to phishing at the organizational level as well. For example, Dey et al. (2012) looked at organizational development and investment in cybersecurity software, including antiphishing software. They found that network effects create incentives for vendors to collocate at the top end of the cybersecurity software marketplace (e.g. volume sales) and not direct attention toward the defense of low-volume high-value targets (e.g. whales). Their findings imply that ISec organizations are more motivated to develop remedies to high-frequency, broadly targeted cyberattacks like phishing and spear phishing than for low-frequency, narrowly targeted cyberattacks like whaling because they hold greater potential for a higher return on investment.

While ISec studies like these shed light on the design of and marketplace for phishing and spear-phishing defense, we found no evidence that the ISec literature has directly examined whaling, which is notable because such targeted attacks contribute to almost 50% of damages in reported BEC scams.

## Is it only the size of the phish? Defining whaling

Whaling involves longitudinal, multifaceted, highly coordinated attacks on high-value, high-status individuals, relying on precise timing and intricate details of the whale. These details include information about his or her associates (e.g. employees, friends, family) and domains (e.g. work and personal), and because whaling attacks are highly customized and tightly socially engineered, they unfold in a number of distinct ways. Whaling attacks typically involve reconnaissance, exploitation, and payoffs (Figure 1). For example, reconnaissance can initially be conducted on a work domain by analyzing the company and associated individuals’ work websites, Twitter, Facebook, LinkedIn accounts, and the personal domain looking at the whale and his or her extended network of friends and family via online social networks (OSNs) (e.g. Facebook, Instagram). After obtaining threat intelligence, the whaler can then attack either the technical vulnerabilities or the human elements in the attack domain.

To attack the technical element, the whaler may inject malicious code into the work or home network of the whale and set up a covert command and control server to monitor all communications for further intelligence. Alternatively, the whaler may socially engineer communications with the whale to prime the target for exploitation. In either instance, timing and detailed knowledge of the whale, the affiliated organization, and his or her network are needed. When exploitation commences, the whaler may request the transfer of funds for a business acquisition, ask for intellectual property, or request a database of confidential information by impersonating the whale or a close associate. Finally, at payoff, the exploited asset is sent directly to the whaler who has an intricate network setup to conceal his or her identity.

While whaling can take a number of different forms, whalers generally rely on social engineering and technical tools to manipulate targeted individuals. As the attack unfolds, whalers deploy diverse techniques to jeopardize financial, digital, and human assets (e.g. wire transfers, Instagram accounts, and virtual kidnappings), among others. One thing is quite clear, whalers seek to take advantage of a collective relaxation of vigilance in ISec staff through a “normalization of deviance” (Vaughan, 1997). This means that whalers exploit multiple acts of negligence by whales, their network, and those that protect them across domains to create more customized attacks. Whaling attacks thus have a potentially greater magnitude of impact (e.g. returns) when compared to phishing and spear-phishing attacks.

In a whaling attack, the whaler may capitalize on the normalization of deviance by first sending a phishing email that he or she knows will not bypass the countermeasures of the organization. This email, although flagged, filtered, and quarantined, can still return valuable information to the whaler, such as whether the email reached the recipient, whether the link was clicked on by the whale, and what technical countermeasures the organization employees had in place—for example, Sender Policy Framework (SPF) (i.e. identification of mail servers associated with a domain), DomainKeys Identified Mail (DKIM) (i.e. signature validation), or Domain-Based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance (DMARC) (i.e. header authentication against the domain). Thus, whalers take an iterative approach to bypass various countermeasures with multiple “bad” emails, yet wait to deliver the most disruptive payload until all intelligence is gathered. In addition, whalers may look to domains that the ISec staff has neglected or are unable to protect, such as the social media accounts of family and friends, to gather intelligence. Whalers also use tactics to identify information about the computing equipment of whales from work and personal domains. For example, open-source software like Nmap, a network mapper, can identify unsecure ports, host information, operating system version detection, and hardware identification, and many

ISec staff are unable to secure devices that cross business and personal domains. Even physical tactics like dumpster diving can provide information if work or personal bills identifying the whale’s Internet service provider are not shredded. Whalers can use something as simple as a discarded bill to identify routers used by a whale that often rely on unchanged default administrative passwords or lack additional layers of protection for home routers. Thus, this collective ease of security across domains allows whalers to formulate and execute effective whaling attacks that maximize damage.

## What makes whaling different

Whaling differs from phishing and spear phishing in terms of target, contextualization, domain violated, resources involved, payoff, vulnerable assets, payload, and attack cycles that all affect the design of the attack (Table 2).

Target. Whaling differs from phishing and spear phishing in that each attack varies in its focus. While whaling focuses on specific targets of interest (e.g. a C-level executive of a bank), phishing targets a mass population of users (e.g. all Gmail users) and spear-phishing targets specific groups of individuals (e.g. all individuals in the accounting department of a financial institution).

Target and social context. In any phishing attack, social context is critical. Social context or milieu refers to the immediate physical and social setting in which people live or in which something happens (Casper, 2001). Context-aware attacks, defined as “attacks mounted using messages that somehow—from their context—are expected (or even welcomed) by the victim,” have been frequently investigated (Jakobsson, 2005: 2). Phishers often obtain information on social contexts through publicly available data from organizations, employees, and related entities. Whalers use similar information, such as an organizational chart found on a firm’s website, to identify a target and his or her circle. Social context has been found to increase the likelihood that individuals will click on a malicious link from a sender (Jagatic et al., 2007).

The social context presented in phishing, spear phishing, and whaling attacks differs in specificity. Phishing and spear-phishing attacks differ according to the degree of intimacy involved in the contextualization, with spear phishing demonstrating more intimate knowledge about a target, such as the role of the individual in an organization (i.e. student, employee, faculty) or a local telephone number placed in the email. For instance, Wang et al. (2016) applied broad phishing tactics to create emails that appeared from PayPal, whereas Jensen et al. (2017b) contextualized spear-phishing emails to a specific university. Such contextualization in ISec spear-phishing research typically focuses on a target organization’s context (Jensen et al., 2017b; Wang et al., 2017, 2014; Wright and Marett, 2010) instead of an individual.

Whereas phishing and spear-phishing attacks utilize messages that focus on the social context of broader behaviors (e.g. shopping) or specific organizations (e.g. a university or employer), whaling attacks involve contextualizing messages that span the organization and the personal domains of targets’ lives. The whaler goes beyond the typical phisher’s activity, by gathering information from a target’s social network on social media sites such as LinkedIn, Twitter, and Instagram, and by friending, following, or directly engaging with the target’s contacts or friends. By doing so, the whaler can tailor attacks that leverage multiple domains (e.g. work and life) and vectors (e.g. co-work ers and personal friends) to persuade a whale to part with information or money.

Whaling uses open-source intelligence gathering (OSINT) techniques to infiltrate the organizational and personal domains of targets. OSINT can be gathered from a corporation’s online social presence (e.g. Twitter, Instagram, Facebook) and the personal OSN account of the whale and his or her family and close friends. For example, a whaler gathering intelligence on a whale may “friend” the spouse, children, siblings, extended family, and close friends with a crafted fictitious profile. This relationship may provide intimate details about the whale outside of his or her organization. Using these sources of information, the whaler crafts messages that appear native to the whale’s life context, as it adheres to the norms found in messages from the target’s workplace and demonstrates knowledge of the broader social context of the target, such as activities of family, friends, and colleagues.

Domain spillover. Because whales may not openly share information online, cybercriminals gather information from actors in multiple domains in order to craft hyperpersonalized attacks. For example, even if a whale does not have a personal OSN presence, a whaler will cycle through connected family and close friends until a “chatterbox” is identified. A chatterbox is a linked member of the whale’s extended network that heavily uses social media and discloses information about the whale. For instance, a whale may purchase a car for his or her child, the child posts a picture of the car on Instagram and discloses the license plate, which the whaler then uses to find out information about the vehicle. The whaler now has intimate details about the whale and, if timed correctly, can send an email with details about the new car that signals that the email is from a trusted source. Whaling attacks, therefore, jeopardize the information not only of the target asset, but also that of his or her inner and outer circle in work and personal domains.

Investment effort. Effort also differs among phishing, spear phishing, and whaling. Cybercriminals invest extensive resources and time to gather and analyze threat intelligence from multiple sources to increase the efficacy of a whaling attack. For instance, the hacking group OilRig created the female character “Mia Ash” on LinkedIn and Facebook to deceive multiple executives and employees, persuading them to disclose information over the course of a year (Fox-Brewster, 2017). This eventually led the group to launch attacks at an opportune time either through installing malicious software on the target’s information technology and/ or by obtaining access to the target’s email account.

Payoff. Phishing and spear phishing seek multiple small to medium-sized financial payments, whereas the payoff for whaling is typically a large one-time lump sum financial payment. For example, a phisher conducting a spear-phishing campaign may attempt to compromise 600 accounts, yielding payments of \$500 to \$1000 USD each, while a whaler will typically aim for a multi-million-dollar payoff.

Vulnerable assets. Where phishing and spear phishing primarily deal with information assets, whaling attack goals span from financial payoffs to the actual physical harm of humans and their interests (Von Solms and Van Niekerk, 2013). A chatterbox may unintentionally disclose global positioning data revealing movements to potential cybercriminals, potentially resulting in virtual kidnapping to extort money. For example, in 2015, Michael Dell’s security detail abruptly shut down his daughter’s Twitter and Instagram accounts due to concerns for the family’s safety (Robinson, 2018). Whalers target different assets to hijack for eliciting payoff. Tactics range from eliciting payment for fake wire transfers due to the loss of an important account to virtually kidnapping loved ones for a ransom payment.

Payload and instance. While phishing and spear phishing tend to be one-shot attacks, whaling attacks are multifaceted, unfold over time, and rely on highly coordinated, precisely timed action. Phishing and spear phishing are typically one-time attacks designed to gain access to login credentials and/or a financial payoff. Consequently, once an attack is identified, cybersecurity professionals employ a relatively simple script, the phishing email and associated site will typically be blacklisted and automatically quarantined. In contrast, whaling can include a series of continuous communications with multiple parties until the target completes the suggested behavior (e.g. wiring a large financial sum to an account). For instance, many whalers will ask an unsuspecting victim (i.e. a “pawn”) to open a bank account to receive a substantial payment from the whale, so it is not in the whaler’s name. They then ask the pawn to immediately transfer the funds into a network of accounts that potentially passes through multiple pawns, countries, and banks to effectively limit the traceability of the large wire transfer. This tight coordination of action and requests by whalers from the target and multiple pawns is necessary so that victims do not become suspicious of the “big picture attack.” In such cases, the whale is just one victim in the whaler’s web of targets and pawns that is used to access financial information and accounts for payload filtering.

## Defending the whale

To defend whales, practitioners often recommend that organizations use countermeasures similar to those deployed against spear phishing. These can encompass (1) technical countermeasures such authenticators, blacklist filters, and link rewriters; (2) organizational countermeasures such as policy, governance, and legal punishment; and (3) individual countermeasures such as motivation, train ing, and culture. However, this generalization from spearphishing tactics and defenses designed for groups to whaling tactics designed to attack high-value individuals may be problematic (Pienta et al., 2018) as they rely on general phishing heuristics (Figure 2). Such generalizations ignore the complexity, sophistication, and nuances of whaling and its apparent efficacy as a dynamic form of social engineering. The variance in target focus influences how cybercriminals design the attack, as well as how academics design their phishing experiments to gain insight into defending these phenomena (Jagatic et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2016, 2017; Wright et al., 2014). For example, Wang et al. (2017) and Wright et al. (2014), use two different targeted populations and phishing techniques: (1) all users of PayPal (i.e. phishing) and (2) a specific university popula tion (i.e. spear phishing). Although similar in nature, the underlying tactics employed by these two studies were tailored to the context to increase the efficacy of the attack.

Although the target is an important defining characteristic of how phishers design the attack, the context of reference, created by the whaler and designed to elicit a behavior in response to a call to action, is equally important. Phishers create context around a general domain (e.g. suspension of Gmail accounts). Spear phishers create context around a specific organizational domain. Whalers create context around the specific whale’s organizational and personal domains. The context that is created through multiple domain details thus makes the individual more susceptible to these whaling attacks and complements a call to action to elicit the requested behavior (Jagatic et al., 2007; Wright et al., 2014; Wright and Marett, 2010).

While we know how phishers construct attacks and use that information to design academic phishing field experi ments, we know relatively little about how to do so for whaling attacks. This may be due to reluctance on the part of organizations to disclose information on attacks on high value targets in organizations, the small number of targets of whaling attacks, or simply a tendency of academics to follow practice and focus on the more pervasive phenomena of phishing and spear-phishing attacks. What is clear is that we need a far more nuanced understanding of whaling than currently exists.

## Real-world whaling

As noted in the introduction, whaling is a real-world problem that can result in financial losses, market share decreases, and the termination of high-ranking executives. We present four succinct real-world cases demonstrating how these attacks manifest in different forms and bypass technological countermeasures. Rather than ask targets to click on a link or download a malicious file, these attacks use a highly intricate, technology-enabled process to elicit large sums of money.

## The case of Ubiquiti Networks, Inc

According to its 2015 US Securities and Exchange Commission filing, Ubiquiti Networks, Inc. (Ubiquiti) was a victim of a whaling attack in 2015 (White, 2017). The company is a manufacturer of wireless network hardware based in San Jose, California. On its k-8 form, the company noted

On June 5, 2015, the Company determined that it had been a victim of criminal fraud. The incident involved employee impersonation and fraudulent requests from an outside entity targeting the Company’s finance department. This fraud resulted in transfers of funds aggregating \$46.7 million held by a Company subsidiary incorporated in Hong Kong to other overseas accounts held by third parties.

Although Ubiquiti did not disclose the exact details of the attack on its k-8 form, investigative technology journalists found that employees in the financial department believed they had received a legitimate email from an executive of the company requesting a transfer of funds. Domain names were spoofed with slight differences, such as adding a letter or punctuation, to trick employees into sending their usernames, passwords, and financial account information.

## The case of FACC AG

FACC AG (FACC) is an aerospace manufacturing company located in Austria that reported a \$54 million USD loss in 2016 due to a whaling attack (Reuters, 2017). This incident caused the company’s share price to fall nearly 17% after the loss was reported and offers a classic example of a successful whaling scheme. FACC disclosed in its third-quarter results that the financial accounting department was the target of fraud with little detail regarding how the attack unfolded.

As more details about the attack were leaked, it was determined that the CEO and CFO of FACC were directly targeted. The cybercriminals responsible for the attack were said to have used a highly customized email to trick FACC’s accounting staff into transferring an estimated \$54 million USD into an undisclosed account. The cybercriminals asked the CFO to transfer money for a fake acquisition project—a tactic commonly known as a “fake president incident” involving impersonating a CEO and requesting that the project be kept secret so as not to jeopardize the acquisition. In this instance, the whaling attack resulted in the dismissal of both the CEO and CFO because they were negligent in preparing the company to defend against this type of cyberattack.

## The case of Mattel

Mattel, Inc. (Mattel), a Fortune 500 international toy manufacturer, experienced a whaling attack that provides a vivid example of the sophisticated and complex nature of the attack (Infosec Resources, 2017). Although not as financially dramatic as other whaling attack cases, the Mattel case demonstrates the multifaceted nature of whaling cybercriminals. While the whaling attack did not occur until 2015, the intelligence gathering required to execute the attack purportedly began much earlier. The cybercriminals behind this attack had gained access (supposedly through other social engineering means) to Mattel’s computer networks long before the attack and studied its internal procedures, protocol, corporate hierarchy, supplier information, and various high-level employee habits and personalities. As this intelligence was gathered, the cybercriminals familiarized them selves with the nuances and intricacies of the company as they patiently waited for the right time to launch the attack.

In January 2015, Mattel appointed a new CEO and the cybercriminals realized that the new appointment presented an opportune time because of the high uncertainty, office politics, and power struggles plaguing the company. Based on their intimate knowledge of the inner workings of Mattel and under the guise of the new CEO, the cybercriminals drafted an email to a high-ranking employee requesting a \$3 million USD transfer to a bank in Wenzhou, China. The funds trans fer request was designated as a payment to a Chinese toy supplier and was marked as late, suggesting a payment deadline had already been missed. It should be noted that policy at Mattel typically requires a dual authorization of two highlevel executives in order to transfer large payments. The recipient of the email determined that, since the request appeared to come from the CEO, the CEO was one such authorization and his or her authorization would be the second. Thus, the employee transferred the \$3 million USD payment to the cybercriminals’ bank account as requested.

## Future whaling research

We lack clear guidance on how to prevent whaling. Groups like the Anti-Phishing Work Group, an international consortium with more than 3200 members from over 1700 agencies worldwide, offer guidance on how to improve phishing defenses for industry, government, law enforcement, and nongovernmental communities. Typically, the guidance provided by these groups is limited to relatively simple heuristics for how to identify phishing, and relies on the recipient of the potential phishing message to (1) ensure the email header is legitimate, (2) review the domain name for legitimacy, (3) review for generic salutations, (4) review for spelling errors, (5) look for urgent requests for sensitive information, and (6) hover and ensure that an embedded link or attached file is legitimate. While these general heuristics improve the ability to defend against phishing attacks, we suspect they may have limited efficacy in defending against more comprehensive, sophisticated attacks such as whaling. Thus, we propose a number of future directions (Table 3) for research aimed at understanding this invasive and damaging form of phishing.

## Understanding the target

Although whaling is most often thought of as targeting a public figure (e.g. a CEO, celebrity, politician), our review of real-world cases suggests that whalers more often target individuals who control firm information assets, such as social media accounts, product design, or financial assets. Notably, while the notoriety of the targeted individual may vary, whalers consistently seek payoffs in large lump sum payments rather than a myriad of smaller payments. Thus, studying differences in targeted individuals is an area of research that will be useful for understanding how whaling attacks are designed and succeed.

ISec researchers should examine whether role-based factors contribute to targets’ susceptibility to whaling, perhaps extending the work of Moody et al. (2017) that studied the role of situational and personality factors in an individual’s susceptibility to phishing. Moody et al. (2017) found that curiosity, risk propensity, Internet usage, and Internet anxiety increase an individual’s susceptibility to phishing emails. Future whaling research, as it is directed at C-level executives or those controlling valuable assets, could look at different situation and personality factors based on organizational role. In psychology, personality traits have been shown to determine perceptions of leadership, ethics, and effectiveness (Avolio et al., 2009; Lord et al., 1986; Walumbwa and Schaubroeck, 2009), yet we know little about how leaders’ personalities shape the effectiveness of ISec tactics designed to protect them. An interesting area of research could be combining work on influence tactics (Wright et al., 2014) with role-based personality traits to see if certain influence tactics elicit different responses based on dispositional factors. More sophisticated research, focused on the interplay of personality, role, and ISec is necessary, because whaling demonstrates that cybercriminals are moving beyond simple tactics to design strategies that exploit the unique personalities, organizational roles, and work context of whales.

A whale’s role may shape the trust they extend to others, and trust expressed by others toward them, thereby influence whaling’s efficacy. As trust has many forms (McKnight et al., 1998, 2011), whalers may leverage different forms of trust in phishing attacks. Trust in people and trust in technology have been found to be different (Mcknight et al., 2011)—trust in people is associated with the dimensions of benevolence, competence, and integrity, whereas trust in technology invokes dimension of reliability, functionality, and helpfulness (Mcknight et al., 2011). It could be beneficial for researchers studying whaling to examine the roles of these different types of trust for whaling. For example, it may be interesting to investigate whether trust in people has a superordinate role in whaling over trust in technology or vice versa. Similar to a value system where there is hierarchal ordering of different values (Rokeach, 1973), multiagents of trust like the organization, technology, and people might also have hierarchal ordering which may affect individual ISec compliance behaviors.

Studying the interplay among organization role, technology-mediated interpersonal trust, and its exploitation in black hat tactics may provide insight into how whalers design “long cons.” Perhaps because they trust protective technologies, whales may disregard protective agents (e.g. professional security staff) and use technologies that make them vulnerable (e.g. Facebook) or force changes in protective strategies (e.g. publicly post schedules). To protect the whale, knowledge of the intimate details of a whale’s life is necessary, which thus exposes the whale to the vulnerability of potential exploitation by those charged with protecting the whale. To better understand how to craft effective protection strategies, we need to realize a deeper understanding of the relative importance of how trust in people and trust in technology shape whales’ willingness to comply with protection strategies.

## Understanding the organization

Whaling targets not only individuals or groups, but also organizations. Organizational targeting underscores the essence of the attack, in that both processes (e.g. dual authorization on a payment) and norms (e.g. communication formality) are exploited. Whalers conduct extensive vulnerability assessments to expose weaknesses in the organization that range from formal mechanisms (i.e. internal structure of the organization) to informal mechanisms (i.e. culture) put in place by whales. Whalers gather intensive knowledge about the organization, the industry it resides in, and when to employ different techniques, such as social media hijacking or wiring a large financial payment for the largest payoff.

Whalers also capitalize on uncertainty in the organization by inducing it or taking advantage of opportune periods. Phishing has been shown to capitalize on the misrepresentation of trusting agents to psychologically manipulate individuals to click on a malicious link (Wright et al., 2014). Trust, in turn, has been shown to reduce uncertainty and concerns for security (Pavlou et al., 2007). Whaling uses the contrasting nature of uncertainty and trust by invoking each to work in complementary manner. For example, in whaling temporal factors like holiday seasons, travel schedules, shift changes, and vacations can result in uncertainty for the individual. During this time, an email from a trusting agent may reduce uncertainty due to intimate details of the relationship. Conversely, phishing and spear phishing may only rely on exploiting trust to complete a successful attack. Similar to how Berente et al. (2016) showed that common routines are used to mitigate shocks, understanding how hypercontextualization in whaling elicits trust might mitigate shed light on how uncertainty brought on by environmental turbulence leads to people making poor decisions.

Whaling, however, has organizational spillover effects that have yet to be studied. For instance, how do employees view a CEO, CFO, and/or CISO who was an unknowing accomplice in a whaling attack? Or, how does the general public view a politician or celebrity who has a hijacked Twitter or Instagram account? There could potentially be ripple effects throughout the organization affecting confidence in management, even among individuals not directly victimized by the attack. In addition, whalers hijacking whales’ social media accounts could manipulate, for example, public trust in candidates for political office, thus potentially influencing election outcomes. Publicness, visibility, power, and influence over a large population are reasons that whales are targets. ISec research could provide theoretical insight into why black hat hackers choose their targets. For example, understanding if there are dispositional factors that lead to whaling, if ego plays a role in targeting whales, and if dark triad traits like narcissism, machiavellianism, and psychopathy lead to whaling. Outside of traits, ISec research has shown that overconfidence makes individuals more susceptible to phishing (Wang et al., 2016), yet ISec research is in the nascent stages of providing insight into the antecedents of overconfidence as well as interjections for its defense.

## Understanding the attack

Whaling attacks unfold in more complex and sophisticated manner than more familiar cyberattacks. Systems 1 and 2 thinking have been shown to be important concepts in phishing (Kahneman and Egan, 2011; Wright et al., 2014). We know that phishers and spear phishers try to invoke System 1 thinking to get individuals to quickly click on malicious links and provide login credentials to cybercriminals (Wright et al., 2014; Wright and Marett, 2010). Alternatively, System 2 thinking has been shown to help in training and preventing attacks through mindfulness (Jensen et al., 2017b). We need to know more about how whalers take advantage of these different forms of thinking.

Whaling may exploit multiple types of thinking through different stages of dynamic longitudinal attacks. For example, whaling may exploit a fast-thinking decision in initially eliciting a reply to a fake email, whereas wiring millions of dollars is a highly engaged process that would likely invoke System 2 thinking (Dennis and Minas, 2018; Kahneman and Egan, 2011). Understanding how highly processed cognitive decisions and activities are leveraged in cyberattacks could provide additional insight into how to defend against whaling. A more complete understanding of the role of fast and slow thinking could provide insight into cyberattacks, in general, from the perspectives of susceptibility, defense, and cognitive engagement. We believe there are opportunities for ISec research that incorporate both types of thinking to understand whaling’s efficacy.

Whaling may exploit information leakage that occurs across platforms. OSNs enable individuals to interact and connect with each other digitally, through creating personal profiles, giving friends and or colleagues access to these profiles, and communicating through a digital medium (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2016). Understanding how data aggregated across OSNs, such as Facebook, Instagram, and LinkedIn, to facilitate deception, influence, and hypercontextualization in whaling could provide insight into the multifaceted nature of this attack. This is important, because each data point in isolation may not be enough to launch an attack; however, three or more data points gathered across platforms can provide insight into the location, status, and more of a whale. Even if whales are careful about the information they disclose on one platform, their unknowing disclosure of sensitive information that can be triangulated across ubiquitous platforms constitutes a serious risk to organizations (Pew Research Center, 2017). Research on how whalers aggregate, synthesize, and predict whale’s and their employing organizations’ behavior would shed substantial light on the threat posed by social media use to firm security.

Whaling exploits generally accepted norms for human behavior. For example, it is normal for users to create and update personalized profiles on OSNs that includes photos, videos, audio files, social connections, and text-based messages (Kaplan and Haenlein, 2016). These forms of sharing, however, can lead to the unintentional disclosure of possibly sensitive or confidential information to whalers hiding in the whale’s network and beyond (Vishwamitra et al., 2017). For example, in sharing visual data such as photos, individuals may disclose sensitive or confidential information to an unintended audience since they have limited control over who accesses a photo beyond their immediate networks (Litt and Hargittai, 2014; Vishwamitra et al., 2017). Recently, in a case of cyberstalking, a stalker was able to identify finite details of a celebrity by obtaining sensitive information disclosed from eye reflections in digital photos (BBC News, 2019). In the hands of a whaler, the disclosure of sensitive information could result in socially engineered attacks against the whale, his or her associates, and/or affiliated organizations. As OSNs play a key role in gathering threat intelligence that can be used in attacks, studying how to better design privacy features, promote awareness of the implications of self-disclosure by executives, and theoretically understand the consequences could shed light on the impact of unintentional disclosure of a whale’s sensitive information.

## Defending the attack

Whaling is a different form of criminality and ISec research has provided rich insight into how to defend against phishing and cyberattacks, in general rather than contextualized to a specific form of criminality. Behavioral researchers have studied protection motivation theory and its ability to predict an individual’s motivation to protect information assets (Boss et al., 2015; Johnston and Warkentin, 2010, 2015). ISec researchers have also studied the effects of government mandates (Wang et al., 2008), enforcement strategies (Willison and Warkentin, 2013), and vulnerability disclosures and attack diffusions (Mitra and Ransbotham, 2015).

Although the concepts and theories presented in these studies are not specifically contextualized to phishing, their findings nevertheless do not necessarily extend defending against whaling. For instance, are whales motivated to defend against cyberattacks differently than rank-and-file employees, as they set the strategy for the organization. In addition, as executive compensation is directly tied to share value and company value perhaps there are different individual, group, and organizational factors that make them targets, victims, or even pawns in attacks. Future research grounded in the cumulative knowledge presented in both the behavioral and design science bodies of ISec research would offer unique opportunities to enhance our understanding of how to defend against hypercontextualized whaling attacks.

Designing and studying the effects of different security “wrappers” for targets that are subject to whaling attacks could further understanding of how to defend against these attacks. Studying the black box of security technologies and their effects on individual behavior may provide insight into whether the technologies act as substitutes or complements to individual security awareness. Also, designing information technology artifacts specifically for different types of phishing and applying a theoretical understanding of how the artifact changes the individual and his or her behavior could prove beneficial.

ISec research may also benefit from building on phishing mindfulness research that provides guidance for how to defend against phishing and spear-phishing attacks (Jensen et al., 2017b). Taking a design-and-action approach with practical implications to provide the ability, skills, and motivation to defend against attacks could offer unique insights into what is needed to defend against whaling attacks. Clarifying whether ability and motivation are both necessary and sufficient to defend against whaling and phishing in general may provide further understanding of how to leverage the human element in cybersecurity. In addition, exploring if whales need different training than typical employees could illuminate the intricacies associated with whale protection, which is important since whales are often the public face of an organization and are typically responsible for controlling major resources.

At its core, information systems research has been characterized as stemming from various disciplines (Hirschheim and Klein, 2012). Embracing design studies that focus on the development of information technology artifacts and behavioral studies that focus on why certain technologies and strategies work to defend against whaling is paramount to advance less understood forms of phishing. Whaling is a wicked problem involving the complex interaction of many subcomponents to exploit human cognition. As such, whaling may be best approached in the context of design science and behavioral research. Research on whaling in these fields would likely stimulate the design of protective information technology artifacts and would give ISec behavioral researchers the opportunity to validate whaling and study the changes effected by protective technologies. Studies seeking to understand whaling from both perspectives would generate the understanding necessary to tackle this complex, hypercontextualized phenomenon.

## Mitigating the attack

A popular saying in the cybersecurity industry is “There are two types of companies: those who have been hacked, and those who don’t know yet they have been hacked.” Many practitioners manage risk analysis through the threats, vulnerabilities, and consequences (i.e. outcomes) framework, which was introduced by the Department of Homeland Security in 2008 (Cox, 2008). Threats are considered “any indication, circumstance, or event with the potential to cause the loss, or damage to, an asset or population” (Cox, 2008: 1750). Vulnerabilities refer to “any weakness in an asset’s or infrastructure’s design, implementation, or operation that can be exploited by an adversary” (Cox, 2008: 1750). Consequences refer to “the outcome or event occurrence, including immediate, short- and long-term, direct and indirect losses and effects” (Cox, 2008: 1750).

Based on the above noted quote, indicating it is a matter of time before having a threat exploit a vulnerability, controlling the outcome would indeed take on more relevance for organizations. Interestingly, ISec has typically concentrated on threats and vulnerabilities with little attention directed at consequences. This is concerning as an organization has control over identifying vulnerabilities and managing consequences, yet is limited in controlling the threats it will face. By studying whaling, we can ground theoretical understanding of outcomes in consequences such as mitigating the effects of C-Level turnover, efficacy of insurance policies, the attribution of fault by organizations, and loss of non-tangible (i.e. indirect) assets like consumer trust and job satisfaction. For instance, C-Level executives define the goals, strategy, and culture of the organization, among other duties, and yet if they are fired or become dissatisfied, it may lead to employee turnover or other adverse effects for the organization. Interestingly, we know little about the wider implications for the organization.

Given that whaling attacks on high-value targets rely on a mixture of social engineering and technical tools, we find it ironic that many insurance companies that offer policies to mitigate the impact of cybercrimes do not offer compara ble coverage for whaling incidents. The insurance company rationale for this is that whaling attacks presumably result from a failure of the human element of cybersecurity rather than from the failure of technical remedies to computer fraud (Reed Smith LLP, 2019). As our cases illustrate, successful whaling relies on breakdowns of social and techni cal security countermeasures. Future research could inform how insurance companies might approach offering coverage against whaling by more precisely identifying where breakdowns in human and technical measures occur.

## Studying whaling

Whaling is an ISec phenomenon that is difficult to study because it focuses on single, high-value targets. Also, gaining access to whales may prove difficult because of their value to an organization. Consequently, researchers may need to employ methods that derive insight from smaller sample sizes. Triangulation of results with descriptive studies may help corroborate these results. Therefore, we recommend that researchers interested in whaling consider using qualitative methods like in-depth interviews, observations, and focus groups. Whaling researchers may have to employ different research designs such as matched-pairs, Delphi techniques, NeuroIS, eye tracking, immersive experimental vignette methods, economics and mixed and multimethod designs to study this phenomenon as well.

Qualitative methods like case studies could prove especially beneficial to studying phenomena with small sample sizes. Case studies have been shown to be especially beneficial when approaching new or less understood topics (Eisenhardt, 1989a). To study whaling, researches might have to take on qualitative approaches and justify the selection of cases based on different factors (Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007). For example, to study how job roles affect susceptibility to phishing polar type contrasting case studies could be conducted to potentially reveal more nuanced findings. Alternatively, multiple case studies using different research sites and/or cases could provide further understanding across contexts. By comparing and contrasting a small sampling of whales in different organizations, an emergent theory could develop providing insight into how protecting whales is different than normal rank-and-file members of an organization. Overall, case studies have proven beneficial to study, understand, and provide theoretical insight into emerging phenomena (Eisenhardt, 1989b; Eisenhardt and Graebner, 2007) and thus could prove a useful method to understand whaling.

Archival studies, which involve the analysis of records held in various repositories could prove useful in gleaning insight into whaling. Many of these archives can be accessed by ISec researchers. The Anti-Phishing Work Group keeps many high-level reports on phishing as well as a database of phishing emails collected by members of the group. Cross referencing archival data from different sources for triangulation could provide understanding into the consequences of whaling. An exemplar study in ISec for secondary research is Mitra and Ransbotham (2015) that showed that disclosing attacks publicly, although making the organization more vulnerable for a short time, diffused an attack more effectively. By using archival or secondary data, this study provided insight into controlling an outcome in future cyberattacks for the overall welfare of society, the organization, and individual.

NeuroIS could provide understanding into how individuals process, pay attention to, and perceive different types of phishing emails. NeuroIS draws upon the cognitive science literature to uncover the neural bases of cognitive, emotional, and social processes derived from the complex interplay of people, technologies, process, and the environment (Dimoka et al., 2011). An interesting direction could be capturing the hidden mental processes that happen when one encounters a phishing email. For instance, Dimoka et al. (2011) point out that NeuorIS could complement existing sources of IS data with data from brain activity. Perhaps, studying System 1 and System 2 thinking and comparing brain activity for cognitive engagement could further build upon the work of Dennis and Minas (2018) about security on autopilot. Vance et al. (2018) and Anderson et al. (2016) have demonstrated the importance of NeuroIS research in the design of polymorphic security warnings to break habituation. Extending this line of research to how individuals process the various forms of phishing based on Pienta et al.’s (2018) taxonomy on phishing could provide insight to how individuals in different roles are susceptible to various forms of phishing.

## Discussion

In this study, we have focused on differentiating whaling from other types of phishing-based social engineering cyberattacks. By providing insight into this understudied form of cyberattack, our work suggests several directions for ISec-based research on whaling and more broadly (Table 4).

First, ISec research needs to consider how the roles of individuals in organizations contribute to how they are a potential threat to ISec. For example, although, IS research has studied leadership (Windeler and Riemenschneider, 2016), leader membership exchanges (Goh and Wasko, 2012), and other high-level executives in different contexts (Banker et al., 2011; Coulson-Thomas, 1991; Hsu et al., 2012; Kayworth and Whitten, 2010; Whitten, 2008), the majority of leadership papers focus on how leaders can improve security, use of IS, and virtual team performance. Scant IS, or ISec research, has studied how leadership creates vulnerabilities in organizations, particularly how it can make a firm susceptible to cyberattacks nor has it clearly differentiated between vulnerabilities posed by organizational members at different levels of the organization.

This study underscores that future ISec research should direct attention to developing theoretical and practical understanding of how cybercriminals target individuals at different levels (e.g. entry, middle management, C-level) of an organization differently. As an example, ISec researchers could study if preferential treatment is given to highlevel executives who fall victim to a cyberattack. Unequal forms of justice applied against the common worker (e.g. more severe penalties) compared to an executive could impact levels of the organization differently (e.g. morale, motivation, job satisfaction) and shape future compliance with security policies. Willison et al. (2018) provide initial insight into how perceived justice impacts the fight against insider abuse which could be extended to understand the impact of differential treatment of the application of security policy. Topics such as employee compliance, morale, and job satisfaction connected to ISec policies could be compelling. Alternatively, future work should examine whether a trade-off exists between firing a high-level executive who has continually increased shareholder value or retaining them as it could be important for understanding cybersecurity’s effect on overall firm strategy. Certainly, there are many other research directions to understand the interplay between cybersecurity and how it shapes the behavior of different levels of employees in organizations.

Second, our study underscores the importance of further work that employs sociotechnical approaches to understand whaling and organizational ISec. We note that with the ubiquity of digital and information technologies, organizational boundaries have been expanded primarily by blurring work and personal domains, increasing the threat landscape, and creating more opportunities for cybercriminals. For example, data exfiltration techniques grounded in digital forensics have allowed cybercriminals the ability to conduct intelligence gathering at a more sophisticated level. Whalers specifically analyze an individual’s personal and professional network for chatterboxes and pawns using these techniques.

ISec security has noted the importance of personal rele vance in eliciting motivation (Johnston and Warkentin, 2015), organizational commitment (Posey et al., 2015), and in defense of phishing (Jagatic et al., 2007). Although, little ISec research has been directed at the role personal ICTs and networks have played in expanding and blurring the boundaries of the organization (Chen and Karahanna, 2019). With the advent of bring your own device (Schmitz et al., 2016), work from home (Ayyagari et al., 2011), and telecommuting poli cies (Iscan and Naktiyok, 2005) confidential and private information of an organization crosses multiple domains (e.g. personal and organizational) and have blurred the boundaries of ISec. In addition, rogue criminals are beginning to target the personal networks of individuals as they are typically less secure than an organization. ISec research though has little understanding if there is spillover across domains from SETA (Security Education, Training, and Awareness) programs. We provide the following suggestions for increasing SETA awareness across domains. It could prove fruitful to study if training in both domains on ISec would increase the efficacy of SETA programs. Similar to how fear appeals effectiveness has been shown to increase with personal relevance (Johnston and Warkentin, 2015), potentially training in personal domains would have similar results. Second, privacy-based ISec researchers could provide understanding of the ethical implications of allowing the cybersecurity staff and personnel access to the personal domain of high-level executives and his or her extended network. ISec and IS research is in the nascent stages of exploring the ethical implications of IS (Marabelli and Markus, 2017; Wade and Roth, 2015; Yapo and Weiss, 2018), and cybersecurity could provide insight into ethics and protection.

Third, while we direct attention to whaling, it is important to note that there are equally relevant cyberattacks that employ similar tactics such doxing, virtual kidnapping, and cyberstalking, with equally pernicious outcomes that are understudied. By illustrating the differences in whaling, phishing, and spear phishing, we demonstrate a need to examine a broader range of criminal activities outside of the conventional domain of ISec research. As we show, whaling involves using multiple forms of ICT-mediated deception to invoke actions in the real world, as the attack is not dependent on a single click, credential theft, or malicious file download. Furthermore, whaling creates intangible (e.g. employee morale) and tangible (e.g. market share) costs that we often do not associate with cyberattacks. Similar to whaling, cyberattacks like doxing, cyber stalking, cyber bullying occur across multiple platforms and create costs for organization, individual, and society. ISec research has yet to provide theoretical or practical guidance for defending these non-traditional cyberattacks.

Fourth, we identify the importance of examining cyber risk that exists outside the defenses of the organizations. Through advances in IoT, social media, and artificial intelligence, we are creating interconnected sociotechnical systems which seamlessly intertwine individual, groups, processes, and technologies (Yoo, 2010). Similarly, these advances in computing present risk not only for organizations and their members but also in groups, technologies, and processes associated with that member. This entanglement of information technology, context, and the individual creates points of vulnerability not only in technology but also in the context and human network of organizational members.

To realize a broad understanding of such complex systems, ISec researchers could take a sociomaterial perspective. This perspective could be useful to understand the intertwinement of social contexts with information technologies that present risk and opportunities for firm security (Orlikowski, 2007; Orlikowski and Scott, 2008; Scott and Orlikowski, 2014). For example, using a sociomaterial approach, ISec research could examine the effects of the interplay of IoT, such as digital technologies stored in automobiles, smart appliances, smart watches, children’s toys, and implantable wireless medical devices with the context and processes. Sociotechnical systems create vulnerabilities for organizations, and understanding the interconnectedness of devices could shed new light on how to construct more secure organizational processes. Many of these products are introduced into networks with little standardization of security protocols and seduce individuals to adopt them by prioritizing features and functions over security (Rowe et al., 2012). Such work is important, because we have little understanding about how the interplay of IoT with home and work life creates sociotechnical threat vectors to organizational information. Thus, further understanding into ISec across the organization, domains, and sociotechnical systems, in general, could provide an avenue that impacts academia and practice.

Overall, by providing a foundational and nuanced understanding of whaling, we underscore the need to broaden the forms of criminality studied in ISec research.

Because such work is new, there is a need for descriptive work that sheds light on the relevance of existing theories or opportunities for crafting new theories that provide value to the IS field. As Hong et al. (2013) imply, doing so will require developing contextualized theory that integrates the social elements (e.g. how it affects people and processes) and technical elements (e.g. intuitively understanding the technical defenses) of cyberattacks. Absent such theory, ISec research will struggle to keep pace with cybercriminals who launch cyberattacks and security professionals who require more sophisticated understanding to defend the boundaries of the firm.

## Conclusion

Whaling is a complex, sophisticated, long con with acute implications, and requires that whalers demonstrate patience, precision, and planning to prove efficacious. Although whaling is a highly effective and damaging type of cyberattack, there has been little ISec research directed to this form of cybercrime. In this catchword, we compare whaling to phishing and spear phishing and provide realworld cases to establish what makes whaling different from other forms of phishing. Whaling is different and more difficult to defend, as it bypasses technological countermeasures with ease and preys on the hypercontextualization of a specific target. It crosses different domains and jeopardizes valuable informational and human assets.

While law enforcement agencies such as the FBI and the European Union Agency for Network and Information Security (ENISA) have identified whaling as a serious problem for organizations in BEC scams, whaling has been understudied by academics and practitioners alike. ENISA has called for cybersecurity research that focuses on incorporating psychological, sociological, and behavioral perspectives, which we believe will be required for whaling research. With this article, we hope to draw attention to this phenomenon and provide a call for action and direction for ISec researchers to conduct research that helps firms defend against whaling attacks.

In this study, we use whaling to demonstrate how providing a nuanced understanding to a wicked problem can provide a foundation for whaling research and more broadly ISec research in general. As this study demonstrates, new forms of criminality appear each day, and by providing understanding into the threat landscape through one phenomenon, it can hopefully provide guidance for ISec research to tackle the wicked problems faced by organizations today.
