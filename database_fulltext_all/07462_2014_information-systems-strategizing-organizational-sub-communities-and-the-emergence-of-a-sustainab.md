# Information systems strategizing, organizational sub-communities, and the emergence of a sustainability strategy

Ola Henfridsson <sup>a,</sup>⇑, Mikael Lind <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, UK <sup>b</sup> Viktoria Institute, Göteborg, Sweden

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Available online 27 November 2013

Keywords: Strategy Information systems Interpretation Sustainability Practice Case study

## a b s t r a c t

The realized strategy contents of information systems (IS) strategizing are a result of both deliberate and emergent patterns of action. In this paper, we focus on emergent patterns of action by studying the formation of strategies that build on local technology-mediated practices. This is done through case study research of the emergence of a sustainability strategy at a European automaker. Studying the practices of four organizational sub-communities, we develop a process perspective of sub-communities’ activity-based production of strategy contents. The process model explains the contextual conditions that make subcommunities initiate SI strategy contents production, the activity-based process of strategy contents production, and the IS strategy outcome. The process model, which draws on Jarzabkowski’s strategy-as-practice lens and Mintzberg’s strategy typology, contributes to the growing IS strategizing literature that examines local practices in IS efforts of strategic importance.

## 1. Introduction

Our understanding of information systems (IS) strategy has been dominated by the idea that deliberate managerial planning is essential when using information systems for strategic positioning and performance (Bakos and Treacy, 1986; Kettinger et al., 1994), aligning IS strategy to the firm’s business strategy (Avison et al., 2004; Henderson and Venkatraman, 1993), controlling the strategic information systems cycle (Galliers, 1991; Gottschalk, 1999), and building IS competence and capability for competitive advantage in turbulent environments (Nevo and Wade, 2011; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006; Peppard and Ward, 2004). Yet, it is also recognized that the pervasive digital infrastructures of today’s organizations offer a setting where the relation between deliberate planning of specific IS initiatives and strategic performance and outcomes is increasingly complex (Henfridsson and Bygstad, 2013: Merali et al., 2012), This insight has spurred increasing recognition of the role of various stakeholders taking action, typically firmly rooted in ongoing digitally-enabled practices, that eventually may develop into IS initiatives of strategic importance (Nolan, 2012). Collecting a number of contributions recognizing the practice basis of IS strategizing, the edited book by Ciborra et al. (2000) manifests an early IS example of a shift in interest from business strategy contents to the strategy process (cf. Pettigrew, 1992). This shift involved an interest in micro-strategizing, that is, the ‘‘processes and practices which constitute the day-to-day activities of organizational life and which relate to strategic outcomes’’ (Johnson et al., 2003, p. 3).

A common motivation for focusing on micro-strategizing, which is not only manifested in IS strategy research with a practice lens (Ciborra, 1997, 2002; De Vaujany et al., 2013; Nandhakumar et al., 2005) but perhaps even more so in the writings of strategy-as-practice (SaP) scholars (Johnson et al., 2003), relates to the turbulent business landscape, where flexibility, agility, and improvisation are increasingly important aspects of devising initiatives of strategic importance (El Sawy et al., 2010). As strategy making is becoming a continuous process (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997), it is natural to direct inquiry toward the activities of actors close to customers, local markets, and products-in-use (Johnson et al., 2007). In so doing, scholars do not only portray IS strategy formation as opportunistic (Hackney and Little, 1999) but also highlight cases of IS strategizing where local initiatives have triumphed over carefully planned and well-resourced ones in different settings such as banking (Baptista et al., 2010), health (Aanestad and Blegind-Jensen, 2011), and pharmaceutics (Ciborra, 1996).

IS strategizing, which typically involves deployment, management, and investment in information technology in organizations, consists of both deliberate and emergent patterns of action. In this paper, we focus on the formation of emerging strategies that build on local technology-mediated practices. However, rather than dealing with cases where local actors manage to conceive initiatives that eventually become recognized as strategically important (cf. Aanestad and Blegind-Jensen, 2011; Baptista et al., 2010; Ciborra, 2002), we seek to investigate the dynamics by which multiple organizational sub-communities can realize the contents of an IS strategy. For the purposes of this paper, we refer to organizational subcommunity as a group of actors who share interests in a particular domain of activity in an organization. In times when diversity of perspectives is vital (Feller et al., 2012; Page, 2007; Yoo et al., 2010, 2012) and speed is an essential quality of strategy-making (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997; Fine, 1998), it is useful to explore emergent strategy grounded in the activities of multiple organizational sub-communities in which actors serve as strategy practitioners as they provide strategy contents that originates, yet transcends, their technology-mediated practices

The empirical setting of this interpretive field research (Klein and Myers, 1999) is a major European automaker, AutoInc, and its efforts to implement a new sustainability strategy in which innovative IS use was a cornerstone. AutoInc, like other automakers of medium-sized and large cars, had little choice but to make sustainable cars a strategic top priority to meet governmental, and eventually customer, expectations. The European Commission inaugurated a legislative framework designed to reduce average carbon emissions to 120 g/km by 2012, and has set a target of 95 g by 2020. Since a car sold in the European Union averaged 163 g in 2004 (AutoInc cars averaged 199.8 g in 2004) and new technological paths had to be created, the new legislation was a major challenge for AutoInc.

The problem addressed in our research is: What is the process by which the micro-strategizing of actors from a variety of organizational sub-communities contribute to realize strategy contents as they use IS to implement a sustainability strategy? Using Jarzabkowski’s (2003, 2005, 2010) activity based approach to strategy as practice as a starting-point, we specifically followed the micro-strategizing of four key organizational sub-communities of two parallel car projects, focusing on the development of a plug-in hybrid car<sup>1</sup> and a fully electric car respectively. We studied the contextual conditions of AutoInc’s so-called ‘‘green, safe, and connected’’ strategy, how each sub-community actively produced strategy contents, and how the contents were shaped by a diversity of perspectives when the strategy was realized.

Our research offers a new process model as the main contribution to the IS strategy literature. Drawing on Jarzabkowski’s (2003, 2005, 2010) activity-based approach and Mintzberg’s (1978) seminal strategy categories, the process model of organizational sub-communities’ production of IS strategy contents links such goal-directed activity with contextual conditions and strategy outcomes. As such, this research represents one of the first studies that explicitly reaches out to the SaP literature in using Jarzabkowski’s work for characterizing the interaction of organizational sub-communities at AutoInc with the emergent IS strategy and the wider organizational community.

## 2. Related literature and conceptual basis

## 2.1. IS strategizing

For the purposes of this paper, we view IS strategizing as a process of goal-directed activity intended to realize a strategy for using information systems in an organization (cf. Karpovsky et al., 2013). Such strategy realization typically involves various activities related to the deployment, management, and investment in information technology. Paraphrasing Mintzberg’s (1978) seminal work, the realized IS strategy contents are a result of both deliberate and emergent patterns of action. The main bulk of the IS strategy literature emphasizes deliberate action undertaken by managers (e.g., Avison et al., 2004; Gottschalk, 1999; Kettinger et al., 1994).

It is nevertheless fair to say that the notion of IS strategy has undergone reinterpretation over the years (Karpovsky et al., 2013; Merali et al., 2012), where the merits of continued investigation of, for instance, IT alignment research is critically discussed (Chan and Reich, 2007; Galliers, 2007). While the belief in deliberate managerial planning is still strong, emergent strategy has been recognized as an increasingly important contributor to realized IS strategy (cf. Hackney and Little, 1999; Nolan, 2012). This recognition coincides with the increasingly pervasive digital infrastructures found in today’s organizations, which makes the classic idea of alignment of IT with business strategies less useful (Henfridsson and Bygstad,

2013; Nolan, 2012). It also coincides with the ‘‘practice turn’’ in our field (De Vaujany et al., 2013; Levina and Vaast, 2005; Orlikowski, 2000), suggesting how strategizing is ‘‘grounded in the ongoing practices of organizational actors, and emerges out of their (tacit and not so tacit) accommodations to and experiments with the everyday contingencies, breakdowns, exceptions, opportunities, and unintended consequences that they encounter’’ (Orlikowski, 1996, p. 65).

A growing number of case studies document the role of local actors in shaping emergent strategy, where technologymediated practices eventually emerge into IS initiatives of strategic importance. For instance, Aanestad and Blegind Jensen (2011) describe how the development of a local system for sharing and accessing patient data became the basis for sectorwide sharing of patient record information across Denmark in 2009. Some nine years earlier, the project, which involved a system that extracted patient data from already existing digital infrastructures, was initiated by a physician together with a former Danish National Board of Health employee, and it was drafted in two Danish counties in collaboration with three IT vendors. As another example, Baptista et al. (2010) show how actions relative to an intranet, deeply embedded in existing work practices, remained unnoticed by management despite the strategic importance of this infrastructure. Lastly, Ciborra (2000) describes Roche’s Internet-based strategy as a result of an emergent process, which eventually resulted in a powerful digital infrastructure outperforming the former system (MedNet).

A common aspect of these studies is their emphasis on the strategy that emerges from organizational sub-communities knowledge about their local problem situation and actions taken to address that situation. Similar to SaP scholars, they view the implementation of a strategy as a process of organizational becoming (Benson, 1977; Jarzabkowski, 2005; Orlikowski, 2000; Tsoukas and Chia, 2002). However, little has been done in information systems to theorize this local activity as a process involving actors from multiple sub-communities, each one involved in realizing strategy contents through technologymediated practices.

## 2.2. Towards an organizational sub-community model of IS strategizing

While strategy content research focuses on what strategic decisions are taken and how they relate to the firm’s industrial context, SaP is typically distinguished with reference to its orientation towards how a particular firm’s strategy emerges (Chia and MacKay, 2007; Bourgeois, 1980). It is fair to say that SaP originates in the works of Pettigrew (1985) and Johnson (1987) and has emerged as an important stream of research focused on the micro strategy and strategizing of the firm (Johnson et al., 2003). In this research, the adoption of a SaP perspective (Chia and MacKay, 2007; Johnson et al., 2003; Jarzabkowski, 2008; Whittington, 2006) provides a basis for understanding IS strategizing as a process involving actors from multiple sub-communities.

Drawing on Jarzabkowski’s (2003, 2005, 2010) activity-based SaP perspective, we specifically look upon the IS strategizing of sub-communities as an activity system. Viewing IS strategizing as an activity system is useful because, compared with other notable theoretical directions of SaP such as structuration theory (Whittington, 2010) and perspectives grounded in Bourdieu (Gomez, 2010) and Foucault (Allard-Poesi, 2010), its theoretical underpinnings in activity theory invites the study of agency in the use of artifacts (Jarzabkowski, 2010). This is consistent with our ambition to specifically trace sub-communities realization of a sustainability strategy through the use of information systems. We distinguish information systems from information technology by considering the information-processing functionality actors can direct and manipulate to achieve their objectives (cf. Piccoli, 2008). We also recognize how information systems can mediate goal-oriented individual and collaborative activity (Nicolini et al., 2012), and that such activity eventually forms practices as it forms patterns repeated in daily organizational work. We refer to such practices as ‘‘technology-mediated practices’’.

Fig. 1 serves as the starting-point for our investigation of organizational sub-communities in IS strategizing. As noted above, we view IS strategizing as a process of goal-directed activity intended to realize a strategy for using information systems in an organization. A sub-community’s contribution to such strategy realization can be understood by considering its technology-mediated practices in the context of the wider organizational community and the emerging strategy (see Fig. 1). First, the local actors of an organizational sub-community shape and are shaped by the emergent strategy. In shaping the strategy, the actors draw on a set of routines, institutionalized norms and beliefs, and technological resources in taking action. Among these, and of particular importance here is the technology-mediated practice through which actors in sub-communities shape the emerging strategy. As actors in an organizational sub-community become committed to contribute to strategy contents, they bring their own unique practices as to appreciate and shape the emerging strategy

Second, the practices of the sub-community enable interaction with the wider organizational community to which it belongs (Jarzabkowski, 2005). The organizational community holds the collective structures shared by different actors in the organization (Jarzabkowski, 2003). These collective structures serve as contextual conditions within which the IS strategy is conceived and brought forth. Still, it should be recognized that sub-communities are heterogeneous, each drawing on their technology-mediated practices to shape the emergent strategy. Lastly, the emergent IS strategy, which refers to a pattern of goal-oriented activity that was unintended at the inception of the IS strategy. It may be seen as a result of a nexus of pluralistic and loosely coupled technology-mediated practices (Berente and Yoo, 2012). Each organizational sub-community will bring their technology-mediated practices in their efforts to transform the familiar past through strategizing. In other words, the organizational sub-community contributing certain strategy contents should be prepared that other sub-communities may just as well resist the path enacted through such strategizing, as it would support it (cf. Jarzabkowski, 2005).

With this understanding of the organizational sub-community’s role in IS strategizing, we embarked on a detailed study of the strategizing of actors from four organizational sub-communities contributed to realize strategy contents at AutoInc as they used IS to implement a sustainability strategy. Our goal was to generate the contours of a process model with which to understand the dynamics of IS strategizing, as multiple sub-communities are involved in realizing IS strategy contents

## 3. Methods

We conducted an interpretive field study (Klein and Myers, 1999) of organizational sub-communities’ use of information systems to implement a sustainability strategy at AutoInc. AutoInc is a renowned European automaker that produces, markets, and sells around 400,000 cars per year primarily in Europe and the U.S. AutoInc is a fully owned subsidiary of Asian-CarCorp, which is a rapidly growing vehicle manufacturer in China. The total number of employees at AutoInc was close to 20,000 in 2009.

Consistent with the genesis of interpretive research to better understand a research phenomenon by inquiring into the everyday meaning-making of human agents (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991; Walsham, 1995), we collected naturalistic data of actors in four AutoInc sub-communities. While the data material covers the period from 2003 to 2011, the last two years involved real-time studies. We concentrated our attention on how the sustainability strategy was manifested in two ongoing car projects that aimed at developing a new plug-in hybrid car and a new purely electric car.

## 3.1. Data collection

In line with the typical interpretive case study (Myers, 2009), our field research involved multiple data sources including: semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and archival data (see Table 1). We organized and recorded this data in a single research database using the Atlas TI software for qualitative analysis. The semi-structured interviews were the primary basis for our analytical efforts. The two other data sources were primarily used for providing the contextual background and for achieving the close involvement characterizing the relationship between the researcher and research subjects in interpretive research (Walsham, 2006).

We conducted 32 semi-structured interviews (32 respondents). The mean length of the interviews was 67 min (S.D. = 18 min). All interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed verbatim, producing more than 35 h of recorded materia amounting to approximately 310,000 words. Following the principle of the hermeneutic circle (Klein and Myers, 1999), the interviewing was conducted in two waves (cf Cardinal et al. 2004) We first interviewed 24 respondents engaged in technical development of sustainable technology, either as employees at AutoInc Technical Development (9), AutoInc Technology (4), AutoInc Trucks (2), suppliers (6), or industry experts (3). Examples of positions held by the respondents were strategy director, strategy officer, test platform manager, HMI engineer, senior technical advisor, technical specialist, and product architecture manager. The objective of the interviewing during this first wave was to generate an in-depth understanding of sub-communities’ appreciation of the sustainability strategy and their views on their potential contribution of strategy contents. The initial selection of respondents was done in collaboration with the Senior Technical Advisor (who gave us access to the research site in the first place) with the intention to cover a variety of actors involved in realizing the sustainability strategy at AutoInc. Once the initial interviews were booked, we were able to recruit new respondents on the basis of advice from respondents already included in the study.

Attempting to see the inter-relation between the whole and parts of the phenomenon studied (Bernstein, 1983, pp. 131– 139; Klein and Myers, 1999), the data collected were continuously sensitized in view of the literatures on sustainability, IS strategy, and strategy-as-practice. Consistent with the typical open-ended interview study (Silverman, 2006), each interview included open-ended questions covering a number of themes to understand the meaning that the respondent attached to AutoInc’s sustainability work in general and the sustainability strategy in particular. Introduced as part of a study of Auto-Inc’s sustainability strategy, the interview themes covered strategies for carbon emission reduction, organizational changes, safety aspects, technical architecture, connectivity and communication infrastructure, competence development, and market. Respondents were probed in detail about their view on each theme to achieve the deeper understanding sought after in interpretive research. For each interview, certain themes were more rigorously covered than others, depending on the respondent’s involvement, competence, and organizational position.

Participant observation was another source of data. The objective of the observations was to provide an in-depth understanding of the context in which the IS strategizing at AutoInc took place. We spent almost 75 h observing 18 meetings related to AutoInc’s sustainability efforts, including project meetings, steering committee meetings, and everyday work activity. During these observations extensive notes were taken for later inclusion in the research database. In addition to these meetings, we conducted debriefings after meetings and a number of informal interviews with AutoInc employees, con sultants, suppliers, and collaborators.

Finally, our study included a significant volume of archival data for understanding the contextual conditions from which organizational sub-communities’ technology-mediated practice emerged. The archival data included market analyses, press releases, project descriptions, and technical specifications. For instance, we collected all AutoInc press releases from 2003 to March 2011 to trace the sequence of events and triangulate this view with the data gathered through interviewing. A total of 1181 press releases were recorded, where we selected 317 for inclusion in the research database. We used a quite inclusive selection criterion by including any press release that, directly or indirectly, mentioned sustainability, powertrain, infotainment, active safety, or special vehicles. We also collected a number of market analyses related to sustainability initiatives in the automotive industry, press releases of competitors and suppliers, as well as other types of secondary data (project descriptions, technical specifications).

## 3.2. Data analysis

As always in interpretive research, the data collection and data analysis were conducted in tandem to benefit from the understanding emerging from recursively iterating between theoretical conceptions and the empirical material (Klein and Myers, 1999). Reflecting upon this process in view of Lee and Hubona (2009), our theory generation was not a linear process from data to process model, but was interactive where initial insights were derived from the data (e.g., during the first interviews), and later on tested in view of our emerging understanding. Along the way, our process model emerged, triggering the need to reinterpret our case narrative and the data on which they are based. The type of generalization employed in our interpretive research resonates well with what Walsham (1995) refers to as ‘‘drawing of specific implications’’. Overall, the attempts to construct relations between closely related empirical concepts and associate the activity they represent with stakeholders have been useful for grounding the process model in the data

Second, one informant, the Senior Technical Advisor mentioned above, initially helped us to identify actors who would be relevant to interview for understanding the strategy process at AutoInc. From this initial selection of respondents we quickly recognized three of the relevant sub-communities. These three sub-communities (powertrain, infotainment and driver information, and active safety) resonated well with our pre-conceptions, although we initially viewed human–machine interface (HMI) as a stand-alone sub-community. Somewhere in the middle of the first wave of interviews, however, we appreciated the need to get access to respondents at AutoInc Special Vehicles, as this department was responsible for the major electric car project at AutoInc. The informant did not identify Special Vehicles, possibly because it did not belong to the same organization as Technical Development. We qualitatively verified our intuitive sense of which actors belonged to a particular sub-community by going back to the descriptive concepts from our open coding to actors and actor groups. For instance, it was quite clear that concepts such as ‘‘battery management’’ and ‘‘control systems’’ could be traced to people sharing interests in the powertrain domain, while people who spoke about ‘‘eco-driving’’ and ‘‘communicating green driving’’ shared interests related to the infotainment and driver information domain.

## 4. The emergence of a sustainability strategy

## 4.1. Contextual conditions

In 2007, a new business strategy called ‘‘green, safe, and connected’’ (GSC) was conceived at AutoInc. AutoInc’s cars have been traditionally recognized for their excellent safety. As such, positioning environmental sustainability and car connectivity at the same level as safety was a major strategic change for everyone involved. A former strategy officer in the product development organization recalled why and how this change was initiated:

After visiting suppliers, research and quality institutes, and other forward-looking actors we realized that our products weren’t well positioned for the future customer. I specifically remember the visit at Delphi’s research institute in California, where we shared future outlooks. Based on this road-trip, we developed the basis for our new strategy, the fact that we need to go towards green, safe, and connected. We took this insight home and I was in charge for laying out this strategy for the product development unit.

AutoInc managers figured a more environment-friendly approach was needed to satisfy the future customer, and knew that they had to lower the average car carbon emissions to comply with EU legislation (120 g/km by 2012, 95 g/km by 2020) One of the main messages in the strategy was therefore to develop a car that would dramatically reduce emissions. Also, management recognized that it would be important to make a purely electric car, not least for branding purposes.

Yet, AutoInc had little experience of building low-carbon emission cars (in 2004 when the EU legislation was inaugurated, AutoInc’s cars averaged 199.8 g), and at this stage, the electric cars market was virtually non-existent without any prior large-scale experience in the automotive industry. The strategy was therefore open-ended. While the strategy provided the structural context and top management support for the development of the green, safe, and connected innovation paths, the actual contents of the strategy were intentionally left open.

Until 2007, AutoInc already had independent activities in these technology areas (in particular, on the safety side). Fig. 1 provides a chronology of these efforts in terms of digitally enabled services or functionality. Placing sustainability at the same strategic level as safety was a bold message to organizational sub-communities at AutoInc. Traditionally seen as a question of engine technology, thus primarily related to the power train sub-community, the sustainability sentiment of the GSC strategy was therefore gradually adopted as a virtue important for several organizational sub-communities at AutoInc.

This was certainly something quite challenging for the automaker and its organizational sub-communities. The tradition of separating concerns in complex product design (cf. Abernathy, 1978; Baldwin and Clark, 2000) certainly existed at Auto-Inc. Manifesting differences in their relative position in the firm and epistemic backgrounds, unique technology-mediated practices reflective of these separated concerns had therefore evolved over time. In the view of the strategy, it became natural for actors in the organizational sub-communities to approach the GSC strategy from these practices as they originated in the power train, infotainment and driver information, active safety, and the special vehicles sub-communities. In what follows, we describe how each of the sub-communities appropriated AutoInc’s GSC strategy and actively produced strategy contents.

## 4.2. The sub-communities’ production of strategy contents

## 4.2.1. The powertrain sub-community

In 2006, AutoInc and its previous owner committed to allocate considerable research and development resources to the powertrain sub-community. This commitment came at a time when AutoInc confronted negative press and political pressure to decrease the fuel consumption of its car product lines. One year later, AutoInc launched a plug-in hybrid concept car framed by AutoInc’s senior vice president of R&D as ‘‘a ground-breaking invention’’. The concept car manifested a starting-point for AutoInc to invest heavily in plug-in hybrid technology.

The plug-in hybrid car soon became AutoInc’s main project for realizing the automaker’s new strategy of greening their products. Given positive feedback on the concept car at motor shows, the power train department got a go-ahead for developing the hybrid engine further. Commenting the carbon emission levels that worked as targets for the power train department, one of the department’s strategists noted:

Three years ago we received a lot of negative press coverage saying that we only build large and fuel-consuming cars. We realized that by 2012 we needed to achieve 140 g CO2 per kilometer on average across our fleet, and given that the European Union discussed 95 g as the target level by 2020, we were really stressed to get going in the green direction.. . .So, we first made all those minor improvements with what we had at hand, resulting in our lean combustion engine product line. Now, the real challenge [for power train] begins, the road towards 2020 and 95 g. Hybrids will be the key. We have our electric car project [at the special vehicles department] to learn about electric engines, control systems, and so on, but the battery technology is not in par with what is needed.

Given the power train sub-community’s legacy in combustion engines, electric cars were controversial at AutoInc. Electric cars would make existing combustion engine competency obsolete. It was therefore not surprising that power train engineers were pushing for plug-in hybrids. As a strategy director noted

If I would drag on talking about electric cars here, no one would listen to me. You take what you have, then this will be a step towards electric engines, but if you face the current cost structure, there’ll never be a pure electric car. [. . .] You know, for the romantics who really believe in electric cars, it’s all about the battery. But cars are so much more, that’s for sure.

Plug-in hybrids provided space for powertrain engineers to build on existing practices while at the same time establishing new ones based on the learning about a range of new components and control systems including battery technology, battery management, and electric engines. One specific practice stood out in this regard. Going for plug-in hybrids entailed significant work on designing new forms of digital control systems. While the traditional combustion engine relies on information systems for real-time control of parameters such as fuel injection, the hybrid engine also needed systems that control the interaction between the combustion engine and the electric engine. The strategy director of electrical engineering noted:

This transformation is obviously a huge challenge [for power train]. [. . .] Battery technology is a big thing here, but not so much the chemistry around it, other companies work on that. We need to focus on the digital control systems involved in battery management and engine management. This includes how to consume the energy in an efficient way, and coordinating the use of the two power sources.

## A test platform manager further noted:

We need to learn how to build models for further developing the [digital] control systems on the complete car level. This is needed to test and develop how the hybrid system operates with transient conditions, different fault cases and driving scenarios. Models help us simulate a larger space of possible scenarios. It’ll contribute to better quality and stability in what we design and deliver.

As summarized in Fig. 3, the GSC strategy was the starting-point for a number of projects on plug-in hybrid technology, reflecting the powertrain sub-community’s vision of lean rather than zero-emission engines. This effort to shape the emergent strategy at AutoInc grew out of existing technology-mediated practices among powertrain engineers, where, for instance, existing competences on digital control design could be further leveraged to the hybrid engine case.

## 4.2.2. Infotainment and driver information

Over the years, the infotainment and driver information sub-community had launched onboard telematics (2000), iPod adapter (2005), Bluetooth handsfree system (2006), portable navigation support (2007), and a new integrated infotainment system (2010). Given the massive increase in functionality and services intended for the driver, the sub-community founded a human–machine Interaction (HMI) unit, responsible for designing the in-car user interfaces of services such as handsfree and navigation systems, as well as the information provided in the instrument cluster and center stack of the car dashboard. In addition, the group dealt with the ergonomics of physical controls and other tangible interfaces

The infotainment and driver information sub-community grew considerably at the time of the conception of GSC-strategy. Reflective of the growing confidence that followed, actors felt encouraged to think beyond the sub-community’s traditional boundaries. An HMI engineer noted:

We should use our energy to influence the driver to drive as green as possible. Without compromising safety, of course.. . .HMI should definitely be a key component in reducing fuel consumption or lowering the battery consumption to extend the range of electric vehicles. [. . .] There is research saying that fuel consumption can be reduced by 10–15% if the driver is coached. Let’s say that means 5% for electrified cars, it is still a huge save. Just imagine the cost of technical power train development for accomplishing the same result as ecodriving applications. It would take enormous amounts of money.

The sub-community's vision of eco-driving as a way to reduce fuel consumption resonated well with the behavioral science background among many of its members. It reflected the user-centered design practice pervading the sub-community’s efforts. While this vision had relatively strong support across sub-communities at AutoInc, there were institutional factors outside AutoInc’s immediate control that were influencing the extent to which the automaker could prioritize such technol ogy for reducing emissions. For instance, powertrain recognized the value of systems that supported a behavioral change among drivers in general terms, but were clear about its relative irrelevance as far as legislation is concerned. As pointed out by a technical specialist at the powertrain department:

It is really important to drive sensibly and we should support this. And perhaps, in the future, we might get credit [as an automaker] from the authorities for having such systems. [. . .] Unfortunately, our targets are defined in relation to a specific driving cycle. The performance of our cars are assessed with regard to a pre-defined driving cycle including a specific speed profile and gear shifts, and so on and forth. [. . .] Today’s system certainly does not value attempts to influence the behavior of the driver in authentic driving.

Given the background in systems and services directed at the car driver and passengers, actors in the infotainment and driver information sub-community pushed eco-driving as a way to contribute to the emergent IS strategy (see Fig. 4). Ecodriving entails providing the driver with information based on real-time data about how to drive the car in a more energyefficient way. Given that drivers would comply with the recommendations, HMI engineers anticipated that the fuel consumption would be reduced considerably. They managed to put systems that supported behavioral changes on AutoInc’s agenda. However, powertrain was clear about the lack of support for behavioral controls in EU legislation, meaning that eco-driving systems were not prioritized as a key element in the emergent IS strategy.

## 4.2.3. Active safety

AutoInc was a leading automaker in passive safety technologies including early introduction of rear-seat safety belts (1969) and side-collision protection systems (1989). Early on, the automaker utilized increasingly advanced computing, communication, and sensor systems for active, or preventive, safety. In 2002, it introduced roll stability control, followed by, e.g., intelligent driver information system (2003), blind spot information system (2004), personal car communicator (2006), collision warning with brake support (2006), adaptive cruise control (2007), driver alert control (2007), city safety (2009), and pedestrian detection with auto brake (2010).

![](/api/attachments/VXPPFHCD/fulltext/images/8f286f638c804316e951b981e82aafdfc477a11c27a02689d435325aa17168d6.jpg)  
Fig. 4. Infotainment and driver information’s of strategy contents.

As a result of rapid functional growth in the safety area and the importance for AutoInc, active safety emerged as a standalone sub-community working on systems and functionality that process large amounts of sensor data in real-time to detect dangers and assist the driver in controlling the vehicle. The Senior Technical Advisor at electrical engineering underlined the value of a sub-community dedicated to these complex functions:

Just imagine a typical challenge in active safety – you have four or five different sensors, aimed at giving you a 360 view of the car’s immediate environment, producing lots of information. Then, suddenly, some traffic incident happens and there is a driver reacting to it in an unpredictable way. The active safety system should then, in real-time, make decisions to avoid a collision. That’s really complex. Compare with the hybrid system, although advanced, the control system managing the two engines is not nearly as complex.

While the active safety engineers were really proud about their leading position in safety technology, they were increasingly concerned with how they would be able to draw on their safety solution practices in relation to sustainability. First, they emphasized how particular active safety functionality already contributes to more ecologically sound driving. As an example, an active safety manager noted:

I very much believe in platooning<sup>2</sup>, which supports a much smoother traffic flow in that the system not only looks at the nearest car [such as an adaptive cruise control does] in front but also at cars ahead. It can determine the traffic rhythm further ahead. It doesn’t have to actively brake that hard but can adopt a calm and situated driving behavior. It improves cue dynamics.

In addition, active safety engineers saw how their information systems related to powertrain’s hybrid engine. At the heart of a hybrid engine, the electric engine’s battery needs to be reloaded during the drive to improve the efficiency of the hybrid concept. For instance, the energy created when the car is breaking is used to reload the battery:

Active safety can contribute much in terms of understanding the car’s environment. The hybrid system knows the vehicle’s speed, acceleration, electric powers, and so on, but it can never tell us anything about the car’s surroundings. It can never make predictions. It does not know the geographical future, whether we are soon going uphill or downhill. While it knows the driver’s requested torque or moment of force, it cannot predict a behavior that supports the green side of things. We have lots of such data, since the whole idea of active safety is to predict and avoid rather than minimizing the harm in case of an accident.

While these opportunities were something that the active safety group pushed in their discussions with powertrain engineers, most of the efforts were consumed on avoiding that conflicting requirements and functionality. The active safety manager continued:

For us, it is really important to develop processes that avoid conflicting requirements and implementations. Our friends at powertrain are now chasing emissions, low fuel consumption. Then they want seamless and frequent switches between the two engines. That really puts stress on our systems. For example, they implement start/stop systems for the combustion engine, because they recognize that the driving allows it. At that point, our systems might be active too, giving counter-productive instructions from our active safety perspective.

The product architecture manager at the electrical engineering department also recognized this interconnectedness between active safety systems and powertrain systems. However, he also remarked that some of the active safety systems had unwanted consequences for environmental sustainability:

Carbon emissions are really driving what we do now, and what's interesting is that some of the safety systems conflict with the ambition to lower emissions. Adaptive cruise controls are one example. . .From a green perspective, you want to allow the car to slow down uphill and increase speed downhill. If you know there is a curve coming up, you want to slow down. . .The adaptive cruise control is designed to follow the rhythm of the car ahead of you, not to drive greener.

Despite recognizing these concerns, active safety engineers took another perspective on these issues. Rather than taking the current design of the car for granted, a long-term sustainability vision for the active safety sub-community was to enhance their sensor fusion practices to the extent that the car’s physical construction (passive safety) would become less relevant. ‘‘Autonomous driving’’ would certainly lower carbon emissions. The active safety manager noted:

In the long run, assuming that active safety systems such as platoons. lane-departure warning, emergency brake assist would work perfectly, passive safety will be unnecessary. If cars don’t crash, the vehicle’s weight can be severely reduced. We can have much lighter structures in the cars and in that way realizing a much greener car.

Challenged by the fact that sustainability suddenly was recognized as equally important as safety, people in the active safety sub-community pushed the idea that active safety would make passive safety unnecessary and pave the way for autonomous driving (see Fig. 5). The idea was simply an extension of their current technology-mediated practices related to sensor fusion.

## 4.2.4. Special vehicles

Skilled in setting up agile project teams, the traditional focus of AutoInc’s special vehicles is on taxi cars, police cars, executive cars, and chassis for ambulances and limousines. In 2008–2009, however, they successfully managed a high-profile project intended to quickly introduce a lean combustion engine car series. The car line became a market success and it accounted for 14% (46,632 cars) of AutoInc’s total sales in 2009 and anecdotal evidence suggests that this portion increased significantly in 2010.

As a result, special vehicles had taken on a sustainability role at AutoInc, which got further accentuated when special vehicles was assigned the future-looking electric car development. An electrical engineer noted:

Someone realized that the brand needed an electric car and at that point it couldn’t be assigned to technical development, so, given the previous success, special vehicles got the assignment despite the fact that they initially didn’t know anything about electric cars.

Lacking the legacy in the electric car domain, it may not have come as a surprise that special vehicles approached the request unconventionally. This was particularly clear with regard to the problem of ‘range anxiety’, a generally acknowledged market concern of electric cars. Range anxiety refers to the fear of being stranded at the roadside due to insufficient battery capacity to reach a charging station. In addition to the battery and battery management issues bothering power train engineers, special vehicles, early on, saw that range anxiety also needed to be handled as an information problem. As the head of special vehicles commented:

An essential component in overcoming range anxiety is to increase the driver’s ability to plan the trip. Then, communication between the driver and the car as well as the ability to find charging spots become important. The driver must feel confident in having the leeway to manage contingencies.

The electric car’s range was set to 150 km on one battery charge. While such a range is on the upper tail of a distribution of all trips (AutoInc calculated that 85% of all car trips are shorter than 40 km), it was nevertheless considered necessary to build a future vision of an intelligent zero-emission infrastructure to manage the anticipated range anxiety of future buyers. Together with an energy firm and a multi-national telco, AutoInc therefore explored how to build an infrastructure including information systems that would help addressing the range anxiety problem and simultaneously feedback information to energy information systems. Such an infrastructure would not only assist the car driver with information on how to locate charge stations but also provide hot-spot functionality and a variety of charging services. This industry-spanning collaboration, actively co-developing an infrastructure beyond the car itself, was outside the normal scope of AutoInc operations.

In 2010, at the launch of the collaboration between AutoInc and the energy company, both parties expressed strong hopes about a sustainable society. In a joint press release, the energy company’s president stated:

We believe in the future of the electric car [. . .] We work to promote a sustainable society in our region [. . .] Participating in the development of new infrastructure for safe electric cars and cooperate with AutoInc at such an early stage feels absolutely right.

![](/api/attachments/VXPPFHCD/fulltext/images/f7d1917eb8d45da4b19630b0bb77a209a9d2f648d4555d1b32fa1557d87db1a9.jpg)  
Fig. 5. Active safety’s production of strategy contents.

When delivering 10 cars in the fall of 2010, the first concrete step towards electrification was taken. Looking for a massmarket, however, the early thoughts about an intelligent zero-emission infrastructure needed to be materialized in the years to come. In 2010, the two companies and the multi-national telco therefore engaged in a new research project where a common infrastructure would be explored. A key objective here was to set the basis for digitally enabled communication between the electric car and the charging infrastructure including the backend energy information systems. Such communication would evolve around information exchange about needs and supply of energy. As expressed in the project description:

The project aims to study how the service development of a smart charging infrastructure for electric vehicles can be realized as an open system and how these services can be boundary spanning. The service platform uses telematics solutions that will be used by automotive companies, energy companies, and third party service development providing an opportunity to realize new services. These services resolve around optimization of the grid, charging and payment, information sharing with smart homes, and the treatment of intermittent power from renewable energy sources.

Early on in the project, the parties also discovered that the introduction of electric vehicles in the transportation system would put new requirements on existing business models in both the automotive industry and the energy sector. For instance, the model used in the energy sector build on the assumptions that electricity is consumed on fixed locations and that the ‘‘owner’’ of that fixed location is the recipient of the invoice related to the consumption. Violating these assumptions, it was believed that mobile solutions for providing electricity (such as electricity roaming), and placing financial charges to other actors than the subscriber of the physical installation, would become essential and serve as incentives for building the intelligent zero-emission infrastructure. At the car end, this infrastructure would require not only increased connectivity, but also a new take on in-car software platform strategies, where third-party development would be encouraged and needed to make the car receptive to new and upcoming services and applications in this heterogeneous infrastructure

Fig. 6 depicts how the special vehicles’ sub-community, in response to management’s request for an electric car for branding purposes, pushed for developing an infrastructure and information systems that address the range anxiety problem of electric cars. Drawing on their agile project management practices, this vision was born out of the collaborative innovation with an energy company and a telecom company.

## 4.3. Emergent strategy at AutoInc

For two of the sub-communities (powertrain and special vehicles), the production of strategy contents was realized in the form of emergent strategy. Management sanctioned Powertrain’s preference for plug-in hybrid technology over pure electric engine technology, enhancing existing digital control design practices, and this technology became a cornerstone in Auto-Inc’s strategy. As a manifestation of the strategic importance, AutoInc claimed a world first diesel plug-in hybrid as they reported selling out the 1000 vehicles scheduled for production in 2013. At the same time, management also supported specia vehicles’ venture of the pure electric vehicle. If nothing else, it was regarded as a brand building activity, meaning that special vehicles’ efforts to build a zero-emission charging infrastructure was supported although not for the same reasons as the sub-community would like to think. In mid-2011, AutoInc reported that they had left the R&D stage and planned to manufacture 250 electric vehicles at the end of 2012, mainly directed at leasing customers, such as companies and governmental bodies. In February 2012, AutoInc, the energy company, and the multi-national telecom company announced the so-called ‘‘intelligent infrastructure’’ for charging electric vehicles to a larger audience, resulting in over 48 million unique Internet visitors and some thousands of articles in media.

![](/api/attachments/VXPPFHCD/fulltext/images/30576e900184eb274917e4cb37369d58dfb2daefb3f9e674e61c3ae0881deef7.jpg)  
Fig. 6. Special vehicle’s production of strategy contents.

In addition to the production of strategy contents that manifested in realized strategy, the accommodation of the sustainability strategy also fostered new linkages between sub-communities. These new linkages were not materialized in emergent IS strategy within the scope of our study but certainly created joint problem spaces considered promising enough for further investigation outside the spotlight of the official strategy. For instance, responsible for finding new ways to reuse designs across departments, a test platform manager noted:

Previously, the organizational units and their counterparts in the product have worked quite autonomously. Now, we need to create new couplings to handle the functional growth [enabled by digital technology]. We need to harmonize the way we’re working. We need to design common models that can be scaled and reused. We need to share our models, because we cannot afford to work as isolated as we have done in the past.

Similarly, the electrical engineering department’s architecture manager observed:

On a complete car level, we do see the need of creating a management layer that can negotiate different needs and resources of the sub-systems. We need to know more about this, how to classify the resources and how to control a complete system.

The sustainability strategizing made actors aware of new possibilities that transcended sub-communities at AutoInc. There were at least two such problem spaces, both involving the powertrain sub-community. First, there existed interesting possibilities to use information originally intended for infotainment systems. As the strategy officer at powertrain department noted:

The onboard computer connected to a GPS, including height information, can supply the hybrid driveline with important information about, for example, slopes. Then, you can use the power of the electric motor as much as possible uphill, and then regenerate energy<sup>3</sup> downhill. The ambition to connect our vehicle to the road network and transport system eventually contributes to smoother and better traffic flows. This is not only useful for the environment but also for the customer who will travel more conveniently.

Second, even though our evidence suggests that the sustainability strategy triggered a host of promising opportunities to collaborate across sub-communities not traditionally working together, the emergence of a joint problem space was perhaps most clearly evident at the boundary between the power train and the active safety sub-communities. In developing the plugin hybrid engine and active safety systems respectively, both sub-communities relied on practices that involved developing methods for controlling the car. In the power train case, the controls were relying on sensor information about the inner workings of the engine, where the objective was extend its digital control design practice to the interaction between the combustion engine and the electric engine. With the intention to support preventive safety, the active safety group also engaged in the development of sensor-fed information systems. Some of the real-time operations of respective group’s systems were possibly in conflict. For example, the powertrain system might want the engine to accelerate to charge the battery, when the active safety system wants to slow down the car to avoid the impending crash. Such discoveries led them to create a new joint problem space between the two sub-communities, with apparent potential to give birth to new technology-mediated practices.

This new situation was seen as both an opportunity and a challenge, triggering the development of new practices well beyond the original scope of the GSC strategy. Yet, it was clearly productive for realizing cleaner cars and for developing a candidate for the emergent IS strategy. In fact, for the plug-in-hybrid car, a project on ‘‘complete vehicle control’’ was established to handle some of the issues. As the active safety manager observed:

For the hybrid car, we have established a cross-functional team that tries to model different scenarios of this type, to understand the complexity and the opportunities. We have already tested the hybrid concept in relation to some of the traditional safety systems such as anti-spin control and anti-lock braking. [. . .] We realized early on that we needed a logical layer that handles the coordination and communication, so that for example the engine and the gear box do not act as independent units. We call it complete vehicle control. This is not only necessary to handle the increasing complexity but also for reuse, and to create a car behavior that is environmental friendly [. . .] We used to think about cruise controls as something that only would keep a constant speed regardless of the traction. This is so different now, when sustainability has come to the table.

## 5. Discussion and implications

The idea that ‘‘a strategy is not a fixed plan, nor does it change systematically at pre-arranged times solely at the will of management’’ (Mintzberg, 1978, pp. 947) has indeed been recognized in the IS literature on strategy (cf. Hackney and Little, 1999; Nolan, 2012). Yet, it is fair to say that the main-part of the literature deals with deliberate strategy (e.g., Avison et al., 2004; Gottschalk, 1999; Kettinger et al., 1994; Pavlou and El Sawy, 2006), and not so much with ‘‘the learning that must often follow the conception of an intended strategy’’ (Mintzberg, 1978, pp. 947). Drawing on a case study of the emergence of a sustainability strategy at AutoInc, this paper deals with the learning side of IS strategy as it unfolds through the technology-mediated practices of organizational sub-communities. We propose that organizational sub-communities, that is, a group of actors who share interests in a particular domain of activity in an organization, oftentimes play an important role in contributing to emergent IS strategy as they seek to implement a deliberate IS strategy. They do not only give birth to promising opportunities for collaboration between people who normally would not work together but also contribute to the creation of joint problem spaces that occasionally can be significant for spurring innovation.

Prior IS literature has recognized how actors in single sub-communities, drawing on their local practices, take initiatives that eventually make strategic impact (Aanestad and Blegind-Jensen, 2011; Baptista et al., 2010; Ciborra, 2002). However, little has been done to consider the dynamics of multiple sub-communities as they interact in their efforts to contribute to emergent IS strategy. Our study at AutoInc is a modest attempt to begin exploring the micro-strategizing of actors from a variety of organizational sub-communities and how it contributes to strategy contents as they use IS to implement a sustainability strategy.

## 5.1. Toward a process model

We sketch the contours of a process model (see Fig. 7) that focuses on (a) the contextual conditions that make organizational sub-communities initiate their IS strategy contents production, (b) the activity based production of strategy contents by organizational sub-communities that contribute to emergent IS strategy, and (c) the IS strategy outcome (cf. Pawson and Tilley, 1997).

## 5.1.1. Contextual conditions

Our process model (Fig. 7) posits that IS strategizing starts once a triggering event occasions a strategy initiation. Whether the strategy initiation is reactive or prospective (Galliers, 2004), it is deliberate and typically taken by top management. In the AutoInc case, the management initiative was clearly reactive, as the triggering event for forming the GSC initiative was EU regulation setting tough carbon emission targets. This does not mean that some parts of the realized strategy cannot be classified as prospective once new insights important for the strategy contents are gained as the process unfolds. Second, the strategic initiative is taken in an institutionalized setting, providing contextual conditions that originate in both an inner and outer environment (Lederer and Salmela, 1996). While the EU legislation is part of the outer environment, our focus is on the multiple sub-communities co-existing in an organization where the strategy is initiated. They do not only define the scope of the firm’s current practices but also serve as an important source of the firm’s emergent strategy.

## 5.1.2. Activity-based production of strategy contents

The production of strategy contents by organizational sub-communities, that is, a group of actors who share interests in a particular domain of activity in an organization, is at the heart of our process model. While senior executives undoubtedly play an important role in shaping the fundamental direction of the strategy, it needs to be interpreted and acted upon by organizational actors as they draw on their sub-communities’ local resources (cf. Johnson et al., 2003).

The technology-mediated practices of the organizational sub-community are a key resource in appropriating a strategic direction given by management (see arrow a). Technology-mediated practices represent the dynamic pattern of activity that is enacted as to realize a coherent set of intentions. As any mediator, these patterns of activity can both enable and constrain action (Jarzabkowski, 2010; Kaptelinin and Nardi, 2006). In the AutoInc case, the powertrain sub-community approached the GSC strategy by turning to their ‘‘digital control design’’ technology-mediated practice. It was therefore natural to support plug-in hybrid technology, as the combination of combustion and electric engines invited further enhancement of existing practices. In the words of the process model, plug-in hybrid technology became powertrain’s emergent candidate as it mediated its action through existing patterns of activity (see arrow b). This particular emergent candidate then became accepted as emergent IS strategy (see arrow c).

![](/api/attachments/VXPPFHCD/fulltext/images/d87eed251b69a71becd54b92849062999791be9ee31d1c1ebc382fe37a7f5ecb.jpg)  
Fig. 7. Process model.

However, it is not always the case that the emergent candidate gains the support needed in the organizational community to realize as contents for the emergent IS strategy. The sub-community’s preferred contribution to the emerging IS strategy will necessarily be shaped by the wider organizational community (Jarzabkowski, 2005) (see arrow d). In the case of the infotainment and driver information sub-community, for instance, actors, supported by their user-centered design practices, were pushing for so-called eco-driving systems that would help lowering emissions by supporting careful and environment-friendly driving. However, the emergent candidate ‘‘eco-driving’’ did not materialize as emergent IS strategy because it did not have the necessary support in the organizational community of AutoInc. In particular, powertrain engineers were clear about the fact that the EU legislation did not include driver behavior in calculating the extent to which an automaker made progress on emissions.

Since an organizational sub-community’s emergent candidate typically is rooted in existing technology-mediated practices, it does not vanish even if it does not contribute substantially to the emergent IS strategy. It builds the basis for further activity whether it contributes to the IS strategy or not. In other words, there is a feedback loop where what was learned will be absorbed in the practices of the sub-community (arrow e). In addition, the development of an emergent candidate may surface practices that were not visible for other organizational sub-communities without the strategy initiation. As a result, the emergent candidate may not result in emergent IS strategy in its original form, but in the birth of new practices that span sub-communities. In other words, the emergent candidate is part of the process by which actors in sub-communities are shaped by the practices that other sub-communities bring to the strategizing process.

## 5.1.3. IS strategy outcome

The outcome of IS strategizing is a realized IS strategy that synthesizes the deliberate IS strategy and the emergent IS strategy (cf. Mintzberg, 1978). In sum, and as depicted in Fig. 7, such an outcome can be appreciated by investigating the production of strategy contents by sub-communities. Essentially, the strategy is initiated as a result of some kind of triggering event perceived by managers with the power to formulate strategy. The initial strategy formulation serves as a call to the organizational community to enact the strategy, as well as producing the strategy contents that would develop the strategy further. As sub-communities approach the deliberate strategy, they draw on their technology-mediated practices. This might generate candidates for emergent strategy, which even if unsupported for the time being may be iterated over time on a local level. In some cases, joint problem spaces are created, as two-sub-communities recognize common grounds for activity that might feed contents into the strategy process.

## 5.2. Implications

This study contributes to the IS strategy literature. In particular, it uses Jarzabkowski’s (2003, 2005, 2010) activity based approach to SaP and Mintzberg’s (1978) strategy typology to offer a process model that describes organizationally sub-communities’ production of strategy contents in terms of contextual conditions, activity-based production of strategy contents, and IS strategy outcome. This detailed specification of the process by which strategies emerge through the technology-mediated practices in IS strategizing augments prior IS literature. In particular, it enhances the emerging stream of literature that focuses on how specific stakeholders may contribute contents of strategic importance (Aanestad and Blegind-Jensen, 2010; Baptista et al., 2010; Ciborra, 1996; Ciborra et al., 2000).

There are a number of ways in which this increased understanding of emergent patterns of strategic practices is important. First, previous process models in the IS literature tends to deal with deliberate strategy as the norm, viewing emergent strategy is something that needs exception-handling. As underlined in this study, emergent strategy is an important resource that may stimulate the building of links between sub-communities who would not usually work together. Such links may develop into joint problem spaces that can trigger new forms of innovation, which would have been difficult to anticipate in beforehand. Given that today’s organizations are increasingly permeated by digital technology, the importance of emergent strategy is increasing, meaning that a more thorough understanding of sub-communities in IS strategizing is valuable. Second, as called for in the special issue in which this paper is included, the process model is a result of examining strategy practices as they evolve in the everyday action of practitioners. As such, the study represents one of the first IS studies that explicitly reaches out to the SaP literature, employing Jarzabkowski’s work for understanding sub-communities’ technologymediated practices and how these can be the origin of emergent IS strategy. Hopefully, this early step of using SaP in IS strategizing may stimulate other IS researchers to approach this promising body of literature in organization studies.

Even though the model itself is intended for academics, it should be emphasized that our study also provides guidance for managers who are practicing strategy. It suggests the value of launching strategies as open-ended initiatives to which subcommunities should be stimulated to contribute. In particular, such stimulation can be valuable for activating the diversity of competences and interests that you would find across the sub-communities of organizations. As the pace and continuity of organizational change is ramping up (Brown and Eisenhardt, 1997), it makes sense to leverage sources of strategy contents who are, first of all, close to the practices and attitudes of customers but also to the products and services that in many cases define the firm.

## 5.3. Limitations and issues for future research

Future studies could address several limitations in our work. First, like many other product developing firms (Sanchez and Mahoney, 1996), AutoInc was highly modularized in its structure. One may expect that the organizational sub-community conceptualization is more straightforward in a modularized firm, since its idealized way to organize around a product’s components is intended to foster groups with shared interests distinct from other groups. The process by which sub-communities contribute to realized IS strategy contents may therefore play out differently in other types of settings. Comparative studies would therefore be useful to detect the nature of that variation.
