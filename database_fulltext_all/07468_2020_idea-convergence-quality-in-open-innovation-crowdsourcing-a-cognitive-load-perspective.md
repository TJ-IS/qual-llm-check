# Idea Convergence Quality in Open Innovation Crowdsourcing: A Cognitive Load Perspective

Xusen Cheng<sup>a</sup>, Shixuan Fu<sup>b,c</sup>, Triparna de Vreede<sup>d</sup>, Gert-Jan de Vreede <sup>d</sup>, Isabella Seeber <sup>e</sup>, Ronald Maier <sup>e,f</sup>, and Barbara Weber <sup>g</sup>

<sup>a</sup>School of Information, Renmin University of China, Beijing, China; <sup>b</sup>School of Tourism Sciences, Beijing International Studies University, Beijing, China; <sup>c</sup>Research Center of Beijing Tourism Development, Beijing, China; <sup>d</sup>University of South Florida, Tampa, FL, USA; <sup>e</sup>Department of Information Systems, Production and Logistics Management, University of Innsbruck, Innsbruck, Austria; <sup>f</sup>Vice Rectorate Digitalization and Knowledge Transfer, University of Vienna, Vienna, Austria; <sup>g</sup>University of St. Gallen, Gallen, Switzerland

## ABSTRACT

Open innovation crowdsourcing enables online crowds to quickly generate a plethora of creative ideas. A key challenge is the convergence of ideas for further consideration from massive numbers of candidate ideas with diverse quality. Based on Cognitive Load Theory, we executed a laboratory experiment to test the associations between three types of cognitive load manipulations and idea convergence outcomes. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that germane cognitive load positively correlates with idea convergence quality, satisfaction with process, and satisfaction with outcome. Intrinsic cognitive load is negatively associated with satisfaction with process and satisfaction with outcome, while extraneous cognitive load negatively correlates only with satisfaction with outcome. We further identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the positive moderation role of knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, perceived goal clarity, and need for cognition on the relationships between germane cognitive load and idea convergence quality. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can inform open innovation organizers when designing tasks and interventions to improve convergence outcomes.

## KEYWORDS

Cognitive load; idea crowdsourcing; idea convergence; knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy; goal clarity; open innovation

## Introduction

Open Innovation initiatives have moved beyond traditional product and service development in R&D departments. They are now being increasingly used to usher organizations toward cutting edge business developments [39]. Given the advancements of technology, an organization can now tap into online crowds to harness the crowds’ collective intelligence and achieve the organization’s innovation goals [27, 29, 43]. Open innovation comes with an added advantage of “wisdom of the crowds” to evaluate ideas and <sup>fi</sup>lter out biases. Often, organizations select solutions or ideas that they are familiar with and support their existing ways of working [59]. To avoid this trend to stick with the familiar and to reduce potential ine<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the innovation process, many organizations are outsourcing idea evaluation to online crowds [39]. Idea crowdsourcing communities such as OpenIDEO, Jovoto, and IdeaScale are some well-known innovation platforms that represent intermediaries to elicit innovation from the crowd for a sponsoring organization.

Idea crowdsourcing is an Open Innovation initiative that allows users with diverse backgrounds and di<sup>f</sup>erent geographical locations to generate and evaluate the most promising ideas [5]. Recent trends in Open Innovation emphasize the potential of crowdsourcing communities to generate, re<sup>fi</sup>ne, evaluate, and select innovative ideas. To that e<sup>f</sup>ect, various crowdsourcing techniques have been proposed to improve the quality of generated ideas. Such techniques include the exploitation of constraint social networks so that ideators elicit information from speci<sup>fi</sup>c people [62], the formation of teams where ideators share a working history [18], and the structuring of the generation process with a mix of individual and group work [27]. However, the simple generation of ideas is usually insu<sup>fi</sup>cient to ensure the success of an idea crowdsourcing initiative. Idea generation is merely the <sup>fi</sup>rst step of idea crowdsourcing. Crowdsourcing initiatives also require ongoing e<sup>f</sup>orts by the crowd to further re<sup>fi</sup>ne the ideas generated [63, 70] and to shortlist those ideas that are worthy of further consideration [70]. The latter step is also referred to as the idea convergence phase.

Idea convergence concerns the reduction and clari<sup>fi</sup>cation of generated ideas. The output of idea convergence is a reduced set with the most promising ideas that feeds into the <sup>fi</sup>nal phase, during which the decision on the winning idea(s) is reached [5]. It represents a cognitive process that requires individuals to retrieve information from working memory during idea processing [55, 64]. However, an individual’s working memory is limited and is in constant demand. For instance, working memory plays a critical role in all forms of complex thinking including reasoning, problem solving, and language comprehension. When the working memory is overloaded with more processing needs than can be attended to at a given time, individuals experience cognitive overload [26]. The risk of cognitive overload makes convergence a challenging process as it requires individuals to deliberate and compare massive amounts of ideas and make decisions on promising ideas [25] under time pressure. They may not possess enough cognitive processing capacity to attend to and acquire all information available to make choices [83]. Thus, they may experience cognitive overload, which threatens the quality of the idea convergence process. As a consequence, open innovation initiatives may end up with redundant and undeveloped ideas or the selected idea(s) may not be optimal for the goals at hand [27].

Despite the importance of idea convergence, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies that focus on cognitive load in idea convergence from an information processing perspective. Our understanding of how individuals cognitively process information during convergence is still nascent. Little attention has been paid to designing and studying crowd convergence techniques that aim at decreasing the likelihood of cognitive overload. This research addresses this gap by using Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) as a foundation to design crowd convergence techniques that improve perceived cognitive load leading to improved idea convergence outcomes. We seek to answer the following research questions:

Research Question 1: How do diferent crowd convergence techniques relate to idea convergence outcomes?

Research Question 2: Under what conditions might such crowd convergence techniques have stronger associations with idea convergence outcomes?

We conducted a laboratory experiment and randomly assigned 172 participants to eight cognitive load manipulation conditions. Building on CLT, we manipulated extraneous cognitive load with varying idea presentation modes, intrinsic cognitive load with varying task complexity, and germane cognitive load with varying procedural guidance. Our study provides new evidence that evaluation techniques that increase germane cognitive load are positively associated with idea convergence quality. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that under high need for cognition, high perceived goal clarity, and high knowledge selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy conditions, the associations between germane cognitive load and idea convergence quality are stronger. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide useful insights on how idea convergence task assignments and idea evaluation techniques can be designed, which in turn may improve the practices of idea crowdsourcing.

## Background

## Crowdsourcing and Open Innovation

Crowdsourcing is “the act of outsourcing tasks originally performed inside an organization, or assigned externally in form of a business relationship, to an unde<sup>fi</sup>nably large, heterogeneous mass of potential actors” [32]. It is enabled by information technology (IT) such that dynamic online crowds can voluntarily contribute and collaborate [56]. There are di<sup>f</sup>erent types of IT-enabled crowdsourcing, including crowd labor markets for microtasking [56], crowd contest platforms for open idea competitions [43], Internetbased communities for peer production [29], and open innovation platforms [5].

Open innovation is a type of crowdsourcing where an organization seeks recommendations and ideas for innovative solutions beyond its organizational boundaries through broad contributions from online crowds [45]. It refers to crowdsourcing to solicit high numbers of creative idea proposals and collective evaluations employed for improving organizational innovation [45, 87]. While crowdsourcing has proven to be an e<sup>f</sup>ective tool to elicit a multitude of ideas in a short period of time, one of the challenges with this method is to identify the best ideas or the ideas that are worthy to pursue further [5, 27].

Existing studies on crowdsourcing have mostly focused on participation incentives and motivation factors on crowdsourcing behaviors, including task announcement motivation [78] and crowd participation motivation [46]. Others studied knowledge management in crowdsourcing practices [49] and organizational learning with crowdsourcing [68]. Recently, researchers have begun to study idea crowdsourcing processes. For example, researchers investigated ideation processes during crowdsourcing in terms of techniques that facilitate idea creativity [18, 27]. However, while existing studies focused on idea generation processes to promote the quality of ideas, the convergence aspect of crowdsourcing has been relatively neglected.

## Convergence

Convergence refers to the process of <sup>fi</sup>ltering, reducing, and clarifying of ideas with the intention to move from having many ideas to having fewer ideas that are worthy of further attention [81]. Convergence is considered a critical part of the collaboration process [41, 75]. According to the Collaboration Engineering (CE) approach, convergence is one of the <sup>fi</sup>ve patterns of collaboration, the other patterns being generate, organize, evaluate, and build commitment [81]. During idea convergence, individuals deliberate and compare large numbers of generated ideas and select the most promising ones [33, 70]. An appropriate convergence process is critical to ensure a preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ltration of ideas from the generated contributions [63, 75] as it can result in a considerable reduction of ideas, especially if the goals of the convergence are clearly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed [25, 60]. Studies show that the idea convergence process is usually more cumbersome, lengthy, and timeconsuming than ideation [27]; yet it is essential to lead the team closer to a <sup>fi</sup>nal decision [41]. Since the success of open innovation depends on the ability of the participants to e<sup>f</sup>ectively and e<sup>fi</sup>ciently identify high quality ideas from the pool of crowd generated ideas, it is critical for participants to have e<sup>f</sup>ective convergence strategies [27].

The quality of an idea convergence process is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate until the selected ideas are implemented [5, 27]. Even with successful implementation, it cannot be assumed that the implemented ideas are the objective measures of idea quality [5, 7]. Thus, in order to assess idea convergence outcomes comprehensively, convergence quality must be measured through the cognitive judgement of the quality of selected ideas as well as a<sup>f</sup>ective perceptions of the idea convergence process and outcome [70]. In this study, we assess idea convergence outcomes both from the perspective of idea convergence quality and individuals’ attitudes.

Past idea convergence research can be categorized into three streams. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stream compares techniques for crowd idea evaluation like up/down voting, Likert scales, multicriteria voting, and crowd comments (e.g., [5, 47, 48]). Both voting and scales have their drawbacks in that the focus of the reviewers is limited to ideas with top ratings [47]. In OpenIDEO, the majority voting technique makes individuals <sup>fi</sup>xate on the few top ideas and leaves other ideas with fewer considerations unattended during idea convergence. Quantitative evaluation techniques may let mediocre ideas end up being ranked higher in voting than excellent ideas [47], since rating scores can be biased and skewed.

The second convergence research stream examines the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of idea evaluation techniques (e.g., machine judging, crowd evaluation, and expert selections) on idea convergence performance and outcomes (e.g., [3, 29]). Crowd evaluations are conducted by heterogenous individuals with diverse backgrounds who assess the value of each idea [73]. Machine evaluations are conducted by algorithms that evaluate the value of each idea based on pre-set parameters. Expert evaluations are done by a group of people who have demonstrable expertise in the content and context within which the ideas are generated. Studies show that evaluation using automated pre-organization is e<sup>fi</sup>cient when selecting general alternatives, but machines cannot optimally judge the creativity of the ideas generated [47]. In addition, research indicates that experts with decision making roles tend to downgrade ideas with low social approval in idea recognition tasks [53]. Moreover, crowd evaluation results in a better convergence quality than expert evaluation when ideas are judged after both convergent thinking and divergent thinking have been deliberately addressed [3].

Finally, the third research stream compares the e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent techniques on the quality of the idea convergence process [69, 70]. Convergence is a cognitively e<sup>f</sup>ortful task [40] as idea descriptions need to be processed and compared [5]. Some studies show that by organizing similar ideas into subsets, individuals tend to perceive lower cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort and make more accurate choices [1]. Other studies show that groups that experience facilitated guidance that trigger productive evaluation and coordination mechanisms outperform non-facilitated teams [69]. In addition, it appears that providing discussion encouragement and attention guidance has a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on convergence quality [70]. Feedback, which is often part of a facilitation strategy, was found to be associated with higher information elaboration and learning in teams when trust in other team members is high [58]

Despite past convergence studies, there still appears to be limited systematic and theoretically grounded understanding of how di<sup>f</sup>erent techniques contribute to convergence quality. Given the nature of the convergence process, it seems promising to adopt a cognitive information processing perspective to understand facilitated convergence processes. Previous studies, however, mostly lack elaborate investigations into individuals cognitive load and its e<sup>f</sup>ects. Our study aims to address this gap through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory (CLT).

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

## Cognitive Load Theory

Cognitive load refers to mental and cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort to complete given tasks [55]. According to CLT, an individuals’ cognitive architecture consists of the working memory (also called short term memory) and long term memory [2, 16]. Originally proposed by educational psychologists, CLT states that tasks that require high cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort do not allow for e<sup>f</sup>ective schema construction and automation and, as a result, lead to learning blockages and productivity barriers [55].

CLT recognizes three types of cognitive load: intrinsic cognitive load, extraneous cognitive load, and germane cognitive load [55]. Intrinsic cognitive load experienced by individuals depends on the natural complexity of the task and individuals’ information processing expertise. Intrinsic cognitive load is high when the complexity of a task exceeds individuals’ information processing capacity. Extraneous cognitive load has to do with the presentation format of information and is contingent on design adequacy and information transmission channels. Extraneous cognitive load is high when the design of an activity requires individuals to process excessive information. Germane cognitive load represents the amount of cognitive resources for schema construction and automation. Long term memory draws on schemas that are interlinked repositories of information. When we are presented with information chunks, we retrieve schemas from long term memory for comparison. Information processing in working memory becomes easier when the information <sup>fi</sup>ts the schema [55]. When no existing schemas <sup>fi</sup>t the information chunks, new schemas need to get constructed. These processes of schema automation and construction refer to germane cognitive load, which is a productive load that accelerates cognitive processes through schema automation from long term memory and schema construction. The construction of schemas requires individuals to exemplify, organize, infer, classify, and interpret information [17, 50].

Based on CLT, managing the three types of cognitive load to achieve appropriate levels of schema construction and automation will reduce the burden on the working memory. Following the logic of CLT, task performance will improve when cognitive load that is not relevant for schema construction, is reduced or removed. Thus, intrinsic cognitive load is encouraged to be managed, extraneous cognitive load should be minimized, and germane cognitive load should be maximized [10].

CLT has been applied in a number of IS studies. For example, in the context of learning SQL for complex data-retrieval tasks, CLT was used to improve query writing performance under conditions of high and low query (code) complexity [80]. In the context of system design, an advanced web-based system was built by applying CLT to support e<sup>f</sup>ective and e<sup>fi</sup>cient searching on a knowledge landscape of the nanotechnology area [15]. CLT was also used by Group Support Systems (GSS) researchers to support arguments on the usefulness of ideation techniques [34]. CLT was applied in an online shopping environment, where website designers could adjust their strategies to reduce the cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort of <sup>fi</sup>nishing tasks and maximize viewers’ visual attention [82]. Finally, manipulating cognitive load was also found to be important for idea convergence in idea crowdsourcing communities [5, 26, 70]. For example, perceived easy evaluation techniques grounded in reducing cognitive load, lead to lower cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts during idea convergence [5].

In the context of our research, CLT provides a promising foundation to develop techniques to facilitate idea convergence outcomes. Since idea convergence tasks are associated with retrieving information from both working memory and long-term memory, individuals may be faced with a substantial amount of cognitive load when processing generated ideas. When the number of ideas and the di<sup>fi</sup>culty of processing ideas exceed individuals’ cognitive capabilities, they will experience cognitive overload. CLT argues that this cognitive overload can be reduced through process optimization techniques such as clear instructions, clear presentation of information, and the formation and use of cognitive schema or mental models [76]. We aim to explore in the context of idea convergence in crowds how such techniques are associated with di<sup>f</sup>erences in idea convergence quality and satisfaction.

## Convergence Quality

The outcome of idea convergence is determined by at least two factors: the idea quality of the shortlisted ideas (in comparison to the initial set of user-generated ideas) and the satisfaction with the idea evaluation [63]. Idea convergence quality refers to the overall quality of shortlisted ideas [25, 70]. It can be considered as a measure for convergence task performance [26]. In contrast, the satisfaction of idea evaluation can concern the satisfaction towards the product, that is, the shortlist, or the convergence process. Any variation in the types of cognitive load is expected to also impact the quality of the shortlist and perceived satisfaction.

CLT posits that intrinsic cognitive load varies according to di<sup>f</sup>erences in task complexity [2]. Existing studies have consistently found that an increasingly complex task leads to a degradation of task performance [15, 80]. An overabundance information in complex tasks leads to cognitive overload, thus the task performance is a<sup>f</sup>ected [88]. Di<sup>f</sup>erent degrees of intrinsic cognitive load are thus expected to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcome of idea convergence. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a): Higher intrinsic cognitive load is associated with lower idea convergence quality.

Extraneous cognitive load concerns the information being presented [55]. Competition for Attention Theory posits that clearly presented information easily attracts viewers’ attention and leads to better task performance [35]. An example of ine<sup>f</sup>ective information instruction occurs when individuals have to merge information that is scattered in pieces, the so-called split-attention e<sup>f</sup>ect [17]. In this case, ideas are di<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate as their features are not aligned requiring individuals to expend more e<sup>f</sup>ort searching for relevant information [6, 30]. However, when similar ideas are presented together, overlap in the ideas’ features (information chunks) exists and comparisons become easier [6, 24]. This was demonstrated in studies that showed that the presentation of manually or automatically categorized ideas leads to lower perceived cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort [1, 6] and better decision quality [1]. In general, empirical studies found that poor idea presentation is negatively associated with learning performance [13] and decision quality [79]. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1b (H1b): Higher extraneous cognitive load is associated with lower idea convergence quality.

Individuals with higher germane cognitive load will perform tasks in a structured way and thus achieve better outcomes [42]. Germane cognitive load is related to schema construction and automation and is encouraged to be maximized [55]. Schemas such as automated deliberation guidance, process facilitation, and feedback mechanisms contribute to the inferring, di<sup>f</sup>erentiating, and organizing of information [55], and improve information processing [10] and task performance [70], which was demonstrated in the context of decision making quality [82], convergence quality [70], and idea generation quality [27]. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1c (H1c): Higher germane cognitive load is associated with higher idea convergence quality.

## Satisfaction

Satisfaction is an a<sup>f</sup>ective response related to the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of needs, expectations, or wishes [8] and one of the most widely investigated constructs to evaluate the success of IT related artifacts [19]. Satisfaction is usually measured with respect to the process or its output or outcome [61]. Satisfaction with process measures individuals’ overall a<sup>f</sup>ection regarding the procedures used. If individuals feel dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed toward the process guidance, people may abandon their tasks and they will be less likely to continue contributing [51]. Even when people still participate when they are dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed, they may not fully engage in the work. Only when individuals are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with the process is it likely that they continuously engage [8].

Cognitive overload leads to frustration and dissatisfaction [10]. Interventions such as team facilitation techniques optimize the capacity of working memory and contribute to the avoidance of cognitive overload [26, 34]. Thus, strategies that set out to reduce cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort and free working memory capacity, are likely to contribute to the improvement of perceived satisfaction [55]. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 2a (2a): Higher intrinsic cognitive load is associated with lower process satisfaction.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): Higher extraneous cognitive load is associated with lower process satisfaction.

Hypothesis 2c (H2c): Higher germane cognitive load is associated with higher process satisfaction.

Satisfaction with outcome concerns a<sup>f</sup>ective perceptions on the deliverables of the tasks. According to Yield Shift Theory, individuals’ satisfaction is high when outcomes meet their expectations and goals [9]. Thus, high satisfaction with outcomes is contingent on the match between task anticipations and actual outcomes. During idea convergence in idea crowdsourcing communities, the goal is to shortlist a smaller set of ideas that are worthy of further consideration. Therefore, individuals’ satisfaction with outcome is expected to be high when they perceive that ideas of high quality have been selected. When cognitive overload sets in, individuals are likely to lose con<sup>fi</sup>dence that they can achieve their goals and thus may experience lower satisfaction [37]. Thus, strategies that improve idea convergence quality may also lead to higher satisfaction with outcome. We propose:

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): Higher intrinsic cognitive load is associated with lower outcome satisfaction.

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): Higher extraneous cognitive load is associated with lower outcome satisfaction.

Hypothesis 3c (H3c): Higher germane cognitive load is associated with higher outcome satisfaction.

## Moderators

## Knowledge Self-E<sup>fi</sup>cacy

Self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy refers to individuals’ inherent beliefs in their ability to succeed in a given situation [72]. Knowledge self-eficacy refers to individuals’ beliefs that their knowledge is su<sup>fi</sup>cient to achieve the given goals. Social Cognitive Theory posits that individuals develop cognitive models to make sense of information resulting from past experiences [72]: Individuals’ task performance will be greatly improved when their perceived selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy is high in social cognitive activities [23]. In the context of idea convergence, knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy pertains to the knowledge and experiences that an individual <sup>fi</sup>nds relevant for the idea convergence task. Idea convergence is a socio-cognitive activity in which individuals deliberately compare each idea and shortlist ideas based on their prior experiences and knowledge. Thus, task performance is expected to be higher for individuals with higher knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. The moderating role of self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy on the association between intervention and task performance was studied in organizational settings where researchers found that transformational leadership interventions aroused individuals who are high on self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy to display more proactive behaviors [20].

Idea convergence involves retrieving knowledge and experience from long-term memory to judge the quality of each idea. Thus, idea convergence quality is contingent on one’s cognitive ability to identify ideas that are promising. In high germane cognitive load conditions, procedural guidance facilitates information processing and schema construction, and consequently contributes to the automation of schemas to help with idea convergence. Individuals with high knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy tend to be actively involved in the tasks and instructions and are thus more likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from procedural guidance to improve germane cognitive load [57]. However, individuals with low knowledge selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy will be less likely to e<sup>f</sup>ectively retrieve information stored as schemas in long-term memory [71]. Hence, we propose:

Hypothesis 4a (H4a): Knowledge self-eficacy moderates the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality. The higher perceived knowledge self-eficacy is, the stronger is the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality.

## Perceived Goal Clarity

Perceived goal clarity refers to the extent to which the outcome of the task has been clearly articulated [31, 67]. According to goal setting theory, setting suitable goals helps with the promotion of task performance [52]. Goal clarity is one of the most important indicators of goal setting [36]. Clear goals allow individuals to prepare for future actions and clarify their expectations regarding the cognitive e<sup>f</sup>ort required to perform the task. Research shows that perceived goal clarity is associated with job satisfaction, team e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, team performance, and organizational citizenship [28, 36, 67].

Germane cognitive load concerns the procedural interventions that promote schema construction to be stored in long-term memory, and eases the demands on working memory during information processing activities, thus improving complex task performance [55]. Individuals tend to perceive high germane cognitive load when process guidance is clear and useful as this stimulates individuals to engage in schema construction and automation [2, 17]. Individuals with high levels of goal clarity understand their roles and targeted task goals better [67]. In other words, goal clarity articulates what needs to be done to complete a task. Clear process instructions provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidance on how to achieve the goals [36]. In addition, process interventions may facilitate goal clarity during task execution [28]. Thus, when individuals experience both goal clarity and clear process instructions, they are better able to identify with their task and are more willing to exert cognitive resources toward it [36, 67]. Since convergence quality is an important indicator of task performance in this research, we propose:

Hypothesis 4b (H4b): Perceived goal clarity moderates the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality. The higher perceived goal clarity is, the stronger is the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality.

## Need for Cognition

According to the elaboration likelihood model, need for cognition refers to the tendency to engage in cognitive endeavors and in-depth thinking [38]. Need for cognition has been shown to be an antecedent to a wide variety of outcomes such as innovation behaviors [85], better task performance [22], and exposure behaviors in online communities [84]. Individuals with a high need for cognition tend to enjoy stretching their cognitive abilities to <sup>fi</sup>nish a task and are eager to enjoy e<sup>f</sup>ortful endeavors in a “quest for reality” [11, 77, 85]. They also prefer deliberate thinking to solve problems and are more likely to follow structured guidance for task completion. During the process of idea convergence, individuals with high need for cognition are expected to follow procedural guidance and deliberately consider and compare each idea to make their <sup>fi</sup>nal decision [11]. Therefore, elaborate explanations combined with clear procedural guidance that serve to facilitate schema construction and automation are also expected to have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect for individuals with a high need for cognition [55].

Yet, individuals with a low need for cognition generally have low preference for intellectual tasks. Excessive cognitive activities make them feel frustrated. Such individuals enjoy past experiences and heuristic cues to perform tasks [38]. They may ignore procedural guidance since processing this guidance requires them to apply additional intellectual cognitive resources. Furthermore, non-content cues are more important for individuals with a low need for cognition. They simply converge ideas based on existing beliefs and peripheral information [21]. Since individuals with a low need for cognition generally resist in-depth thinking on idea-relevant information, the role of procedural guidance on task performance is weakened. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 4c (H4c): Need for cognition moderates the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality. The higher perceived need for cognition is, the stronger is the association between germane cognitive load and convergence quality.

## Method

We conducted a laboratory experiment with a between-subject 2 x 2 x 2 factorial design with eight experiment conditions. The three treatments were task complexity (high vs. low), idea presentation (organized presentation vs. unorganized presentation) and instructional guidance (guidance vs. no guidance). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we investigated whether di<sup>f</sup>erent cognitive load-related techniques facilitate higher quality results from idea convergence. We also determined whether the techniques were related to changes in satisfaction with process and outcome.

## Task

We used user-generated ideas from an existing open innovation crowdsourcing community, i.e., OpenIDEO, as idea samples. We randomly selected 100 user generated ideas that answered the challenge “How might we use technology to inspire all socioeconomic and multicultural groups to lead healthier lives” as the starting idea pool for the experiment.

We asked participants to converge on a number of ideas within a <sup>fi</sup>xed time frame for all conditions (45 minutes). The goal the idea convergence task was to reduce and clarify the given idea set into 10 ideas that are relevant to technology support for healthy life innovations. Thus, the end deliverable was a list of 10 ideas that each participant considered as most promising.

Table 1 shows the manipulations on each condition. We manipulated intrinsic cognitive load by varying task complexity. We randomly selected 50 ideas from the idea pool for low intrinsic cognitive load groups and all 100 ideas for high intrinsic load groups. The timeframe for both groups was <sup>fi</sup>xed to ensure that intrinsic cognitive load di<sup>f</sup>ered between the experiment conditions with high and low task complexity. We tested the di<sup>f</sup>erence between the original idea quality scores of the groups with 50 and 100 ideas (rated by separate raters). The di<sup>f</sup>erence was not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (t = 1.629, ns.), which suggests that the average idea quality for both groups is comparable. Also, through piloting the design, we found that the lower complexity condition of 50 ideas was still su<sup>fi</sup>ciently complex that participants experienced a certain level of cognitive load and did not get bored.

We manipulated extraneous cognitive load through di<sup>f</sup>erent forms of idea presentation. Redundant and unclear idea presentation leads to high extraneous cognitive load since individuals have to process extra information during idea convergence [55]. Accordingly, for low extraneous load groups the user generated ideas were organized into di<sup>f</sup>erent categories such that the ideas were presented in a clearly structured way. The same categories were used for the 50 idea treatment (low intrinsic load) and the 100 idea treatment (high intrinsic load). The high extraneous load groups were presented as a single long idea list.

Germane cognitive load was manipulated through the degree of procedural guidance. For high germane load conditions, in addition to general task instruction, participants were instructed on the convergence of ideas by following a speci<sup>fi</sup>c procedure. First, participants were instructed to sort the raw ideas into high quality, medium quality, and low quality subsets. Then, if the number of ideas in the high quality category was less than 10, they were asked to compare the ideas in the medium quality groups and drag those of higher quality into high quality group until they had 10 high quality ideas. If the number of ideas in the high quality category was more than 10, they were asked to compare the ideas in the high quality group and drag those of lower quality into the medium quality group until they were left with 10 high quality ideas. For low germane load conditions, the participants only received a general task introduction.

## Procedure

We conducted a pilot study with 97 students in a public university in China. The participants were asked to select 10 promising ideas on cross-border E-commerce innovations as a part of an in-class assignment. They used ThinkTank by GroupSystems, an online collaboration tool, for this task. Participants’ perceptions were collected using questionnaires. After the pilot, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed issues that occurred that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the results. For example, participants did not always read the instructions. They just created a deliverable that they thought was requested. Thus, for the experiment, we enforced reading instructions before the task could be commenced. Also, some questionnaire items were slightly adjusted for higher clarity. We removed the items that lead to low reliability and validity.

We conducted the experiment in a classroom with desktop computers with 24-inch monitors. Eight ThinkTank sessions were set up corresponding to the eight experiment conditions. ThinkTank sessions were precon<sup>fi</sup>gured with the ideas that we asked participants to converge into a shortlist. Sessions were assigned with consecutive ThinkTank room numbers, ranging from one to eight. Task descriptions and process instructions were pre-loaded into each ThinkTank session.

Upon their arrival in the laboratory, participants were given written tasks instructions. In addition, we also provided verbal instructions to the participants and reminded them that it was critical to read the instructions before engaging in the task. Participants could freely ask questions for clari<sup>fi</sup>cation before the experiment commenced. Participants then performed the idea convergence task. In ThinkTank, they could comment, remove, reorder, add, and classify ideas as they wished or as they were allowed under the speci<sup>fi</sup>c conditions. Participants <sup>fi</sup>nished their tasks independently from each other; interactions were not permitted. Upon completion of their task, participants <sup>fi</sup>lled out the questionnaires.

## Participants

A total of 172 individuals voluntarily participated in the study. They were randomly assigned to one of the eight conditions by providing them with a unique code including the condition number and a participant number. Using this code, the task deliverables stored in ThinkTank were matched with the submitted questionnaires. Questionnaires with over 20% missing items were removed from the sample. After matching and screening was completed, 157 valid participants remained: 21, 20, 19, 19, 20, 19, 21, and 18 participants for conditions 1 through 8, respectively.

Table 2 summarizes the demographic pro<sup>fi</sup>le of the participants. The average age was between 21 and 23. Participants’ demographic pro<sup>fi</sup>les varied evenly across the subsamples of the treatment conditions. ANOVA tests showed that there were no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences between di<sup>f</sup>erent conditions in terms of age (F(7,150) = 1.739, p =0.115), gender (F(7,150)= 0.495, p = 0.811), knowledge (F(7,150) = 1.843, p = 0.08), experience (F(7,150) = 1.115, p = 0.356), and CET 4 score (College English Test which measures individuals’ reading comprehension skills) (F (7,150) = 0.641, p = 0.356).

## Measures

We collected perceptual measures for each type of cognitive load. Measures on intrinsic cognitive load, extraneous cognitive load, and germane cognitive load were collected through a post-experiment survey. All items were measured through a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 4 = neutral, 7 = strongly agree).

For the moderators, we used post-experiment questionnaires to measure perceived goal clarity, need for cognition, and knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. The questionnaire items for each construct were adapted from existing research [12, 14, 31]. Some items were reverse coded. All items were measured on a seven-point Likert scale.

## Discussion and Conclusions

The primary goal of this study was to examine the e<sup>f</sup>ects of di<sup>f</sup>erent evaluation techniques on idea convergence in open innovation idea crowdsourcing through the lens of Cognitive Load Theory (CLT). We developed a model based on CLT to theorize that three types of cognitive load would be associated with idea convergence outcomes (including idea convergence quality, satisfaction with process, and satisfaction with outcome). We also posited that these associations would be moderated by individuals’ knowledge selfe<sup>fi</sup>cacy, need for cognition, and perceived goal clarity. A laboratory experiment found support for most hypotheses.

Our results provide several contributions. First, higher intrinsic load in form of a large set of ideas to converge on is associated with lower satisfaction with outcome and process. Hence, this study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that high intrinsic load is detrimental to satisfaction [34, 55, 65]. Second, higher extraneous cognitive load in the form of presenting uncategorized sets of ideas is associated with lower satisfaction. This con<sup>fi</sup>rms previous research that suggests that extraneous cognitive load should be minimized in order to achieve positive e<sup>f</sup>ects [2, 12]. Third, higher germane load in the form of providing guiding instructions is associated with higher convergence quality as well as higher satisfaction with outcome and process. This supports the general assumption that process guidance is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial [14] and that extraneous load should be kept low in order to achieve good outcomes [82]. Finally, this study contributes three moderators that appear to strengthen the positive relationship between germane cognitive load and convergence quality: knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, need for cognition, and perceived goal clarity. Idea convergence quality was highest when germane cognitive load was high and individuals had high knowledge-self e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, high need for cognition, and perceived their task goals as clear. Our work has several theoretical and practical implications.

## Implications for Theory

This research contributes to the literature on crowdsourcing in general and on idea crowdsourcing in particular [39, 45]. Past research mostly focused on crowdsourcing platforms for transaction matching, contest platforms, and labor markets [43, 86]. This study investigated crowdsourcing initiatives that rely on online crowds for idea generation and convergence, such as open innovation. The success of such e<sup>f</sup>orts not only relies on the number of ideas generated by the crowd, but also depends on identifying and re<sup>fi</sup>ning the most promising ideas [63, 70]. Without an e<sup>f</sup>ective idea convergence process, open innovative initiators may end up with redundant and undeveloped ideas. Thus, we go beyond existing studies on ideation and creativity research in idea crowdsourcing [18, 62], and contribute to a call for studies to support idea convergence outcomes [70].

Further, our work extends the application of CLT that was proposed and widely adopted in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of educational psychology [2, 55]. Convergence techniques are a sine qua non to execute idea convergence [69, 75], yet there has been limited research on how individuals cognitively process information during a convergence process. We designed techniques based on CLT to explore their e<sup>f</sup>ect on idea convergence outcomes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on intrinsic load suggest that a higher number of ideas that need to be converged on is associated with reduced satisfaction. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on extraneous load suggest that satisfaction with process increases when ideas presented in structured categories rather than as a long unstructured list. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on germane load suggest that individuals converging on ideas according to procedural instructions achieve better convergence quality and satisfaction. Moreover, we found that several moderators need to be considered in the context of the positive association between germane cognitive load and idea convergence quality. Thus, this work contributes to our current understanding of how di<sup>f</sup>erent crowd convergence techniques in<sup>fl</sup>uence di<sup>f</sup>erent cognitive load types [55] which, in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uence idea convergence outcomes. This has important implications for future research on crowd convergence techniques and cognitive information processing in open innovation settings. Particularly procedural guidance appears to be an important precedent for convergence success.

We also contribute to the body of research on satisfaction with process and outcome [9, 61] related to idea convergence. Existing studies have proposed that individuals are less likely to continue contributing their cognitive e<sup>f</sup>orts when their satisfaction level is low [61]. Our study corroborates the <sup>fi</sup>ndings that satisfaction with process and satisfaction with outcome do not always coexist [9]. Interventions that improve satisfaction with process may not necessarily lead to higher satisfaction with outcome, especially in the context of variations in extraneous cognitive load.

## Contribution to the Information Systems Knowledge Base

Beyond crowdsourcing, our research also contributes to the knowledge base on online communities, which has been widely researched in IS [44, 49]. Our research speci<sup>fi</sup>cally contributes to the body of literature on online crowdsourcing community management [5, 80].

This study further reinforced the role of cognitive load theory and information processing in addressing IS related problems [55]. Idea crowdsourcing hosts and sponsors have reported that high quantities of ideas pending to be shortlisted represent an emergent challenge [66]. Compared with existing IS studies that have investigated electronic commerce website layout [82], query task complexity manipulations [80], and the design and evaluation of information systems [15] from cognitive load perspectives, the results of our study inform techniques for organizations that alter individuals’ cognitive load during idea convergence. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we showed that the allocation of the number of generated ideas, the (non)categorization of ideas, and the speci<sup>fi</sup>city of the procedural guidance in<sup>fl</sup>uence intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load, respectively.

Finally, the results of this study also contribute to research on distributed collaboration mediated by IT platforms. Compared with existing work on traditional small-scale distributed collaboration [34, 41], our research speci<sup>fi</sup>cally focused on online mass collaboration such as innovation crowdsourcing communities. This study also extends previous collaboration research by focusing on idea convergence which is one of the least studied patterns of (virtual) collaboration [81].

## Implications for Practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several practical implications. First, our results suggest that satisfaction with process is associated with intrinsic cognitive load, extraneous cognitive load, and germane cognitive load, while satisfaction with outcome correlates with intrinsic cognitive load and germane cognitive load. Online crowdsourcing providers could take this into consideration when preparing open innovation initiatives: If the targeted goal is to achieve high levels of satisfaction with outcome rather than satisfaction with process among crowdsourcing participants, they could aim to decrease extraneous cognitive load to some degree. Second, we suggest that initiators of idea convergence tasks should consider how they can leverage task design to improve idea convergence outcomes. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that lower task complexity is associated with lower perceived intrinsic cognitive load, and correlates with higher satisfaction with process and satisfaction with outcome. Task designers could, for example, divide the generated ideas that need to be converged on into small idea sets and present these separate idea sets to participants in sequential fashion in order to decrease perceived intrinsic cognitive load. Finally, we suggest that practitioners should consider embracing appropriate process guidance to facilitate the convergence process. Previous studies showed that a lack of time and instructions lead to unbalanced contributions resulting in poor adoption of crowdsourcing initiatives [54]. Following our investigation, we suggest that it is important for idea crowdsourcing communities to consider incorporating procedural support to facilitate the idea convergence process. Individuals that are guided to e<sup>fi</sup>ciently process ideas in a structured way are more likely to be productive and satis<sup>fi</sup>ed.

## Limitations and Future Research

Several limitations need to be considered when interpreting the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study. The <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation concerns the recruitment of students as the participants in the experiment. As is common with using student populations in laboratory experiments [5, 70], the external validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings needs to be established in future research, for example by recruiting a wider range of participants, using a commercial crowdsourcing community in a <sup>fi</sup>eld setting, and employing di<sup>f</sup>erent idea convergence tasks. Furthermore, future research could employ additional research methodologies, such as case studies and design science research to explore the behavioral patterns in idea crowdsourcing communities and design technological process support and additional techniques that address di<sup>f</sup>erent cognitive load challenges.

Second, we measured three types of cognitive load through post-experiment questionnaires. However, extraneous cognitive load and germane cognitive load are correlated to some degree. Some techniques could decrease extraneous cognitive load and increase germane cognitive load at the same time. Therefore, it is recommended to collect physiological measurements of cognitive load in future studies to triangulate data with perceptual measurements through questionnaires.

## Conclusions

We examined the role of three types of cognitive load in idea convergence. Building on CLT, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings o<sup>f</sup>er insights that higher convergence quality is associated with higher germane cognitive load. The association between germane cognitive load and idea convergence quality is positively moderated by need for cognition, knowledge self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and perceived goal clarity. Moreover, we found that techniques focusing on minimizing intrinsic cognitive load were positively associated with satisfaction with process and outcome, and interventions focusing on minimizing extraneous cognitive load were positively associated with satisfaction with process. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings shed light on the role of cognitive interventions on idea convergence outcomes and motivate future explorations on idea convergence in open innovation idea crowdsourcing settings.
