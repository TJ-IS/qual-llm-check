## a b s t r a c t

The ability to sense developments in operational (steady-state) and dynamic (growth) capabilities provides early signals about how the firm adapts its operations to ongoing changes in the environment. Frontline employees engage in the daily transactions and sense the firm's operating conditions and ability to deal with the environment that eventually will affect performance and strategic outcomes. The environmental sensing is a central cognitive feature and constitutes an information source for operations strategy decisions. Drawing on aggregated judgmental time-series forecasting techniques, this article develops a sensing instrument an employee-sensed operational conduct (ESOC) index for updated information as an essential decision support mechanism. This sensing capacity is firm-specific and difficult to replicate once in place and thus can provide a basis for sustainable competitive advantage.

## 1. Introduction

The management literature acknowledges that environmental scanning is important to inform decision makers about the changing context in which the firm operates (e.g., [2,3,5,10]). Sensing is an important cognitive element of dynamic capabilities [49,63,64] and efforts to scan emergent risks and opportunities before they fully evolve is important information for strategic decisions [7,11,18]. While strategy scholars engage to conceptualize the sensing construct (e.g., [31,34,63]) the empirical foundations and its relationship to firm performance have received little attention.

Operations strategy has considered how operating practices affect strategy [58] and how operational capabilities can explain competitive advantage [28,29]. Operational capabilities execute existing procedures to generate a profit where search routines of dynamic capabilities change existing routines or develop new ones [47]. Similar distinctions are made between steady-state capabilities and growth capabilities [61]. Hence, management scholars suggest different ways to categorize those capabilities typically distinguishing between operational and dynamic capabilities [30,32,69,70]. An operational capability enables the firm to perform specific activities drawing on existing techniques in support of current products and services [32,69]. In contrast, a dynamic capability enables a firm to change how it conducts its business [65]. Thus, the sensing of changes in operational and dynamic capabilities can provide essential information for strategic decisions where a method to collect and analyze this data can become an effective decision support system alongside other collective intelligence approaches [9,15].

Dynamic (manufacturing) capabilities are generally recognized as important sources of sustainable competitive advantage [29,61,65] because they develop in unique ways and are hard to imitate [32,59,72]. Despite this realization there has been little effort devoted to measure operational conduct as a function of supportive capabilities. To our awareness there are no prior attempts to develop an indicator of sensed adaptation in capabilities used to obtain early signals that can inform strategic decisions.

Developing new operations management (OM) practices is a way to adapt capabilities and achieve a better fit with the changing conditions [48] and a stream of research has examined the performance effect of these practices [68]. This study uses such insights to construe an index reflecting the effectiveness of operational and dynamic capabilities, which when fully operationalized is a low cost decision support system providing first-hand accurate information about a firm's operational conduct and performance.

Frontline employees involved in the daily operations are the first to note changes in operating practices, as they see directly what goes on in the organization and how the firm interacts with customers, suppliers, and other important stakeholders. Hence, this paper develops an index to evaluate firm capabilities and their adaptation to environmental change based on sensing information collected from frontline employees. It demonstrates how frontline sensing can be collected

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

## 2.1. Capabilities and strategic adaptation

The operations management literature suggests that integration and best practices matter to business success [55] although manufacturing firms arguably suffer from an oversimplified view of the practice-performance link [58]. It calls for a more comprehensive view of the link between capabilities and strategic fit with the environment but there is surprisingly little empirical evidence specifying the effect of operating practices on performance [62]. The theoretical underpinnings of integration is rarely addressed directly [54] but rather highlight certain integration-related operations practices [16,20,24,54]. Hence, the decision support literature can gain further insights into how changes in firm capabilities will adapt operational practices and link to performance.

Adaptive organizational actions derive from interpretation of environmental information [17]. However, once action is taken, the environment is already changed again, thereby triggering ongoing cycles of information gathering, interpretation, and adaptation. In the microfoundations of dynamic capabilities these actions are framed by three sequential capacities for change: sensing, seizing, and reconfiguring [63]. Strategic reference point theory suggests that organizations able to adapt their resource deployment to obtain a better fit with the changing environment will outperform [3,23,37]. The implied adaptation process is a function of observance and understanding that informs the formation of effective responses by modifying and reconfiguring resources and capabilities [30].

The operations management literature pinpoints the importance of operating practices as a source of dynamic capabilities that allow the organization to respond to ongoing changes and adapt (e.g., [45,62,72]). The dynamic capabilities framework in strategic management [65] is consistent with the concept of core dynamic manufacturing capabilities in the operations management literature [72]. Like core dynamic manufacturing capabilities, dynamic capabilities embrace the firm's capacity to change the ecosystem it occupies, develop new products and processes, and modify existing business models [63]. Helfat and Winter [32] argue that dynamic capabilities enable a firm to change how it makes its living, whereas operational capabilities are routines for the status quo. However, they also argue that it is hard to distinguish between the two because many incremental changes in operating routines over time can add to a substantial dynamic change [32].

The operations management literature identifies operating practices [38,57,61] that affect operational conduct and adaptation. The capability framework proposed by Swink and Hegarty [61] incorporates growth capabilities that change the company and steady-state capabilities that represent current operational routines. Swink and Hegarty [61] refer to seven core capabilities of relevance to adaptive change: improvement, innovation, integration, acuity, control, agility, and responsiveness – all capabilities linked to firm performance. To exemplify the NUMMI plant, a joint venture between General Motors and Toyota, had excellent improvement capabilities as a result of worker motivation, learning, problem solving, waste reduction, and standardization [1]. Acuity refers to insights managers gain from operations and transactions that translate customer needs into better manufacturing specifications. Allegheny Ludlum Corporation, a specialty steel manufacturer, exemplifies superior acuity [39] developed through remodeling and experimentation. Processing data on productivity, utilization, yields, rejects, and variances coupled with an understanding of customer needs provided a basis for effective strategic responses that gave the company a clear advantage [61]. It is argued that a focus on common traits that identify specific capabilities should guide the development of proper measures [21]. So, characteristic artefacts can be collected from respondents to capture good self-reported capability measures.

## 2.2. The sensing of frontline employees

The sensing capacities of individuals are associated with updating of their mental schemas [6,31,34] as the environment changes. Sensing constitutes an important cognitive element of dynamic capabilities [49,63,64] and dynamic managerial capabilities [31]. Teece [63] argues that “while certain individuals in the enterprise may have the necessary cognitive and creative skills, the more desirable approach is to embed scanning, interpretative, and creative processes inside the enterprise itself.” Pavlou and El Sawy [45] refer to these surveillance activities as the firm's sensing capacity, conceived as the ability to spot, interpret, and pursue opportunities that adapt the operational capabilities to accommodate changes in the environment.

A part of the strategic management literature indicates that employees below the top-management level have insights of strategic importance due to their interactions with relevant stakeholders (e.g., [12, 67]). Particularly, frontline employees gain intimate knowledge about operational performance through the reactions observed among multiple stakeholders. Frontline employees are closest to serving the customers and hold an unfiltered view of stakeholder interaction [66]. They are important boundary spanning individuals with updated insights about changing conditions, stakeholder sentiments, and the quality of internal competencies (e.g., [67]). These insights provide an intuitive understanding of internal strengths and weaknesses and the ability to handle emergent risks and opportunities that cannot be accessed elsewhere in the organization.

Capabilities are shaped by firm-specific skills and processes used to manage operations and solve emerging issues by configuring resources in line with customer preferences as a source of competitive advantage [72]. They are developed through complex interactions within the firm's social structure, where the relevant knowledge is held collectively among many different individuals [72]. Hence, the cognitive interpretation among many organizational members constitutes a sensing capability of potential strategic significance [42,45]. Since frontline employees are involved in the daily operations, they sense the effectiveness of the firm's capabilities from personal hands-on experiences. The direct actions in operations and engagement with external and internal stakeholders make frontline employees the first to observe subtle changes that will affect how the firm operates and performs. Hence, we suggest that:

Hypothesis 1. Frontline employees can sense developments in operational and dynamic capabilities that are linked with future firm performance.

Winter [69] and Helfat et al. [30] argue that dynamic capabilities enable a firm to change existing routines and adapt organizational processes whereas operational capabilities retain and optimize existing routines. According to Helfat and Winter [32], “a dynamic capability is one that enables a firm to alter how it currently makes its living”. Dynamic capabilities are constituted by resources and operational routines that transform existing processes into new configurations of resources and operational routines [14,65] that can provide better strategic fit. Hence, it can be argued that frontline sensing of dynamic capabilities, i.e., the firm's ability to adapt organizational processes to a changing environment, is a stronger predictor of firm performance compared to operational capabilities. This leads to the suggestion that:

Hypothesis 2.1. The sensing among frontline employees of developments in dynamic capabilities is a stronger predictor of firm performance than the sensing of developments in operational capabilities.

However, Helfat and Winter [32] also point out that it is difficult to distinguish between operational and dynamic capabilities, because “change is always occurring to at least some extent and some capabilities can be used for both operational and dynamic purposes” (p. 1245). Hence, many incremental changes in operating routines can agglomerate over time into very substantial changes. Accordingly, Swink and Hegarty [61] consider growth capabilities that change existing practices and steady-state capabilities for operational efficiencies implying that they all influence adaptive change. This reasoning leads to the alternative suggestion that:

Hypothesis 2.2. The sensing of developments in operational and dynamic capabilities among frontline employees are equally strong predictors of firm performance.

These hypotheses suggest that indices of operational and dynamic capabilities capture the state of the firm's operating practices and constitute good predictors of performance. That is, frontline employees sense the ability to adapt existing capabilities to the changing environmental conditions, which in turn predicts medium-term firm performance as early signals about the effectiveness of the operations strategy. In the following section, we formalize these relationships in a mathematical model to develop an employee-sensed operational conduct (ESOC) index.

## 3. Model

The unique advantage of individual sensing is its link to human predictive processing of high volumes of updated environmental information [34]. Hence, employees can sense the effectiveness of firm capabilities and use these insights to predict firm performance even though they do not have complete information. Sensing the effectiveness of operational and dynamic capabilities by frontline employees can be captured using aggregated judgmental forecasting captured in an employee-sensed operational conduct (ESOC) index that is associated with future firm performance (Fig. 1).

The implied performance relationship can be represented in a simple mathematical model. The sensing of frontline employees about the effectiveness of different firm capabilities performed in operational activities at time t is represented by a vector $\mathbf { C } _ { \mathrm { t } }$ consisting of i individual capabilities. Firm performance is also influenced by a vector E of k external economic factors like industry capacity, demand, and seasonality. The presence of different capabilities across various activities, e.g., sales, marketing, finance, production, services, maintenance, and so forth are assumed to influence future performance in different nonlinear ways [4] expressed in a function f:

$$
\text { Performance } _ {\mathrm{t}} = f (\mathbf {C} _ {\mathrm{t}}, \mathbf {E} _ {\mathrm{t}})\tag{1}
$$

The partial derivative of the firm performance function f, with respect to an individual capability $\mathsf { C } _ { \mathrm { i t } }$ is assumed to be equal or greater than zero because of a positive relationship between capabilities and performance. Moreover, at least one partial derivative of the i capabilities must be strictly positive. That is, if all the i capabilities increases with a same factor then firm performance must also increase. In summary, firm performance is a function of the state of all the firm capabilities and the external economic factors. Insofar that frontline employees' beliefs approximately reflect the true firm capabilities, then their sensing about the capabilities can be used in decision support as proxies for the true values of $\mathbf { C } _ { \mathrm { t } }$ in Eq. (1). This is also reflected by the model in Fig. 1 and the empirical section of this study, which use the frontline employees' knowledge about capabilities to predict firm performance.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Participants and data collection

The capability measures were observed in an on-line survey distributed to frontline employees over 18 months in three international fourand five-star hotel chains in Scandinavia (Radisson Blu Hotels and Resorts, Marriott Hotels, and Choice Hotels). Large upscale hotel chains often operate hierarchical divisions with a certain power distance [44] and, therefore, constitute relevant sites to analyze the relationship between frontline sensing and firm performance. These are labor intensive service organizations where capabilities to a large extent are linked to people applying their individual skills and collaborative competencies working together. High labor turnover rates are challenging for hospitality firms and can quickly lead to deskilling and loss of capabilities [35,41]. Individual skills are important for service quality and operational productivity, for example, through functional flexibility, where employees can switch between different functions when demand conditions fluctuate [51]. If core personnel leave, the functional flexibility will suffer and lead to deteriorating service quality. Collecting updated data from the staff on a monthly basis will capture these ongoing changes in firm capabilities.

The judgmental forecasting survey was administered monthly from February 2006 to September 2007 generating a total of 626 completed questionnaires. The three organizations employed 120, 110, and 125 people respectively thus constituting small and medium-sized enterprises. Frontline personnel in these firms work in shifts that require longer data-sampling periods. Hence, we collected data over a five-day window between the 10th and the 16th of each month. The midmonth time interval was chosen to let employees accumulate sensing impressions from activities performed over the past month and form anticipations for future months. Table 1 provides descriptive statistics on frontline respondents by type of activity per firm and suggests significant levels of industry experience across diverse functional insights.

Department managers distributed the surveys among participating employees who used a personal code when answering the surveys. New hires were not sampled in order to avoid judgmental biases as they have not gained sufficient insights about the operational conduct. The sensing information from frontline employees taps into a diverse pool of functional knowledge reflecting expert assessments of current operational experiences, which reduces sensitivity to variations in monthly response rates and increases the validity of measures.

## 4.2.1. Interviews

The items identifying capabilities were extracted from conversations with executives, managers, employees, and academics with deep industry knowledge. They were asked to identify operational practices that represent employee expert knowledge. Two top managers at a worldclass hotel in Singapore reputed for superior service and internal communication were interviewed as were eight executives and ten employees in different Scandinavian hospitality firms. This input was included as preliminary survey items that were discussed further at tourism management conferences in Australia, Sweden, and Portugal. This resulted in a list of 13 survey items presented in the following sections (see Appendix).

## 4.2.2. Dynamic (growth) capabilities

Improvement indicates the ability to enhance productivity through motivation, learning, and process simplifications [1]. It refers to incremental improvements that translate into superior performance [47]. It improves current techniques to develop new ways of working for existing customers creating competitive advantage from short-term benefits [8,27] and enhanced service experiences [53]. Improvement through many small changes is perceived directly by employees [61] who sense how the firm improves its operating performance compared to its peers on this account (ESOC 1). It also derives from motivation and organizational learning reflecting an ability to engage people in efforts to improve the operational conduct [61]. It is expressed by how interesting employees expect their jobs to be and how operational efficiencies affect future earnings (ESOC 2 and ESOC 3). It is further reflected in the sense of career possibilities and chances of being promoted (ESOC 4 and ESOC 5).

Innovation refers to the ability to improve operating performance employing new resources, methods, and technologies [56]. It relates to experimentation that creates needed variance in operating technologies and service-delivery systems [8,40]. It is about experimenting with new technologies to meet customer needs and create opportunities [61]. The employees sense ongoing service renewal efforts and form expectations about the firm's ability to create new services, systems, and processes (ESOC 6).

Integration is an ability to incorporate new products and processes into current operations. It reflects learning of new skills and the ability to adjust operations to meet special needs [61]. It depends on knowledge sharing among individuals across teams to enable superior service [50,72] and establish effective service teams [53]. The employees sense these capabilities directly from team experiences within and across tasks and functions (ESOC 7 and ESOC 8).

## 4.2.3. Operational (steady-state) capabilities

Acuity refers to the understanding of customer needs and acquisition of insights for product and process development that depends on information sharing with customers in effective delivery systems [39]. It is also reflected in the ability to enhance sales by exhibiting production systems that convey value and quality [61] as well as customer loyalty [53]. The employees sense the firm's ability to understand customer needs reflected in customer willingness to promote the firm to other potential customers (ESOC 9). The frontline employees also sense the quality of operational capabilities when they interact with colleagues in the industry reflected in the level of respect received from associates with other firms in the industry (ESOC 10).

Control is the firm's ability to direct operating processes efficiently [61] based on feedback from operating and service-delivery systems [52,53]. The employees sense how departmental and general managers direct the operating processes as well as their ability to deal with problems in the organization (ESOC 11 and ESOC 12).

Responsiveness refers to the ability to adjust operational processes to changes in input-output requirements embedded in the flexibility of the operating structure. Responsive processes can shift work schedules, job sequences, and physical routings to deal with unexpected changes and depend on an ability to retain skilled people [53]. Excessive turnover will harm responsiveness and employees can sense the ability to retain key people as expressed in their interest in looking for alternative job opportunities (ESOC 13).

## 4.3. Instrument development

We considered the breadth of the ESOC items (1−13) in a correlation matrix (Table 2) to assess if there is a broad set of factors to explain the dependent variable as suggested for development of formative indices [19,43]. Only 8 out of 78 correlations are above 0.50, and the average correlation coefficient between items is 0.29 with a standard deviation of 0.21 showing that the indicators seem to capture different types of information.

The items in the on-line questionnaires were framed as anticipating questions, e.g., “How do you think the ability of Hotel X in developing new and creative services, systems, and processes will be over the next 12 months compared with now?” Each item was a declarative sentence followed by response options with varying degrees of agreement assessed on a five-point Likert scale, where values 1 and 2 indicate negative and modest negative developments, a value of 3 indicates no change, whereas 4 and 5 indicate modest positive and positive developments.

If positive responses outnumber negative responses, the diffusion measure is above 100, and if negative responses outnumber positive responses, the measure is below 100 [25]. The aggregated ESOC index is then calculated by taking the sum of the indices for the 13 ESOC indicators, divided by the sum of the base period values of the same indicators. January 2007 is used as the base period. Finally, the ratio of the sums is multiplied by 100 to obtain the conventional index number representation.

$$
\mathrm{ESOC} _ {\mathrm{t}} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {1 3} \mathrm{ESOC} _ {\mathrm{it}}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {1 3} \mathrm{ESOC} _ {\mathrm{i0}}} \times 1 0 0\tag{3}
$$

An ESOC value above 100 indicates that frontline employees have more positive sensing responses to the future state of the firm's operational capabilities, and a value below 100 indicates that employees mostly sense a negative development.

One of the most common performance indicators in the hotel industry is revenue per available room (REVPAR) [22]. It is a financial ratio that allows benchmarking across hotels and is therefore used in this study. A relative firm performance measure for time t (denoted Pfm ) is calculated as the percentage change in firm REVPAR $( R _ { f i r m } ) _ { 1 }$ compared to the percentage change in industry REVPAR $( R _ { i n d u s t r y } )$ using the natural logarithm to approximate percentage change [25,26].

$$
P f m _ {t} = \Delta \ln (R _ {\text { firm }}) _ {t} - \Delta \ln (R _ {\text { industry }}) _ {t}\tag{4}
$$

Hence, Pfm measures excess return of the individual firm compared to the industry thereby filtering out the effects of the external economic factors (E ) so the measure only captures firm-specific outcomes. As a result, $\operatorname { E S O C } _ { \operatorname { t } }$ which in an aggregate sense represents the individual capabilities $\mathbf { C } _ { \mathrm { t } }$ in Eq. (1), indicates how the frontline is sensing that the firm will perform compared to competitors in the industry and $P f m _ { \mathrm { t } }$ indicates realized firm performance compared to the industry at time t.

## 4.5. Model estimation

We measured excess returns (Pfm ) for the three firms as the dependent variable testing whether the ESOC index can predict firm performance. The transformation of survey data into an aggregate ESOC index implicates a move from cross-sectional data analysis to time-series regression modeling. For this purpose we use a distributed lag (DL) regression model to assess ESOC as a leading performance indicator. We opted for parsimoniously specified DL models and applied the data in first-difference form to make the time series stationary. The ESOC index is treated as exogenous since the index is derived from a questionnaire where items have no direct relation to performance. Since the exogenous economic elements of monthly variations in REVPAR have been filtered out in Pfm, less information is needed to account for the remaining variation thus reducing the chance of omitted variable bias. We used ordinary least squares regression to yield unbiased coefficients in the following model.

$$
P f m _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t}} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {q} \delta_ {j} \Delta \ln \mathrm{ESOC} _ {\mathrm{i}, \mathrm{t} - j} + u _ {i, t}\tag{5}
$$

We used a general-to-specific modeling approach, where the different lags, or lead times for frontline sensing of the ESOC index were reduced from high lags towards lower lags in consecutive regressions until the most significant lag was obtained [13,33]. In all of the models the two month lead time obtained significant results with normally distributed error terms without serial correlations. Moreover, autocorrelation functions indicate that there is no information value in including lagged values of the dependent variable on the right hand side. A constant term is neither included in the empirical model because it would imply a return different from the industry average, which is an unreasonable assumption. Nonetheless, model specifications including constant terms were tested, but none of them were statistically significant. This also supports the parsimonious formulation of Eq. (5).

## 4.6. Validity and reliability

The construct validity and reliability of the ESOC index was tested with partial least square structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to show the relative importance of the different capability measures and investigate how they contribute to the Swink and Hegarty [61] framework. In PLS-SEM an exogenous variable has path arrows pointing outwards and none leading to it, while an endogenous variable has one or more paths leading to it representing the influence of other variables [71]. Hence, PLS-SEM is less concerned with statistical accuracy of estimates but is more concerned with maximizing explained variance and making the best predictions.

We used improvement as dependent capability variable (ESOC 1, 2, 3, 4, 5) to assess the relationships to the other capabilities, acuity (ESOC 9, 10), control (ESOC 11, 12), innovation (ESOC 6), integration (ESOC 7, 8), and responsiveness (ESOC 13) [61] since it reflects incremental wins into superior performance that intuitively is a relevant outcome variable. The PLS-SEM modeling produces loadings above 0.6 with indicators explaining 42% of the variance in improvement. All path coefficients of the capabilities are significant (Table 3) validating the theoretical dimensions of the Swink and Hegarty [61] framework applied to the study.

## 5. Results

The prediction models were run for the three samples of participat ing firms (Table 4).

In sample 1, the ESOC index appears useful to forecast firm performance as the second lag of the ESOC variable is statistically significant with the expected sign in both models. The effect of a 1% change in the ESOC index is a 0.45% change in the relative firm performance two months forward, which is a substantive effect. The $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ is 25%, indicating a fairly high degree of explanation in an otherwise competitive industry. The model was tested for normality, heteroscedasticity, autocorrelation, and model specification, which did not contest the underlying assumptions. A test of Granger causality rejected the null hypothesis that the lagged ESOC indices are not significantly different from zero at the 5% significance level and with an $\mathrm { F } _ { 2 , 1 2 }$ value of 6.7547.

Sample 3 provides the strongest predictions of future firm performance. As in sample 1 the ESOC lags have the expected sign with the second lag of the variable statistically significant. The effect of a 1% change in the ESOC index corresponds to nearly a 2% change in relative performance after two months with an $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ of 51%. The specification tests show well-behaved residuals and a test of Granger causality rejects the null hypothesis that the lagged ESOC indices are not significantly different from zero at the 5% significance level with an $\mathrm { F } _ { 2 , 1 5 }$ value of 7.52. These findings provide strong support for Hypothesis 1 stating that frontline employees can sense developments in operational and dy namic capabilities that are linked with future firm performance.

In sample 2, the firm unexpectedly changed the pricing strategy from fixed-scheme pricing early in the data-sampling period to a dynamic-pricing approach where prices fluctuate with changes in market demand. In a dynamic-pricing scheme, prices are automatically updated based on current price information on the Internet. This allowed management to negotiate discounts from the “best available price” rather than setting a fixed price where the strong correlation between firm and market reduces the variation needed for prediction. The correlation between $\mathsf { R } _ { f i r m }$ and $\mathrm { R } _ { i n d u s t r y }$ for samples 1, 2, and 3 are 0.94, 0.97, and 0.86, respectively indicating that sample 2 has less variation in Pfm to predict compared to the other samples and as a consequence fail to show statistically significant model coefficients.

We also tested the performance predictions of separate operational (steady-state) and dynamic (growth) capability indices (Tables 5 and 6). However, there are no significant differences between the regression coefficients and R-squared in the two regressions using different sub-indices. The operational capabilities have significant regression coefficients and there is no indication that dynamic capabilities have higher significant coefficients. Hence, the analysis does not provide support for Hypothesis 2.1, and we cannot claim that the sensing of dynamic capabilities provides stronger predictions of firm performance. The evidence rather favors Hypothesis 2.2 stating that both sensing of operational and dynamic capabilities are significant predictors of firm performance. The results also indicate that the strongest predictions are obtained with the ESOC index that combines both operational and dynamic capabilities (Table 4).

To show the dynamic signaling effects from the ESOC index, we analyzed the development of the monthly ESOC value throughout the data collection period in each of the three case firms (Table 7). The color coding indicates the direction and magnitude of changes in the ESOC index where absolute increases above 5% are colored light grey and dark grey for comparable decreases whereas less pronounced changes are white.

The color coding makes it easier to detect substantial changes that forewarn substantial effects on medium-term performance as sensed by the frontline employees. The underlying signaling shows a dynamic pattern with drops and increases over the period indicating the medium-term effects of the current operational conduct. While there may be some consistency in signaling patterns it is apparent that the sensed performance effects are distinctly firm-specific as is expected.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This study presents four key findings. First, it demonstrates that an ESOC index can be as a decision support mechanism. Second, it shows that the sensing among frontline employees of developments in firm capabilities can be collected and aggregated in judgmental time-series forecasting. Third, it shows that the sensing among frontline employees of both operational and dynamic capabilities combined are strong predictors of firm performance. Finally, it demonstrates that an ESOC index can produce accurate predictions of firm performance based on frontline sensing of firm capabilities.

The study articulates a set of fundamental ESOC capabilities (Appendix) developed from the operations management literature [61] and adjusted to the service sector. It develops a judgmental forecasting aggregation mechanism to capture the sensing by frontline employees of operational and dynamic capabilities. In two out of three samples the ESOC index predicts future firm performance. The second sample has insignificant results due to unexpected changes in pricing policies early in the experiment that obscured the relative performance measure (Pfm). Therefore, we consider the judgmental forecasting time-series as being generally supportive of the hypothesized predictive power of frontline sensing. The study has three implications for business research: (i) it operationalizes the sensing capacity of frontline employees as important informants, (ii) it demonstrates that the sensing of frontline employees can predict firm performance and thus constitutes information of potential strategic value, and (iii) it shows that an ESOC index can provide early performance signals as a new decision support system.

## 6.1. Sensing of capabilities for decision support

Environmental sensing is a microfoundational [63] and cognitive capability [31] that can help assess whether the operational and dynamic capabilities are able to adapt the firm to changing business conditions (e.g., [2,5,10,42,63]). The microfoundations of dynamic capabilities emphasize the importance of individual sensing as intuitive judgments, or predictions that can assess future states from current observations of operating practices (cf. [34]). This study argues that sensing derived from experiential insights of individuals involved directly in the daily transactions in the firm's operations management system is an accurate and reliable predictor.

Building on operations management [61], service management [53], and dynamic capabilities [32,63,72], the study collects frontline sensing of capabilities to empirically investigate the relationship to future firm performance. The analysis shows that operational and dynamic capabilities sensed by frontline employees can predict firm performance and thereby provide early signals about the consequences of the current operational conduct. Hence, the ability to sense the capabilities of the firm can signal critical issues of emergent risks and challenges of new opportunities that need the attention of managerial decision-makers.

## 6.2. The usefulness of capabilities sensing

The ability to get updated information about the firm's ability to adapt its operations to changes in the environment as information for ongoing decisions has long been stressed in the strategy and operations management fields (e.g., [2,5,10,58,63]). To this end, the ESOC index is a bottom-up driven information instrument that aggregates current knowledge from employees operating directly with diverse core stakeholders. Hence, the ESOC index provides predictive information from the organizational grass roots as advance signals about potential future effects of the current operations strategy. The predictive nature of the ESOC index makes it an effective early performance signal that can complement existing management controls. To our knowledge, no prior study has operationalized the sensing of operational and dynamic capabilities in an index linked to firm performance as a decision support mechanism generating early performance signals.

## 6.3. Reflections, limitations, and future perspectives

The judgmental time-series forecasting datasets include a limited number of sampling sites, confined industry contexts, and time periods for data collection calling for further empirical studies to reproduce these initial findings. However, the results obtained in a dynamic and highly competitive international service industry based on judgmental time-series forecasts collected in different samples propose a certain robustness of findings with promising prospects.

A basic limitation of this exploratory study arguably is its focus on firms operating in the hospitality industry. However, the significance of frontline sensing demonstrated in this study has subsequently been validated in the telecommunications industry, where call center employees were more accurate predictors of, e.g., customer satisfaction, than the customers themselves [46]. Consequently, we believe the decision support mechanism demonstrated in this study can be applied to many types of service companies.

Future studies may consider larger samples to explore possible restrictions in the prediction models and determine specific prediction patterns that may apply to different industry contexts. Future studies could be extended to include other sectors, such as, manufacturing and other capital intensive industries covering different time periods and geographical regions. While this is a demanding methodological exercise, the current results suggest that it is a viable path to further validate the preliminary findings. Future studies can examine additional indicators of relevant operational and dynamic capabilities to refine the ESOC construct. This may require further development of dynamic factor models based on covariance structure modeling to test the explanatory power of different capabilities. The development of more refined combinations of operational and dynamic capabilities can enhance the application of information support systems using updated insights of frontline employees.
