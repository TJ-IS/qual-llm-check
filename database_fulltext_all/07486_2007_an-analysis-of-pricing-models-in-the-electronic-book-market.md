# AN ANALYSIS OF PRICING MODELS IN THE ELECTRONIC BOOK MARKET<sup>1</sup>

Lin Hao

Mendoza College of Business, University of Notre Dame, Notre Dame, IN 46556 U.S.A. {lhao@nd.edu}

Ming Fan

Foster School of Business, University of Washington, Seattle WA 98195 U.S.A. {mfan@uw.edu}

In this paper, we develop a game theoretic model to study the pricing of e-books and e-readers under two pricing models: wholesale and agency. We analyze pricing strategies for a publisher and a retailer. We identify the complementary relationship between e-books and e-readers as the main reason for the retailer to set a low e-book price in the wholesale model. Comparing the wholesale and the agency models, we find, in a wide range of market conditions, the price for e-book readers is lower in the agency model, leading to a higher e-book market share. However, a higher e-book price in the agency model lowers e-book consumption. Overall social welfare is lower in the agency model than in the wholesale model. While total consumer surplus is slightly higher in the agency model, largely because of a lower e-reader price, business profit is lower. The publisher, surprisingly, is worse off under the agency model.

Keywords: Electronic book, e-reader, pricing, agency model, wholesale model

## Introduction

When Amazon.com launched its e-book reader, Kindle, in 2007, its founder Jeff Bezos believed that it was time for “the last bastion of analog,” books, to be digitized and sold in electronic format (Levy 2007). In 2011, less than four years after introducing Kindle books, Amazon.com sold more ebooks than print books, hard cover and paperback combined (Miller and Bosman 2011). Although consumers have become more comfortable with e-books, many complain about their high retail prices. As it costs less to produce and distribute e-books compared to physical ones, consumers are expecting lower prices for e-books. Instead, they see rising ebook prices (Rich and Stone 2010). To discern the causes of rising e-book prices, we investigate in this study the pricing models in the e-book industry.

There are two main e-book pricing models: (1) the wholesale pricing model, and (2) the agency pricing model (Trachtenberg 2011a). In the wholesale model, a publisher such as Random House charges a retailer such as Amazon.com a wholesale price. Then, the retailer decides the final retail price for the e-book. In the agency model, the publisher decides the final retail price for an e-book and the retailer receives a fixed percentage of the sales revenue. Amazon.com started with the wholesale model and priced digital versions of New York Times best-sellers and new releases at just \$9.99 (Stone and Rich 2009). Publishers were unhappy with the low e-book retail prices and, in 2010, several publishers switched to the agency model and took control of their own ebook retail prices (Trachtenberg 2011b).

The dynamics in the e-book market are quite different from those in a traditional retail setting. First, a traditional retailer usually has an incentive to set a product's retail price high over its wholesale price. In contrast, Amazon wants to set low retail prices for e-books. Second, a supplier typically wants to limit the price the retailer can charge because double marginalization, in which the supplier and the retailer both want to mark up the product’s price over their respective marginal costs, lowers the overall demand and profit (Rey and Tirole 1986; Tirole 1988). In the e-book market, it seems the publisher is concerned more about the low, rather than the high, retail price. Why would a retailer, such as Amazon, which has a dominant share of the e-book market, want to charge a low e-book retail price in the wholesale model? How does the new agency model affect e-book prices? Does the agency model lead to an increase of e-book prices? The aim of this study is to provide explanations for these questions.

We use a game theoretical approach to analyze pricing strategies for a publisher and a retailer. In the wholesale model, we identify the complementary consumption between ereaders and e-books as the main reason for the retailer to set a low e-book price and mark up the price of e-readers. We further compare the market equilibrium outcomes between the wholesale and the agency models. We find, in a wide range of market conditions, that the price for e-book readers is lower in the agency model, which leads to a higher e-book market share. The equilibrium e-book price in the agency model is higher than in the wholesale model. As a result, e-book consumption for an individual customer drops. Overall social welfare is lower in the agency model than that in the wholesale model. While total consumer surplus is slightly higher with the help of a lower e-reader price, business profit is lower in the agency model. The publisher, surprisingly, is worse off under the agency model.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. After reviewing relevant literature in the next section, we set up the demand functions and analyze both the wholesale and the agency models. Further, we compare the equilibrium prices, business profit, and social welfare between the two pricing models. In the “Extension” section, we relax the homogenous demand assumption and provide additional analysis when consumers can have heterogeneous demands on books. We then discuss the results and implications, and follow with the presentation of our conclusions.

## Literature Review

This study is related to the literature of double marginalization and vertical constraints. The problem of double marginalization happens when both the upstream and downstream firms want to mark up the prices of a product above their marginal costs. The markups raise the retail price, lower the demand, and reduce the combined profit for the supplier and the retailer (Spengler 1950). Franchise fee or resale-price maintenance (RPM) could be the tools to solve the double marginalization problem (Rey and Tirole 1986). In the former case, the supplier charges the retailer a wholesale price equal to the supplier’s marginal cost for the product plus a fixed franchise fee. Then, the retailer has all the incentive to set the retail price as if the supplier and the retailer were vertically integrated. In RPM, the supplier directly imposes a price ceiling for the retailer to mitigate the unfavorable effects of double marginalization.

This study is also related to multiproduct pricing models. A vast body of literature has been devoted to both linear and nonlinear multiproduct pricing models (Armstrong 1996; Rochet and Chone 1998; Whinston 1990). Church and Gandal (1996) assessed the effect of hardware control on software provision in the markets where the consumption benefit of hardware is a function of the variety of available software. Mulhern and Leone (1991) studied the optimal pricing and promotion policy when retailers sell multiproducts for which demand is interdependent. It was shown that retailers could exploit the substitute or complement relationship among products using implicit price bundling.

In the information systems area, there is a rich literature on information goods pricing and distribution strategies. Sundararajan (2004) analyzed two nonlinear pricing models, the unlimited-usage (fixed-fee pricing) model and the usagebased model, for their applications in pricing information goods. The results suggested that using fixed-fee pricing in addition to nonlinear usage-based pricing always improves the profit when the transaction cost is nonzero. Lang and Vragov (2005) examined a pricing scheme for distributing digital content over centralized and decentralized networks. Chellappa and Kumar (2005) studied how “free” productaugmenting services affect online sellers’ pricing and customer retention strategies. Fan et al. (2007) developed a model to examine optimal strategies for media providers to utilize online channels to distribute digital media. Mantena et al. (2010) studied the exclusive contract between vendors of platforms such as video game consoles and vendors of complementary goods. Feng et al. (2009) and Li (2010) examined optimal channel structures and corresponding pricing strategies in distributing digital content. Choudhary (2010) studied vendors’ choices of pricing schemes under market competition and showed that the choice of pricing scheme affects buyers’ usage levels as well as revenue distribution over different segments of buyers. Thus, vendors could differentiate themselves by choosing different pricing schemes, for example, per user pricing and site licensing. Yu et al. (2011) studied optimal pricing schemes for both digital devices and contents when they are tied.

Our study contributes to the literature in the following ways. With respect to the literature on double marginalization and vertical constraints, we show that in the wholesale model, the complementary consumption of e-readers and e-books drives the retailer to price e-books with no or just a small margin. Ereaders serve a similar function as a franchise fee to extract consumer surplus and, therefore, the retailer has little incentive to raise e-book price above the wholesale price. With respect to the multiproduct pricing and information goods pricing literature, we develop demand functions that are specific to the e-book market. In our model, an individual consumer purchases multiple e-books for an e-reader. We use a horizontal model to capture consumer choice between ebooks and physical books. We later extend the consumer model, allowing a second dimensional heterogeneity on demand levels for books, and the results of the extended model are consistent with the findings of the earlier model. Further, this study contributes to the IS literature by examining the pricing for both information goods and the complementary devices. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to compare two popular pricing models in the ebook industry. We find, in both the wholesale and the agency models, e-reader price decreases in e-book price. We compare the two pricing models in terms of their equilibrium outcomes and our results have important business and social welfare implications.

## The Model

## Model Setup

We model a publisher and a retailer in the marketplace. The publisher supplies the contents of e-books and physical books to the retailer. The retailer acquires the contents of e-books and makes them available to the marketplace along with its ebook reader. The retailer also sells the physical books supplied by the publisher. Denote the representative e-book retail price by $p _ { E }$ and the representative physical book retail price by $p _ { F }$ . This representative price setting is consistent with prior studies in multichannel research (Balasubramanian 1998; Chiang et al. 2003) that also use representative product price to abstract different prices of a variety of products belonging to same genre. The e-reader price is denoted by $p _ { D } .$

We start our analysis by characterizing the individual consumer’s purchase quantities on both e-books and physical books. As we mentioned earlier, a consumer decides to buy an e-reader because the consumer plans to use the device repeatedly (i.e., to read multiple e-books). Therefore, we use a linear demand function to characterize a consumer’s purchase quantity of e-books as

$$
q _ {E} = (b - p _ {E}) / m
$$

As the e-book price $p _ { E }$ drops, a consumer will buy more ebooks. The consumer’s purchase quantity of physical books follows a similar function: $q _ { F } = ( b - p _ { F } ) / m$ . We can see that 1/m and b/m are the slope and the intercept of the book demand function, respectively. We assume that the e-books and the physical books have the same parameters (b,m) as the contents they deliver are identical.

Consumers are heterogeneous in terms of their preference toward electronic reading. Following Hotelling’s location model (Tirole 1988), we assume consumers are uniformly distributed in α 0 [0,1]. At α = 0, consumers mostly favor electronic books, and at $\alpha = 1 _ { : }$ , consumers mostly favor physical books. As α increases, the consumer’s preference for electronic books decreases and her preference increases for physical books. Thus, the net utility for the consumer located at $\alpha$ is $\nu _ { E } \mathrm { ~ - ~ } t \alpha$ if she chooses e-books, where $\nu _ { E }$ is the consumer’s surplus of purchasing e-books. If the consumer chooses physical books, her net utility is $\nu _ { F } - t ( 1 - \alpha )$ , where $\nu _ { F }$ is her surplus in buying physical books and t represents the rate of utility change when consumer preference parameter α changes. We can compute the consumer’s surplus based on her demand functions (Laffont et al. 1998). We get $\nu _ { E } = ( b -$ $p _ { E } ) ^ { 2 } / ( 2 m ) - p _ { D }$ and $\nu _ { F } = ( b - p _ { F } ) ^ { 2 } / \left( 2 m \right)$ . Despite the large sales volume of e-books, the penetration of e-books in the general population is still low (Greenfield 2012). Therefore, we consider the adoption of e-books in the consumer model.

Suppose the consumer with the preference parameter $\alpha ^ { * }$ is indifferent between choosing physical books and e-books. We have

$$
v _ {E} - t \alpha^ {*} = v _ {F} - t (1 - \alpha^ {*})
$$

We then derive the number of consumers who choose ebooks, which is the size of the e-book market

$$
s _ {E} = \alpha^ {*} = (t - p _ {D}) / (2 t) + (p _ {F} - p _ {E}) (2 b - p _ {E} - p _ {F})
$$

The size of the physical book market is therefore $s _ { F } = 1 - s _ { E }$

## The Wholesale Model

In the wholesale model, the publisher offers the retailer the wholesale price $w _ { E }$ for an e-book and the retailer then decides the e-book retail price $p _ { E } .$ Let $c _ { A }$ be the royalty that the publisher pays the author on the sale of each e-book. We normalize the marginal costs of storing and distributing ebooks to zero. First, those costs are very low (Trachtenberg 2011a). Second, normalizing them to zero does not affect the results of the analysis. We also disregard the cost of digitalizing books since it is one-time fixed cost.

For the physical book channel, the publisher also pays $c _ { A }$ for the author’s royalty on the sale of each physical book. In addition to the royalty, the publisher incurs $c _ { P }$ for other costs of selling a physical book. The wholesale price for a physical book is $w _ { F } .$ . The publisher’s total profit function is

$$
\pi_ {P} = (w _ {E} - c _ {A}) q _ {E} s _ {E} + (w _ {F} - c _ {P} - c _ {A}) q _ {F} s _ {F}
$$

where $q _ { E }$ is the purchase quantity of e-books for a customer and $s _ { E }$ is the e-book market size. Similarly, $q _ { F }$ is the purchase quantity of physical books for a customer and $s _ { F }$ is the size of the physical book market. As shown in this equation, the publisher’s total profit $\pi _ { P }$ comes from both e-books and physical books.

Suppose the retailer’s marginal cost of selling a physical book is $c _ { F }$ and the physical book is sold at retail price $p _ { F } .$ . The retailer’s total profit $\pi _ { R }$ is

$$
\pi_ {R} = \big ((p _ {E} - w _ {E}) q _ {E} + (p _ {D} - c _ {D}) \big) s _ {E} + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) q _ {F} s _ {F}
$$

where $c _ { D }$ is the marginal cost for e-readers. The above equation shows that the retailer’s total profits have three parts: $\mathrm { e \mathrm { - } }$ books, e-readers, and physical books.

We consider a two-stage sequential game. At the first stage, the publisher decides $w _ { E } .$ At the second stage, given $w _ { E } ,$ the retailer decides the retail prices for the e-books and the ereader $( p _ { E } , p _ { D } )$ . Using backward induction, we first solve for the retailer’s problem, assuming $w _ { E }$ is already given.

Proposition 1 Given the publisher’s wholesale price $w _ { E } ,$ the optimal e-book retail price $p _ { E } ^ { * }$ and e-reader price $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ in the wholesale model are

$$
p _ {E} ^ {*} = w _ {E}
$$

and

$$
\begin{array}{c} p _ {D} ^ {*} = (t + c _ {D}) / 2 + (p _ {F} - w _ {E}) (2 b - p _ {F} - w _ {E}) / \\ (4 m) + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) (b - p _ {F}) / (2 m) \end{array}
$$

The proofs for this and all subsequent propositions are provided in the appendix.

Proposition 1 suggests that the retailer should set the e-book retail price at the wholesale price received from the publisher. It implies that the retailer’s strategy is to have a two-part tariff pricing structure, under which the retailer makes profit a from e-readers rather than from e-books.

It is interesting to see that the retailer’s pricing strategy is different from the traditional double marginalization case in which retail stores have incentives to set the retail price above the marginal cost. The major difference here is the existence of the e-reader, which is required to read e-books. This complementary relationship between e-reader and e-books incentivizes the retailer to adjust prices for the e-reader and ebooks in order to maximize the overall profit. Proposition 1 suggests that it is optimal for the retailer to price e-books at the wholesale price in order to enlarge consumer surplus, and then mark up the e-reader to extract that surplus.

Examining the optimal e-reader price in Proposition 1, it is easy to derive that $\partial p _ { D } ^ { * } / \partial w _ { E } < 0$ , that is, the optimal e-reader price decreases in e-book wholesale price. When the e-book wholesale price rises, the e-book retail price rises as $p _ { E } ^ { * } . = w _ { E } .$ A higher e-book retail price will reduce consumer surplus from using e-books, which means the surplus that the retailer can extract from consumers through the e-reader also declines. Thus, the price for the e-reader has to drop.

We substitute the retailer’s optimal prices $( p _ { E } ^ { * } , ~ p _ { D } ^ { * } )$ from Proposition 1 into the publisher’s profit function and solve for the publisher’s problem to obtain the subgame perfect Nashequilibrium (SPNE). Although a closed-form solution for $w _ { E }$ is not available, we examine the publisher’s problem and find the following properties for the SPNE:

Proposition 2 In the wholesale model, the publisher’s optimal wholesale price $w _ { E } ^ { * } \left( i \right)$ decreases in the publisher’s physical book cost $c _ { P } ( i . e . , \mathcal { \partial w } _ { E } ^ { * } / \mathcal { \partial c } _ { P } < 0 )$ , and (ii) increases in the retailer’s physical book cost $( i . e . , \partial { w _ { E } ^ { * } } / \partial { c _ { F } } > 0 )$

The first part of Proposition 2 demonstrates how the publisher’s physical book cost affects the publisher’s price level for e-books. Since e-books and physical books are substitutes, physical book cost affects e-book price. If the physical book cost $c _ { P }$ increases, the publisher’s profit margin for the physical book will decrease. Then, e-books will become more profitable to the publisher. As a result, the publisher should encourage more customers to use e-books by decreasing the wholesale price $w _ { E } ^ { * } .$ As suggested in Proposition 1, this will lead to a lower e-book price $p _ { E } ^ { * }$ at the second stage.

The second part of Proposition 2 suggests that the publisher wants to balance the two channels of distribution because its revenue comes from both physical books and e-books. When the retailer’s cost in selling physical books $c _ { F }$ rises, physical books become less profitable to the retailer, who will then try to promote e-books. The publisher can raise the wholesale price for e-books so that physical book sales do not drop too quickly. The first part of this proposition is about e-book prices with regard to changes in the profit margin of physical books for the publisher, and the second part assumes that publisher’s profit margins for both e-books and physical books remain unchanged. Under this assumption, the publisher is better off not to cannibalize the physical channel and the publisher can use wholesale price to maintain the balance.

## The Agency Model

In the agency model, the publisher determines the e-book’s retail price. Also, the retailer and the publisher agree upon a revenue sharing contract to split the revenue of e-book sales. We denote the retailer’s revenue share percentage by r $( 0 < r < 1 )$ . Consider a two-stage sequential game. At the first stage, the publisher determines the e-book retail price $p _ { E } .$ At the second stage, the retailer decides the e-reader price $p _ { D } .$

In the agency model, the publisher’s profit $\pi _ { P }$ is

$$
\pi_ {P} = \big ((1 - r) p _ {E} - c _ {A} \big) q _ {E} s _ {E} + (w _ {F} - c _ {P} - c _ {A}) q _ {F} s _ {F}
$$

where $( 1 - r ) p _ { E }$ is the revenue the publisher receives from one e-book sale. Similar to the wholesale model, the publisher’s profit comes from both e-books and physical books.

The retailer’s profit $\pi _ { R }$ is

$$
\pi_ {R} = \big (r p _ {E} q _ {E} + (p _ {D} - c _ {D}) \big) s _ {E} + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) q _ {F} s _ {F}
$$

where $r p _ { E } q _ { E } s _ { E }$ is the revenue the retailer receives from e-book sales. The retailer’s profit comes from three parts: e-books, e-readers, and physical books.

Proposition 3 Given the e-book retail price $p _ { E } ,$ the optimal e-reader price $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ in the agency model is

$$
\begin{array}{c} p _ {D} ^ {*} = (t + c _ {D}) / 2 + (p _ {F} - p _ {E}) (2 b - p _ {F} - p _ {E}) / \\ (4 m) - r p _ {E} (b - p _ {E}) / (2 m) + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) (b - p _ {F}) / (2 m) \end{array}
$$

Proposition 3 shows the retailer’s optimal e-reader price $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ given the e-book retail price $p _ { E } .$ By deriving ${ \partial p _ { D } ^ { * } } / { \partial p _ { E } }$ , we can find how e-book price affects e-reader price. We can prove that when $p _ { E }$ is relatively low $( \mathrm { i . e . , } p _ { E } < ( 1 + r ) b / ( 1 + 2 r )$ $\partial p _ { D } ^ { * } / \partial p _ { E } < 0 )$ This suggests that the retailer should decrease e-reader price $p _ { D }$ when e-book price $p _ { E }$ increases. Although this result is similar to that in the wholesale price model, the underlying motivation for the retailer is different. In the wholesale model, the retailer makes money from e-readers. With a higher e-book price, the retailer has to reduce e-reader price simply because consumer surplus has dropped. In the agency model, the retailer’s profit comes from both e-books and e-readers. When e-book price level is low, the retailer’s profit from e-books can increase with a slight increase of ebook price. However, the retailer then has to lower e-reader price to maintain or grow the e-book market size. However, when e-book price is already high $( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , p _ { E } { > } ( 1 + r ) b / ( 1 + 2 r )$ we have $\partial p _ { D } ^ { * } / \partial p _ { E } > 0$ . It suggests that e-reader price could move in the same direction of e-book price if e-book price is already quite high. Later, we will prove the second scenario is unlikely to happen.

If we compare the e-reader prices under both pricing models, we can find that the agency model $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ has an additional term $- r p _ { E } ( b - p _ { E } ) / ( 2 m )$ . This suggests the higher the retailer’s revenue share percentage $r ,$ the lower the e-reader will be priced. On the other hand, if the revenue share for the retailer is lowered, the retailer will have to price e-reader higher.

Substituting the retailer’s price decision $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ from Proposition 4 into the publisher’s profit function, we can solve the publisher’s problem and obtain the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPNE).

Proposition 4 When the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage $r { < } ( p _ { F } { - } w _ { F } { + } c _ { P } ) / p _ { F }$ , the equilibrium e-book retail price $p _ { E } ^ { * }$ in the agency model satisfies $p _ { E } ^ { * } < \bar { p } _ { E } ,$ where $\bar { p } _ { E } =$ ${ \big ( } b + c _ { A } / ( 1 - r ) { \big ) } / 2$

The condition in Proposition $4 , r < ( p _ { F } - w _ { F } + c _ { P } ) / p _ { F } ,$ is quite mild. It can be satisfied when the retailer’s revenue share percentage in the e-book channel is less than $( p _ { F } - w _ { F } ) / p _ { F } .$ . As was illustrated in Trachtenberg (2011a), this condition is reasonable and can be easily satisfied.

In addition, in examining $\partial \bar { p } _ { E } / \partial r$ , we find that as the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage r increases, the upper bound for the e-book’s price $p _ { E }$ increases. The explanation is that the increase in the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage reduces the publisher’s profit. The publisher then wants to raise the e-book price to make up for the loss. We illustrate these results in Figure 1. The parameter values used for drawing the graph basically follow Trachtenberg (2011a) with the following values: $p _ { F } = 2 6 , w _ { F } = 1 3 , c _ { A } = 3 . 9 , c _ { P } = 3 . 2 5 , c _ { F } =$ 2, and $c _ { D } = 7 0$ We also set $b = 5 0 , m = 1$ , and $t = 2 0 0$ to make sure that we have a reasonable price elasticity and the market for books is fully covered.

Proposition 4 also sheds more light on the interpretation of Proposition 3. As discussed earlier, under Proposition 3, the equilibrium e-reader price $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ could either decrease or increase the e-book price $p _ { E } ,$ depending on the level of $p _ { E } .$ We now show that the latter case $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \partial p _ { D } ^ { * } / \partial p _ { E } ^ { } > 0 \mathrm { i f } p _ { E } ^ { } > ( 1 + r ) b / ( 1 +$ 2r)) is unlikely to happen simply because this high price level of e-books is not quite possible. The upper bound of the ebook’s price $p _ { E }$ is less than $( 1 + r ) b / ( 1 + 2 r )$ as long as $r \leq 1 / 2$ and $c _ { A } < p _ { F } / 4$ . In practice, these two conditions $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , r \leq 1 / 2$ and $c _ { A } < p _ { F } / 4 )$ are easily satisfied as a retailer’s revenue sharing percentage is usually around 30 percent and the author’s royalty fee is typically less than 20 percent of the physical book price (Trachtenberg 2011a). Therefore, the ebook price should satisfy the condition $p _ { E } < ( 1 + r ) b / ( 1 + 2 r )$ and $\partial p _ { D } ^ { * } / \partial p _ { E } < 0$ should hold. It suggests, in the agency model, the equilibrium e-reader price $p _ { D } ^ { * }$ typically moves in the opposite direction of e-book price $p _ { E } .$ This result is illustrated in Figure 2, which is drawn based on the same set of parameter values as in Figure 1.

We further investigate how changes in physical book cost affect the equilibrium e-book price in the agency model.

Proposition 5 The equilibrium e-book retail price $p _ { E } ^ { * }$ in the agency model (i) decreases in the publisher’s physical book cost $c _ { P } ~ ( i . e . , ~ \partial p _ { E } ^ { * } / \partial c _ { P } < 0 )$ and (ii) increases in the retailer’s physical book cost $c _ { F } \left( i . e . , \partial p _ { E } ^ { * } / \partial c _ { F } > 0 \right)$

The publisher’s decision on e-book price in the agency model is analogous to its decision on wholesale price in the wholesale model. A lower publisher’s physical book profit margin caused by her higher cost in publishing physical books will give the publisher incentives to increase e-book market share by reducing e-book price. On the other hand, when the cost for retailers to sell physical books increases, but the publisher’s profit margins for physical books and e-books remain the same, the publisher has the incentive to raise the e-book price in order to avoid cannibalizing the physical books market.

## Comparison of the Wholesale and the Agency Models

The two different pricing models should have implications on the price levels of e-books and e-readers, on the profits for publishers and retailers, and on the welfare for the consumers. In this section, we compare the wholesale and agency models and examine these implications.

As discussed before, the publisher decides the e-book wholesale price and the retailer decides both e-book and ereader prices in the wholesale model. In the agency model, the publisher decides the e-book price while the retailer decides the e-reader price. Let p<sup>W</sup><sub>E</sub><sup>\*</sup> and $p _ { E } ^ { A ^ { * } }$ be the equilibrium e-book retail prices under the wholesale and agency models respectively. Let $p _ { D } ^ { W ^ { * } }$ and $p _ { D } ^ { A ^ { * } }$ be the equilibrium e-reader prices for the wholesale and agency models respectively. We can prove the following results regarding the equilibrium prices under the two settings:

Proposition 6 When the retailer’s revenue share $r \_ <$ min{3/4, $( p _ { F } - w _ { F } + c _ { P } ) / p _ { F } \}$ , author’s royalty fee $c _ { A } \leq p _ { F } / 4 ,$ and $w _ { F } - c _ { P } < p _ { F } / 2$ for the publisher, (i) the equilibrium ebook retail price in the agency model is higher than that in the wholesale model $( i . e . , \stackrel { \textstyle ^ { - } } { p _ { E } ^ { w ^ { * } } } < p _ { E } ^ { 4 ^ { * } } )$ and (ii) the equilibrium e-reader price in the agency model is lower than that in the wholesale model $( i . e . , p _ { D } ^ { W ^ { * } } > p _ { D } ^ { A ^ { * } } )$

Please note that the condition $r { < } ( p _ { F } { - } w _ { F } { + } c _ { P } ) / p _ { F }$ is identical to that in Proposition 4 and it is quite mild as explained before. It is also reasonable to assume that the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage for e-books is less than 75 percent. Therefore, the condition $r <$ min $\{ 3 / 4 , ( p _ { F } - w _ { F } + c _ { P } ) / p _ { F } \}$ should be easily satisfied. The condition $c _ { \scriptscriptstyle A } \leq p _ { \scriptscriptstyle F } / 4$ on author royalty should also be satisfied as explained before. The condition $w _ { F } - c _ { P } < p _ { F } / 2$ suggests that the publisher’s wholesale price on a physical book minus its production cost is less than half of its retail price, which is reasonable since the wholesale price is usually half of the retail price in practice (Trachtenberg 2011a).

Proposition 6 suggests that after the publisher switches from the wholesale to the agency model, the publisher tends to set a higher e-book price and the retailer will then lower the ereader price. We plot Figure 3 to illustrate the price differences. The parameter values are the same as the ones used in Figure 1 and 2.

First, as we can see under the agency model, e-book price is higher and e-reader price is lower. Second, as the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage increases, e-book price increases while the e-reader price decreases under the agency model. Since an increase in the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage reduces the publisher’s profit, the publisher has to raise the ebook price to make up for the loss. Meanwhile, as the retailer gains more profit with a higher e-book price and the revenue sharing percentage, it can afford to lower the e-reader price in order to stimulate the demand for e-books.

Next, we compare the social welfare effects of the two pricing models. Our numerical analysis uses the same parameter values as the ones in Figure 1. Social welfare under the agency model is denoted by SW<sup>A\*</sup>and the social welfare under the wholesale model by $S \bar { W } ^ { * }$ . Total consumer surplus under the agency model is denoted by $C W ^ { * }$ and the total consumer surplus under the wholesale model by $C S ^ { W ^ { * } }$ . Business profit is defined as the sum of the retailer’s profit and the publisher’s profit. Total business profit in the agency model is denoted by $\pi _ { B } ^ { A ^ { * } }$ and the total business profit in the wholesale model by $\pi _ { B } ^ { W ^ { \ast } }$ . The comparisons are displayed in Figure 4.

In general, social welfare, which is the sum of total consumer surplus and total business profit, is lower in the agency model compared to that in the wholesale model $( \mathrm { i . e . , } S W ^ { A ^ { * } } { < } S W ^ { W ^ { * } } )$ Specifically, the total consumer surplus does not significantly change in the two models. When revenue sharing percentage r is low, the total consumer surplus is slightly higher in the agency model $( \mathrm { i . e . , } C { S ^ { d ^ { * } } } { > } \mathbf { C } \mathbf { S } ^ { W ^ { \bar { + } } } )$ . One reason for this is that the e-reader price is significantly lower (Table 1). Although the e-book retail price is moderately higher in the agency model, a significantly lower e-reader price makes consumers slightly better off in terms of overall surplus compared to that in the wholesale model. Furthermore, business profit is lower in the agency model (i.e., $\pi _ { B } ^ { A ^ { \ast } } < \pi _ { B } ^ { W ^ { \ast } } )$ , for which we provide more analysis using Figure 5.

Figure 5 provides more details on business profit. We can see that the publisher is worse off in the agency model although it has control of the retail price. Table 1 offers some explanation for this. As we can see from Table 1, profits from both physical books and e-books decrease in the agency model for the publisher. Lower physical book profit is due to the smaller physical book market size, which is a direct result of lower e-reader price. Although e-book market size increases, e-book purchase quantity for an average consumer drops because of higher e-book prices in the agency model. In comparison, the wholesale model is better off for the publisher. The main reason is that the pricing structure under the wholesale model works better for different customer segments. A high e-reader price could avoid cannibalization of the physical book channel by creating an adoption barrier for those customers who like physical books. At the same time, consumers who are enthusiastic about electronic reading are still going to buy e-readers despite the high price. A lower e-book price will stimulate their e-book consumption.

## Extension: Heterogeneous Consumer Demands

In this section, we relax the assumption that consumers have homogeneous demands for books and assume that consumers have different demand levels for books. After describing the model setup, we first analyze the two pricing models and then compare their results.

We assume there are two types of consumers, high type and low type, each with a different level of demand for books. We use $\theta _ { i } = \{ \theta _ { H } , \theta _ { L } \}$ with $\theta _ { H } > \theta _ { L }$ as demand coefficients. High type consumer purchase quantities for e-books and physical books are $q _ { E H }$ with $q _ { E H } = \theta _ { H } q _ { E }$ and $q _ { F H }$ with $q _ { F H } =$ $\theta _ { H } q _ { F }$ , respectively. Low type consumer purchase quantities for e-books and physical books are $q _ { E L }$ with $q _ { E L } = \theta _ { L } q _ { E }$ and $q _ { F L }$ with $q _ { F L } = \theta _ { L } q _ { F } ,$ , respectively. Given the same price, high type $( \theta _ { H } )$ consumers purchase more books than low type $( \theta _ { L } )$ consumers. We also denote the fraction of the high type consumers by α and the low type consumers by $( 1 - \alpha )$

In this model, we have a two-dimensional heterogeneity [0,1] $\times \ \{ H , L \}$ to characterize the consumer preference. The first [0,1] dimension represents consumer preference for e-books or physical books. As discussed in earlier sections, consumers who are located near 0 prefer electronic books while consumers who are located near 1 prefer physical books. The second dimension {H,L} represents consumer demand levels on books.

We also relax the assumption that a single e-reader is offered in the marketplace. With different types of consumers, it is natural that retailers offer different devices. The higher quality e-readers have higher resolution, more memory, and more functionalities. We have witnessed this type of practice by Amazon and Apple in the marketplace. We denote the price for higher quality e-readers as $p _ { D I }$ and the price for lower quality e-readers as $p _ { D 2 }$ . Without loss of generality, we normalize the quality level of higher quality e-readers as 1 and the quality level of lower quality ones as k with $0 < k < 1$ The net utility for consumers who choose higher quality ereaders is $U _ { E 1 } = \nu _ { E 1 } - t \alpha = \theta _ { i } ( b - p _ { E } ) ^ { 2 } / ( 2 m ) - p _ { D 1 } - t \alpha .$ . The net utility of choosing the lower quality e-reader is $U _ { E 2 } = \nu _ { E 2 } - t a$ $= \theta _ { i } ( b - p _ { E } ) ^ { 2 } / ( 2 m ) - p _ { D 2 } - t \alpha .$ The net utility of choosing a physical book is $U _ { F } = \nu _ { F } - t ( 1 - \alpha ) = \theta _ { i } ( b - p _ { F } ) ^ { 2 } / ( 2 m ) - t ( 1 - \alpha )$

The above utility functions incorporate both horizontal and vertical differentiations for consumers. The horizontal differentiation between e-books and physical books are based on the location model. The vertical differentiation is on high or low book consumption quantities. The incentive compatibility (IC) constraints that high demand consumers choose higher quality e-readers and low demand consumers choose lower quality e-readers are $U _ { E 1 } ( \theta _ { H } ) \ge \operatorname* { m a x } \{ U _ { E 2 } ( \theta _ { H } ) , U _ { F } ( \theta _ { H } ) \}$ and $U _ { E 2 } ( \theta _ { L } )$ \$ max $\{ U _ { E 1 } ( \theta _ { L } ) , U _ { F } ( \theta _ { L } ) \}$ , respectively. Consumers choose physical books if $U _ { F } \geq$ max $\{ U _ { E 1 , } U _ { E 2 } \}$ . We derive the indifference points on e-books and physical books for $\theta _ { H }$ and $\theta _ { L }$ respectively:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \alpha^ {*} (\theta_ {H}) = (t - p _ {D 1}) / (2 t) + \theta_ {H} (p _ {F} - p _ {E}) (2 b - p _ {E} - p _ {F}) / (4 t m) \\ \alpha^ {*} (\theta_ {L}) = (t - p _ {D 2}) / (2 t) + \theta_ {L} ((b - p _ {E}) ^ {2} k - (b - p _ {F}) ^ {2}) / (4 t m) \end{array}
$$

Thus, the e-book market size or demand function is $s _ { E H } =$ $a ^ { * } ( \theta _ { H } )$ for $\theta _ { H }$ and $s _ { E L } = a ^ { * } ( \theta _ { L } )$ for $\theta _ { L }$ . The demand for physical book $s _ { F H } = 1 - s _ { E H }$ for $\theta _ { H }$ and $s _ { F L } = 1 - s _ { E L }$ for $\theta _ { L }$ .

In the wholesale model, the retailer’s total profit is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \pi_ {R} = \alpha \left(\left((p _ {E} - w _ {E}) q _ {E H} + (p _ {D 1} - c _ {D 1})\right) s _ {E H} + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) q _ {F H} s _ {F H}\right) \\ + (1 - \alpha) \left(\left((p _ {E} - w _ {E}) q _ {E L} + (p _ {D 2} - c _ {D 2})\right) s _ {E L} + (p _ {F} - w _ {F} - c _ {F}) q _ {F L} s _ {F L}\right) \end{array}
$$

## Discussion

Our study has the following results:

(1) The complementary consumption between e-readers and e-books is the main reason for the retailer to set a low ebook price and a high e-reader price in the wholesale model.

(2) E-book price and e-reader price typically move in opposite directions under both the wholesale and the agency models.

(3) As the publisher’s cost of physical books rises, the ebook price should be lowered to grow the more profitable e-book market.

(4) E-book price is higher while e-reader price is lower in the agency model compared to their respective prices in the wholesale model.

(5) While total consumer surplus is slightly higher, overall business profit, especially publisher’s profit, is lower in the agency model.

The differences between the two models are primarily driven by two factors. First, the decision makers for e-book prices are different. In the wholesale model, the retailer determines the e-book retail price, while the publisher decides the e-book price in the agency model. Since different decision makers choose prices to maximize their respective profits, the equilibrium results are quite different. Second, the revenue sources for the two pricing models are different. In the wholesale model, the retailer gets her revenue from the markup over the wholesale price. In the agency model, the retailer gets her revenue from a fixed revenue sharing percentage of the e-book retail price. We have demonstrated that, even without sales uncertainty, the two pricing models lead to different equilibriums. For example, we find that e-book price is higher and social welfare is lower in the agency model.

![](/api/attachments/WC7KAXYC/fulltext/images/dd2d71788de74c769c511c8960687f03b7944555987c882202a592769010a775.jpg)

Our model has interesting implications to theory as well as to pricing policies regarding digital contents and devices. We can explain why a retailer such as Amazon.com would want to charge a low e-book price in the first place. Under the wholesale model, the optimal strategy for the retailer is to price e-books relatively low and make a profit from e-readers. The e-reader is the vehicle for the retailer to extract consumer surplus derived from using e-books. In this way, the retailer maximizes her overall profit. Meanwhile, the combined publisher and retailer profits are also higher compared to the agency model. This two-part tariff pricing structure essentially mitigates the traditional double marginalization problem in which the final retail price is too high and the overall demand is too low. The e-reader, which is required to consume e-books, is the critical component in the wholesale model.

When publishers switch from the wholesale model to the agency model, our research offers two interesting insights. First, a high e-book price in the agency model does not hinder the falling market share of physical books. The market share for physical books is lower in the agency model because of a low e-reader price. The retailer has the incentive to reduce ereader price in order to grow the e-book market since the retailer shares the revenue from e-book sales in the agency model. Second, a high e-book price under the agency model reduces consumption of e-books for average customers. Although the e-book market share increases because of a low e-reader price, e-book purchases from individual consumers decrease, which reduces the profit for the publisher after revenue sharing.

From our analysis, it is clear that the publisher’s decision to move from the wholesale model to the agency model is not optimal. Essentially, e-book price is set too high under the agency model. At the same time, it does not slow down the trend of growing electronic reading, because, in response to the publisher’s high e-book price, the retailer reduces the ereader price. This encourages more people to use e-books and further lowers market share for physical books. Meanwhile, the higher e-book price in the agency model lowers e-book consumption for individual customers, which has a negative effect on the publisher’s e-book profit after revenue sharing. In contrast, in the wholesale model, a high e-reader price and a low e-book price can be quite effective in segmenting the market. A high e-reader price helps to protect the physical book by creating an adoption barrier for those who like physical books. On the other hand, for consumers who are enthusiastic about electronic reading, they are willing to pay a high price for e-readers and a lower e-book price can increase e-book consumption for those consumers.

It will be interesting to explore ways to remedy the agency model. Given the low e-reader price to which consumers are accustomed, it is difficult to go back to the original model with high e-reader price. Instead, the publisher should focus on a combination of steps to reduce the price for e-books and enhance the value. First, the e-book price should be lowered in the long run. Although a lower e-book price could increase overall social welfare, it will further lower the publisher’s profit. Thus, a more sustainable strategy is to reduce both the e-book price and the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage. As a high retailer’s revenue sharing percentage distorts the retail price for e-books, lowering the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage gives the publisher incentives to reduce retail price. Second, in order to cut back the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage, which directly affects the retailer’s profit, the publisher has to find ways to compensate the retailer and help develop alternative revenue models. The publisher could enhance the value of e-books by providing additional content to the retailer to accompany the original e-book. This gives the retailer opportunities to capture the enhanced value of ebooks. One possibility is to encourage the retailer to innovate and develop more advanced, high-margin devices that can take advantage of the interactive digital content. The retailer could use differentiation strategy and target these devices to certain e-book customer segments.

## Conclusions and Future Research

In this paper, we study the pricing of e-books and e-readers. Our model setup has the following features. First, we consider that consumers buy e-readers for the purpose of reading more than one e-book. Thus, the prices for both ereaders and e-books not only affect the e-book market share, but also e-book consumption by each e-book customer. Second, we model the substituting effects between e-books and physical books. We also consider consumer demand heterogeneity over books in a model extension. Further, we use a game theoretical approach to analyze strategies for both the publisher and the retailer in two relevant pricing models that have been used in the marketplace. Our results have interesting and relevant implications to companies that sell ebook content and devices.

We analyze strategies under the wholesale and agency models. Under the wholesale model, we find that the optimal strategy for the retailer is to price e-books low and mark up ereaders. In this way, the retailer maximizes her overall profit. This result explains the phenomenon that retailers such as Amazon.com priced e-books low while charging a high price on Kindles under the wholesale model.

We find that the cost of physical books (e.g., printing, distributing, and storing books) affects e-book price under both the wholesale and the retail models. Physical book and e-book are two competing and substituting channels in providing content to consumers. In particular, if the publisher’s cost of physical books rises as a result of rising labor and material costs, the publisher’s profit margin of selling physical books will drop. Then, the publisher is better off shifting the focus from high cost physical books to the more profitable e-book market by lowering e-book price. In the long run, if we expect the relative cost benefit of e-books will continue and the profit margin of e-books is sustainable, pricing e-book at a correct level in order to encourage e-book consumption is strategically important. Our findings also suggest that e-reader price generally decreases e-book price. A lower e-book price in the wholesale model implies a higher e-reader price, while a higher e-book price is associated with a lower e-reader price.

We compare the equilibrium prices and social welfare of the wholesale and the agency models. We find the e-book price is higher while the e-reader price is lower in the agency model. Overall social welfare is lower in the agency model than that in the wholesale model. While total consumer surplus is slightly higher, largely because of a lower e-reader price, overall business profit is lower in the agency model. Our numerical analysis suggests that a higher e-book price causes the retailer to lower e-reader price, which leads to a smaller market share of physical books. In addition, a higher e-book price reduces consumption of e-books for average ebook customers. We have discussed ways to improve publisher profit and total social welfare from the current model. One possible avenue is for the publisher to both enhance the value of books and reduce the retailer’s revenue sharing percentage and the price for e-books.

There are several limitations of the study and future research can extend the current model in several directions. First, in this study, we model a publisher and a retailer. An extension of the study is to examine the strategies of retailers when they face competition. With competitive e-readers, it is interesting to examine retailers’ strategies on pricing as well as compatibility decisions. It is quite possible that competition also provides an incentive for a retailer to set a low e-book price. Second, it will be interesting to examine the long term price change of physical books.<sup>2</sup> We assume an exogenous physical book price in our model, which is a limitation of the study. Future research can examine, for example, how quality improvement of e-books and changing cost of distribution may affect physical book price. Third, we assume a demand function without uncertainties. When there are uncertainties in selling books, the model could lead to different equilibrium results. As discussed earlier, it is important for both the publisher and the retailer to innovate in order to satisfy the changing needs of consumers. With possible new devices and applications that can provide more value to customers, both the retailer and the publisher can explore strategies to capture the value and enhance the overall social welfare.
