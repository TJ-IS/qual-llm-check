## A B S T R A C T

Cellular phones have gained popularity in emerging and developing economies over the last two decades. In this study, we show that the phenomenon of falling prices over time is applicable to the difusion of prepaid mobile phones. This article extends the logistic difusion model to accommodate disadopters of the service category. Goodness of fit of the extended model is found superior to the original version, which does not consider disadoption. The empirical findings show that for the 12 countries considered in the analysis, the difusion speed and the disadoption rates are influenced by one marketing factor and three socioeconomic factors.

## 1. Introduction

Worldwide mobile phone use surpassed that of fixed telephone networks in 2002. It took a century for the world to accumulate the first billion fixed telephones, but only a decade or so to do the same with mobile phones. It is interesting to note that mobile phone usage has doubled worldwide since 2000, and the bulk of that growth came from emerging markets and developing economies [1].

Emerging markets are developing countries which exhibit rapid growth with a concurrent expansion of both middle-class and rich consumers, but which still possess a large segment of poor citizens. Examples include the BRIC countries (Brazil, Russia, India, and China), Mexico, Turkey, and South Africa [2]. Emerging markets now account for 65 % of the world’s population and 40 % of the world’s economic outputs. Multinationals see potential in these emerging markets as their home markets for goods and services become saturated [3].

While the International Monetary Fund (IMF) in its 2018 World Economic Outlook (WEO) [4] grouped both emerging economies and developing countries into one category, emerging economies may actually be considered a subset of developing countries and are typified by a rapid growth rate and a liberalized economic system [5]. While developed countries may share a liberalized free-market policy similar to an emerging economy, the latter difer from developed countries by exhibiting: lower GDP, lower per capita income, lower export diversification (e.g., oil exporters that possess a higher per capita GDP would not be classified as “developed,” as approximately 70 % of its exports are petroleum), a lower degree of integration into the global financial system, and a less advanced technological infrastructure [4,6]. Emerging markets are also characterized by a large proportion of their populations living and working in the informal economy (i.e., most consumers have low incomes, typically earn and spend on a daily basis, and operate beyond the reach of formal institutions such as banks and courts [7]).

A major route to success in emerging markets is bringing large numbers of non-users into the consumption fold. This can be a sig nificant challenge due to the negative impact of five unique characteristics of these markets: market heterogeneity, sociopolitical governance, unbounded competition, chronic resource shortages, and inadequate infrastructure [3]. In their critical review of the difusion literature, Peres et al. [8], expressed the need to undertake further difusion research related to emerging economies by writing, “the main body of difusion research rarely relates to difusion in emerging economies. The unique difusion patterns in those parts of the world have been cited as anecdotal evidence but have not been incorporated into difusion models. With the exception of a few papers (e.g. [9],), research has not addressed whether such patterns are country specific or abundant across emerging economies….”

Mobile phone usage is an especially important area of difusion research in emerging economies. “With more than three billion sub scribers around the world, mobile phones have out-difused virtually every prior technology… [10].” However, there is a curious degree of variation in how adoption has occurred in diferent emerging economies. Recent projections suggest that the world will continue to add mobile access faster than traditional land-lines (fixed-lines) [11,12].

The lack of individual credit ratings has led to an increased use of prepaid cell phone plans in emerging markets [10]. Further, cellphone adoption might be accelerated among the poor (who “leapfrog” to the new technology) as land-lines and physical banking alternatives are often not available [2].

Mead and Islam [13] reviewed empirical studies that dealt mostly with the difusion of mobile technology in developed countries. However, those studies treated mobile telephony as if it were a durable good. Unfortunately, in mobile telephony, a service customer can disadopt but later rejoin [14,15]. This is markedly diferent than a durable goods market. Further, due to the learning curve efect (e.g. [16],), it is widely known that prices fall as difusion of consumer durable in novations occur. However, the issue of the learning curve in subscrip tion services has been either ignored or assumed present without empirical support [17].

Hogan et al., [14] propose that when a customer disadopts, the firm loses not only that purchase (the direct efect) but also a brand advocate who is no longer recruiting future customers (the indirect efect). Therefore, prior models developed to investigate the difusion of dur able consumer innovations must be modified to accommodate the phenomenon of customers’ disadoption.

As each developing country has the potential to become an emerging market, any country that is not developed is considered an emerging market for the purposes of this research [18]. The present study seeks to examine the difusion of prepaid mobile telephony in emerging markets and highlight the potential implications of its results for global marketers and policy makers. Because the stated “big picture (the complete, overarching idea)” represents a complex whole composed of interrelated queries, systems thinking is seen as an adequate approach to pursue [19]. To guard against missing the big picture, Rust [20] suggests that a study like ours would include multiple goals represented by a series of interrelated research questions. To that end, the present study attempts to answer the following three key research questions:

(i) Following the learning curve, costs fall as difusion of consumer durable innovations occur. Is this phenomenon extendable to prepaid mobile telephony services?

(ii) How could the logistic difusion model for durable goods be adapted to accommodate customers’ disadoption for prepaid mobile telephony services?

(iii) What are the determinants of the difusion speed, and the disadoption rate within the prepaid mobile telephony market?

In addressing the above-mentioned key questions, the following secondary research questions will also be considered:

\- What is the impact of changes in the disadoption rate on perfor mance measures of the prepaid mobile telephony service firm?

\- What is the role of the prepaid mobile telephony’s cost learning curve on firm’s profitability?

\- Is the extended logistic difusion model for services superior to the original logistic difusion model for durable goods?

\- The above-mentioned key and secondary research questions have not been fully explored and their answers constitute the main theoretical and empirical contributions of this study to the information systems literature.

## 2.1. Learning curve for services

The organizational learning curve illustrates the increases in pro ductivity that are obtained as organizations gain production experience [21]. Standard Operations Management literature associates the learning curve phenomenon with both continuous improvement and quality management in processes [22]. Wright [23] observed that, in the production of airplanes, manufacturing time per unit decreased (at a constant rate) as quantity produced increased. Concurrently, loglinear organizational learning curves exhibiting an inverse relationship between productivity improvements and cumulative experience have been observed in a wide variety of industries [24].

The concept of declining costs and prices resulting from learning as expressed in the accumulated experience of a firm has been extensively developed and applied by the Boston Consulting Group [25] which was based on Arrow’s [26] “learning by doing.” Bass [16] empirically found that through learning, costs and prices for new consumer durables fall continuously, while demand first grows, and then declines as the market becomes saturated. The studies reviewed below also show that experience curves are present in service operations in both developed countries and emerging markets.

In developed countries, Darr et al., [27] evaluated the learning curve efects in a chain of pizza restaurants. They found that “learning by doing” led to significant reductions in unit cost. Goodale and Tunc [28] explored labor scheduling problems in service operations. They found that learning curve methodologies predominantly applied in manufacturing environments also performed well in customer contact environments for both part-time and full-time workers. Sturm [29] verified a cost reduction in the process of filling out forms, which ap proximated Wright’s [23] findings.

Chambers and Johnston [30] applied the learning curve to both organizational and operating unit levels. The authors found at both levels: i) “experience curves can be applied to great efect in high volume service organizations…” and ii) “how an experience curve might be used to monitor improvement and establish future cost-related performance objectives….”

Chen et al., [31] confirmed the presence of a dynamic learning effect in Japan’s mobile phone market. Their empirical analysis suggests the learning coeficient of the market dropped due to technology ob solescence but turned upwards upon the introduction of handsets with camera features.

Boone et al., [32] conducted an empirical study based on seven years of project data collected from an architectural engineering firm. The analysis showed that professional services display learning curves, which have been used to estimate the costs of consulting and tech nology implementation [33].

In the relevant emerging markets literature, the learning curve for services (predominantly confined to the energy sector) has led to cost reductions in Brazilian ethanol [34,35]. Further, cost reductions in renewable power generation in China have been associated with the learning curve [36,37], as well as in reducing the unit cost of both water pumping and renewable electricity generation in India [38,39].

In recognition of the continual global growth in prepaid mobile subscribers combined with falling prepaid prices [40], we assert that the learning curve phenomenon for services observed in developed and emerging economies is extendable to prepaid mobile telephony in emerging economies, giving rise to the following hypothesis:

H1. In emerging markets, the service cost per additional prepaid mobile phone subscriber is a decreasing function of the number of prepaid subscribers.

## 2.2. Prepaid cellular phones in emerging markets

Over the last two decades, mobile phone use in the developing world has increased to over one billion users [41]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, mobile subscriptions now exceed fixed-line subscriptions [42]. The developing world is skipping both fixed-line communication and desktop computers, and is instead adopting mobile phones. Relevant literature reviewed below demonstrates that socio-economic and marketing factors have the potential of afecting the difusion of prepaid mobile phones in emerging markets.

Many developing countries’ governments are focusing on extending telecommunication services into rural areas, as they seek to alleviate poverty and promote economic and societal connectivity growth [43]. Those authors identify five potential mechanisms through which mobile phones can provide economic benefits to consumers and producers. i) Mobile phones can improve access to, and the use of information, thereby reducing search costs. ii) Increased communication should improve firms’ productive eficiency by allowing them to better manage supply chains. iii) Mobile phones create new jobs in mobile-related services, thereby providing income-generating opportunities in rural and urban areas. iv) Mobile phones facilitate communication among members of social systems in response to shocks, thereby reducing households’ exposure to risk. v) Mobile phone-based applications and development projects will facilitate the delivery of financial, agricultural, health, and educational services, as in Rwanda where the use of mobile phones has allowed micro-entrepreneurs to create new contracts and to communicate with repeat customers [44].

As developing countries are resource constrained, their mobile phone users gain access to the service diferently from residents of developed countries. While the developed countries have a majority of subscriptions on contract, most subscriptions in developing markets are pre-paid. According to Kaplan [42], the pre-paid model is more accessible to consumers who often possess neither bank accounts nor the identity documentation required to enter into a contract. Thus, marketers have used pre-paid subscriptions as a quicker way to both launch and to connect customers.

Kalba [10] found that prepaid plans played an important role in stimulating the adoption of mobile phones in emerging markets. In 2012, an estimated 87 % of mobile subscriptions in emerging markets were pre-paid [45]. Esselaar and Stork [46] showed a correlation between prepaid mobile plans and lower per capita GNP. Further, lowincome users under 30 preferred prepaid plans, while users who operated businesses or who had higher incomes preferred contracts.

Bahadir et al., [47] studied seven emerging and seven developed markets accounting for 62 % and 58.3 % in terms of the global GDP and purchasing parity respectively in 2013 to provide evidence of how five country characteristics (market heterogeneity, sociopolitical governance, unbounded competition, chronic resource shortages, and inadequate infrastructure [3]) impact the relationship between marketing-mix variables and brand sales performance. The authors found that country-market characteristics moderate the relationship between the marketing-mix variables (advertising, product innovation, display, distribution, and price-and-brand sales performance) diferently, when it comes to emerging versus developed countries. While distribution and price have the largest impact in emerging and developed countries respectively, product innovation and advertising have significantly larger impacts in emerging markets relative to developed countries. To attenuate the negative impact of emerging market characteristics, Seth and Sisodia [48] propose the 4 A’s framework that focus on key desired customer outcomes.

i) Afordability – customers’ ability and willingness to pay

ii) Accessibility – extent to which customers can easily acquire and use a good/service

iii) Awareness – extent to which consumers know about the character istics of goods and services

iv) Acceptability – extent to which a good/service meets or exceeds the needs of consumers from a cultural and functional perspective.

Examples of strategies for achieving each $^ { \mathfrak { a } } \mathbf { A } ^ { \mathfrak { n } }$ in conjunction with the difusion of prepaid mobile phones in emerging markets are high lighted below.

Walton and Donner [49] showed that in low-income countries prepaid subscriptions are sold as data bundles allowing users to secure significantly lower rates, when sold in small increments [50]. This has enabled the poor to manage expenditures in an inexpensive, discrete manner accelerating widespread accessibility and adoption of mobile phones in emerging economies [51].

To make mobile phone use universally accessible, the distribution of handsets expanded to include electronic retailers, mass merchandisers, and kiosks, while the geographic reach of networks was increased. The industry rapidly accelerated its promotional eforts, thereby increasing awareness and boosting consumers’ negative perceptions. Public opinion improved further with the advent of smaller phones, more features (especially digital technology [48]), better voice quality, and greater network coverage. Finally, prior research on wireless telephone services found that in emerging markets, customer satisfaction is driven by perceived value (as customers are more sensitive to factors such as price and relative income stability [52]), while customer satisfaction in developed markets is driven by perceived quality.

The above discussion influenced by research findings in developed countries (e.g., [53–55]) touches on how some socio-economic factors (e.g., per capita GNP and income) together with marketing factors (e.g., price and distribution) influence the difusion of prepaid mobile phones. The current study extends this research by analyzing additional socio-economic factors and marketing indices giving rise to the fol lowing hypothesis:

H2. Socio-economic and marketing indices influence the difusion of prepaid mobile phones in emerging markets.

## 2.3. Difusion models

The difusion-of-innovation is defined as the process by which in novation is communicated through certain channels, over time, among the members of a social system [56]. The objective of a difusion model is to present, mathematically, the level of spread of an innovation among a set of prospective adopters in terms of the amount of time elapsed since the introduction of the innovation. Although several dif fusion models exist, four are reviewed below. They are the exponential difusion model ([57]), the S-shaped logistic difusion model ([58]), the Gompertz difusion model ([59]), and the Bass difusion model ([60]).

Defining the cumulative number of adopters at time t by $N _ { t }$ and the potential number of adopters for a given new technology by M, a model that attributes difusion to only innovators can be formulated as

$$
n _ {t} = d N _ {t} / d t = p (M - N _ {t}),\tag{1}
$$

where n stands for the number of new adopters at t. while $p$ is the coeficient of innovation. The solution of this first-order diferential equation is the exponential difusion model given by

$$
N _ {t} = M (1 - e ^ {- p t}), N _ {0} = 0\tag{2}
$$

(detailed derivation is found in [57]).

When a significant group of customers will only try a new technology based on positive word of mouth from early adopters, the related difusion model is

$$
n _ {t} = d N _ {t} / d t = q (N _ {t} / M) (M - N _ {t}),\tag{3}
$$

where q is a constant parameter. The solution of the above diferential equation produces the logistic difusion model (detailed derivation is found in [58])

$$
N _ {t} = M / [ 1 + e ^ {- (c + q t)} ],\tag{4}
$$

where c is a constant. Van den Bulte [61] shows that parameter q is a valid measure of difusion speed.

When expression (3) is modified to take the form

$$
n _ {t} = d N _ {t} / d t = q (N _ {t} / M) (l n M - l n (N _ {t})),\tag{5}
$$

the solution of the diferential equation brings about the Gompertz difusion mode

$$
N _ {t} = M e ^ {- e ^ {- (c + q t)}},\tag{6}
$$

where c is a constant (see Hendry [59] for an early application).

The Bass model [60] unifies models (1) and (3). Its related difer ential equation is

$$
n _ {t} = d N _ {t} / d t = (p + q (N _ {t} / M)) (M - N _ {t}),\tag{7}
$$

where p and q are termed the coeficients of innovation and imitation, respectively. The solution for $N _ { t }$ is

$$
N _ {t} = \mathrm{M} [ 1 - e ^ {- (p + q) t} ] / [ 1 + (\mathrm{q/p}) e ^ {- (p + q) t} ],\tag{8}
$$

where $N _ { O } = O .$

Meade and Islam [13] reviewed 25 empirical studies that deal with the difusion of mobile technologies. All 25 studies employ one or more of the difusion models shown above. A major shortcoming of such studies is that they treat subscription services (mobile phones in this case) as if they were durable goods. Customers’ disadoption is ignored. Libai, et al. [62], are probably the first to incorporate customers’ disadoption into the Bass difusion model [60]. Libai, et al. [62], conclude that ignoring customers’ disadoption would result in obtaining biased estimates for the parameters $M , p ,$ and a of the Bass diffusion model.

This article develops and employs an extended logistic difusion model to analyze the difusion of pre-paid mobile phone subscribers while incorporating customers’ disadoption. To the authors’ knowledge, this model has not been employed in previous difusion studies related to emerging markets.

Conceptually, at any time t, the market potential M is divided into two segments. The first segment represents the number of subscribers $N _ { t } .$ The second segment, M- $N _ { t } ,$ represents the number of individuals who have not yet adopted the service (the untapped market). During the interval $( \mathfrak { t } , \mathfrak { t } + 1 )$ , new numbers of subscribers n<sub>t</sub> adopt the service, and thus the untapped market would accordingly be reduced by the same amount. During the same interval, δ N of subscribers will defect and join the untapped market, reducing the number of subscribers who would renew to (1-δ) N . Thus, the number of subscribers at time $\mathbf { t } + \mathbf { 1 }$ becomes $N _ { t + 1 } = ( 1 { - } \delta ) \ N _ { t } + n _ { t }$ and the untapped market at $\mathbf { t } + 1$ becomes ${ \cal M } \ : - \ : { \cal N } _ { t } - { n } _ { t } + \ : \delta \ : { \cal N } _ { t } = { \cal M } \ : - \ : { \cal N } _ { t + 1 } .$ . Consequently, the number of subscribers at time $\mathbf { t } + 2$ becomes $N _ { t + 2 }$ and the untapped market becomes M- $N _ { t + 2 }$ . As the difusion and defection processes move forward over time, the ultimate number of subscribers approaches a ceiling $\overline { { N } } <$ M (see section 3.1).

To consider customers’ disadoption explicitly, the diferential equation of the logistic difusion model (3) is modified as:

$$
n _ {t} = d N _ {t} / d t = (1 - \delta) q (N _ {t} / M) (M - N _ {t}) - \delta N _ {t}, 0 \leq \delta <   1.\tag{9}
$$

Here, $n _ { t }$ is the rate of change of subscribers at time $t , N _ { t }$ is the number of subscribers at time t, M is the market potential, q is the coeficient of imitation, and δ is the disadoption rate. The extended logistic difusion model (9) is a modified version of difusion model (3). The extended logistic difusion model (9) conceptualizes the growth rate in the number of subscribers as the resultant of two opposing processes: (i) the acquisition of new subscribers and (ii) the defection of current subscribers. The first process is represented by difusion model (3), whereas the second process is depicted by the last negative term included in the extended logistic difusion model (9). It is further as sumed, as in Libai, et al., [62], that only current users (non-disadopters) spread positive communication about the service by word-of-mouth. Therefore, the level of word-of-mouth promotion by retained customers remained the same (q), but its efective impact is reduced due to dis adoption from [ ]/qN M to [(1-δ)qN ] /M . As disadopters consider readoption, the remaining market potential $M - N _ { t }$ is not afected by the disadoption process. Thus, the following hypothesis is introduced:

H3. In emerging markets, the goodness of fit related to the extended logistic difusion model (9) is superior to the original logistic difusion model (3) when fitted to prepaid mobile phone subscription difusion data.

## 3. An extended logistic difusion model for subscription services

In this section, a logistic difusion model for subscription services that explicitly considers customers’ disadoption is developed, and its main properties highlighted. The impact of changes in its parameters on the performance measures of difusion speed, equilibrium number of subscribers, and long-term profit rate are also examined.

## 3.1. A difusion model for subscription services

The essential diference between subscription services and durable goods is that services are intangible (cannot be seen, handled, smelled, etc.), heterogeneous (making mass production dificult), perishable, and are produced and consumed simultaneously (lack of transport ability) (Zeithaml and Bitner [63]). Further, unlike the difusion of durable goods, the growth of a subscription service depends on both the number of adopters in each period and the number of subscribers who renew the subscription from previous periods.

$$

$$

$$

$$

## 4.2. Estimating the learning curve model

In the current study, we use nonlinear regression to estimate the learning curve related to prepaid mobile subscription phone services.

$$
P M P P I _ {t} = C _ {1} N _ {t} ^ {- \mu},\tag{15}
$$

where $C _ { 1 }$ and $\mu$ are parameters, $N _ { t }$ is the number of prepaid subscribers in a specific country in year t, and PMPPI is a prepaid mobile phone pricing index. The pricing index is operationally defined, using avail able data, as the geometric mean of a connection fee index (I ) and a prepaid price per local call per minute index (P ) given by

I =[(connection fee in year t/CPI in year t)/(connection fee in year1/CPI in year1)], and

P = the geometric mean of $P _ { 1 t }$ and $P _ { 2 t }$ both adjusted for CPI given by

$P _ { 1 t } =$ [mobile-cellular prepaid price of a one minute local call (of peak, on net) in year t /CPI in year t]/[mobile-cellular prepaid price of a one minute local call (of peak, on net) in year 1/CPI in year1], and

$\boldsymbol { P } _ { 2 t } ~ =$ [mobile-cellular prepaid price of a one minute local cal (peak, on net) in year t /CPI in year t]/[mobile-cellular prepaid price of a one minute local call (peak, on net) in year 1/CPI in year1], so that $I _ { 1 }$ $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { \Psi } = P _ { 1 } = P M P P I _ { 1 } = \mathbf { \Psi } 1 } \end{array}$

Similar to previous empirical studies related to the difusion of consumer durables and mobile telephony $( \mathbf { e } . g . , [ 3 1 , 6 0 ] )$ , PMPPI is used as a surrogate variable for marginal cost.

The nonlinear model (15) is estimated using the SAS nonlinear Marquardt method. The results are shown in Table 3.

Table 3 reveals that the data fit the learning curve (15) for all countries except Morocco, the Seychelles and the UAE. An alternative model $P M P P I _ { t } = C _ { 1 } ~ e ^ { - \alpha N _ { t } }$ included in Grosse et al., [66] appears to fit the data better for those three countries. Nonlinear estimates for the alternative model are also reported in Table 3. We report mean square errors (MSE) as a measure of fit, since traditional measures such as $R ^ { 2 }$ are not appropriate for non-linear regression modeling [67]. It should also be noted that the graphs of $I _ { t } , P _ { 1 t }$ and $P _ { 2 t }$ (not reported) are decreasing over time, thus supporting hypothesis H1.

## 4.3. Estimating alternative difusion model specifications

We estimate a discrete analogue of the pre-paid mobile extended logistic difusion model (9) incorporating disadoption with a dynamic market potential (M ) using the SAS Marquardt method.

$$
\mathrm {N_ {t} - N_ {t - 1} = (1- \delta) q (N_ {t - 1} / M_ {t})(M_ {t} - N_ {t - 1}) - \delta N_ {t - 1}},\tag{16}
$$

where $M _ { t }$ represents the market potential at time t given by the total number of mobile phone subscribers in the country in year t (prepaid users plus postpaid subscribers).

For comparison purposes, we also estimate a discrete difusion model (3) that does not incorporate disadoption with a dynamic market potential:

$$
\mathrm {N_ {t} - N_ {t - 1} = q(N_ {t - 1} / M_ {t})(M_ {t} - N_ {t - 1}).}\tag{17}
$$

It is noted that difusion model (17) represents a “reduced model” (designated by R) of the full difusion model (16), designated by $\mathrm { F } ,$ obtained by setting $\delta \ = \ o$ in (16). To compare the performance of difusion models (16) and (17), a partial F-test statistic, $\mathrm { F _ { p a r t i a l } } ,$ (Kutner et al., [67]) is computed using the following formula:

$$
\mathrm{F} _ {\text { partial }} = \left[ \left(\mathrm{SSE} _ {\mathrm{R}} - \mathrm{SSE} _ {\mathrm{F}}\right) / \left(\mathrm{df} _ {\mathrm{R}} - \mathrm{df} _ {\mathrm{F}}\right) \right] / \left(\mathrm{SSE} _ {\mathrm{F}} / \mathrm{df} _ {\mathrm{F}}\right),\tag{18}
$$

where $S S E _ { R }$ and $S S E _ { F }$ are the sum of squares of the errors related to the reduced and full models, respectively. The quantities $d f _ { R }$ and $d f _ { F }$ are the error degrees of freedom for the reduced and full difusion models, respectively. The estimation results for models (16) and (17) are summarized in Table 4.

The results depicted in Table 4 reveal that the extended logistic difusion model (16) developed in this article is superior to Mansfield’s [58] logistic difusion model (17) as the partial F-values shown in the last column are larger than their tabulated counterparts at the 0.05 level of significance. Therefore, hypothesis H3 is supported.

As we have 12 countries and as the number of explanatory variables in the pool is large, we will adopt the procedure employed earlier by Helsen, et al., [70], followed later by Dwyer, et al. [71], and others. That is, regressing the diference in the estimated difusion speed (Δ S<sup>ˆ</sup> ) and the diference in the estimated disadoption rate (Δ <sup>ˆ</sup>) between two countries, on the diference of the respective explanatory indicators. This procedure enlarges the efective sample size from 12 to 66 [(12 × 11)/2]. The models to be estimated for two countries, ℓ, m, and P explanatory indicators, take the following form:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta \widehat {S} & = \widehat {S} _ {l} - \widehat {S} _ {m} = B 0 + B 1 (X 1 \ell - X 1 m) + B 2 (X 2 \ell - X 2 m) + \dots \\ & + B P (X P \ell - X P m) + U \ell m, \end{array}\tag{19}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta \hat {\delta} = & \hat {\delta} _ {l} - \hat {\delta} _ {m} = \gamma 0 + \gamma 1 (\mathrm{X} 1 \ell - \mathrm{X} 1 \mathrm{m}) + \gamma 2 (\mathrm{X} 2 \ell - \mathrm{X} 2 \mathrm{m}) + \dots \\ & + \gamma \mathrm{P} (\mathrm{XP} \ell - \mathrm{XPm}) + \mathrm{V} \ell \mathrm{m}, \end{array}\tag{20}
$$

Note that the number of observations n (sample size) difers from one country to the other. The quantities $B _ { O }$ through $B _ { P } ,$ and $\gamma _ { 1 }$ through $\gamma _ { \mathrm { { P } } }$ are parameters to be estimated. The quantities $U _ { \ell m }$ associated with Eq. (19) are the usual error terms assumed to be independent and identically, normally distributed of a zero mean and constant variance. The error terms, $V _ { \ell m s }$ in Eq. (20) are assumed to have the same properties, but could be correlated with the errors $U _ { \ell \mathrm { m } }$ (this situation poses no estimation problems). Expressions (19) and (20) can be estimated separately using ordinary least squares (OLS) as the explanatory variables are the same [72]. The difusion speed in a country j is computed upon the estimation of (16) as $\widehat { S } _ { j } = \widehat { q } _ { j } ( 1 - \widehat { \delta } _ { j } ) - \widehat { \delta } _ { j } .$ Based on the correlation matrix (Table 6), a factor analysis using principle components analysis (followed by orthogonal varimax rotation) is employed. The variable PPGR is only significant in the difusion speed model and its parameter possesses a negative sign in the expected direction. ## 5.1. Summary and contribution

This study shows that the phenomenon of falling prices over time, following the learning curve apparent in new consumer durables, is applicable to prepaid mobile phone services in emerging markets (section 4.2). We extend the logistic difusion model of Mansfield [58] to accommodate disadopters who join the untapped market (section 3.1). A related sensitivity analysis of how a change in the disadoption rate impacts i) the service difusion speed, ii) the ultimate number of service subscribers, and iii) firm profitability, has been performed (section 3.2.1). We find a reduction in the disadoption rate would result in greater profits if service cost learning was present, rather than absent (section 3.2.2), and that the goodness of fit of the extended logistic difusion model is superior to the original version that did not consider disadoption (section 4.3). The empirical findings show that for the 12 emerging markets and developing economies considered in this ana lysis, difusion speed and the disadoption rate are not afected by the World Bank’s classification of countries (Rouvenin [68]), but are in fluenced by: one marketing factor (prepaid pricing) and three socioeconomic factors (country wealth, urban concentration, and literacy level). More specifically, prepaid pricing and country wealth are both found to afect the difusion speed and the disadoption rate. Finally, while urban concentration is found to afect only the difusion speed, the literacy level will afect only the disadoption rate (section 4.4).

The above summary highlights the contribution of the study by answering the key and secondary research questions depicted in the introduction section.

## 5.2. Implications

Although this study’s results are preliminary, there are potential implications for global marketers and policy makers.

First, the difusion speed of mobile telephony should be accelerated by making the service more afordable and functionally more efective. This can be accomplished through the development of low-cost handsets bundled with digital services, and by the improvement in the quality of service (Chen et al., [31]), both of which would enhance the learning curve. In India, Nokia captured nearly 60 % of the rapidly growing mobile phone market, using the company’s most successful product, the simple, but rugged Nokia 1100, which was sold for \$54. This practical phone is designed for rural conditions, and it includes a dust cover, slip-free grip, and a built-in flashlight (Sheth, and Sisodia [48]).

Projected growth in GNP, international trade, and improvements in the literacy level are very promising for several emerging countries. Demographic trends indicate most of the growth in world urban population over the next decade will be in such countries [7]. Given our findings (e.g., expression (10)), economic and demographic trends would indicate a significant boost to the penetration potential of new mobile technologies in emerging markets, which should be of interest to potential service providers.

Service providers who were successful in developing afordable and useful mobile technologies in emerging markets can upgrade these products for the developed world. This process of reverse innovation can create competitive advantage through lower prices and value added services that target specific segments in developed markets [74]. Many older consumers in the 35–55 age group in European countries are often intimidated by unused features and capabilities on their mobile phones. Furthermore, approximately 45 million US consumers (like many citizens of developing countries) cannot aford a bank account [48].

Except for the marketing mix factor of price, business managers and foreign investors have relatively little control over the three socioeconomic factors that afect the difusion speed and the disadoption rate. Such socioeconomic variables, however, would be considered in evaluating diverse emerging markets when performing a sensitivity analysis with respect to their projected trends (perhaps along the guidelines depicted in section 3.2).

From a theory-building perspective, steps should be taken to explore new product development, research directions, and to generate an inventory of propositions geared specifically to emerging markets. The recent contributions of Mahajan and Banga [75], Prabhu et al. [7], and Sinha and Sheth [76] represent notable examples along this guideline.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study is exploratory in nature and thus has inherent limitations. Given that, this study primarily examines the association of relative diferences in difusion speeds, customers’ disadoption rates, and socioeconomic measures among 12 emerging markets related to prepaid mobile telephony, the studied subscription service and difusion time horizons are suficient to meet the exploratory objective of this study. However, generalizing the findings of this study to other subscription services and countries should be exercised with caution. Future research should examine a wider range of subscription service innovations and time spans on a global scope.

Our extended logistic difusion model incorporates disadoption into the Mansfield’s (1961) difusion model. Libai, et al., [62] extend (in essence) the Bass difusion model [60] to incorporate the same. Future research would extend the Gompertz difusion model together with other difusion models by the incorporation of disadoption into such difusion models. Comparison of the forecasting accuracy of such diffusion models for a variety of subscription services represents a plau sible avenue for future research.

This study uses a two-step approach in its empirical investigation (as does most cross-product and cross-country difusion research (e.g., [70,71,76]). An alternative direction for future research is to use a single-step approach in which all innovations are pooled and their difusion parameters are constrained to vary according to some parametric distribution (e.g. [61],). As Gatignon, et al. [53], advocate, such an approach (generally) is more eficient than a two-step approach. However, we suggest caution as single-step approaches often introduce other problems (e.g., many of the convergence issues about parameter stability are exacerbated as the models become increasingly complex and nonlinear ([77]).

Our sample of countries does not include BRIC (Brazil, Russia, India, and China) or developed countries because data related to their prepaid subscriptions were unavailable to the authors. The lack of available data on the difusion of technological innovations in general and for emerging markets in particular has been recognized by several authors (e.g., [78–80]). Obtaining access to mobile phone coverage and usage data, even at aggregate levels, is notoriously dificult and often bound by strict rules of nondisclosure and privacy concerns [43]. However, given the empirical findings reported in section 4.3 (when the countries were classified into two groups), the incorporation of BRIC countries’ difusion data is hypothesized not to change our conclusions. Empirical validation of such an assertion is considered a plausible direction for future research.

Developed countries do not face the same hurdles of market heterogeneity, socio-political governance, unbounded competition, chronic shortages of resources, and inadequate infrastructure [3] as do undeveloped countries. It would therefore be interesting to examine whether, on average, developed and developing countries difer in difusion speed and disadoption rates. Also, the dificulties encountered in collecting marketing-mix-data in a large number of countries have limited their number in this study to only one – service price.

Future researchers might also examine whether national culture afects difusion speed or dispadoption rates. Unfortunately, Botswana, Maldives, and the Seychelles all lack measures of cultural dimensions, while the cultural similarities of seven Arab countries (Algeria, Egypt, Morocco, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and UAE) and the two West African (Sub-Saharan African) countries (Benin and Senegal) have prevented the authors from pursuing this research direction [81].

Future research may investigate the timing of new innovations as they are introduced to diferent countries. That study should also in clude national cultures [82], and specific marketing-mix-variables (advertising, access, number of competitors in each market) [83].

Finally, research in the future may explore the impact of disruptive innovation in mobile telephony (e.g., growth of internet speed / multigenerational difusion models) on the disadoption rate (Barnes [84], Gruber & Koutroumpis [85]) together with the dynamic optimization of related models [86].
