# Economic sustainability of closed loop supply chains: A holistic model for decision and policy analysis

Sudip Bhattacharjee ⁎, Jose Cruz

Department of Operations and Information Management, School of Business, University of Connecticut, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 12 March 2014 Received in revised form 6 March 2015 Accepted 25 May 2015 Available online 4 June 2015

Keywords: Closed-loop supply chain Economic sustainability Environmental sustainability System dynamics Product design End of life returns

## a b s t r a c t

We develop an end-to-end model of a closed-loop supply chain (CLSC), and identify the systemic decision making required for economic viability of participants in this chain. Economic viability is a key ingredient for environmentally sustainable behavior and policies, and our decision making framework includes producers, refurbishers and recyclers. We model the lifecycle of a consumer electronics product to examine the complex interactions among different participants and their decisions. Using data from recyclers and marketing and supply chain literature, we find that i) sustainable CLSC policies require a well-calibrated return rate among types of products (new and refurbished) and consumers (innovators and imitators): ji) a sustainable return policy increases sales significantly for both types of customers; and iii) product design that improves refurbishability, and marketing efforts that create perceived differentiation between products and at the same time social awareness and acceptability for refurbished products, is critical for economic sustainability of refurbishers and recyclers. Our model can be used at a company or industry level to conduct cost–benefit scenarios for all participants of the CLSC, and make decisions based on systemic value. It can also be used to create public policies and incentives to reward companies that meet these benchmarks, for sustained economic and environmental benefits

## 1. Introduction

Today, the life cycle of many consumer electronic products (e.g., cell phones, computers) is becoming shorter [26]. Cutting-edge products can quickly become obsolete or outdated with the introduction of new ones. Therefore, millions of products in good working condition are replaced every year and in many cases laid to waste. These can create new business opportunities for refurbished products on secondary markets; on the other hand, create a global environmental concern due to the growing amount of electronic waste (e-waste) generated. Gartner [22] estimated that more than 120 million PCs will be refurbished, remarketed and reused over the next five years (2012–2016) in the global secondary market. The Environmental Protection Agency estimates that “…recycling 1 million cell phones can recover about 24 kg (50 lb) of gold, 250 kg (550 lb) silver, 9 kg (20 lb) palladium, and more than 9000 kg (20,000 lb) copper” [17]. At 2013 metal prices, that is over 1.45 million USD. In 2010 the U.S. got rid of 2.4 million tons of e-waste, of which, only 17.7% was recycled [16]. The rest was trashed in landfills or incinerators. Electronics is a growing component of waste; however the recycling rate is about 15% only [44].

Under such scenarios of increasing waste, businesses need to include decision strategies for environmental sustainability. This includes higher rates of recycling and refurbishing of older products. Legislation in the United States as well as in Europe and Japan has refocused attention on recycling for management of wastes and, specifically, e-wastes (see e.g. [1]). Consequently, it is imperative for businesses to take a holistic view of their supply chain that covers the entire product life-cycle from design to end of life. Products that cannot be refurbished need to be responsibly recycled; business must also consider the second-hand market value of their products as they design the products. Consumers' perceived value of refurbished products impacts their decisions to buy such products, which can affect the sales and end of life management of the new product [40]. Product design can affect the cost of refurbishing and recycling [44]. Taken together, legislative decisions and consumer perceptions can lead to increased costs for higher environmental sustainability.

Here we develop a methodology to study the economic impact of environmentally sustainable operations, and identify economically beneficial policies and decision making. Only those technologies, processes and products that are economically viable will be long term winners. Although extensive study exists in supply chain management research, there is a lack of a holistic approach that focuses on decisions made on the entire supply chain from an economic and environmental sustainability perspective. Such research would combine the forward and reverse logistics of the supply chain [2]. Importantly, a holistic decision making approach needs to take into consideration the economic incentives of all participants in the closed-loop supply chain (new product sellers, refurbishers and recyclers, in addition to consumers) to implement sustainable policies. The viability and business decisions of one determine the profitability and sustainability of other participants, creating a critical and complex chain of impact [21,67,68,70]. Hence, it is important to model these decision parameters in an integrative manner, with a focus on all supply chain participants. In other words, recycling and refurbishing rates may need to improve for environmental sustainability; however for economic sustainability, which is the true driver of decision making for firms, all participants in the supply chain need to be profitable for the “closed-loop” to function. Our research provides a path towards evaluating environmentally beneficial economic scenarios. Research and current events also suggest that company actions to improve social responsibility and resultant consumer awareness can improve goodwill and firm value [48,59,60]. This can be coupled with the benefits from increasing market size and related economies where a product seller may be profitable.

We contribute by bridging the above-mentioned existent gap in the literature and develop a holistic model of a closed-loop supply chain (CLSC). We focus on a type of product (e.g. mobile phone) that has high initial value and short lifecycle — a potent combination that creates significant e-waste with low recycling rates, short lifecycles and low values for refurbished products. These products use precious metals that have significant geo-strategic importance for companies and countries involved [7,13]. Such products also cause high cannibalization within themselves. In particular, our research focuses on the dynamics among the different participants of the closed-loop, namely the product manufacturer and its product design and marketing strategies, consumers and their product preferences, including length of product ownership, and end of life (EOL) product return options that impact refurbishing and recycling partners at the downstream of the supply chain. Typically, EOL return options and incentives are not offered or advertised by most electronic product manufacturers. This is in contrast with warranty period return mechanisms, which are quite well established, both for new and refurbished products. Arguably, warranty returns are a small component of the CLSC, and EOL returns hold significant potential. Specifically, we provide insights to the following questions: (1) what is the impact of EOL return rates on economic sustainability?, (2) what is the impact of premature returns, which are typically before EOL occurs?, (3) what are the impacts of price and product depreciation on the sales of new and refurbished products?, and (4) what is the impact of refurbishable product design on closing the supply chain loop? We find, predictably, that higher product depreciation (perceived value) of refurbished products increases its price differential with new products, thus lowering the prospects for refurbishers. While refurbishable design improves their prospects and helps to sustain the CLSC, it creates pricing pressures for the new product manufacturer. To balance these opposing forces, we find that a diversied product market (new and refurbished) is sustainable only at EOL return rates substantially greater than zero. This strongly suggests that explicit EOL return policies and decisions need to be implemented, which are typically absent in the market for electronic products. Contrary to perception, both new product and refurbisher profits can be significantly increased by increasing planned EOL product returns. As a sustainable byproduct, recycling rates and revenues can also be improved significantly, which improve both economic and envi ronmental metrics. A profitable operation for each business stakeholder (new product manufacturer, refurbisher, and recycler) in the chain is essential for the closed-loop to be economically – and environmentally – sustainable, and we focus on identifying the return levels, price, product depreciation and design decisions that impact and sustain such processes. Our research has a large number of applications, including tracing the lifecycle of a product and associated economics, and the manufacture, recycling and remarketing decisions of industrial products such as aircraft engines and medical devices, among others [11,58].

## 2. Literature review

Closed-loop supply chain (CLSC) has been a theme of much research. Guide and Wassenhove [34] argued that CLSC research needed to shift from individual activities in the reverse supply chain to interdisciplinary research focus. Iglin and Gupta [36] concluded that reverse logistics literature is dominated by the studies on location–allocation models, and remanufacturing systems are often analyzed by considering only one specific operations management issue. Most CLSC research focuses on different components of the loop. Substantial research exists on the capture of value remaining in products at the end of a products life through remanufacturing [20,27–30,35], and on design considerations for product recovery networks [6,12,19,21,38,68]. For example, Jayaraman et al. [38] developed a location model for remanufactureable products and grounded the model with information from a mobile telephone remanufacturer. Krikke et al. [39] considered alternatives for the design of a reverse logistics network for photocopiers. The impact of product design and its influence on the degree to which a product can be reused, remanufactured, recycled, incinerated or disposed of has also been a theme of research [25,46,47,63].

In terms of business policy, Guide et al. [28,30] and Guide and van Wassenhove [31–34] have consistently promoted the importance of business perspective to make CLSC attractive to managers and decision makers, and focused on profitable value recovery from returned products. Wells and Seitz [73] proposed business models and a typology of CLSC, where business model should be redesigned and placed at the heart closed-loop systems. Wei and Zhao [74] proposed an optimal pricing decision problem of a fuzzy CLSC with retail competition, associated with customer demands, remanufacturing cost, and collection cost.

We integrate the individual activities and develop a holistic model of a CLSC of a consumer electronic good, and analyze the economic payoff for all partners. We incorporate a dynamic model for the overall CSLC for a product with two types of consumer (innovators and imitators) buying either a new or refurbished product. Each component of the model is “derived from” existing research — i.e. “we stand on the shoulder of giants”. For example, marketing and pricing models exist for demand modeling and diffusion of new products in the marketplace; operations models have been proposed for logistics management; product design models suggest the lifecycle and associated costs of new and refurbished products; and consumer behavior research provide guidance for the attractiveness of new versus refurbished products, and the impact of pricing and product design decisions. Each sub-process area is well-researched and contains mathematically robust and wellunderstood formulations. The overall CLSC presents a complex and interrelated decision making problem that involves multiple shared resources, several areas of expertise from design, marketing, operations and strategy, and potentially non-linear relationship patterns among different elements of the chain. Hence optimizing the different components of the chain may not provide a generalizable understanding of the dynamics of the overall system. While the interaction of a small number of components of the supply chain has been analytically modeled and solved in previous research, our approach merges several analytical models from existing literature into a comprehensive dynamic model. This allows us to explore complex impacts of strategic policy decisions on the profitability and sustainability of the overall CLSC.

To investigate this complex and interrelated model of wellarticulated sub-processes, we implement a system dynamics (SD) approach to examine the dynamics of the problem space and generate policy insights for managing the overall closed-loop chain. This methodology has been applied to various business policy and strategy problems [15,43,50,51,54,55,64,65], manufacturing and supply chains [41,66] and specifically in CLSC [23,24,42,71,75].

Georgiadis and Besiou [24] studied CLSC system behavior using SD approach for sustainability in electrical and electronic equipment closed-loop supply chains. Kumar and Yamaoka [42] applied system dynamics approach to study CLSC for the Japanese automotive industry, and argue that export of Japanese used cars to secondary markets may not be sustainable in the long run. However, they did not address how sustainability is impacted when new, refurbished, and recycled products jointly affect one another. Vlachos et al. [71] proposed a SD model for dynamic capacity planning of remanufacturing, taking into account not only economic but also environmental issues, while Georgiadis and Athanasiou [23] studied the impact of two-product joint lifecycles on capacity planning of remanufacturing networks an extension from Georgiadis' work under single product. Winkler [75] recommended the establishment of closed-loop production systems in a supply chain level by introducing the concept of sustainable supply chain networks.

## 3. A dynamic model

We model a CLSC of a high value consumer electronic product with a short lifecycle, such as a mobile smartphone. Conceptually, a new product manufacturer produces a product that is first adopted in the market by market leaders (innovators) and subsequently by followers (imitators). The new product stays in the market for a period of time, when some consumers may return the product. Some returns are reprocessed and brought back into the market as refurbished products, which may be preferred by some imitators over the new product, depending on market conditions (product, pricing, etc.). Other returns may be processed for recycling, where components are either broken down to their elemental states and resold, or it goes into landfill (Fig. 1).

Each of these sub-process areas presents a complex set of interrelated problems and decisions, which can usually be taken in isolation and solved to optimality. The research objective here is to study the integrative impact of pricing, design and promotion decisions on the revenues of new product manufacturer, refurbisher and recycler; and the ratio of new and refurbished products in the market for profitable and sustainable business of all supply chain partners. We focus on understanding the overall systemic decisions necessary for economic viability of all the business participants in the chain.

The parameters and decision variables of the model are described in Table 1 [3,11]. Note that by definition, innovators buy new products only, while imitators may buy new or refurbished products [3]. Refurbished products have a depreciated value to consumers, defined as δ. The “end of life” (EOL) can be defined as the designed end of life of a product, or the end of a contract period, or other timelines specific to a given product. Consumers may continue to use the product beyond the EOL (e.g. if they do not want to upgrade at the end of a contract period). Returns before the defined EOL is termed “return before end of life” (RBEOL), and apply to new and refurbished products.

The reverse supply chain of refurbishing is also well-modeled (see [11]), where only imitators choose between new and refurbished products. While warranty returns are well-understood in the industry, RBEOL and EOL return strategies are neither well-developed nor advertised to consumers of most electronic products. Hence a major focus here lies in understanding the impact of return strategies on the system's economics. It is important to note that returns can occur for various reasons — technical, social, individual, and may be induced by explicit incentives. Our focus is on estimating whether systemic return rates would enable an overall economically sustainable market — an important yet little known market metric. This determination has significant implications for product policy and industry regulatory purposes. The solid lines in Fig. 2 signify decisions that can be impacted explicitly by incentives, product design or marketing initiatives, and are the decision variables presented in Table 1. Defining incentives for different products in diverse markets to achieve target return rates need careful field experiments, and is a rich empirical research area.

## 3.1. Market mechanism

The market mechanism captures the progression of market penetration of new and refurbished products through a diffusion model [3], where prices of new $\left( { { p } _ { n } } \right)$ and refurbished products (p ) and consumers' perceived difference between new and refurbished product (δ) dictate the diffusion process. The diffusion process is driven by innovator adoption rate, imitator adoption rate [3,11] and potential market size for innovators and imitators. As in Debo et al. [11], both innovators and imitators buy new products, while only imitators buy refurbished products. The market mechanism can be adapted or changed for products that follow other market models.

The producer is a price-taker for new products [72], whose exogenously-determined price $p _ { n } ,$ is normalized so that $0 < p _ { n } \leq 1$ . It sets the price for refurbished products, p , such that $p _ { r } < p _ { n } ,$ , signifying a competitive market setting between new and refurbished products. As in Debo et al. [11], we assume $\mathrm { D N } { } = \left( 1 - \left( p _ { n } - p _ { r } \right) / \delta \right)$ is the normalized demand rate for new products which represents the proportion of a fixed number of customers per unit time who buy the new product. Fig. 3 lays out the system dynamic framework and Table 2 details structural elements and parameters. For example, sales to innovators is computed as: Innovator adoption rate (α) ∗ (100 − totalmarket) ∗ DN, where the market capacity is 100 units of product. The corresponding demand rate for refurbished products $\mathrm { D R } = ( p _ { n } - p _ { r } ) / \delta - p _ { r } / ( 1 - \delta ) .$ . In our model we define three types of sales: innovator sales, imitator new product (NP) sales, and imitator refurbished product (RP) sales.

Fig. 4 and Table 3 demonstrate the sub-process of the innovator market. Some consumers return the product (new phone) within its lifetime, while others keep using it beyond that period. Returns within the defined lifetime are termed RBEOL, while those beyond are end of life (EOL) returns.

## 3.5. Return sector

We model returns from the three constituents — innovators who buy new products, and imitators who buy new or refurbished products. As detailed before, returns can occur at the end of the product lifecycle (EOL), or prematurely (RBEOL). Fig. 7 and Table 6 depict the subprocess. Both RBEOL EOL returns are modeled as exponential distributions. In RBEOL, the rate of product return increases with the time the product is under use. The assumption is that electronic products have a higher chance of failure or lifestyle mismatches within a certain period of time, and may be returned within that period.

## 3.6. Refurbish sector

Returned products are sent for refurbishing or recycling, depending on their condition. Products sent for refurbishing typically have low cosmetic damage and have fully working parts. These are inspected, refurbished and placed into refurbish stock (inventory) to be sold as refurbished products according to the market mechanism described in Section 3.1. Some refurbished products may be sent to the Recycle Sector at the RefurbRec rate, and Fig. 8 and Table 7 model the process.

## 3.7. Recycle sector

The structural details of the Recycle Sector appear in Fig. 9 and Table 8. The recycle sector receives products from the return sector at the rate of (1 − RetRefurb rate) and from refurbished sector at the rate given by RefurbRec. We assume that recycled materials can be sold to other markets at the recycling rate, Recycle rate, and (1 − Recycle) will go to landfills. Note that we do not model all the details about recycling and which sub products (e.g. metals) are recovered. These details, while important, would add complexity of base metal markets without shedding light on the interactions studied here.

In summary, we set up a system dynamic model of the closed loop supply chain, with innovators buying new products in the market, and imitators buying both new and refurbished products. There is a perceived difference (δ) in the value of new and refurbished products. Products have a lifetime, and can be returned before or after the lifetime. All returned products can then be refurbished and resold in the market, or recycled for base materials and to landfills. New product manufacturers, as well as refurbishers, recyclers, and different types of consumers, are participants in this supply chain. The stocks and flows of the dynamic model are mathematically modeled and depicted in tables and figures above.

## 4. Experimental design and analysis

The model is extensively validated internally and externally (see Appendix A.1). It is implemented in iThink, a high performance system dynamics modeling environment [37]. We lay out the experimental design in Table 9 to analyze the impact of market conditions, product design and marketing, and RBEOL and EOL return strategies on economic sustainability of the supply chain participants and environmental sustainability [45]. The total market size is normalized to 100 consumers. α and β define consumer types in the market, which can be impacted by the seller through marketing campaigns. The impact of marketing on δ is arguably very important, since it can enhance the value of refurbished products in consumers' minds (social responsibility in addition to value consciousness), and increase sales of refurbished goods. The product price varies from a normalized value of 0.1 to 1; we hold ${ \mathfrak { p } } _ { r } < { \mathfrak { p } } _ { n } ,$ and $\left( p _ { n } - p _ { r } \right) / \delta \leq 1$ for consistency. The normal return rate of products (EOL) is varied at the same rate in the basic experimental setup to study their overall impact; they are then modified and studied in finer detail in subsequent analysis. Similarly, premature returns (RBEOL) can range from 0 to positive. RBEOL could be high for innovators (who might opt for other products even within a product's lifetime period) and imitators with refurbished products (whose products may fail earlier), and low for imitators with new products (who may not wish to break a contract, or may continue to use the product beyond its designed lifetime). Our focus is on understanding and determining return rates that are necessary for sustainable markets. How companies and markets can achieve such return rates are product dependent, and are reliant on incentives, local policies and regulations. Finally, RefurbRec rates are studied between 0.1 and 0.7, signifying rates between 10% and 70% of the respective input to these processes. The values in Table 9 are determined based on information from recyclers, manufacturers, trade publications and academic research. $\alpha , \beta$ and δ values are well-known from marketing literature [3,11]; $p _ { n }$ and $p _ { r }$ assume all values between 0 and 1, and is a normalized instance of actual prices. In all cases, we extend the design and parameter values in both directions to capture outlier scenarios. For example, EOL<sub>imitnew</sub> would hardly reach 90% under normal market conditions. We include all such scenarios to determine their market impacts.

In the return processes, industry norms suggest that the condition of the product typically determines its path after return rather than its life in the system [26]. Hence there is no distinction between product types (EOL or RBEOL) after product return. Our discussions with refurbishing companies suggest that a higher proportion of RBEOL returns are refurbished compared to EOL returns. Hence, we model the refurbishing process as below:

$$
\text { RetRefurb } = 0. 7 * \text { RBEOL } / (\text { EOL } + \text { RBEOL }) + 0. 2 * \text { EOL } / (\text { EOL } + \text { RBEOL }).
$$

This ensures RBEOL returns refurbished at a higher rate than EOL returns, and an overall RetRefurb rate between 0.2 and 0.7 [26]. RetRefurb and RefurbRec rates are based on data from nationally wellknown recyclers [58].

## 4.2. Impact of price and product depreciation on sales

Having identified sustainable market conditions, we next investigate revenue and market implications of various decision variables. Product depreciation rate (δ), a perceived value and a function of marketing, technology and other factors, affects the imitator market, as imitators are value-sensitive and may choose between new and refurbished products based on their price differential $( p _ { n } - p _ { r } )$ and intrinsic value difference captured by δ. Fig. 11 shows the new product (NP) and refurbished product (RP) sales for various price differentials and depreciation rates. For $\delta = 0 . 4 ,$ the NP market has an increasing trend which accelerates slightly as the price differential increases. The RP market similarly has an increasing trend with lower numbers. (Zero sales at $\delta = 0 . 4$ and price differential = 0.4 illustrates the limiting case for demand DN and DR as defined in Section 3.1) For $\delta = 0 . 7$ , the pattern is similar; however NP total sales shows non-monotonic behavior. Overall, sales to the imitator market increases with the price differential; and the sales of both new and refurbished are higher with lower perceived product depreciation rates (δ). Taken together, high product depreciation rate and price differential diminish both new and refurbisher prospects and do not provide conducive conditions to close the supply chain loop.

## 4.3. Impact of refurbishable product design on sales

To moderate the impact of product depreciation rate δ, as discussed in Section 4.3, we investigate the influence of product design on closing the loop. As Fig. 12 demonstrates, products designed for high refurbishability have a positive impact on sales of refurbished products, given δ (=0.4 here), ceteris paribus. Of course, this decreases sales for new products slightly. Even with a product designed for refurbishability, the price differential needs to be large for a substantial market share of refurbished products (for a given δ). This may create profit pressures for both new and refurbished product sellers. Next, we turn to the profit implications of new and refurbished products within the supply chain to identify economically sustainable policies.

## 5. Managerial and policy insights

We develop a holistic model of a closed-loop supply chain, and analyze it from a consumer electronics product perspective. We consider all participants of the CLSC (new product manufacturer, refurbisher, different types of consumers) and investigate the impact of their decisions in a dynamic environment with multiple possible interactions. We do not limit our focus on a particular element or participant of a CLSC. Our focus is on decision making that enables economic viability of participants in this shared chain, which is a key ingredient to sustainable behavior and policies.

At a strategic and profitability level, we find – unsurprisingly – that new product manufacturers do not have a short term benefit in ensuring the viability of the CLSC, given no well-defined systemic return policies and development of a reverse supply chain. Hence the possible status quo — less than 20% of consumer electronics is recycled in the US [16,44]. Our results here and others show that reuse and recycling increases market size [52] and throughput. We show conclusively, and holistically, that the return and recycling process needs to be calibrated market-wide for the best balance of products, and to increase market size and throughput of both new and refurbished products. We also show how product design and refurbishability, as well as product marketing and perceived differentiation between new and refurbished products, and price differentials can improve overall sales and profitability. Our research demonstrates that decisions made by a new product designer and manufacturer have a disproportionate impact on the viability of refurbishers and recyclers. Product design (to increase refurbishability and lower refurbishing costs) and marketing decisions of the new product seller (to create healthier perceived differentiation) is critical to the sustainability of refurbishers and recyclers in the CLSC. To operationalize this important insight, our holistic modeling (and not focusing on a particular participant alone) can be used to estimate parameters from a systemic or company perspective, and reward new product sellers who meet defined benchmarks of design and marketing.

While some of these policies can be implemented by a new product seller, the overall benefit is difficult to achieve under a competitive market structure alone, where all companies may not follow the same long run principle. Interestingly, consumer electronics product lifecycles are growing even shorter over the years [26,44], which creates even more pressure in the downward supply chain. Hence market forces by themselves may not be sufficient to compel a new product (NP) seller to embrace refurbished products (RP) in their product design and market strategy. Policy decisions and regulation have been advanced (such as ROHS, WEEE); however by themselves they do not create the economic incentives for a NP seller to improve profitability, or increase recycling [26]. Adopting a more environmentally proactive posture for the long run may have, in addition to any direct environmental and cost reduction benefits, a significant and favorable impact on the firm's perceived riskiness to investors and, accordingly, its cost of equity capital and value in the market place [18]. Moreover, it can generate customer loyalty and improve the firm's brand image [4,5]. Company incentives may range from carbon tax credits [57] to customer awareness of their actions and resultant impact on firm value [59,60].

While company market value created through goodwill is a component that can incentivize such behavior, a significant rationale for “economically” sustainable behavior (as opposed to “environmentally” sustainable) results from gains in sales and resulting economies where a product manufacturer may profit from services targeted towards the product. Product design innovations that improve refurbishability while decreasing its cost will holistically benefit the NP seller as well as RP seller. Our results also demonstrate that product marketing efforts that differentiate NP and RP, and create social and customer awareness and acceptability for refurbished products, will also help. Future research should focus on creating metrics to measure the monetization of these efforts, and innovative public policies and incentives need to be developed to reward companies that meet these benchmarks. Such metrics are in development for some industries (e.g. [8–10,14,53,56]), which provides a pathway for decision making for an economic and environmentally sustainable CLSC for the electronics industry.

The model of the CLSC is implemented in iThink, a high performance system dynamics modeling environment, and validated using both qualitative and quantitative approaches [37]. There are over 35 million experimental design points, and each is run for 1000 days (over 2.7 years), and results computed using Euler integration method with a time step of one day.

To validate the model qualitatively, we define causal loops for each component of the model based on existing literature, described in Section 3. We test partial model results of each component to confirm that it closely replicates existing research results. Next, for quantitative validation of the overall model, we plot various output measures and compare them with published models and public data (where available). These provide confidence on the robustness and validity of our model, which we discuss below.

Fig. A1 shows the innovator market size for different prices $\left( { { p } _ { n } } \right)$ and product adoption rates (α), at a fixed $\delta = 0 . 6$ and $p _ { r } = 0 . 2 $ . While the innovator market is not dependent on δ, it is impacted by the available market size, where the total market size is fixed at 100. For increasing price, the innovator market size decreases at an increasing rate. This holds true at all adoption rates. Similarly, Fig. A2 demonstrates that for increasing adoption rates, the innovator market size increases at a decreasing rate. As the total market size is capped, this shows the effect of a decreasing available pool of innovators, and the results hold for different prices charged. Similarly, Fig. A3 shows that as the premature return rate of products increase $( R B E O L _ { i n n o \nu } )$ , the innovator market size decreases (almost linearly), for different adoption rates. As products are returned prematurely, those consumers may not be buy a new product immediately, or may buy other products. These results are consistent with existing theory and lend validity to the innovator market design.

A.2.3. Joint effect of premature and End of life return policies on market sustainability

We now investigate the joint impact of both types of returns on market sustainability. As before, we set $\alpha = 0 . 0 1 , \beta = 0 . 4 , \delta = 0 . 5 , p _ { n } = 0 . 7 ,$ $p _ { r } = 0 . 3$ . Table A5 lists the return rates. The objective here is to compare the results with earlier analysis, using industry standard overall refurbishing rates. Other weighting schemes also produce results with similar trends. Note that cases 1, 3 and 5 here correspond to Section 4.1 cases 1, 2 and 3.

As Fig. A11(a) to (d) suggests, only cases 4–5 depict scenarios where both the innovator and imitator markets are stable. For cases 1 and 2, while imitator market is stable, the innovator market collapses, which is similar to cases 1 and 2 in Fig. A7(a). This undesirable result occurs as no imitator customer relinquishes their products at the end of its life, and the proportion of these customers reentering the market as potential innovators is close to nil. At the same time, the proportion of innovators dwindles as they return their products at the end of life. With a majority of the market dominated by imitators at the time when innovators return their products, the proportion of innovators decreases precipitously in cases 1 and 2. Case 3 is similarly untenable, where the market is filled with refurbished products. Hence cases 4 and 5, with above-zero return rates, are the only possible sustainable policies in the market. Taken together, the market sustainability analysis demonstrates that above-zero return rates for products are critical to sustain both new and refurbished product sales in the market, and judicious policies are necessary to achieve return rates that provide a balance of products in the marketplace.
