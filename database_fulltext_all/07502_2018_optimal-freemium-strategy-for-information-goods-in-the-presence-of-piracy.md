## Abstract

Information goods providers adopt freemium strategies to reduce consumer uncertainty and combat piracy. However, offering a free version can cannibalize the demand for the premium version. In this paper, we investigate the trade-off between the effect of decreasing uncertainty and that of cannibalization, and explore the conditions under which firms should adopt freemium strategies in the presence of piracy. We employ a two-stage consumer perception change model to examine optimal pricing and the feasibility of a freemium strategy. Our results show that a higher piracy enforcement level may hurt the firm if the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of a premium version is higher than the quality perception increment of the pirated version. This indicates that the presence of a pirated version is not always harmful for the firm. We also find that when the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of a premium version is lower than the quality perception increment of the free version, the traditional strategy (i.e., not offering a free version) weakly dominates the freemium strategy. In contrast, when the quality perception increment derived from the premium version is higher than that of the free version, the optimal strategy depends on the piracy enforcement level and the change in consumer quality perception. If the piracy enforcement level is high, the freemium strategy dominates the traditional strategy when the consumer quality perception increment associated with the premium version is higher than a given threshold. However, for a low piracy enforcement level, the traditional strategy dominates in the context of both a high consumer quality perception and a low consumer quality perception increment concerning the pirated version versus the free version.

Keywords: Information Goods, Piracy, Freemium Strategy, Quality Perception, Pricing

## 1 Introduction

In the face of advanced information technology, the piracy of information goods has become an increasingly serious problem. Due to the low marginal cost of information goods, pirated information goods can be easily produced—especially music, software, e-books, and similar products. According to a global software survey conducted by the Business Software Alliance in 2013, the total commercial value of the world's unauthorized PC software reached \$62.7 billion in 2013, and the installation rate rose from 42 percent in 2011 to 43 percent in 2013. Additionally, a statistic from the piracy-tracking firm Excipio shows that pirated downloads of the popular political drama House of Cards on the Season 3 premiere date was as high as 681,889; viewers were widely distributed through the United States, Britain, India, Australia, Poland, Greece and other countries. GlobalWebIndex notes that in addition to pirated downloads, millions of viewers watched the show using a virtual private network (VPN) to access Netflix (Spangler, 2015).

Piracy significantly affects the interests of information goods providers. To resolve this problem, two business strategies are usually adopted. One is product differentiation between the commercial version and pirated version, in terms of product quality, in order to avoid direct competition (Hui, Yoo, & Tam, 2008; Jaisingh, 2009). Another is to increase the costs and risks associated with piracy through legal or copyright protection technologies (Choi, Bae, & Jun, 2010). However, these kinds of protection technologies can reduce the value of the legal product and constrain the flexibility of legitimate consumers (Sundararajan, 2004). It is feasible to protect consumers' rights and providers' interests legally through government copyright enforcement (Chen & Png, 2003). However, relying on government copyright enforcement alone is not sufficient (Chen & Png, 2003).

Therefore, firms seek other business models to combat piracy. Freemium, an emerging business model, has been widely used in recent years. Freemium combines free with premium—that is, consumers obtain the basic core features of a given product for free and pay a subscription fee for its premium features (Kumar, 2014). This strategy of offering free functionality is effective for capturing powerful marketing effects. As such, expensive advertising costs can thereby be reduced on a large scale. Through offering free functionality, firms can quickly attract customers by introducing them to the product's attributes, features, and compatibility (Niculescu & Wu, 2014), which reduces consumer uncertainty about the quality of the product.

Freemium is most common in the multiplayer online game industry. For example, League of Legends offers a free mode and charges for premium content. In Dota 2, users buy accessories from the online store to personalize their heroes. Dungeon Fighter Online also charges for personalizations and premium content. In the era of mobile Internet, more and more companies and mobile apps are adopting the freemium strategy, such as Pandora Radio, Bejeweled Blitz, and Supercell's Clash of Clan. In the context of software, Kingsoft Antivirus offers free basic functionality, such as computer antivirus, disk cleaner, and software management. However, if users need photograph restoration, computer remote maintenance, or an online accelerator, they must pay a subscription fee. Two other examples involve an online music platform and the media industry: Spotify offers free music streaming, but charges a monthly fee for downloading; the New York Times offers a limited number of articles for free, charging a subscription rate when this number is exceeded.

Uncertainty about product quality is one of the reasons why consumers use pirated goods before they buy the commercial version (Shore, Venkatachalam, Solorzano, Burn, Hassan, & Janczewski, 2001). Evidence indicates that piracy may be reduced to some extent if firms provide free functionality for users to experience (Cheng, Sims, & Teegen, 1997). According to a survey on Spotify which has adopted the freemium strategy, the downloading of pirated music via BitTorrent dropped by 20 percent in Australia in the first year after the streaming platform became operational. Nevertheless, a certain number of unethical consumers prefer pirated goods because pirated goods usually have more functionality than free versions. Furthermore, both free and pirated versions can cannibalize demand for premium versions. Therefore, whether a firm should follow a freemium model in the presence of piracy has become a critical issue for information goods providers.

We provide a two-stage analytical model to study the effects of piracy on firm profits and then address the issue of which strategy—freemium or traditional model—the firms should adopt to maximize their profits in the presence of piracy. In particular, our research examines the following critical issues: First, for those who use the free version or the pirated version in the first stage, which version do they choose in the second stage? Second, how does the piracy enforcement level impact the firm’s optimal profit? Finally, under what conditions will freemium be dominant over the traditional strategy?

Our results provide several useful insights. First, when piracy enforcement levels are relatively high, consumers tend not to use pirated goods. In certain situations, a portion of free users turn into paying users, demonstrating why firms choose to provide freemium, while a portion of piracy users also purchase the premium version in the second stage. This demonstrates that the pirated version also serves as a product sample to some extent.

Second, the firm's profit does not decrease with the increase of the piracy enforcement level if there is a low consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version in the second stage after using the pirated version in the first stage. However, a higher level of piracy enforcement may hurt the firm when the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version in the second stage is high after using the pirated version in the first stage.

Finally, when the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is higher than the quality perception increment of the free version, the freemium strategy will become the dominating strategy if the following conditions are satisfied: First, the piracy enforcement level is high and the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is higher than that for the quality of free version. Second, for the low piracy enforcement context, there is either a lower quality perception increment or a higher quality perception increment associated with the pirated version versus the free version.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. We present a review of related work in Section 2. Section 3 describes a two-stage theoretical model that determines consumer behavioral changes in order to determine the optimal prices for premium versions and the firm's optimal profits in the presence of piracy. This section also explores the effect of the piracy enforcement level on firm profits. In Section 4, we study in what conditions freemium vis-à-vis the traditional strategy function as the dominant strategy for combating piracy. Section 5 summarizes this study and presents directions for further research.

## 2 Literature Review

There is a growing literature on information goods (Hao & Fan, 2014) including piracy and versioning (Bhargava & Choudhary, 2008; Shivendu & Zhang, 2015). Previous research related to ours can be broadly grouped into two streams: (1) social and economic impacts of piracy, and (2) the freemium model and its impact on piracy.

The first stream of literature relevant to our study examines the social and economic impacts of piracy. One main area of research investigates the social impacts of piracy; for example, government policy and digital rights management (DRM) (Petiz & Waelbroeck, 2006). Chen and Png (2003) examine the government's attitude toward copying. In implementing DRM, Sundararajan (2004) shows that a monopolist's optimal pricing strategy is to make all consumers indifferent concerning pirated versus legal usage. More recently, Vernik, Purohit, & Desai (2011) demonstrate that piracy may decrease in the absence of DRM through competition among digital retailers, traditional retailers, and pirated goods.

Another aspect explores economic impacts of piracy. Pricing, as an efficient approach to fighting piracy, has received much attention in the past few years. For example, Khouja and Smith (2007) find that skimming pricing is suboptimal in the context of saturation effects and strong piracy. Khouja, Hadzikadic, Rajagopalan, & Tsay (2008) analyze pricing decisions by adopting complex adaptive systems in the presence of piracy. Kogan, Ozinci, & Perlman (2013) show that piracy costs can influence consumers' behavior and firms' pricing strategies. Waters (2015) examines the impact of piracy on welfare under dynamic pricing. In addition to studies on optimal pricing strategies in the presence of piracy, several studies investigate the impact of piracy on the quality of information goods. Johar, Kumar, & Mooker (2012) demonstrate that content publishers should improve content quality, increase content distribution delays, or do both in order to combat piracy. Jaisingh (2009) finds that stronger piracy policies increase piracy and decrease product quality in some cases. Lahiri and Dey's (2013) study shows that lower enforcement of piracy increases the quality of legal products in certain situations. Similarly, Guo and Meng (2014) show that stronger copyright protections can result in lower-quality products.

Several studies analyze the impact of piracy on firms' profitability. August and Tunca (2008) explore whether security patches should be provided for pirated software. They show that stronger piracy enforcement can reduce firms' profit in some situations. In contrast, Jeong, Khouja, & Zhao (2012) study the impact of piracy on the profits of the digital music supply chain. They find that profits increase as market size increases, piracy risk costs increase, and marginal costs decrease. Notably, Tunca and Wu (2013) demonstrate that individual piracy increases the profits of legal firms when commercial piracy exists.

The aforementioned research focuses on studying various aspects of piracy, from fighting piracy to the economic impacts of piracy. In particular, these studies discuss possible business models for combating piracy, such as bundling (Gopal & Gupta, 2010), sampling (Hui, Yoo, & Tam, 2008), and versioning (Wu & Chen, 2008). Specifically, Wu and Chen (2008) show that providing one version is profitable in the absence of piracy, while adopting versioning could combat piracy in some specific cases. Even though free versions are, in essence, a version of commercial counterparts, these results cannot directly applied to our study, because it would involve setting the price of the lower quality version at zero. Accordingly, these conclusions and pricing models are not suitable for firms that adopt a freemium business model.

The second stream of literature is related to the freemium model and its impact on piracy. This model is particularly appealing to start-up firms and mobile app developers. Freemium mainly includes functionality-limited freemium and time-limited freemium (Niculescu & Wu, 2014). Cheng and Tang (2010) offer an analytical model for studying the limited functionality free trial strategy in the presence of network effects. They show that the monopoly offering a free trial is an optimal strategy in the context of strong network intensity. Cheng and Liu (2012) extend the model in Cheng and Tang (2010) to examine the optimal strategy for a time-limited free trial when consumers' valuation updating depends on the length of the free trial. They show that providing a time-limited free trial is better than offering a functionality-limited free trial if network intensity is moderate.

Additionally, Niculescu and Wu (2014) develop a two-period consumer valuation learning model that can be influenced by word-of-mouth effects to compare three business models: functionality-limited freemium, seeding, and the conventional business model. They derive the equilibrium result and the optimal strategy. In fact, free product can be regarded as an inferior version of the commercial version. There exists a rich literature to study vertical differentiation products. For example, Conner (1995) finds it is profitable to allow a lower quality “clone” to exist when network intensity is strong. Jain (2008) focuses on the effect of pirated products on the prices and profits of vertical differentiation competition firms. The network effects, in particular, are also assessed. Niculescu, Wu, & Xu (2016), for example, study whether an incumbent opening a proprietary technology platform for an entrant produced different qualities of software products under network effects.

Few articles study the impact of the freemium model on piracy. For example, Choi (2015) empirically investigates the impact of an in-app purchase option, free version, and app price on the level of app piracy. This study also suggests that providing a free version negatively affects the number of downloads of the pirated apps. Halmenschlager and Waelbroeck (2014) regard the freemium model as an instrument for combating piracy, and develop a simple freemium model in the presence of piracy. The results show the conditions in which firms adopt a freemium strategy and reveal that it is not optimal to restrict the functionality of a free version too much.

In contrast with previous research, we develop a two-step model to focus on the role of functionality-limited freemium versions for combating piracy, and to consider changes in consumer quality perceptions. In particular, we investigate the effect of piracy enforcement on firm profits, and also evaluate under what conditions freemium is a better strategy.

In terms of the research background, the most relevant study to ours is Halmenschlager and Waelbroeck (2014). However, their model ignores the role of free versions and consumer attributions. We extend this model by developing a two-stage adoption framework based on changes in consumer perceptions. From the perspective of the research model, the most relevant study to ours is Chellappa and Shivendu (2005). They develop a two-stage model to study the piracy of information goods, and focus on fully functional product sampling that is given away for a short period of time. Specifically, consumers may update their fit perception in the second stage when they use pirated information goods in the first stage. This research also examines the pricing of information goods in a segmented market and demonstrates the optimal role of sampling for fighting piracy. Chellappa and Shivendu show that sampling may be the dominant strategy for underestimated consumers when the piracy enforcement level is high. Interestingly, our work reveals that firms are better off adopting a limited-version freemium model in the context of relatively low piracy enforcement levels in some specific situations. This finding demonstrates that these two modes of sampling are complementary for combating piracy.

## 3 Freemium Model

The freemium model is based on a product with two modes: a free core version that has basic functionality and a premium version that has add-on functionality. It is worth noting that the premium version can be accessed or used only when the free core version is installed or used. Information goods are vulnerable to piracy, and pirated goods are typically considered to be a potentially competitive products. However, there is no pirate firm involved in setting prices. In terms of the characteristics of information goods (Tan, Carrillo, & Cheng, 2015; Tan & Carrillo, 2016), it is assumed that the development cost is sunk (Guo & Meng, 2014) and the marginal reproduction cost is zero (Guo & Meng, 2014; Jones & Mendelson, 2011).

Piracy depends on the quality of the pirated version being higher than that of the free version. To illustrate, a firm offers a premium version with quality index 1 at a price p. Because the pirated version has no firm technology support and may contain viruses or malicious software, the quality of the pirated version, denoted as $q_{1}$ , is lower than that of premium version (i.e., $q_{1}<l$ ). The piracy cost is directly determined by the enforcement level, which we here denote as r>0, with $q_{2}$ being the quality of the free version, and $q_{2}<q_{1}$ . Thus, no piracy exists if the pirated version does not exceed the free version in terms of quality. The notations are described in Table 1.

This paper examines the case, in which consumers underestimate a product's functionality because they assess a product after experiencing some or all of its functionality, which may affect their evaluation of its quality. It should be noted here that purchasing a premium version is associated with permanent ownership of the product. In this paper, we develop a two-stage model that evaluates consumers' behavioral changes in order to examine the optimal pricing strategy for premium versions, and explore whether firms should provide freemium to combat piracy. Similar to most previous studies, we allow each consumer to use a maximum of one unit of product at each stage of the study. While firms only have access to the distribution of their consumers, each consumer knows which type of product they are using.

## 3.1 Consumer Valuation Learning and Timeline Sequence

Consumers of type $\theta$ are uniformly distributed in the interval [0, 1]. A consumer's type represents the expected value for the product. At the beginning of Stage 1, let $\alpha$ be the consumers' initial belief degree, where $0 < \alpha < 1$ . The initial belief is proportional to the real quality of the product. Therefore, consumers' initial quality perception is $\alpha$ for the premium version, $\alpha q_{1}$ for pirated version, and $\alpha q_{2}$ for the free version. At the beginning of Stage 2, consumers who use the pirated version or the free version during the first stage may change their selections. Consumer perception updating is based on direct and indirect learning. Direct learning means that consumers update their initial quality perception degree $\alpha$ to 1 from actual use, while indirect learning is based on experiencing part of the functionality. Specifically, consumers update their quality perception for the premium version and pirated version after they use the free version. For simplicity, let $\delta$ serve as the same perception increment for both the pirated and premium versions. Normally, consumers' quality perception of the premium version is higher than that of free version (i.e. $\alpha + \delta > q_{2}$ ). Analogously, consumers who first use the pirated version will update their quality perception of premium version. The perception increment is defined as $\delta_{1}$ . Because the quality of pirated version is higher than that of the free version, consumers' perception of the pirated version will be better. Thus, $0 < \delta < \delta_{1}$ . Additionally, the pirated version includes the entire functionality of the free version, so we assume that consumers using the pirated version are also experiencing the quality of the free version.

This model follows the timeline sequence. First, the firm determines the quality of free version and premium version. Second, the firm announces the price of the premium version. Third, consumers make their first-stage purchase decisions. Finally, consumers update their quality perceptions and make their second-stage purchase decisions.

## 3.2 Optimal Pricing

We first analyze consumer behavior under the freemium model. In the first stage, consumers choose one of the following four available options: buy premium version, use pirated version, use free version, or abandon use completely. As a result, consumers derive the first-stage utilities from these options of $\alpha\theta - p$ , $\alpha\theta q_{1} - r$ , $\alpha\theta q_{2}$ and 0, respectively. In Stage 2, consumers who declined to use any version continue to use nothing because they did not demonstrate interest in the product. Note that consumers who did not purchase the premium version in Stage 1 will update their quality perception. Consumers who use the free version in the first stage obtain the second-stage utility of $(\alpha + \delta)\theta - p$ from the premium version, $(\alpha + \delta)\theta q_{1} - r$ from using the pirated version, and $\theta q_{2}$ from using the free version. Those who use the pirated version in the first stage also have three choices in the second stage: buy the premium version, use the pirated version, or use the free version. Consumers derive the second-stage utilities from the options of $(\alpha + \delta_{1})\theta - p$ , $\theta q_{1} - r$ , and $\theta q_{2}$ , respectively. Therefore, consumers' decisions in two stages concerning buying the premium version, using the pirated version, using the free version, or forgoing use is based on the IC and IR constraints as shown in Figure 1.

In the first stage, the pirates and the legal users coexist if $\frac{p}{r} > (1 - q_{2})/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ . Consumers of type $\theta\epsilon[0, r/(\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2})]$ use the free version and consumers of type $\epsilon(\frac{p-r}{\alpha-\alpha q_{1}}, 1]$ use the premium version, and the rest use the pirated version (all proofs are available in the Appendix). In other words, piracy exists only if the price of the premium version is sufficiently high relative to the piracy cost. Specifically, consumers do not use pirated versions when $\frac{p}{r} \leq (1 - q_{2})/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ . Consumers of type $\theta\epsilon[0, p/(\alpha - \alpha q_{2})]$ use the free version and high-type consumers $\theta\epsilon(\frac{p}{\alpha-\alpha q_{2}}, 1]$ use the premium version. That is, if the price of the premium version is sufficiently low, then piracy is a suboptimal choice for consumers.

## 3.2.1 Optimal Results in the Absence of Piracy

In this subsection, we state the conditions that lead to changes in consumer behavior in the absence of piracy, and then obtain the firm's optimal profits.

LEMMA 1 In the second stage, when $\frac{p}{r} \leq (1 - q_{2})/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ , if the perception increment concerning the quality of premium version for consumers (who use the free version in the first stage) is not lower than that of free version (i.e., $\delta \geq q_{2} - \alpha q_{2}$ ) used in the first stage, then a portion of the high-type consumers ( $\theta \in [\frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}}, p/(\alpha - \alpha q_{2})]$ ) will buy the premium version.

Lemma 1 clarifies the free users in the first stage with regard to behavioral changes in certain conditions. The consumers will not choose the pirated version in the case of $\frac{p}{r} \leq (1 - q_{2})/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ due to the relatively high cost. Under this condition, the price of the premium version is lower and offers higher quality. Consumers could then choose between the premium and the free versions to acquire the version offering the maximum surplus. Thus, a portion of high-type consumers ( $\theta \in [\frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}}, p/(\alpha - \alpha q_{2})]$ ) will purchase the premium version, and consumers with lower preference ( $\theta \in [0, p/(\alpha + \delta - q_{2})]$ ) will use the free version as before. The consumer of type $\theta = p/(\alpha + \delta - q_{2})$ is indistinguishable in terms of buying the premium version versus using the free version.

From Lemma 1, the premium version's demand $D$ in two stages is described by $D = 1 - p / (\alpha + \delta - q_2)$ . The firm seeks to maximize profit as follows:

$$
\max _ {p} \pi = p D.
$$

Solving the above problem yields the following optimal price of the premium version and the firm's profit:

$$
p _ {1} ^ {*} = (\alpha + \delta - q _ {2}) / 2, \pi_ {1} ^ {*} = (\alpha + \delta - q _ {2}) / 4.
$$

LEMMA 2 In the second stage, when $\frac{p}{r} \leq (1 - q_{2})/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ , if the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is lower than that of the free version (i.e., $\delta < q_{2} - \alpha q_{2}$ ) for consumers who used the free version in the first stage then none of the consumers will change their selections.

To understand Lemma 2, we consider an extreme scenario in which the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is zero in the second stage. In this situation, consumers' perceptions concerning the quality of the free version increases from $\alpha q_{2}$ to $q_{2}$ , while their quality perception of the premium version does not change. Intuitively, the consumers' perception change does not influence demand for the premium version in the second stage. As a result, free users will not alter their selections and will continue using the free version. A case in point is as follows: Each user was allowed to read 20 articles per month for free on the New York Times online in 2011; however, users rarely paid to read more articles, because their demands were satisfied and their perceptions concerning the number of free articles was good enough. Consequently, the firm reduced the number of free articles to 10 per month in 2012.

Under the conditions in Lemma 2, the demand for the premium version in two stages is $D = 1 - p / (\alpha - \alpha q_2)$ . We derive the following optimal price of the premium version and firm's profit:

$$
p _ {2} ^ {*} = (\alpha - \alpha q _ {2}) / 2, \pi_ {2} ^ {*} = (\alpha - \alpha q _ {2}) / 4.
$$

## 3.2.2 Optimal Results Under Piracy

In this part, we first state the conditions that lead to changes in consumer behavior under piracy, and then demonstrate the optimal results depending on consumers' behavioral changes. We first analyze those who use the free version in the first stage. The following lemma shows consumers' behavioral changes according to their perception updating.

LEMMA 3 (a) When consumers' quality perception and the perception increment of the pirated version are higher than those regarding the free version (i.e., $(\alpha + \delta)q_1 > q_2, q_1 > q_2 - \alpha q_2)$ ,

(1) If $\frac{\alpha + \delta - q_2}{\alpha q_1 + \delta q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p}{r} < \frac{\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_2 - \delta q_1}{\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2}$ , then consumers of type $\theta \in \left(\frac{p - r}{\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_1 - \delta q_1}, \frac{r}{\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2}\right]$ will buy the premium version in Stage 2 and consumers of type $\theta \epsilon [\frac{r}{\alpha q_1 + \delta q_1 - q_2}, (p - r) / (\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_1 - \delta q_1)]$ will use the pirated version in the second stage.

(2) If $\frac{p}{r} > (\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_{2} - \delta q_{1}) / (\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2})$ then high-type consumers $\theta \in [\frac{r}{\alpha q_{1} + \delta q_{1} - q_{2}}, \frac{r}{\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2}}]$ will use the pirated version in the second stage.

(3) If $\frac{1 - q_2}{q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p}{r} < (\alpha + \delta - q_2) / (\alpha q_1 + \delta q_1 - q_2)$ , then consumers of type $\theta \in [\frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_2}, r / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)]$ will buy the premium version in the second stage.

(b) When $\frac{1 - q_2}{q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p}{r} < (\alpha + \delta - q_2) / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)$ ,

(1) when consumers obtain a lower quality perception from using the pirated version than from using the free version (i.e., $(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} < q_{2}$ ) such that consumers of type $\theta\epsilon[\frac{p}{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}, r/(\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2})]$ will buy the premium version in the second stage.

(2) if consumers derive a higher quality perception from using pirated goods compared with the quality perception that they derive from using the free version (i.e., $(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} > q_{2}$ ), and their quality perception increment related to the pirated version is not as high as that of the free version (i.e., $\delta q_{1} < q_{2} - \alpha q_{2}$ ), and their perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is higher than that of the free version (i.e., $\delta > q_{2} - \alpha q_{2}$ ), consumers of type $\theta \in [\frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}}, r / (\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2})]$ will buy the premium version in the second stage.

(c) If all of the above conditions are not satisfied, all consumers will continue using the free version.

In case (a), both consumers' quality perception and their quality perception increment related to the premium version are the highest, whereas these elements related to the free version are the lowest. In this situation, consumers usually compare the price of the premium version and the piracy cost before making a decision. As a result, a portion of consumers will buy the premium version and a portion will use the pirated version when the piracy cost and the price of the premium version are both moderate. High-type consumers—i.e. consumers with higher expectations of product value—will purchase the premium version to maximize their surplus if the price of the premium version is relatively low and the piracy cost is relatively high. In contrast, some hightype consumers will use the pirated version if the piracy cost is lower. In case (b), when the piracy cost and the price of the premium version are moderate, if consumers' quality perception of the pirated version is lower than that of the free version, then consumers will discard the pirated version. On the other hand, in case (b [2]), we consider the extreme case in which consumers' quality perception increment regarding the pirated version is zero (i.e., $\delta q_{1} = 0$ ), and it is thus clear that the pirated version represents a suboptimal choice. However, when the cost performance of the premium version is high, a portion of consumers will purchase the premium version.

Next, we will consider those who use the pirated version in the first stage. Note that consumer perception concerning the quality of the free version is lower than that of pirated version, thus these consumers would not consider the free version in the second stage. The following lemma derives different cases for consumers' behavioral changes.

LEMMA 4 (a) When $\frac{1 - q_2}{q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p}{r}$ , if consumers derive a higher quality perception from buying the premium version than from using the pirated version (i.e., $\alpha + \delta_1 > q_1$ ), and their quality perception increment regarding the pirated version is lower than that of the premium version (i.e., $\delta_1 > q_1 - \alpha q_1$ ), high-type consumers ( $\theta \in [\frac{p - r}{\alpha + \delta_1 - q_1}, (p - r) / (\alpha - \alpha q_1)]$ ) will buy the premium version in the second stage.

(b) Otherwise, consumers will continue using the pirated version in the second stage.

All the above conditions in Lemma 4 imply that the price of the premium version is higher with better quality. Consumers will compare their surplus extracted from buying the premium version with the surplus derived from using the pirated version. Thus, a portion of high-type consumers will purchase the premium version. In contrast, if these conditions are not satisfied, none of the consumers will change their selections.

## 3.3 The Role of Piracy Enforcement

In the previous sections, optimal results are derived for different market structures. For given piracy enforcement levels, we rewrite the optimal results in the Appendix. We defined eight case scenarios according to consumers' perception changes.

In this subsection, we study the effect of piracy on the firm's profit. Usually, an increased piracy enforcement level increases the monopoly firm's profit. However, in the presence of consumers' perception change, the role of piracy enforcement is comparatively complex. Surprisingly, a higher piracy enforcement level could even harm firm profits under certain conditions. The elaborated results are given in the following propositions.

PROPOSITION 1 The firm's profit does not decrease with the increase of the piracy enforcement level when the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is not higher than that of the pirated version (i.e., $\delta_1 \leq q_1 - \alpha q_1$ ) for consumers who use the pirated version in the first stage.

The impact of the piracy enforcement on the firm's profit has some important trade-offs. On the one hand, an increase in the piracy enforcement level can reduce piracy and lead to the higher profits. First, an increase in piracy enforcement would directly improve the price perception of the premium version. Second, in terms of consumer behavioral change, a higher piracy enforcement level would induce some pirates to buy premium version. On the other hand, stricter enforcement might deter some consumers who would use the pirated version, thus weakening the role of sampling. Specifically, stricter enforcement might reduce the likelihood that certain users would convert from using a pirated version to buying the premium version. As a result, the aggregate effect of piracy enforcement depends on the combination of consumer behavioral changes and pricing strategies in the face of these trade-offs.

Proposition 1 demonstrates that the effect of piracy enforcement on the firm's profit is not negative. When $\delta_1 \leq q_1 - \alpha q_1$ , all pirates continue using the pirated version. Intuitively, one might believe that firms would seek to eliminate piracy by preventing consumers from using the pirated version in the first stage; however this is not always beneficial. Two common ways of preventing piracy are increasing piracy enforcement and lowering the price of the premium version. However, since price lowering would reduce the firm's profit, increasing piracy enforcement would be the optimal intervention. This result verifies that the optimal profit increases with the existence of piracy behavior through piracy enforcement. In other words, when piracy enforcement levels are relatively low, there are a mix of legal users and pirates, and the firm's profit increases with piracy enforcement (Figure 2). PROPOSITION 7 From a profit perspective, when $\delta_{1} > q_{1} - \alpha q_{1}$ , if $(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} < q_{2}$ , the following holds true: (a) The freemium model is the weakly dominating strategy when

(1) $r < \max [R_6, \min [r_5, \max [R_6, \widetilde{r}_5]]]$ , or  
(2) $\delta > q_2$ and $r > \max [R_6, \min [\max [r_5, r_2'], r_3']]$ , or

(3) $\delta \leq q_{2}$ and $max[r_5, r_2'] < r < max[r_5, r_2', \alpha q_1 - q_1\sqrt{\alpha(q_2 - \delta)}]$ and $r_3' > r_2'$ and $r_3' > max[R_6, r_5]$ , or

(4) $max[0, R_{6}, \widetilde{r}_{5}, r_{5}, R_{7}] < r < max[0, R_{7}, r_{2}']$ and $r_{2}^{\prime} > max[R_{6}, r_{5}]$ , or

(5) $R_{6} < r_{2}' < r_{5}$ and $r_{3}' > r_{2}'$ and $min[r_{2}', max[0, r_{6}]] < r <$ $min[max[0, r_{6}], r_{3}', r_{5}], or$

(6) $R_{6} < r_{5}$ and $R_{6} < r_{2}'$ $max[R_{8}, min[r_{5}, max[R_{6}, \widetilde{r}_{5}]]] < r <$ $min[r_{5}, r_{2}', R_{9}]$ .

(b) Otherwise, the traditional commercial model is the dominant strategy.

Proposition 7 identifies the optimal choices when the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is higher than that of the pirated version (i.e., $\delta_{1} > q_{1} - \alpha q_{1}$ ) for consumers who used the pirated version in the first stage. In such cases, a portion of pirates will buy the premium version (or commercial version) unless piracy does not exist. Similar to the results in Proposition 4, this proposition shows that when the piracy enforcement level is low (i.e., $r < R_{5}$ ), the profits produced by the two strategies are identical. If a portion of free users buy the premium version in Stage 2, freemium is the dominate strategy (i.e., $R_{5} < r < R_{6}$ ). For a relatively high enforcement level (i.e., $r > max[R_{6}, \tilde{r}_{5}]$ ), the optimal strategy depends on the combination of $\alpha$ , $\delta$ , $\delta_{1}$ , and $q_{2}$ .

However, for moderate piracy enforcement levels, the profit generated by the freemium strategy is not lower than that generated by the traditional strategy. The reason is that it is not always true for $R_{6} > \widetilde{r}_{5}$ . Through computational analyses, the order of the two threshold values may be reversed (i.e., $R_{6} < \widetilde{r}_{5}$ ). In such cases, the firm could adopt two pricing strategies using freemium. The first one would be that all free users would continue using the free version and the price $p = (r + \alpha + \delta_{1} - q_{1})/2$ would be set. The second would be that a portion of free users would buy the premium version and no one would use pirated versions, where $p = (1 - q_{2})r/(q_{1} - q_{2})$ . The firm adopts whichever pricing strategy yields a higher profit. On the other hand, the firm sets the price at $p = (r + \alpha + \delta_{1} - q_{1})/2$ in the presence of the traditional model when $r < \widetilde{r}_{5}$ . Thus, the freemium weakly outperforms the traditional model in this case.

Next, we discuss the situation when the consumer quality perception of the pirated version is not lower than that of the free version (i.e., $(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} \geq q_{2}$ ), and the quality perception increment of the pirated version is not as high as that of the free version (i.e., $\delta q_{1} \leq q_{2} - \alpha q_{2}$ ). Similarly, when $\delta_{1} > q_{1} - \alpha q_{1}$ , the optimal results are consistent with the results in Proposition 7 except for the condition when $R_{5} > r$ . ## 5 Conclusion

Our work presents a two-stage model based on the change in consumer perception. Specifically, we focus on whether a firm should provide feature-limited freemium to combat piracy. Free versions can allow consumers to experience aspects of functionality, which can reduce uncertainty. However, free versions also cannibalize the demand for premium versions to some extent. Thus, determining the optimal policy is complex and firms must take consumers attributions and market conditions into account. In our paper, we explore the optimal strategy for firms and examine the effects of piracy enforcement on firm profits.

We draw attention to the research in experience goods pricing that focuses on consumer perception changes caused by direct and indirect experiences. We find that under some conditions, some first-stage free users will buy the premium version in the second stage and that some will use pirated version. The results show that offering free versions not only reduces consumer uncertainty, but also replaces traditional promotion and marketing to some extent. After all, some free users do transform into paid users, which is the purpose of providing freemium. Therefore, managers should consider providing free product instead of investing in expensive advertisement.

On the other hand, some pirates will buy the premium version under certain conditions. That is, the aim of consumers using pirated goods is to sample the goods. When the pirated versions act as a sampling product, managers should reduce technological and legal deterrence to use, such as copyright protections. Additionally, we prove that the firm's profit does not decrease with the increase of the piracy enforcement level if the consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version in the second stage is low after using the pirated version in the first stage. However, higher piracy enforcement levels may be disadvantageous when consumer perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version in the second stage is high after using the pirated version in the first stage. The results indicate that pirated versions are not always harmful to the firm.

Our results also demonstrate the optimal strategy according to all consumer attributions and market conditions. We find that when the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is not higher than that of free version, the traditional model is the dominant strategy. When the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version is high, the optimal strategy is as follows: When the piracy enforcement level is high, the optimal strategy depends on the value of the perception increment concerning the quality of the premium version. Freemium is optimal when the perception increment value is higher for the premium version than it is for the quality of free version. For low piracy enforcement levels, freemium dominates the traditional strategy except when the consumer quality perception of the pirated version is not lower than that of the free version, and the quality perception increment of the pirated version is not as high as that of the free version. The optimal strategy is complex and it depends on the consumer perception increment for the moderate piracy enforcement level.

The insights of this paper are important for managers. If the firm does not invest in hype-advertising to raise prior perceptions, the firm should not provide a high-quality free product. Otherwise, low-type consumers will continue to use a free version rather than purchase a premium version. Additionally, the high quality free product will lower the high-type consumer surplus. As a result, the firm providing the freemium strategy will lose some of its profit and then adopt the traditional strategy anyway. When a firm provides a low-quality free product, the firm should strengthen technological and legal deterrence, such as copyright protections, to eliminate piracy behavior. In this case, a portion of high-type free users will turn into paying premium users. If the price competition between the free product and the premium version is reduced, the firm will accordingly derive a relatively high profit. On the other hand, when the firm engages in hype-advertisement, the firm should adopt the traditional strategy if and only if the firm lowers copyright protections.

The directions for future research could include the following aspects. One could consider the duopoly setting in the presence of piracy, in which both firms offer freemium and their products are interchangeable. In this case, consumer perceptions in two stages of the functionality of each firm's goods would be compared with the features of its rival's goods. It would be interesting to simultaneously study the pricing decisions in the duopoly case scenario. Another research direction might consider the optimal quality and the optimal price simultaneously, which is important for product line design. Finally, it would be interesting to investigate the competition between a legal firm and a pirate firm and further examine consumer surplus and social welfare in the presence of commercial piracy.

## PROOF OF LEMMA 3

In Case 2, we consider those who use the free version in the first stage. When $p / r > (1 - q_2) / (q_1 - q_2)$ , we consider the situation of $(\alpha + \delta)q_1 - q_2 > 0$ .

If $\frac{p}{r} \geq \frac{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}}{(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}}$ , we have $\frac{p - r}{(\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_{1})} \geq \frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}} \geq \frac{r}{(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}}$ . When $\delta q_{1} - q_{2} + \alpha q_{2} \leq 0$ , we obtain $r / (\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2})) \leq r / ((\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2})$ . Thus, all consumers continue using the free version. When $\delta q_{1} - q_{2} + \alpha q_{2} > 0$ , it results in $r / (\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2})) > r / ((\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2})$ . In this situation, when $p / r > (\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_{2} - \delta q_{1}) / (\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2})$ , we obtain $\frac{p - r}{(\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_{1})} > \frac{r}{\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2})} > \frac{r}{(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}}$ . Therefore, consumers of type $\theta \in [r / ((\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}), r / (\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2}))]$ will use the pirated version in the second stage. When $\frac{\alpha + \delta - q_{2}}{(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}} \leq \frac{p}{r} \leq \frac{\alpha + \delta - \alpha q_{2} - \delta q_{1}}{\alpha q_{1} - \alpha q_{2}}$ , we have $\frac{r}{(\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}} \leq \frac{p - r}{(\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_{1})} \leq \frac{r}{\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2})}$ . Thus, consumers of type $\theta \in [r / ((\alpha + \delta)q_{1} - q_{2}), (p - r) / ((\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_{2}))]$ will use the pirated version, and consumers of type $\theta \in ((p - r) / ((\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_{1})), r / (\alpha(q_{1} - q_{2}))]$ will buy the premium version.

If $\frac{1-q_{2}}{q_{1}-q_{2}}<\frac{p}{r}<\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{(\alpha+\delta)q_{1}-q_{2}}$ , we derive $\frac{p-r}{(\alpha+\delta)(1-q_{1})}<\frac{p}{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}<\frac{r}{(\alpha+\delta)q_{1}-q_{2}}$ . In this situation, when $\delta\leq q_{2}-\alpha q_{2}$ , it results in $r/(\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2})\leq p/(\alpha+\delta-q_{2})$ . Consequently, all consumers continue using the free version. When $q_{2}-\alpha q_{2}\leq\delta q_{1}$ , we obtain $r/(\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2})\geq p/(\alpha+\delta-q_{2})$ . Thus, consumers of type $\theta\in[p/(\alpha+\delta-q_{2}),r/(\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2})]$ will buy the premium version. When $\delta>q_{2}-\alpha q_{2}$ and $q_{2}-\alpha q_{2}>\delta q_{1}$ , it has $\frac{1-q_{2}}{q_{1}-q_{2}}<\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2}}<\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{(\alpha+\delta)q_{1}-q_{2}}$ . Therefore, we have $\frac{r}{\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2}}<\frac{p}{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}$ if $\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2}}<\frac{p}{r}<\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{(\alpha+\delta)q_{1}-q_{2}}$ . That is, consumers continue using the free version. When $\frac{1-q_{2}}{q_{1}-q_{2}}<\frac{p}{r}<\frac{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}{\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2}}$ , we have $\frac{r}{\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2}}>\frac{p}{\alpha+\delta-q_{2}}$ . Thus, consumers of type $\theta\in[p/(\alpha+\delta-q_{2}),r/(\alpha q_{1}-\alpha q_{2})]$ will buy the premium version.

Second, when $(\alpha + \delta)q_1 < q_2$ , we derive $\frac{r}{(\alpha + \delta)q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p - r}{(\alpha + \delta)(1 - q_1)} < \frac{p}{\alpha + \delta - q_2}$ . When the condition (1) $\delta < q_2 - \alpha q_2$ or (2) $\delta \geq q_2 - \alpha q_2$ and $p / r \geq (\alpha + \delta - q_2) / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)$ is satisfied, we derive $p / (\alpha + \delta - q_2) \geq r / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)$ . All free users continue using the free version in Stage 2. When $(1 - q_2) / (q_1 - q_2) < p / r < (\alpha + \delta - q_2) / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)$ , we obtain $p / (\alpha + \delta - q_2) < r / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)$ . Thus, consumers of type $\theta \in [p / (\alpha + \delta - q_2), r / (\alpha q_1 - \alpha q_2)]$ will buy the premium version.

## PROOF OF LEMMA 4

We consider consumers who use the pirated version in the first stage. They will update their quality perception in the second stage. When $p / r > (1 - q_2) / (q_1 - q_2)$ , first we consider the situation of $\alpha + \delta_1 - q_1 > 0$ , and we derive $\frac{r}{q_1 - q_2} < \frac{p}{\alpha + \delta_1 - q_2} < \frac{p - r}{\alpha + \delta_1 - q_1}$ . When $\delta_1 > q_1 - \alpha q_1$ , it has $\frac{r}{q_1 - q_2} < \frac{r}{\alpha(q_1 - q_2)} < \frac{p - r}{\alpha + \delta - q_1} < \frac{p - r}{\alpha(1 - q_1)}$ . Then, consumers of type $\theta \in [\frac{p - r}{\alpha + \delta_1 - q_1}, \frac{p - r}{\alpha(1 - q_1)}]$ will buy the premium version. When $\delta_1 \leq q_1 - \alpha q_1$ , it has $\frac{r}{q_{1}-q_{2}}<\frac{r}{\alpha(q_{1}-q_{2})}<\frac{p-r}{\alpha(1-q_{1})}\leq\frac{p-r}{\alpha+\delta-q_{1}}$ . Thus, the first-stage pirates continue using the pirated version in the second stage. Second, when $\alpha+\delta_{1}-q_{1}<0$ , we have $\frac{p-r}{\alpha+\delta_{1}-q_{1}}<\frac{r}{q_{1}-q_{2}}<\frac{p}{\alpha+\delta_{1}-q_{2}}$ . Thus, all consumers continue using the pirated version in the second stage.

(2) $r > R_{6}$ . If $\delta > q_{2}$ , we have $\pi_{f}^{*} > \pi_{t}^{*}$ . Otherwise we obtain $\pi_{f}^{*} \leq \pi_{t}^{*}$ when $\delta \leq q_{2}$ .

## PROOF OF PROPOSITION 8

When $r > R_{5}$ , the optimal results in Proposition 8 are consistent with the results in Proposition 7. As a consequence, we discuss the results when $r \leq R_{5}$ . When $r < \frac{(\alpha + \delta_{1} - q_{1})(\alpha q_{1} + \delta q_{1} - q_{2})}{2\alpha + 2\delta - q_{2} - \alpha q_{1} - \delta q_{1}}$ , we have $\pi_{f}^{*} < \pi_{t}^{*}$ . When

For any $\alpha$ , $\delta$ and $\delta_1$ , we derive $q_1(\alpha + \delta_1 - q_1) / (2 - q_1) > \tilde{r}_2$ .
