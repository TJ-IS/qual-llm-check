# The Framing Effects of Multipart Pricing on Consumer Purchasing Behavior of Customized Information Good Bundles

Kim Huat Goh

Division of IT and Operations Management, Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore 639798, akhgoh@ntu.edu.sg

Jesse C. Bockstedt

Department of Management Information Systems, Eller College of Management, The University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721, bockstedt@email.arizona.edu

pplying behavioral economic theories, we hypothesize that consumers have sticky reference prices for individual information goods, but their perceived value for customizable bundle offers can be significantly influenced by the framing of a multipart pricing scheme. The potential impacts of these framing effects are measurable changes in consumer behavior and sales outcomes. We conducted a series of behavioral experiments and a large-scale natural field experiment involving actual purchases of customized information good bundles to confirm and analyze the hypothesized effects. We discuss the potential tradeoffs in economic outcomes that result from price framing (e.g., likelihood of sale versus size of purchased bundles) and the implications for information good retailers.

## 1. Introduction

Digitization of information goods makes them easily and cheaply distributed, transferred, reproduced, and reconfigured (Shapiro and Varian 1999). Because of these properties, information goods are particularly well suited for new sales strategies that involve disaggregation and customization. Increasingly, information goods sellers are providing consumers the ability to purchase information good subunits, such as songs or television episodes, that have been traditionally purchased in preset bundles (Akst 2005). Recent innovations in this context have also involved selling consumer-designed custom bundles of information goods. For example, several online services let consumers create customized music or video playlists (e.g., Playlist.com, Hulu.com) and multiple publishers have provided services that allow consumers to create custom books by combining content from several sources (e.g., Wiley, McGraw-Hill). The increased variety in information good product offerings brings about more options for consumers, and the low marginal costs of reproducing digital information allow for these products to be bundled in a relatively costless manner. Therefore, customization strategies can be easily implemented to offer significant value to the consumer. Multipart pricing schemes have become a common sales strategy in this context because they complement product customization well and can be used effectively in the sale of information goods (Bodily and Mohammed 2006).

The seller’s design choices for a multipart pricing scheme, such as the tariff, incremental price, and product constraints, can significantly influence consumer behavior, and little research has explored these issues. In this paper we address the following research question. How does the framing of a multipart pricing scheme affect consumer behavior in the context of customizable information good bundles? Building upon theory from behavioral economics and marketing, we develop a set of hypotheses that are tested using data collected through a set of controlled behavioral experiments (both within and between-subject designs) and a large-scale natural experiment involving real-world purchases of customized information good bundles. Our analysis provides strong evidence that the framing of a multipart pricing scheme significantly impacts a consumer’s willingness to purchase a customizable bundle of information goods, the content of the purchased bundles, and the consumer’s perceptions of the transaction, even when differing schemes result in the same final bundle and price to the consumer.

Decision making in the purchase of customized bundles is significantly different from that for fixed bundles and individual information goods. The consumer must not only decide to purchase but also determine the contents of the bundle. The studies presented in this paper apply behavioral economic theories to this new sales environment, and as a result this research makes several contributions. First, there have only been a handful of studies on the effects of multipart pricing schemes on consumer behavior, none of which have explored these issues for product bundles or information goods. This paper is one of the first to do so and provides insights relevant to information systems, marketing, and behavioral economics research. Second, the bundling literature has focused almost exclusively on seller-designed, noncustomizable bundles of goods. Considering the increasing trend of customizable information products and services, it is important to investigate the impacts of such sales mechanisms on consumer behavior and economic outcomes. Third, most research on the pricing of information goods has been built on normative economic theories and has ignored behavioral economic issues. This paper provides new insights based on behavioral economic theories and demonstrates that the technology-driven context of a purchase decision can have significant effects on consumer choices and economic outcomes. In general, although there has been research on various aspects of bundling, consumer behavior, and multipart pricing, no prior work has synthesized these streams of research and applied them to contemporary issues related to the distribution of digital information goods. This paper aims to provide such a synthesis; the results extend prior research in several disciplines and provide new insights that will benefit practitioners and researchers.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of related literature on framing effects in bundling and multipart pricing. Section 3 presents our research model and hypotheses. Section 4 provides the methodology and data for our behavioral experiments and field study. Section 5 discusses the analysis and results. Section 6 includes discussion of the theoretical and practical implications of this research, limitations, and opportunities for future research. Section 7 provides concluding remarks.

## 2. Literature Review

Customized bundling of information goods is a relatively new strategy that takes advantage of the low distribution and reconfiguration costs associated with digital information goods. Unbundling and customization can be useful strategies for selling information goods because they provide low-cost mechanisms that offer significant value to the consumer (e.g., Elberse 2010). Multipart pricing strategies allow sellers to flexibly offer customizable product bundles while managing fixed costs and the variable demand for information goods. Little research has focused on customized bundles of information goods, and even less on the related behavioral economic issues.

## 2.1. Bundling

Bundling provides several potential advantages to the seller, including reduced production costs, reduced transaction costs, complementarities among bundle components, and increased profits (Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999, Adams and Yellen 1976). Information goods are particularly well suited for use in bundling strategies because their low marginal costs allow for easy distribution and customization of products (e.g., Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999, 2000). Bundling allows sellers to offer a menu of different options and product configurations, which is very useful in segmented markets where consumers differ systematically in their valuations. The majority of research on bundling information goods has focused on optimal pricing (e.g., Hitt and Chen 2005) and the application of bundling strategies (e.g., Geng et al. 2005), but little research has explored the impacts of bundle pricing and framing on consumer behavior.

However, there has been marketing research on the general effects of price framing on consumer evaluation of standard, noncustomizable product bundles. Bundling can appeal to consumers because of its potential to reduce search, transaction, and installation costs (Monroe 1990). Consumers may even implicitly bundle individually sold items together to make the total price more appealing (Mulhern and Leone 1991). Several studies have demonstrated that the presentation of a bundle price can significantly influence consumers’ evaluations and choices (e.g., Drumwright 1992, Yadav and Monroe 1993, Chakravarti et al. 2002). Two key concepts underlying the research on consumer evaluation of product bundles are the reliance of consumers on reference prices and the differing effects of perceived gains and losses. Additionally, the manner in which the bundle price is presented can significantly impact a consumer’s propensity to make a purchase and his reliance on reference prices (e.g., Yadav and Monroe 1993, Gilbride et al. 2008). The general theme of this prior research is that task and context factors can influence how consumers perceive the value of bundle components and thus their purchasing behavior for product bundles. Customized bundling is a new phenomenon enabled by information technology that has not been previously explored in behavioral marketing, and we extend bundling research into the emerging context of customizable bundling sales strategies for information goods.

## 2.2. Multipart Pricing

Multipart pricing, or more generally nonlinear pricing, is a form of price discrimination. The most common form of multipart pricing is the two-part tariff, also known as affine pricing (Tirole 1988), in which the consumer is charged an initial lump sum or fixed fee as well as a per-unit charge. Often the fixed fee includes an initial allowance of the product or service.<sup>1</sup> Multipart pricing schemes help deal with issues of consumer heterogeneity in demand (Schmalensee 1981), where consumers will self-select into various choice sets with graduated price levels (Narayanan et al. 2007). Traditionally, research on two-part tariffs and multipart pricing has focused on monopoly pricing of public goods and usage-based services (e.g., Clarke 1971, Auerbach and Pellechio 1978). Only recently have researchers, mostly in marketing, begun to explore the effects of multipart pricing on consumer behavior (e.g., Lambrecht and Skiera 2006, Train et al. 1987), including work on fixed-fee framing effects (Danaher 2002, Ho and Zhang 2008) and the role of uncertainty (Lambrecht et al. 2007, Iyengar et al. 2008, Redden and Hoch 2010) in a consumer’s evaluation of a two-part tariff.

In practice, multipart pricing schemes have become effective sales strategies for information goods and services (Lambrecht et al. 2007, Bodily and Mohammed 2006). For example, it is common in the video game industry for production companies to offer the standard game for a fixed fee (e.g., \$4.99 for the basic smartphone version of the game) and provide additional downloadable content at a per-unit price (e.g., \$0.99 for each additional game level or feature). However, little to no research has explored the impacts of this class of pricing strategies on consumer behavior and sales outcomes. We address this gap in the research and provide insights on the framing effects of multipart pricing schemes in the purchase of customized information good bundles.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Framing and the Attractiveness of a Customized Bundle Offer

Prior research has suggested that when a consumer assesses the attractiveness of a price, the first step in the process is to compare the given price against a reference price (e.g., Niedrich et al. 2001, p. 340), which tends to be based on prior purchase experiences and past prices (e.g., Kalyanaram and Winer 1995, Johnson and Schkade 1989). These initial expectations impact future purchase decisions (e.g., Ariely et al. 2003, Ariely 2006) and the evaluation of product bundles (Chakravarti et al. 2002, Suri and Monroe 1999). Evidence suggests that a consumer’s initial perceptions of a product’s price tend to be “sticky” and not easily affected by later decisions and purchasing situations; once a consumer becomes used to paying a certain price for a product, it is typically difficult to modify those perceptions.

However, other research suggests reference prices can be affected through framing of an initial offer price (Heath et al. 1995) and that reference price points serve to frame the consumer’s choice process in a purchase decision (Monroe and Chapman 1987). Therefore, in order to understand how the framing of a multipart pricing scheme impacts a consumer’s evaluation of a customized bundle offer, we first must understand how the multipart pricing scheme impacts his reference prices. In the context of information goods, consumers likely have prior purchasing experiences and thus prior price expectations for individual information goods (e.g., \$0.99 for a digital song). Additionally, two-part tariff pricing schemes separate the fixed fee and allowance from the per-unit price. Therefore, we argue that the framing effects of the multipart pricing scheme on the consumer’s reference price for an individual good will be minimal. Thus we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> The framing of the multipart pricing scheme for a customized bundle of information goods will not affect the consumer’s reference price for a single item.

Consumers evaluate price attractiveness by comparing their reference price against the offered price for a specific quantity of product, in a specific purchasing context, at a specific time (Niedrich et al. 2001). To assess the attractiveness of a multipart pricing scheme for a customizable bundle, it is important to understand the process consumers will follow to conduct this price comparison for bundle offers. Historically, economists supported the simple additivity assumption (Adams and Yellen 1976, Schmalensee 1984)—consumers’ valuation of a bundle is simply the sum of the values of each of the items. This view, however, has been shown to be an unrealistic representation (e.g., Guiltinan 1987, Hanson and Martin 1990), and two alternative theories of bundle evaluation have been proposed. The weighted additive model (Yadav 1994, 1995) argues that consumers apply unequal weights to products when they are evaluating the total bundle. Under this paradigm, decision makers are thought to anchor on the most important component in the bundle and then make adjustments on the basis of an aggregate evaluation of remaining pieces (Lopes 1982). Therefore, bundle items tend to be examined in decreasing order of perceived importance, and bundle evaluations tend to be biased toward the initially evaluated items. The reference-dependent model (Tversky and Kahneman 1991, Janiszewski and Cunha 2004) relies on the prospect theory value function and argues that individual bundle items are compared against a reference point and perceived losses have stronger effects than perceived gains with respect to the reference point.

What is common in all of the theories is that consumers assess the value of a bundle in a piecewise fashion; different items of the bundle impact the evaluation of the total bundle differently, and consumers assess components of the bundle separately if they are framed that way. How this applies in the case of customized bundles remains an empirical question. Framing of a multipart pricing scheme involves calibrating the relative price magnitude between the fixed fee and the per-unit charge. If it is likely that each piece of a multipart pricing scheme is evaluated separately (Danaher 2002), the framing of the scheme can significantly impact a consumer’s evaluation of the bundle offer. Therefore we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Framing of the multipart pricing scheme (i.e., presentation of the component prices) will affect the consumer’s perceived attractiveness of the customized bundle offer.

## 3.2. Assessing the Impacts of Price Framing on Sales Outcomes

Under a multipart pricing scheme, the minimum bundle size (i.e., the fixed fee and allowance) has the potential to act as an anchor upon which a consumer may base his decision to make a purchase. We expect the attractiveness of the minimum bundle to decline if the price-to-allowance ratio increases. The inverse relationship between price paid and bundle size is well supported in the literature (e.g., Yadav 1994). Ho and Zhang (2008) demonstrated that when a buyer views the status quo as a reference point (e.g., buying a single item) while evaluating a two-part tariff contract offer, the fixed fee often represents an up-front loss and additional purchases are perceived as a gain. The buyer may weigh the up-front loss more heavily than subsequent gains, thus impacting his evaluation of the full offer. Other recent research has shown that in a multipart pricing scheme, the framing of the fixed fee has a larger impact on consumer purchase choices than incremental per-unit prices (Lambrecht et al. 2007, Danaher 2002). Additionally, consumers may have trouble calculating costs in a multipart pricing scheme and rely more heavily on anchors (Redden and Hoch 2010). Janiszewski and Cunha (2004) found that consumers are poor at adjusting their evaluations of bundles and are highly susceptible to anchoring on the first quantities and items presented to them. Thus we propose the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Consumers presented with a less attractive initial minimum bundle tariff ( fixed fee and allowance) will view the overall bundle offer to be less attractive and thus be less likely to purchase a customized bundle of information goods.

General prospect theory posits that consumers view losses differently from gains and the context of a decision can significantly affect a person’s choice. For example, Tversky and Kahneman (1981) found that systematic preference reversals are possible when the same choice is reframed from a positive to a negative light. The reference-dependent model of consumer choice (Thaler 1985, Tversky and Kahneman 1991) argues that consumers have a higher sensitivity to losses than gains. Furthermore, Kahneman and Tversky (1979) showed that people do not ignore sunk costs and often make choices they perceive will help them recoup losses (see also Thaler 1980, Arkes and Blumer 1985). Upon entering a transaction a psychological link, called “coupling,” between costs and benefits is created and used in evaluation of the transaction (Prelec and Loewenstein 1998). Normative economic theories suggest that a higher minimum tariff in multipart pricing will result in lower consumption of additional units because of the income effect. However, behavioral economic theory suggests that a higher tariff may result in sunk cost biases, which lead to increased consumption of additional units (e.g., Ho and Zhang 2008, Thaler 1980) to lower the average cost for each unit in the bundle. For example, Drumwright (1992) showed that consumers are willing to purchase more automobile options when an option that resulted in a consumer surplus was bundled with options resulting in a consumer deficit.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, we propose the following:

## 4. Data and Methodology

We adopted a holistic approach to test our hypotheses with six controlled behavioral experiments and a natural field experiment. Testing of hypotheses related to consumer thought processes is most effective using controlled behavioral experiments, as compared to other research methods (Box et al. 2005). We utilized both between-subjects and within-subjects designs to demonstrate robustness of the results and ensure that the observed results were not driven solely by idiosyncratic characteristics of the study participants. A common drawback of behavioral experiments, however, is reduced realism, and it often remains an empirical question whether observed behavioral phenomenon actually exist in practice, beyond the noise of conflicting exogenous factors. To address these issues, we also collected data from a natural field experiment in which a music retailer experimented with different pricing schemes for customizable bundles across multiple retail locations. The results of our field study support our hypotheses with actual consumer purchasing data and demonstrate the practical significance of our hypotheses beyond behavioral experiments. Furthermore, the use of natural field experiments is especially important for the testing of hypotheses that relate to tangible purchasing outcomes (i.e., Hypotheses 3 and 4), where one can objectively quantify the effects of price framing on actual sales. In our econometric analysis, we also employed several estimators for robustness and to control for potential issues common in field studies, such as selection and treatment bias.

## 4.1. Methodology for Controlled Behavioral Experiments

We conducted a series of six controlled behavioral experiments (referred to as CE1 to CE6 going forward). In each experiment, participants were presented a customizable bundling scenario for online streaming movie rentals. For CE1–CE4 and CE6, participants (residing in the United States) participated through an online experiment, which was distributed and managed through an online survey service. Participants were paid an average of \$15–\$20 per hour and given a chance to win a \$100 cash prize. CE5 was a replication of CE3 and CE4 with college students in a class setting using paper-based stimuli and response forms. The student participants in CE5 did not receive any payment or course credit for participation. This replication was performed to ensure that the results are generalizable across different subject sampling pools with different motivations for participation.

We chose online streaming movie rentals as the focal product in our experiments because they are contemporary information goods with which many consumers are familiar. Additionally, they differ from the information goods used in our field data (customized music CDs), which helps to demonstrate generalizability of results across different types of information goods. We adapted the work of Grewal et al. (1998) to design our experiments. First, we conducted a series of pretests to establish the approximate fair market price of the referent product (i.e., online streaming movie rentals) and used this price to calibrate the prices of the customized bundle offers presented to participants in the experimental stimuli. Following the suggestions in Janiszewski and Cunha (2004), the prices were calibrated to at least twice the reference price to invoke negative utility sentiments. We also pretested to ensure that participants were, in general, knowledgeable about the product and able to understand the experimental stimuli. Full details on the stimuli and questions used in these experiments can be found in online Appendix A (available at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/isre.1120.0428).

In CE1 through CE5, we used a between-subjects design and participants were randomly presented one of two customizable bundle offers for the online streaming movie service: \$10 for the first movie plus \$2 for each additional movie (referred to as Treatment 1 going forward) and \$14 for the first three movies plus \$2 for each additional movie (referred to as Treatment 2 going forward).<sup>3</sup> The price for adding movies to the bundle is the same in both schemes, and for bundles of at least three movies the total bundle price is identical in both schemes. The difference in framing of the minimum bundle requirement permits us to study the effects of fixed-fee and allowance framing on purchasing decisions as outlined in our hypotheses. Participants were then asked a series of questions to determine their valuations and perceptions of the bundle offers. Although the pricing manipulations were the same, multiple experiments were conducted, each with different questions and measures, to minimize priming effects. Therefore, each experiment focused on a specific set of questions related to our hypotheses. In CE1, participants were presented a pricing scheme and asked to provide a fair price for a single streaming movie rental. In CE2, participants were presented a pricing scheme and asked to rate the attractiveness of the offer assuming a purchase of a bundle of three movies. In CE3, participants were asked to rate the attractiveness of the initial bundle offer and the full offer and to estimate their likelihood of making a purchase. In CE4, participants were asked the size of the bundle they anticipated creating if they chose to purchase the service.

We also conducted a within-subjects experiment (CE6) as an additional safeguard against acrosssubject bias (even though randomization of treatments in CE1–CE5 minimized this bias). Participants in CE6 were exposed to two price treatments: \$9.50 for the first movie plus \$1.50 for each additional movie (referred to as Treatment 3 going forward) and \$13.50 for the first three movies plus \$1.50 for each additional movie (referred to as Treatment 4 going forward). Note that the assumed less attractive pricing scheme (\$9.50 for the first movie) is actually cheaper than the assumed more attractive pricing scheme for all bundle sizes. We purposely chose this arrangement to test whether a price framing can lead to situations where a person perceives a worse price deal as more attractive than a better price deal. As observed in prior studies (Yadav and Monroe 1993, DelVecchio et al. 2007), alternative frames that are straightforward<sup>4</sup> are seldom presented to the same subjects for comparison because respondents are able to observe the equivalence of outcomes through simple arithmetic. Because this was a within-subjects design, we introduced a filler task between each treatment to reduce the likelihood of straightforward arithmetic comparison of the frames. We also changed the pricing frames slightly to ensure greater generalizability and introduced cent-endings to reduce the effects of recall and comparison across treatments. For each treatment in CE6, subjects were asked a series of questions similar to those used in CE1–CE5. The order each price framing treatment was presented to a participant was randomized to prevent ordering effects.

We captured demographic information in all experiments, which was subsequently used as covariates in our statistical analysis. The demographics collected include age, gender, knowledge of streaming movies, average number of movies rented per month, and annual household income. A total of 454 subjects participated in the four experiments. Each subject participated in only one of the experiments. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the respondents’ demographics. Results of the controlled behavioral experiments are presented in §5.

## 4.2. Field Experiment Data

We obtained 20 months of browsing and sales data from a North American firm that sold customizable bundles of songs (as a custom compact disc) at brickand-mortar retail points-of-sale. Data were collected from January 1, 2005, through September 9, 2006, on the browsing activity and sales of music from digital kiosks located at eight retail locations dispersed geographically across the United States. Each retail location used two or more digital kiosks; each kiosk accessed a centralized digital music library that contained both full albums (more than 70,000, whose tracks could be sold separately) and other individual music tracks (more than 1 million). In all, 153,190 individual browsing sessions took place, of which 7,046 (∼ 5%) resulted in the purchase of a customized bundle of songs.

Using the digital kiosks, consumers purchased music by creating a custom playlist containing music tracks from artists and albums of their choice. Song samples were available to the consumers, and the custom playlists were subsequently burned to physical CDs at the retail location upon purchase. The number of tracks in a customizable playlist was limited only by the space on the CD. Two multipart pricing schemes for the custom CDs were employed in a random fashion across the retail locations; the music kiosk company was experimenting with pricing models at the time of data collection, thus providing an ideal setting for a natural experiment. The two pricing schemes were \$3.99 for the first song and \$0.99 for each additional song (referred to as Pricing Scheme #1 going forward) and \$4.99 for the first two songs and \$0.99 for each additional song (referred to as Pricing Scheme #2 going forward).<sup>5</sup> Similar to the schemes used in our behavioral experiments, both pricing schemes used by the music retailer had practically identical prices for bundles of at least two songs (with a maximum difference of \$0.01). Each kiosk had access to the same centralized database of songs, and store locations were either identical or very similar in terms of brand and sales environment (we take care to control for location in our analysis). It is important to note that each store employed only one pricing scheme, and the pricing scheme was identical for all retail outlets within a geographic location. Therefore, it is highly unlikely that consumers were aware of the alternative pricing scheme.

## 4.3. Methodology for Field Experiment

We utilized browsing and sales data to test the impact of price framing on sales probability and bundle size (H3 and H4). In our model, we assume that the probability of a successful sale given that the consumer has started a kiosk session is a function of the pricing scheme and other control variables. We utilize variables related to session usage patterns prior to purchase (i.e., total session duration and the mean time browsed per song) as important controls because framing effects can be impacted by the length of time an individual spends on processing information cues (Svenson and Benson 1993). Also, we split the sample into two subsamples based on the transaction date (0 for first half of the sample and 1 for later half). This variable (labeled as time control) controls for any potential novelty or learning effects because our sample extends 20 months. Though it was not possible to determine if a consumer was a repeat user in our data set, we believe this dummy variable can act as a reasonable proxy. For kiosk sessions that resulted in a sale, we further controlled for the composition of the bundle by measuring the number of unique artists within the bundle of songs.

Although the pricing schemes were randomly assigned to the stores, we collected additional demographic data on the residents living within approximately five miles of the store location to further ensure that consumer heterogeneity across store locations did not impact the results. The demographic data collected were based on U.S. ZIP code (source: U.S. Census at http://factfinder.census.gov/) and included the mean income level, the population size, and percentage of population older than 65 years. These variables measure the socioeconomic characteristics of the potential customer base for the store (Kelton and Rebelein 2007) and are good indictors of the purchasing power, mobility, and size of the customer base. To further control for any heterogeneity due to the store characteristics, we collected the number of full music album downloads (noncustomizable bundles) sold in the store from the kiosk. This variable is a proxy for the overall customer traffic a store experiences on average. We also measured the number of high-end department stores<sup>6</sup> within a five-mile radius of the store to proxy the market structure and sales environment around the retail location. Finally, we also included the size of the physical store (in square feet) where the kiosks were located, which serves as a proxy to control for the selection of physical CDs available for purchase in the store. The descriptive statistics of the data are provided in Table 2.

4.3.1. Research Model for Field Experiment. In our empirical model we consider the purchasing processes that consumers typically follow. Consumers adopt a variety of shopping patterns; however, these patterns can be broadly classified into casual (recreational) buying or directed buying behavior (see Moe 2003, Bellenger and Korgaonkar 1980). Under the casual buying process, a consumer of a customizable bundle would browse the items $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ songs) while casually adding items to the bundle. The decision to purchase the bundle is made if the value derived from the bundle exceeds a particular threshold (i.e., purchase is determined given bundle size and contents). This form of casual browsing and deliberation leading to purchase is widely recognized within the marketing literature (Moe 2003, Babin et al. 1994) and is suggested to represent a sizable proportion of consumers (Bellenger and Korgaonkar 1980). Therefore, we believe casual buying is most appropriate for our empirical model, but we also consider directed buying as an alternative estimator (see §4.3.2 and Appendix B).

Notably, in the data collected, there existed numerous browsing sessions that did not result in a sale, and only those that resulted in a sale have a corresponding purchased bundle. Hence, to obtain an unbiased estimate of the impact of initial minimum bundle price on the final bundle size, we have to consider the potential effects of sample selection (Heckman 1979). Another key characteristic of this context is that the dependent variable (i.e., bundle size) is a count variable that takes on only positive integer values, and therefore conventional least-squares estimation is not appropriate. Poisson or negative binomial regression models provide more efficient estimators with count dependent variables. Although overdispersion was not an issue, we used both Poisson and negative binomial (NB) regressions to estimate the model and triangulate our results.

Considering the purchasing process and sample selection constraint, we model the purchasing outcome as a conditional probability function:

$$
P (y _ {i} = n \mid z _ {i} = 1),\tag{1}
$$

where $y _ { i }$ is the size of bundle $i , z _ { i } = 1$ if a purchase has been made and 0 otherwise, and

$$
y _ {i} = f (x _ {i}),\tag{2}
$$

where $x _ { i }$ represents variables that impact the final bundle size. They include the pricing scheme, session duration, time control, time browsed per song, average population annual income, population size, percentage of population greater than 65 years old, full album sales, number of high-end stores, size of physical store, and number of artists in a bundle. In this conditional probability function, the purchasing state of the bundle remains at 0 during the browsing and deliberation process—common in a causal buying setting. However, this state changes to 1 when the bundle crosses a hurdle such that the bundle is of high enough value to the consumer that a purchase is made. To estimate this, we express the log-likelihood function of the process that determines the bundle size to be the following:

$$
\mathscr {L} = \ln [ P (z _ {i}) ] + \{\ln [ 1 - P (z _ {i}) ] + \ln P (y _ {i}) \}\tag{3}
$$

Mullahy (1986) showed that the log-likelihood function for this two-phase process can be estimated by incorporating a logit model to estimate the first phase, when the effective bundle size remains zero because no purchase has been made (i.e., browsing phase of the causal buyer), followed by a zero-truncated Poisson (or a zero-truncated NB) model to estimate the second phase, when the bundle size becomes a positive integer and the value hurdle has been crossed. The log-likelihood function (3) thus can be translated into a Poisson (or NB) hurdle model with a two-part log-likelihood function. Estimates for the empirical relationship are obtained by simultaneously estimating the maximum likelihood estimators for the two-part log-likelihood function.<sup>7</sup> The first part of the log-likelihood function estimates the probability of sale (Hypothesis 3) and the second part of the log-likelihood function estimates the bundle size (Hypothesis 4) conditional upon the presence of a sale (see Hurdle Model 1 in Table 3, §5).

4.3.2. Alternative Estimators: Unequal Treatment Sizes and Directed Buying Process. In this section, we discuss eight alternative estimators that represent departures in both statistical and conceptual assumptions from our main regression. These estimators are required to address four issues: (1) unequal treatment sizes; (2) differences in transformation of estimators in the model (i.e., probit versus logit; poisson versus NB); (3) conceptual differences in buying process (e.g., casual versus directed buying processes); and (4) use of different control variables. Triangulating these alternative estimators demonstrates the robustness of our main regression results.

Unequal Treatment Sizes. Treatments with unequal sample sizes are common in field experiments (D’Agostino 1998) and these generally do not cause biases if the treatments are administered randomly (Schulz and Grimes 2002). In the case of our field experiment, 76.5% of the subjects were presented with Pricing Scheme #1. Although pricing treatments were administered randomly across different stores, there was a slight possibility that the unequal sample sizes across treatments might skew the empirical results. To mitigate this potential problem, we employed propensity score controls (D’Agostino 1998) in our estimation, which measure the likelihood that a subject is presented with a particular treatment in the first place and control for treatment selection bias. As noted earlier, our model also faced sample selection bias and required a two-stage estimation (i.e., the hurdle and selection-corrected models discussed below) to control for the probability of a successful sale. To ensure both treatment selection and sample selection bias are controlled simultaneously, we used propensity scores as a covariate for regression adjustment (see Hurdle Models 2 and 4 in Table 3) as suggested in prior research (e.g., D’Agostino 1998, Berk and Newton 1985, Muller et al. 1986). Ideally, individual level characteristics should be used in the computation of the propensity score. However, the field experiment does not provide this set of granular information, so we used store-level variables such as the population size, percentage of population over age 65, sales of noncustomizable full albums, and number of high-end stores as predictors for the probability an individual visits a particular store because these factors are fair indicators of the population mobility, density, and consumer traffic within the store vicinity. We also computed the corresponding results without propensity score adjustments (Hurdle models 1 and 3 in Table 3) for robustness comparisons given that the likelihood of treatment bias is remote.

Estimator Transformations. A robust set of empirical results should remain relatively invariant across competing estimators. The hurdle model described in the preceding section combines logit with zero truncated Poisson regression. Based on Hilbe (2007), the discrete component of this estimator can be substituted with a probit regression and the Poisson component can be substituted with a zero truncated negative binomial regression. For comparison, we present an alternative regression model, which considers these other competing estimation forms (Hurdle Model 5 in Table 3).

Alternative Conceptual Buying Processes. We also consider an estimator that stems from conceptual variation in our model of the purchasing process; specifically, the case of directed buying (Moe 2003). Here, the consumer first decides to make a purchase and then adds items to a bundle until it reaches an acceptable size (i.e., bundle size and content is determined given the consumer had already decided to make a purchase). Prior to the purchasing process, a “directed buying” consumer does not lack any substantial information and is certain of her intentions to purchase prior to the purchase session. This purchasing process differs from our earlier representation of a casual buyer where the consumer hedonically searches and deliberates on the items available. Although prior research has suggested that consumers are more likely to search and deliberate during the purchase of an experience good (Huang et al. 2009), conceptually, it is important to show that competing forms of empirical abstraction of the purchasing process yield similar results. For this alternative estimator, the purchasing outcome is represented by the same conditional probability shown in Equation (1), and we consider the directed buying as a two-stage process. The first is a binary process where the individual decides whether to make the purchase or not, and the second involves the individual creating the bundle, the opposite of the stages in the casual buying process. This two-stage process is similar to that of sample selection estimation suggested by Heckman (1979). However, because the final dependent variable (bundle size) is a count variable, standard Heckman regression is not appropriate. We therefore applied a modified sample selection adjusted Poisson model for this context. Details on this estimation are provided in Appendix B, and the results are labeled as the Selection-corrected Poisson regression in Table 3.

The results still showed no significant difference in reference prices between price framings (F = 00296, p-value = 00587). To provide stronger evidence of equivalence, we also applied the two onesided tests (TOST) technique common in experimental research using randomized treatments (Barker et al. 2002, Rogers et al. 1993) to test the equivalence of outcomes. Here, we test for a difference in treatment groups, , in both negative and positive directions;  is commonly set at 20% of the treatment group’s mean (Rogers et al. 1993). Results from TOST rejected the null hypotheses of nonequivalence in reference prices (both p-values < 0005). The results from CE1 suggest that, despite exposure to different price framings, the internal reference prices for consumers are “sticky” and do not change because of framing effects, supporting H1.

Results for H2. In CE2, participants were randomly presented with a pricing scheme and asked to rate the attractiveness of the offer, assuming they were to purchase a bundle of three movies. Note that for both pricing schemes, the final price paid and products received were identical: \$14 for three movies. The only difference is in the framing of the pricing scheme, where subjects in Treatment 1 were presented with the offer to pay \$10 for the first movie and \$2 for subsequent units and in Treatment 2 the subjects are presented with the offer to pay \$14 for three movies. The questioning we used is adapted from Grewal et al. (1998) and Niedrich et al. (2001)

to elicit the subjects’ perceived attractiveness of the bundle. Specifically, we asked participants to rate on a scale from 0, representing a “very poor deal,” to 10, representing a “very good deal,” “How good of a price deal do you think this would be?” We found that the treatment groups exhibited significant differences in the perceived attractiveness (3.25 versus 4.85, t = −20246: p-value < 0005). Subjects presented with Treatment 2 on average found it to be more attractive than the pricing scheme in Treatment 1, supporting H2. We also observed a similar significant difference in perceived attractiveness (F = 30871: p-value < 0005) after controlling for demographics.

Participants assess attractiveness of a deal by comparing the offer prices against their internal reference prices (Niedrich et al. 2001). As shown above, our results support H1; the internal reference prices did not change with the price framing treatments. Therefore, any difference in perceived attractiveness of the bundle offer must be attributed to differences in perceptions of bundle offer prices. If subjects evaluate the attractiveness of a bundle by comparing the final bundle size against the total price paid, both treatments have identical offer prices (i.e., \$14 for three movies) resulting in similar price attractiveness. The results of CE2 show that this is not the case. The difference in perceived attractiveness of a bundle offer suggests that consumers are likely evaluating the bundle based on the price framings for each treatment. This also suggests that consumers do not assess the attractiveness of the offer at the end of the bundling process, an interesting result that is especially applicable in the context of customizable bundles. The valuation process is more likely to be a piecewise process aligned with the price framing, where consumers assess the value of the purchase throughout the bundling process.<sup>8</sup>

However, we find that this is not the case; instead, the purchases made under the pricing scheme with the smaller minimum bundle size, Pricing Scheme #1, had a higher mean bundle size, as we hypothesized in H4.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Contributions and Implications

One of the key findings in this paper is that through framing of the minimum bundle tariff, we observed a potential tradeoff between probability of sale and eventual bundle size. A less attractive minimum bundle tariff significantly reduces the consumer’s willingness to purchase; however, consumers that eventually purchase under this tariff tend to purchase larger bundles. The results of our studies demonstrate that the decision making process for consumers of customized bundles is significantly different than for standard fixed bundles and suggest significant implications for practice. Although customized bundling gives consumers greater control over the content of bundles they purchase, from the sellers’ point of view, this may translate into potentially lower sales volumes due to smaller bundles sold and higher transaction costs. If all units of a customizable bundle are priced identically, a consumer may be less compelled to create a bundle of larger quantity because she can always return to purchase additional items. This phenomenon is evident in practice: more than one-third of all transactions on Apple’s iTunes consist of single song purchases and the median size purchase is only three songs (Bernoff 2006). Such small bundles are less profitable (possibly unprofitable) when transaction costs are considered.<sup>9</sup> Results from our research show that a well-framed multipart pricing scheme may encourage a consumer to purchase larger bundles<sup>10</sup> with the caveat that a less attractive minimum tariff might turn away consumers altogether.

To further understand the impact these tradeoffs have on real sales and revenue, we present Table 7, which summarizes relevant revenue figures for the stores that participated in the field study. In general, we see a slightly higher sales conversion rate for stores that utilized Pricing Scheme #2, which agrees with our results. The average bundle sale price is higher under Pricing Scheme #1 and stores using Pricing Scheme #2 produced higher average revenue per kiosk session. This comparison implies that although Pricing Scheme #1 was associated with larger bundle sizes, the extra revenue from larger bundles did not make up for the higher probability of sale at stores using Pricing Scheme #2 in this sample. It should be noted that this revenue analysis uses averages weighted by the individual store sales and sessions because the number of customers visiting each store varied across locations.

The use of a framing strategy to promote larger bundle sizes while maintaining successful sales outcomes is applicable in many retail settings. In general, it is in the sellers’ interest to maximize the total number of units sold, but transaction costs involved in the purchase might be substantial and not linearly proportionally to the sales quantity (e.g., credit card processing fees). For example, a daily sale of 100 units from 100 separate transactions might result in lower profits than a daily sale of the same 100 units from 10 transactions, after adding transaction costs. In situations where transaction costs are significant, proper calibration of the multipart pricing frames is essential. In cases where the demand of initial or small quantities is highly inelastic, it might be worthwhile to set higher prices for the minimum bundle tariff to promote larger bundle sizes. For example, in the sale of customized textbooks, sellers can price required chapters (inelastic demand) higher with incremental chapters of optional reading priced at a lower perunit rate. In such instances, the negative impact of a lower sales probability is potentially reduced because of the inelastic demand for the initial minimum bundle. The magnitude of these tradeoffs and the design of optimal multipart pricing schemes are important questions for future research.

Designing effective pricing schemes is a constant challenge for sellers; they must consider various factors such as costs, demand and supply characteristics, and market conditions. It is equally important for commercial success to understand the effects of pricing schemes on consumer behavior. The sale of information goods often involves new business models, many of which fail because of a lack of guiding principles in the design of the pricing scheme. For these new business models, sellers may have limited opportunities to successfully adjust their pricing because price changes often lead to significant consumer discontent (e.g., consider the recent problems plaguing the online video provider Netflix that were caused by pricing changes). Sellers of information goods are under significant pressure to get the pricing design right the first time, and the results from our study add a new perspective toward the understanding of the complex pricing design problem.

Normative economic theories often provide stronger face validity in their explanations of phenomena, and to quote Schumpeter, “[it is a] much better claim to being called a logic of choice than a psychology of value” (Schumpeter 1954, p. 1058). The application of behavioral economic theories to explain real-world phenomenon, in contrast, is never straightforward and requires extensive understanding and experimental testing to unravel the theoretical relationships. Despite the inherent difficulties, behavioral economic theories have an important role to play in understanding deviations in actual behavior from normative predictions—deviations that cannot be easily addressed by relaxing assumptions or dismissed as random errors (Tversky and Kahneman 1986). Customized bundling of information goods is an emerging phenomenon, and it is necessary to consider the role of behavioral economics in explaining sales outcomes in this context. We contribute to the literature of information goods bundling by introducing a different theoretical lens to understand, explain, and predict sales outcomes, a contribution that complements existing research and benefits both sellers and consumers within this industry.

## 6.2. Limitations

The research presented is not without limitations. From a theoretical perspective, it is possible that other decision-making theories could explain the results observed in this study. In particular, a set of theories related to decision making under uncertainty may be relevant. Regret theory (Loomes and Sugden 1982) posits that decision makers tend to choose outcomes that minimize the maximum possible regret of the choice set. A similar theory, Wald’s maximin model (Wald 1939, 1945), argues that decisions are ranked on the basis of their worst-case outcomes and the best choice is the option whose worst outcome is at least as good as the worst outcome of any other decision. More recently, Ben-Haim (2001) introduced the infogap theory of decision making under uncertainty that aims to help decision makers determine acceptable levels of uncertainty under which reasonable levels of robustness and opportuneness can be achieved. We recognize these theories as potential alternatives to the arguments presented but note some key differences making them less appropriate in this context. Specifically, in the context of information goods, samples are typically available (as they were in our field study), which reduces consumer uncertainty. Furthermore, although these theories may explain the avoidance of purchases, they do not directly explain the recouping effects observed in the purchase of larger customized bundles.

From a methodological perspective, we note two potential limitations. First, we recognize that our use of propensity scores as covariates to control for potential treatment biases in some of our estimations was not a standard application of the technique. Specifically, individual-level characteristics are typically used to generate propensity scores, which were not available in our field study. We used locationbased attributes as a proxy and note that the propensity estimations were intended as additional models provided for comparison and as robustness checks. Second, the results of our field experiment are limited because we are unable to measure thought processes of the consumers during the process of browsing and purchasing of the music bundles. To mitigate this problem, the behavioral experiments provide us the opportunities to isolate the stepwise process involved in the assessment of bundles. Further, we model two competing consumer purchasing processes (casual versus directed buying) in our econometric analysis.

## 6.3. Future Research

The results of the studies presented in this paper suggest that behavioral economic issues play a significant role in consumers’ purchase decisions for infor mation goods. Several promising avenues of research can extend these findings and provide further insights for information goods retailers. A large proportion of the current innovations in information technologies is consumer-centric and focuses on individual experiences. Therefore, there is a general opportunity for IS researchers to apply behavioral economic theories in their analysis of consumers and decision making in technology-mediated environments. Theories such as mental accounting, prospect theory, and transaction utility theory provide interesting perspectives on how people make economic decisions, and very little IS research has considered these types of theoretical arguments. Second, the economic effects of IT (e.g., lower search, transaction, coordination, and production costs) have rewritten the calculus of business and transformed retail strategies. The introduction of new retail practices such as customizable bundling, group buying, and disintermediation of retailers will provide an opportunity to incorporate insights from behavioral economics in the design of sales mechanisms, incentives, and pricing schemes under these new retail practices. The results presented in this paper show a strong impact of price framing on economic outcomes under modern IT-mediated retail conditions, and researchers should consider extending this work to explore the boundary conditions of these effects. Finally, this paper empirically analyzes the role of product customization in digital sales channels. Digitization has enabled unprecedented levels of customization in information goods, and there is an opportunity for researchers to consider how consumer-driven customization can be effectively utilized in new business models and sales strategies.

## 7. Conclusion

In this paper, we examined the effects of price framing on the consumption of customizable information good bundles. Applying theories from behavioral economics and marketing, we empirically tested four hypotheses about the decision-making process and purchasing outcomes related to customizable information good bundles. Triangulating empirical findings from controlled experiments with a natural field experiment, we conclude that price framing significantly impacts consumer behavior in the purchase of customizable information good bundles. Specifically, the framing of the minimum bundle tariff directly impacts the perceived attractiveness of a customizable bundle offer. This perceived attractiveness in turn leads to tradeoffs in purchasing behavior, where lower perceived attractiveness results in lower likelihood of purchase but larger resulting bundle sizes when purchases occur.
