# The effect of social network structures at the business/IT interface on IT application change effectiveness

## Abstract

The challenge of managing the relationship between a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s business and IT in order to derive business value from IT is an important topic on researchers’ and practitioners’ agendas. The focus of most related research and management actions has been on the top management or project management levels. However, con<sup>fl</sup>icts frequently arise within the line organization when applications are extended, enhanced, maintained, or otherwise changed operationally outside software development projects. This study focuses on the impact of relationships at the application-change level and strives to identify and explain favorable social structures for effective business/IT dialog at the operational level. We collected data in seven comprehensive case studies, including 88 interviews and corresponding surveys, and applied social network analysis to show that three social structures at the implementation level in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which IT applications are maintained and enhanced in line with business requirements: (1) interface actors connecting business and IT, (2) the relationships between interface actors and the corresponding unit, and (3) the relationships between interface actors and other employees in their unit. In three cases, less favorable structures are revealed that correspond to low application change effectiveness and software applications that do not meet business requirements. The other cases bene<sup>fi</sup>t from favorable social structures and thus enhance ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of business requirements and result in higher IT business value. This paper contributes to IS research by helping to explain why companies may not provide favorable IT services despite favorable relationships at the top management level and successful application development projects.

## Introduction

he business/IT relationship has frequently been described as problematic. It has been compared to ‘a troubled marriage in need of guidance’ (Ward and Peppard, 1996: 37) and business and IT employees have been called ‘strangers Ton a train who may travel together but never get to know one another’ (van den Hooff and de Winter, 2011: 255 referring to Day, 2007). A great deal of research has been undertaken on the top management level (e.g., Reich and Benbasat, 2000;

Preston and Karahanna, 2009; Tallon and Pinsonneault, 2011) and in the context of software project management (e.g., Lynch and Gregor, 2004; Markus and Mao, 2004), in order to understand and improve this relationship.

Nonetheless, the business/IT relationship remains highly problematic (van den Hooff and de Winter, 2011; Luftman et al., 2013). One reason seems to be that most research has limited its focus to the top management level (Dhaliwal et al.,

2011) and to software development project settings but does not provide guidance for line organizations (Walentowitz and Beimborn, 2011). A strong and effective business/IT relationship goes far beyond the strategic level (Chan and Reich, 2007) and project contexts (Sun et al., 2012). Operations and the change implementation level are crucial for strategies to become effective (Maes, 1999) and should be taken into account (Dhaliwal et al., 2011). Day-to-day operational processes among the business and IT line organizations (i.e., beyond dedicated project organizations) continuously extend, enhance, and maintain software applications in order to keep them aligned with business requirements (Cabinet-Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, 2011). Such efforts do not only include minor changes to the application, but also include signi<sup>fi</sup>cant enhancements and extensions that can be business critical.<sup>1</sup> Thus, the ultimate form of alignment, which is not ‘a matter of aligning business and IT goals but, rather, aligning business processes with IT applications, the IT processes, and supporting architecture’ (Holland and Skarke, 2008: 45), is ensured, over the long term, at the operational level within the line organization outside projects.

Unfavorable social structures between business and IT are likely to disrupt the alignment of business processes and thus the degree to which applications meet business requirements. In BeSafe II, one of the cases studied in this paper, employees from the business side and the IT unit do not have a good relationship: they struggle with responsibilities and require lengthy and con<sup>fl</sup>ict-laden discussions to agree on the details of changes. The IT unit fails to maintain IT applications satisfactorily and even though all parties share the same strategic aim if implementing market requirements quickly, the actual situation is marked by excessively long implementation cycles and poorly implemented changes.

Despite the importance of software maintenance and enhancement activities in the line organization, the literature provides only very few insights into relationships at this level. Some research, for example, in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of requirements engineering, brie<sup>fl</sup>y discusses the importance of interaction between business and IT employees at all levels and introduces the concept of liaison persons or ‘business analysts,’ who are intended to bridge the business/IT gap. In addition, practitioner frameworks such as ITIL and COBIT offer valuable insight into good practice design of IT processes but only formally describe organizational structures like roles and change management processes.

However, these research insights and the industry frameworks fail to address important issues. First, if business analysts or other formal liaison roles are referred to, they are only mentioned as an empirically observed mechanism to overcome the business/IT boundary (e.g., Schwarz and Hirschheim, 2003; Peppard and Ward, 2004); researchers do not theoretically explain their importance nor do they analyze how they create value. They are mentioned as a ‘role,’ re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the strong focus on the formal organization. Their social relationships in the informal, that is, actual organization, which determine their value much stronger (Schwarz and Hirschheim, 2003), are usually not considered. Hence, it remains theoretically unclear how liaisons function and practitioners lack insights into how to optimally set up liaison roles to create maximum value. Second, when discussing relationship contents, such as cross-domain knowledge or shared goals, researchers fail to address the structure of such relationships. They emphasize the importance of the contents, but do not describe where they need to be present (van den Hooff and de Winter, 2011). Third, <sup>fi</sup>ndings from top management research or from research on project settings cannot be directly transferred to the line organization. Relationships at the implementation level involve more actors than at the top management level and are more stable<sup>2</sup> than those in projects, thus making the consideration of social structures even more relevant. In summary, research into the business/IT relationship needs to be extended to the level of implementation where software applications are maintained, enhanced, and extended. Our study focuses on socio-structural patterns at the non-executive level and outside projects and strives to answer the following research question:

How do social structures between business and IT at the non-strategic level affect the degree to which the IT unit operationally provides, maintains, and enhances IT applications in line with business requirements and thus creates IT business value?

To answer this question, we follow an explanatory approach and derive a theoretical research model, which we evaluate based on seven case studies. In addition to conducting interviews, we collected survey data to conduct social network analysis (SNA) on these <sup>fi</sup>rms to compare social structures quantitatively across cases. In the following, we provide an overview of research on social structures at the business/IT interface and we introduce two theories that will inform the development of our research model. The case study results are then presented to evaluate the research model. Finally, the results, the limitations, and contributions to research and practice are discussed.

## Theoretical foundations

## Social structures in IS research

The literature investigating social relationships and their structure typically applies either a dyadic view or a structural view (Wasserman and Faust, 2007). The dyadic view focuses on the content of individual relationships between pairs of actors like trust, shared language, or friendship, and the consequences of these relationship contents (e.g., Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). The structural view considers the whole network structure composed of multiple ties and analyzes this structure and actors’ positions within this structure, as well as related outcomes (e.g., Granovetter, 1973; Burt, 1982). Our paper takes a combined perspective of both views and de<sup>fi</sup>nes business/IT social structures as social network structures of different relationship contents, which may occur at the interface between business and IT or within these units.

Business/IT social structures may be analyzed with regard to three different loci within the organization: at the top management level, in application development projects, and in the context of operational application maintenance and enhancement within the regular line organization. While there has been plenty of research on the <sup>fi</sup>rst two loci, knowledge about the latter is limited – although it is highly critical in organizational contexts to sustain business/IT alignment (Zolper et al., 2013). As highlighted in Figure 1, our research focuses on changes to existing applications (maintenance, enhancements, and extensions) in the line organization, that is, happening outside explicitly established and staffed software development projects. In the following we will refer to the focus of analysis as the ‘change implementation level.

Next, we summarize the state of current research with respect to social structures at all three loci.

## Social structures at the top management level

Social relationships between business and IT executives are of vital importance for business/IT alignment, and ‘may be the most enduring aspect of alignment’ (Chan, 2002: 108). Four social aspects recur in the discussion of relationships among top business and IT managers (Walentowitz and Beimborn, 2011): (1) IT executives need to interact frequently with their business colleagues to gain mutual knowledge about strategies (Johnson and Lederer, 2005) and to develop integrated business and IT plans (Kearns and Sabherwal, 2007; Valorinta, 2011); (2) Business and IT executives need to possess cross-domain knowledge (i.e., knowledge of the respective other <sup>fi</sup>eld of knowledge (business vs. IT)) because that facilitates their participation in each other’s processes and creates mutual respect and support (Preston and Karahanna, 2009; Valorinta, 2011), and IT managers knowledgeable of the business processes can anticipate future business needs (Peppard and Ward, 2004); (3) Top managers on the IT and business sides need to share a common goal, which is critical for aligning business and IT strategies (Preston and Karahanna, 2009); (4) Business and IT executives need to trust each other to discuss issues openly (Willcoxson and Chatham, 2004). In a nutshell, this discussion adopts a dyadic perspective, that is, it analyzes contents of individual relationships rather than the structure of a portfolio of relationships.

## Social structures in application development projects

There are two different perspectives on social structures in application development projects: the <sup>fi</sup>rst addresses projectinternal aspects (Kirsch et al., 2002; Brennan, 2008), such as the creation and implications of social capital (Chua et al., 2012) and clan control (Soh et al., 2011), and may focus particularly on virtual teams that collaborate across organizational, geographical, and cultural boundaries (Kotlarsky and Oshri, 2005; Persson et al., 2012). The second perspective stresses the importance of user participation in application development projects (Markus and Mao, 2004) and the interaction with the business side (Gasson, 2006; Rose and Schlichter, 2013) or even with external parties, such as consultants (Pozzebon and Pinsonneault, 2012), analysts (Shuraida and Barki, 2013), or software vendors (Goh et al., 2013). Scholars have developed several theories on how user participation contributes to application development success (Lynch and Gregor, 2004; Markus and Mao, 2004). Researchers have investigated how the interaction between business and IT should be managed during different project phases and what the success factors for spanning the business/IT boundary are to enable suf<sup>fi</sup>cient information exchange (Orlikowski,

2002; Levina and Vaast, 2005), such as liaisons from a project team to the business unit (Gasson, 2006) or among the business users who serve as key users and who are multipliers to and from the regular user base (Tarafdar and Qrun<sup>fl</sup>eh, 2010). In a nutshell, the dyadic perspective demands frequent interaction between projects and business departments, which can, from a structural perspective, be enabled by liaisons between a project and the business unit.

## Social structures at the change implementation level

Compared to the previous two loci of social structures in the business/IT context, social structures in the context of operational maintenance and enhancement processes have been discussed much less frequently, even though many scholars have acknowledged their importance (e.g., Chan and Reich, 2007; van den Hooff and de Winter, 2011).

The literature proposes that the relationship contents required at the implementation level are similar to those required at the top management level (shared knowledge, interaction, and personal and trusted relationships) (van den Hooff and de Winter, 2011). Chi and Deng (2011) argue that IT support staff needs to interact frequently with multiple user groups and other support employees to provide value. These rather dyadic views are complemented by some initial structural propositions. For example, several authors describe the concepts of liaisons between business and IT, who may be IT professionals who belong to the IT unit but are situated in the business, manage the relationship, ensure that IT is knowledgeable about business initiatives and resulting IT needs, and translate needs into requirements so that IT staff understands them (Schwarz and Hirschheim, 2003; Tarafdar and Qrun<sup>fl</sup>eh, 2009).

Yet, these are only <sup>fi</sup>rst steps toward understanding bene-<sup>fi</sup>cial social structures at the change implementation level. While van den Hoof and de Winter (2011) and Chi and Deng (2011) do not discuss structure at all, Schwarz and Hirschheim (2003) and Tarafdar and Qrun<sup>fl</sup>eh (2010) do not analyze how such liaisons should be connected to their counterparts and to colleagues in their own units. This is, however, important for ensuring that liaisons are effective.

Thus, while the top management relationship between business and IT and project settings have already been researched extensively, more research is needed to close those research gaps regarding the structure of change implementation level relationships. While the literature about top management relationships and project settings can inform this research, it is not possible to transfer these <sup>fi</sup>ndings mechanically to the implementation level assuming that all three loci follow the same rules.

Compared to the top management, a lot more actors are involved at the implementation level. At BeSafe II, for example, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 33 actors from the line organization (business units and IT units) involved in the application change process. Thus, analysis of relationships at the implementation level must consider more complex social structures among multiple actors. Should business and IT employees, for example, form a heterogeneous group or should they form a network with two cliques (Wasserman and Faust, 2007)? How should these cliques be connected? How should they be structured internally? Second, compared to project settings where teams are staffed and assigned on a temporary basis (Faraj and Sproull, 2000), maintenance and enhancement activities usually draw on stable social relationships among business and IT people in the respective line organizations, sometimes developed over years (regarding the assumption of stable relationships, cf. endnote 2). Obviously, these condi tions raise different questions regarding effective business/IT collaboration because existing organizational and social structures have to be taken into account.

After having discussed what is already known about social structures at the business/IT interface and thus can inform our research on social structures at the change implementation level, we will next introduce two theories that will serve as the foundation for model development.

## Social network theory

Since the relationship contents highlighted above (frequent interaction, shared language, common goals, trust) mirror the facets of strong ties (Granovetter, 1973) and social capital (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998), those serve as a starting point for our research.

Strong ties are de<sup>fi</sup>ned based on the ‘amount of time, the emotional intensity, the intimacy (mutual con<sup>fi</sup>ding), and the reciprocal services’ (Granovetter, 1973: 1361) that they provide. Strong ties foster ‘greater motivation to be of assistance and are typically more easily available’ (Granovetter, 1982: 113) and they increase the ability to exchange knowledge (Tiwana, 2008).

Social capital is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit’ (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998: 243). It comprises a structural dimension describing whom an actor knows and with whom he interacts, a cognitive dimension describing the degree to which actors share a common language or knowledge base, and a relational dimension describing how an actor knows other actors (e.g., trust, friendship) (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

There is considerable research on the effect strong ties and social capital have on the creation of knowledge (e.g., Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Adler and Kwon, 2002). New knowledge is created by combining and exchanging previously unconnected pieces of knowledge, making knowledge sharing a necessary prerequisite if knowledge pieces are held by different entities (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

Social capital theory argues that strong ties support knowl edge exchange and combination in four ways:

● They provide access to parties to exchange and combine knowledge. Interaction makes people aware that other people possess relevant information (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998) and is critical for the development of new ideas (Nonaka, 1994); shared cross-domain knowledge, especially the ability to understand each other’s language, is a primary condition for access to another person’s knowledge (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

● They foster anticipation of value through exchange and combination. Shared cross-domain knowledge provides an actor with the ability to understand roughly the other’s domain even before the exchange of information and thus to estimate the value that may result from further exchange with that other person (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

● They foster motivation to exchange and combine knowl edge. People who share information need to trust that the recipient will not exploit this information in a way that contradicts the focal actor’s interests (Misztal, 1996). Thus, if she trusts another person, she will be more motivated to engage in combination and exchange with that person (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

● They improve the exchange and combination capability. Shared cross-domain knowledge allows each party to understand the additional information the other party is providing and thus to evaluate and combine this new piece of information effectively with knowledge from the actor’s own domain (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998).

In addition to fostering knowledge creation through exchange and combination, strong ties increase solidarity, which is characterized by greater commitment, con<sup>fl</sup>ict prevention through less parochial thinking, and faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution (Adler and Kwon, 2002). This is due to the fact that actors are willing to compromise without fully meeting their individual demands, knowing that their counterpart might similarly compromise in the future (Adler and Kwon, 2002). This depends on the existence of common norms and rules, which can be interpreted as a shared vision or shared goal. When norms and rules are commonly accepted, adherence to them becomes a shared goal. A high degree of interaction among members of a network ensures that a violation of rules will most probably be detected. Thus, even people willing to violate the rules or norms may not dare to do so (Adler and Kwon, 2002). Solidarity is further increased by a shared language because it prevents con<sup>fl</sup>ict, avoiding a ‘feeling of distance’ and ‘internal con<sup>fl</sup>ict’ between interface actors and their counterparts (Nelson and Cooprider, 1996) with regard to what the other is talking about.

The discussion of strong ties and social capital adopts a rather dyadic view while other social network researchers rather adopt a structural perspective. Granovetter (1973), for example, argues that weak ties may also be advantageous because they are more probable to be bridging ties in a network, that is, ties that connect otherwise not connected subgroups. Therefore, they enable actors to tap into new knowledge pools (Tiwana and Konsynski, 2010). Since bridging ties create more and shorter paths in a network, they also enhance the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of knowledge <sup>fl</sup>ows in a network (Granovetter, 1973).

## Boundary-spanning theory

Boundary-spanning theory discusses mechanisms to overcome boundaries between different <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice such as organizational units like IT and business units (Levina and Vaast, 2006). One such mechanism is the existence of boundary-spanning individuals (Tushman, 1977), who are responsible for a unit’s interface to other units. Transferring knowledge between <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice is a major task of boundary spanners. They foster knowledge transfer by scouting for information relevant for their unit or advocating a unit’s information to the outside (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). To ful<sup>fi</sup>ll these knowledge-transfer tasks, boundary spanners need to speak the language of both <sup>fi</sup>elds between which they span the boundary (Carlile, 2002). Boundary spanners are comparable to the liaisons discussed in IS research (Schwarz and Hirschheim, 2003; Peppard, 2007; Tarafdar and Qrun<sup>fl</sup>eh, 2010).

Friedman and Podolny (1992) claim that boundary spanners may be socio-emotional brokers who socially integrate two groups. Levina and Vaast (2006) describe communitylike boundary-spanning practices. These are based on interpersonal ‘embodied’ (Levina and Vaast, 2006: 17)

relationships between two individuals, mutually developed by actors from different <sup>fi</sup>elds of practice over time. Thus, this type of boundary spanning builds on the relationship contents described above and on strong ties. Community-like boundary spanners collaboratively develop boundary objects and thus together span the boundary between two units (Levina and Vaast, 2006). Something different than these are market-like boundary-spanning practices, which are more ‘objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed’ (Levina and Vaast, 2006: 17) and do not rely on the social linkages between individual actors. Rather, actors from two units develop objects separately and exchange them facilitated by a broker (Levina and Vaast, 2006).

However, boundary-spanning theory does not explain which social structures to set up to ensure community-like or market-like boundary-spanning practices.

## Theory development

## De<sup>fi</sup>ning the dependent variable

To de<sup>fi</sup>ne the dependent variable of our theoretical model, we start with alignment in terms of how well IT supports business activities, which is – as cited above – the ultimate form of alignment (Holland and Skarke, 2008) or <sup>fi</sup>t. Seddon et al. (2010) de<sup>fi</sup>ne this as ‘the extent to which the functional capabilities embedded and con<sup>fi</sup>gured within [a business software] package match the functionality that an organization needs to operate effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently’ (p. 312). We build on this de<sup>fi</sup>nition and add operational IT services<sup>3</sup> by de<sup>fi</sup>ning our dependent variable as the degree to which IT applications ideally support business processes as well as the degree to which they are effectively changed within daily operations (‘application change effectiveness’).

We therefore focus especially on those IT services and IT processes that require signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interaction and knowledge exchange between business and IT, primarily at the change implementation level. As described above, it is within these processes and services that IT applications are extended, enhanced, and maintained in alignment with the business processes over the long term.

## Model overview

In the following, we develop the research model based on the theories and IS research insights discussed above. We hypothesize that three characteristics of social structures contribute to the effectiveness of operational application changes in terms of ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the business requirements: the existence of interface actors who connect their unit (business or IT) to the other unit (structural characteristic S0); the strength of the connection of these interface actors to many interface actors of the other unit (S1); and the strength of their connection to many actors within their own unit (S2). The following three sections provide more detail to these three structural characteristics; they de<sup>fi</sup>ne them in detail and develop the hypotheses about their positive impact on application change effectiveness.

## Existence of interface actors between business and IT (S0)

The social structure characteristic S0, that is, existence of interface actors between business and IT, builds on liaisons mentioned by IS research to bridge the business/IT gap, without further conceptualizing them (e.g., Schwarz and

Hirschheim, 2003). Building on the concepts of boundary spanners and bridging ties, we further develop this concept by theoretically explaining why they are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and by explaining how they should be integrated with the other unit (S1) and their own unit (S2).

Interface actors are persons who interact more frequently with the other unit (business or IT) than the majority of the colleagues in their unit. Since at the implementation level, compared to the top management level, many more actors are (directly or indirectly) involved, we argue that such interface actors are important for operational maintenance and enhancement of IT applications. From a social network point of view, these interface actors are structurally similar to the concept of a small cut-set in SNA. A cut-set is a group of actors that, if removed from a network, causes it to break into two or more sub-groups (Wasserman and Faust, 2007), such as business and IT. Since actors in a cut-set create shorter paths between actors in a network without creating redundancies like bridges do (Granovetter, 1973), interface actors in a small cut-set offer an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient network structure to exchange knowledge with other units compared to a dense network between two units (Granovetter, 1973). However, while one single interface actor would be most ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in terms of network structure (Granovetter, 1973), this person might be overwhelmed by the communication she has to channel and thus she might become a bottleneck.

Even though paths are even shorter when there is a very dense network between business and IT, that is, a lot of business actors interact directly with many IT actors instead of having a cut-set of interface actors, this social structure would be less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and probably even error-prone. If everyone in the IT unit gets contacted by various persons from the business side, all IT members would need to possess a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient portion of business knowledge to understand their counterparts and maintain ties with them to combine and exchange knowledge (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). However, building and maintaining such knowledge and ties is very time consuming and maintaining a dense network of such ties requires more effort than having a small number of interface actors (Granovetter, 1973).

Even when a dense network is already in place, it is still preferable to have interface actors between business and IT. In a dense network, every employee in the IT unit might be contacted by different employees from the business unit. Continual interruptions would make it impossible for these IT employees to do their work in an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and focused manner. They may also hear about the same issue multiple times and in contradictory ways, which will force them to invest time to resolve those contradictions. Even worse, different business actors may contact different IT actors regarding the same issue, which bears substantial con<sup>fl</sup>ict potentials in terms of coordination and prioritization.

Interface actors can increase their ability to interact with other units. Because they discuss frequently with actors from the other unit, they become accustomed to integrating knowledge from the other unit. They translate and transform knowledge they receive, and continuously practice their ability to do so (Tortoriello et al., 2012). We thus propose as baseline hypothesis that:

Hypothesis 0: Dedicated interface actors between business and IT are important for effective application changes in terms of extending, enhancing, and maintaining systems.

Interface actors strongly connected to many interface actors of the other unit (S1)

Research has shown that business and IT executives should be strongly connected, that is, they should frequently interact, share cross-domain knowledge, goals, and trust with their counterparts (Reich and Benbasat, 2000; Preston and Karahanna, 2009; Valorinta, 2011). In the following, we will transfer this argument to the change implementation level.

IT and business interface actors involved in maintenance and enhancement activities need to combine and exchange their pieces of knowledge about business requirements and implement them in such a way that IT applications can optimally support business processes. Such ‘know-how’ (Orlikowski, 2002) or ‘tacit’ knowledge (Nonaka, 1994) about how the business performs its tasks is particularly ‘sticky’ and thus dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to combine and exchange with other parties (Orlikowski, 2002). As discussed above, social network theory argues that strong ties and social capital enable individuals to exchange such tacit knowledge by fostering exchange and combination.

When business and IT interface actors interact frequently, they are easily accessible to each other when, for example, a critical change needs to be implemented. If they share crossdomain knowledge and can communicate using joint vocabulary, they will also be capable of discussing these issues because they can learn and understand what their counterpart is explaining to them by associating it with knowledge they already have about the other’s domain (Tortoriello et al., 2012). Knowing that the respective other actor possesses cross-domain knowledge and is thus in a position to understand the issue and contribute to its solution, the actors will anticipate value from engaging in exchange and combination with the interface actor of the other unit, rather than trying to resolve the issue in their own units. Anticipation of value is increased by frequent interaction and trust between the interface actors because they reduce skepticism (Tortoriello et al., 2012). Also, if they trust the other interface actors and hence expect that they will not take actions that are not appropriate from their point of view (Misztal, 1996), they will more likely share their knowledge and discuss problems (Nelson and Cooprider, 1996). Thus, the motivation to engage with other actors increases.

Thus, interface actors who are strongly connected to each other will have more motivation and opportunity to exchange and combine knowledge with each other, and thus will create new business/IT knowledge through combination and exchange of existing knowledge about how to best support the business in the future and thus will contribute to the effectiveness of application changes, keeping them aligned with business requirements.

If, due to a lack of tie strength, interface actors do not discuss such topics, issues might be only discussed within the units without involving the other unit, or one interface actor could bypass the other and directly contact people in the other unit. In cases in which the business unit tries to solve the issue on its own, it may implement a non-IT-related workaround or an IT-based workaround that is not integrated with the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT architecture (‘shadow IT’), which causes discontinuities and inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the process and reduces the <sup>fi</sup>t of IT applications and business process requirements (Behrens, 2009). If the business actor bypasses the of<sup>fi</sup>cial counterpart (the IT interface actor), the network becomes denser, resulting in the negative consequences of dense networks described above.

Further, the connection of business and IT interface actors via strong ties increases solidarity, that is, it fosters commitment and con<sup>fl</sup>ict prevention through less parochial thinking and faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution. On the basis of the shared goal to support business processes optimally using IT and a common understanding of how to reach this goal reinforced by frequent interaction and cross-domain knowledge, business and IT interface actors can build on their solidarity when they are facing issues while trying to ful<sup>fi</sup>ll requirements (Rai et al., 2009). Due to increased commitment, the IT unit may be willing to put additional effort into the requirement’s implementation, knowing that the business unit might be more willing to meet the interests of the IT unit in the future as well. Due to con<sup>fl</sup>ict prevention through less parochial thinking and faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution, more time can be spent on constructively solving issues instead of managing con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Hence, through solidarity, which is based on strong ties between business and IT interface actors, the requirement might be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled earlier or better.

Having argued that strong connections between interface actors are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial, we explicitly add that interface actors should also be strongly connected to many of their counterparts in the other unit. If interface actors are only strongly connected to one of their counterparts, they will prefer to interact with this person who in turn might be overwhelmed by the communication to be channeled.

In conclusion, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: Interface actors strongly connected to many interface actors of the other unit foster motivation and opportunity to exchange and combine knowledge and thus the creation of new business/IT knowledge, which in turn drives application change effectiveness (i.e., the degree to which IT applications are enhanced, extended, and maintained in line with business requirements) and thus contributes to IT business value.

Hypothesis 2: Interface actors strongly connected to many interface actors of the other unit foster solidarity, which in turn drives application change effectiveness and thus contributes to IT business value.

Interface actors strongly connected to many actors within their own unit (S2)

Next, we argue that the social structures within the IT and the business units play an important role for application change effectiveness as well. We propose that a strong connection of interface actors to many actors in their own unit (S2), that is, a central position of these interface actors within their own unit, is another important social structure improving application change effectiveness.

The above discussion about strongly connected interface actors assumes that interface actors on both sides possess all relevant information. This assumption is valid only when the interface actors are in a position to receive information from their own unit, that is, to exchange and combine information with the staff implementing the business processes or maintaining the relevant IT application. Ultimately, it is not the interface actors but the colleagues in their unit who need to transfer knowledge to the other unit. If the interface actor is strongly connected to many colleagues, the paths of within-unit actors to actors in the other unit are short and the ties they follow are strong. Hence, the other actors who ultimately possess the knowledge also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from an (indirect) trusted relationship with the other unit.

![](/api/attachments/RW3G92NC/fulltext/images/b78d2eae864fecfe9b2aa0281574bfd7f745b66a0f5df328c8ba583288d33e68.jpg)  
Schematic illustration of structural characteristics.

This bene<sup>fi</sup>t arises from the same mechanisms that we described above for the immediate business/IT interface and which are now applied within the business and IT units (Tortoriello et al., 2012) (cf. Jetu and Riedl, 2012 for related arguments in an software project context). Similarly to interface actors, non-interface actors will have more moti vation and opportunity to exchange and combine knowledge if they are connected to each other via such strong ties because they will then have access to each other, will anticipate value, and will be motivated and capable to exchange and combine information with each other (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998; Sasidharan et al., 2012). Hence, being strongly connected to many within-unit colleagues, the interface actor will be able to answer questions of and discuss issues with the other unit and indirectly connect many within-unit colleagues to the other unit (Tortoriello et al., 2012). If interface actors are not strongly connected to many within-unit colleagues and, thus, do not exchange information with them, they may not be able to discuss issues with the other unit, but may need to forward any question or issue to their colleagues, resulting in inef<sup>fi</sup>cient communication and increasing the risk of information distortion (Granovetter, 1973). Hence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Interface actors strongly connected to many actors within their own unit foster motivation and opportunity to exchange and combine knowledge and thus the creation of new business/IT knowledge, which increases change effectiveness and thus contributes to IT business value.

Figure 2 illustrates these structural characteristics schematically.

Finally, we argue that it is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to have either S1 or S2 in place but that both social structures are needed to reach effective and aligned enhancement and maintenance of applications.

If only S1 is present, business and IT interface actors may exchange information among each other, yet they themselves will not be informed suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently about critical topics that the colleagues within their own units face, or they might not understand these issues correctly. Analogously, if only S2 is present, interface actors might be well informed about what happens within their respective units, yet they will not be able to share this information with their counterparts. In either case, critical business and IT topics will not be exchanged and combined ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and correctly.

## Approach

To evaluate our hypotheses, we followed an explanatory case study approach, which is best suited to ‘how’ questions that require in-depth analysis of a situation that cannot be moved to the laboratory such as business/IT social structures (Dubé and Paré, 2003; Yin, 2009). Explanatory case studies employing multiple cases are used to test a previously developed theory (Yin, 2009).

We conducted seven case studies, collecting data from 88 interviews and 87 completed SNA-based questionnaires. Before entering the <sup>fi</sup>eld, we composed a case study protocol (Yin, 2009) describing the research question and hypotheses, the unit of analysis and our data collection and analysis approach. We followed this protocol strictly to ensure rigorous research and reliable results (Yin, 2009). We also ensured that our case studies followed all applicable guidelines and showed all applicable characteristics necessary in IS positivist research as identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Dubé and Paré (2003). Our approach is documented in more detail below. Appendix C provides an overview of the mechanisms applied to ensure validity and reliability in our case study research (Yin, 2009).

## Unit of analysis

Our unit of analysis is the IT change process (acc. to ITIL (Cabinet-Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, 2011)). The change process is an important part of the IT services portfolio within the regular organization (i.e., outside a project context) and contributes directly to the alignment of business processes and IT applications: within this process, changes to running applications are implemented and released according to business requirements. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we limit our focus to application changes that are both large enough to require some interaction between business and IT (e.g., for clari<sup>fi</sup>cation), but also small enough to not require establishing a distinct joint project team. Across our cases, such changes typically range from 5 to 60 developer days. An example is the implementation of a new insurance product in the sales system of an insurance company. As mentioned above, these kinds of changes are of major importance even though they do not usually require a major process redesign or another organizational change. In each case, we focus on changes to one particular IT application to clearly delimit the unit of analysis.

## Data collection

We collected data from seven case studies within four companies and sampled them so as to receive high variation regarding our core theoretical concepts and a range from very successful to con<sup>fl</sup>ict-laden situations, which allowed us to literally and theoretically replicate our hypotheses (Yin, 2009). On the basis of this replication approach, we were able to increase the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and ensure analytical generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Yin, 2009).

For each case, we conducted 360° interviews with staff (e.g., business users and developers) and managers from all involved business and IT units, which allowed us to gain comprehensive insights from various perspectives, hearing different voices and to avoid elite bias (Myers and Newman, 2007). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we interviewed all employees from business and IT who had an active role in the change process of the IT application in focus, that is, those employees who implemented or requested changes or de<sup>fi</sup>ned or communicated requirements. To get the complete picture, we also collected data from end users who work with the application. In case of larger applications with too many developers to interview them all, we collected data from all team leads responsible for developers and administrators working with the concerned application, as well as from representative key players among the staff.

In each case, we <sup>fi</sup>rst interviewed an IT manager to understand the application change process in detail. In later interviews, we focused on the social structures between business and IT. We asked the interviewees <sup>fi</sup>rst to draw their perception of the social network structure between the involved actors and to evaluate interaction intensity and relationship quality between the involved teams or individuals on a scale from 1 to 10. We then drilled down into these social structures and asked the interviewees to provide adjectives to describe the social structures with the respective other unit and with their own unit to identify different relationship contents. Following the call for more use of established interviewing methods in IS research (Schultze and Avital, 2011), we drew on the interview technique used in laddering to understand more deeply how the relationship contents in<sup>fl</sup>uence the operational maintenance and enhancement of IT applications. We also asked interviewees to highlight critical success factors and issues in their change process; those were often related to aspects of social structures, and gave us the opportunity to apply the laddering technique again (see Appendix A for the interview guideline and Appendix B for a brief description of laddering).

## Analysis of social network data

Using the questionnaire-based social network data, we calculated SNA measures to quantitatively evaluate the social network structures. These calculations were based on two networks drawn based on our data. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is undirected and re<sup>fl</sup>ects frequent interaction among the actors, with a tie between actor i and j representing that they communicate at least once per week either in person or by phone. It serves to identify interface actors (S0). The second network describes the relationships between actors, their content, and structure more comprehensively, capturing trust and shared knowledge. It is a directed network indicating whether an actor has a strong relationship to another one. A strong relationship exists when the average of answers to the different questions regarding relationship contents<sup>4</sup> are positive (i.e., >4 on a scale from 1 to 7).

In a nutshell, the pure existence of a tie is determined by the <sup>fi</sup>rst network described above (i.e., actors communicating at least once per week), while a strong tie is determined by the second network (i.e., relationship contents). With these two networks, we follow the call of Azarian (2010) to distinguish between social interaction structures and social relationship structures.

Using network 1, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned interface actors (S0) as those actors who interact with actors from the other unit at least once a week. Since in larger cases there may be additional actors who occasionally interact with the other unit in trilateral meetings involving the interface actor, we adjusted that set of interface actors to make cases with more actors comparable to those with fewer actors. To do so, we counted the number of actors from the other unit with whom any of the actors interacted. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned a threshold n as the maximum number of actors from the other unit with whom one actor interacted. Actors who interact with more than n/2 actors from the other unit are categorized as interface actors – this threshold allowed us to <sup>fl</sup>exibly adapt to different contexts and network sizes since it is tied to that person’s out-degree (toward the other unit) that is most strongly involved in the business/IT interaction. Moreover, the denominator ensured that the group of interface actors did not become too heterogeneous in terms of ego-network size. We calculated the percentage of interface actors among all actors providing us with the relative number of interface actors to quantify S0. <sup>5,6</sup>

To evaluate S1, that is, the strength of the connection of the interface actors to many interface actors of the other unit, we calculated the indegree centrality of each interface actor in the relationship network. We used indegree instead of the total degree in order to allow for asymmetries in the second network. For example, Actor A might consider Actor B an important consultant, but Actor B might not think of Actor A the same way. Since S1 focuses on an interface actor’s relationship to many interface actors of the other unit, we considered only relationships between the focal actor and her counterparts in the other unit, as shown in Figure 4a.

Thus, a value of 0% means that an interface actor is not strongly connected to any counterpart in the other unit, while a value of 100% represents strong connections to all interface actors of the other side.<sup>7</sup>

## Analysis of interview data

All interviews were transcribed by students and coded by the lead author. The coding process began with provisional codes (Saldaña, 2011) based on the concepts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from the literature: the existence of interface actors, the elements of strong ties, solidarity, etc. We used structural coding (coding of text segments with a code describing the topic they deal with to identify quotations relating to these concepts) and simultaneous coding (applying multiple codes to one text segment) to identify quotations that causally relate concepts to one another and to evaluate a construct’s strength (Saldaña, 2011). For example, we applied the code ‘cross-domain knowledge’ when an interviewee from IT described that the business counterpart was knowledgeable in IT, and simultaneously applied the code ‘creation of new business/IT knowledge through combination and exchange of existing knowledge,’ when she mentioned that IT knowledge from her counterpart enabled her to combine and exchange knowledge. Or we applied the code ‘application change effectiveness together with the evaluative code ‘strong’ to mark high application change effectiveness.

The resulting codings were compared with the research model described above in order to verify whether all theoretical concepts (individual codings) and causal relations between them (simultaneous codings) were mirrored in the interviews, whether new concepts or causal relations emerged, and whether details had to be added to existing concepts. Thus, the primary source of hypotheses evaluation was done based on the evaluation and explanation of causal relationships by interviewees, that is, the interviews explained why the causal relationship was present enabling us to challenge the propositions.

Further, we used the codings to triangulate data from different interviews within one case and to triangulate interview and SNA results (‘methodological triangulation’ (Yin, 2009)). To do so, we evaluated the social structures S0, S1, and S2 based on every interview. We then compared the evaluations from interviews within a case. When interviewees from the same case made contradicting evaluations, we contacted them again and discussed their statements with them. Thus,

## Empirical results

In a nutshell, our case studies support our propositions that both social structures at the interface between business and IT and within the IT and the business units are important drivers of application change effectiveness, that is, of applications being maintained and enhanced in line with business requirements. All cases build on interface actors (S0) and appreciate that some actors are primary contact persons for the other unit.

Figure 5 illustrates a pattern matching (Yin, 2009) for S1 and S2 and shows that all cases with strong social structures between many business and IT interface actors (S1) and with interface actors strongly connected to many actors in their own unit (S2) show highly effective application change processes. By contrast, the cases of BeSafe II, Money III, and AutoSupply have weaker social structures and have less effective application change processes. The following discussion of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed structures shows how the cases support our hypotheses.

## Existence of interface actors between business and IT (S0)

All cases showed dominant interface actors between business and IT. In Money II, though, their number was comparably higher and their interfacing role was less distinct due to the low overall number of actors involved. Table 2 presents an overview about the results.

First, the existence of interface actors bears the potential to make the IT change process more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient:

I think that developers prefer not talking directly to the business. The IT interface actors understand the developers issues better and faster and the developers appreciate a certain quality assurance by the IT interface actors.

One reason for higher ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency is their interface actors’ ability to translate between business and IT:

[As an interface actor], I speak the language of the business unit better, especially in case of critical issues. For example, when [the chief developer] explains a complex issue, he does it in a very detailed and technical way. The business is overwhelmed with such details.

However, Money I and BeSafe I, in particular, showed that interface actors may not be the only point of contact between business and IT. Other actors on business and IT sides interact with each other, too, which makes communication faster and more direct. Yet, this interaction is accompanied or coordinated by the interface actor and interviewees appreciate that the interface actor serves as main contact.

Interface actors strongly connected to many interface actors of the other unit (S1)

With respect to the social structure and relationship contents at the interface between business and IT, Table 2 shows that the successful cases bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from strong relationships in terms of relationship content along favorable structures, that is, strong relationships were present between many business and IT interface actors, while BeSafe II with weak S1 suffers from lower application change effectiveness. In all cases, the SNA data supported the interview-based observations.

Interviewees in the positive cases highlighted that they shared a trusted partnership, that they frequently met in person, and had shared cross-domain knowledge with the majority of their counterparts. All these aspects were deemed important for the dialog needed during the application change process.

In-depth knowledge of each other’s domain was highlighted as one key success factor for effective application changes because it increases the efficiency of knowledge exchange and combination. An interviewee from the business side in BeSafe I described:

The knowledge of the IT about [the business domain] is good, maybe very good. And that is very important, because otherwise I would have to explain everything multiple times.

All cases also highlighted the importance of frequent personal interaction. Personal interaction based on cross-domain knowledge of the involved actors results in fewer misunderstandings, and thus makes the communication more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. The IT manager in BeSafe I highlighted:

Now that we frequently meet in person, we have a much better relationship with the business [interface actor]. There are fewer misunderstandings.

Thus, self-orientation, a lack of trust and of common goals result in a lack of solidarity and time wasted on bureaucracy and con<sup>fl</sup>ict, leaving less time available for constructive IT change realization.

This case also showed that the anticipation of value through exchange and combination with the interface actors’ counterparts is reduced due to a lack of social structure strength. Especially the IT representatives frequently disregarded the contribution of their business counterparts, asserting that they had neither the knowledge nor the project management skills required. The [main IT interface actor] sometimes thinks he has managerial command. We [as developers] do not like that.

## Rival explanations

Testing rival explanations is a very powerful tool to validate one’s <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Yin, 2009). Of course, changes of IT applications are not solely a socio-structural phenomenon. In this section, we address other factors that may have determined the degree of change effectiveness in the analyzed cases.

With the exception of MyPower, AutoSupply, and Money III, all software systems in focus were criticized for their high age and the constraints this imposed on supporting business requirements. However, while this factor obviously in<sup>fl</sup>uences the effectiveness of IT change processes, we found ample evidence that interviewees could clearly differentiate between the service delivered by their counterparts in IT and the underlying technological constraints of old systems. Business interviewees understood the decision not to replace the system as a strategic one made outside the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of their counterparts in IT and evaluated the operational maintenance and enhancement of IT applications independently of this constraint.

Further, the competence of the involved actors, especially on the IT side, is a major factor for change effectiveness. If developers are not well-skilled, they will not be able to implement changes successfully. And indeed, the heterogeneous competence of developers was a major factor for low change effectiveness at AutoSupply. Yet, interviewees in all other cases explicitly underlined the outstanding competence of most persons involved in the application change process. Nonetheless, BeSafe II and Money III suffered from reduced application change effectiveness.

Another rival explanation may be scarcity of resources, which was brought up by many interviewees and, indeed, negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the processing time of changes. Yet, this is true for all cases, and hence does not explain differences between them. Further, there was no evidence relating it to the quality of implemented changes.

Geographical dispersion may also have an in<sup>fl</sup>uence, but we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any evidence pointing in this direction. To the contrary, when asked for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of geographical dispersion, actors in Money II, MyPower, and AutoSupply claimed that this was not an issue. They frequently travelled and used various collaboration tools, and in Money II and MyPower the good relationships among actors additionally helped them bridge the geographical distance.

## Discussion and conclusions

## Comparing <sup>fi</sup>ndings with theory

In general, all arguments of the theoretical model were mirrored in the case studies and our cases fully underlined the importance of all three social structures (S0, S1, S2).

Extended research mode

At the explanatory level, the case studies informed several extensions of the research model, which are highlighted in the dark-shaded boxes in Figure 6 and will be discussed in the following:

(1) We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed complementary knowledge to be an additional relationship content of great importance at the interface between business and IT (S1).

(2) In addition to the strong connection between business and IT (S1), we found that a strong connection among interface actors with actors in their own unit (S2) also in<sup>fl</sup>uences solidarity.

(3) We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several sub-concepts of the mediating factors described in the literature.

(4) We deepened the understanding of Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s antecedents of knowledge creation by dividing their outcome into higher quality knowledge creation through exchange and combination and more efficient knowledge creation through exchange and combination.

## We describe these extensions in the following:

Extension 1: Initially, we had proposed that the business/IT network at the change implementation level should be characterized by frequent interaction, trust, and cross-domain knowledge (relationship content). However, another important social aspect frequently highlighted during our interviews was complementary knowledge between business and IT. Complementary knowledge is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a combination of knowledge pieces that, if applied together, generate a higher return than the sum of the individual returns of knowledge pieces (Tanriverdi, 2005). This is a major source of cross-unit synergies (Tanriverdi and Venkatraman, 2005) and is at the core of social capital theory. Complementary pieces of knowledge need to be exchanged and combined to create intellectual capital (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). However, this is seldom discussed in IS research (Kim et al., 2010), which focuses more on cross-domain knowledge<sup>9</sup> . Complementary knowledge, as our study shows, warrants explicit recognition. Our focus on the implementation level, in contrast with the strategic and managerial focus of most previous research, might help explain its importance. While lacking shared cross-domain knowledge may be a substantial problem at the strategic level or in newly formed projects, we did not encounter many problems caused by lacking cross-domain knowledge at the implementation level. While the interviewees acknowledged a certain level of shared knowledge and mutual understanding to be necessary, they rather emphasized the importance of complementary knowledge. Shared knowledge is a necessary but not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition; the combination of complementary knowledge is what the parties actually bene<sup>fi</sup>t from.

Extension 2: During our model development, we argued only that strong connections among interface actors increase solidarity among them. However, our cases revealed that strong connections among interface actors and actors in their own unit also had an important in<sup>fl</sup>uence on solidarity, in turn affecting change performance. Such solidarity fosters more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient collaboration within a unit by preventing con<sup>fl</sup>ict and <sup>fi</sup>nger-pointing, enabling faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution and fostering commitment.

Extension 3: The results gave richer insights into the causa relations between social network structures and change effectiveness, calling for a more detailed conceptualization of the a priori de<sup>fi</sup>ned literature-based mediating constructs. It became evident that ‘access to knowledge’ consists of two sub-concepts: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the ability to engage in discussions without having to explain the basics, that is, having access to the knowledge of one’s counterpart without depending on an introduction from his side; and second, the reduction of misunderstandings, which reduces time spent on resolving misunderstandings and thus facilitates access to the counterpart’s knowledge.

Further, we found ample evidence of the relevance of motivation to exchange knowledge based on trust, which interviewees mostly referred to as greater openness to share critical information such as confidential information or warnings of escalations. Interviewees also frequently mentioned the capability to exchange knowledge. As they usually phrased it, the ability to combine complementary knowledge and contradictory perspectives in vivid discussions was a key success factor.

Second, with respect to solidarity, we found support for all three literature-based concepts: con<sup>fl</sup>ict prevention through less parochial thinking, faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution, and commitment. However, our interviews revealed a fourth sub-concept, which overlaps with fast con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution and less parochial thinking: easier persuasion. Interviewees described that strong ties between business and IT enabled them to persuade their counterpart more easily, both to prevent and to resolve con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

Extension 4: Finally, the case studies revealed two important intermediate effects of Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s (1998) mediating factors: (a) efficiency and (b) quality of knowledge creation through exchange and combination. Both in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which IT applications are maintained and enhanced in line with business requirements. The interviews revealed that openness and the combination of complementary knowledge in particular increased the quality of exchange and combination of knowledge and, thus, the effectiveness of application change activities, tremendously. Particularly, openness made more information explicitly available so that both parties could consider the entire picture.

Efficiency of knowledge creation through exchange and combination results from access to knowledge and from value anticipation. Interviewees explained that communication was much more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient when they did not need to explain basic information. This aspect of knowledge creation was also in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by solidarity, especially by faster con<sup>fl</sup>ict resolution. When less time is spent on explaining and resolving con<sup>fl</sup>icts, more time can be spent on productive tasks.

In summary, our case studies support the proposed theory and provide evidence for the hypothesized criticality of bene<sup>fi</sup>cial social structures at the level of change implementation outside projects. The case studies also helped us detail the theoretical model and further extend its ability to explain effective changes of applications in line with business requirements.

## Limitations

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings might be limited by a potential bias in the interviewees’ answers. They may have been tempted to present themselves in a better light or to overemphasize problems they perceive, knowing that the case study results would be discussed with the top management. To reduce this bias, we conducted interviews not only with people at the implementation level but also with their managers, and discussed topics with employees from all involved units to generate a consistent understanding. When contradictions arose, we clari<sup>fi</sup>ed them with the respective interviewees.

Another possible and important limitation of this research is that we were not able to send the SNA questionnaire to all actors in business and IT involved in the application change process (peripheral actors, in particular) and thus were not able to diagram the exact network factoring in their perspective. Simulation studies have shown that the incompleteness of SNA data can have substantial effects on the validity of SNA measures (Kossinets, 2006). However these effects are weakest in case of non-response effects and <sup>fi</sup>xed-choice designs in one-mode networks, which are those two threats that could occur in our data. In our case, missing values might have increased the centrality of those actors who returned the questionnaire because they themselves provided their links to other actors, while actors not completing the questionnaire were only part of the network when other actors named them. However, we made sure that all key actors completed the SNA questionnaire by identifying them in the initial management interviews and by adding additional participants while in the <sup>fi</sup>eld.<sup>10</sup> We also addressed this limitation by using the indegree for S1, thus annulling the effect of respondents’ answers on their own centrality. For S2, we focused on the core network as discussed above, so that peripheral actors would not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the SNA data.

Of course, since the content of the relationship network is not necessarily symmetric, calculating the indegree, which allows for asymmetric relationships, also for S2 would have been preferable. However, since the number of actors within the units is comparably higher, especially here we were not allowed to send the questionnaire to all of these actors. Thus, using only the indegree would not mirror the network correctly. Hence, we calculated the degree assuming all relationships were symmetric.

We are aware that the limited number of completed questionnaires and the usage of degree-centrality for S2 might still represent a limitation to the SNA data. We assume that this is also the reason why the SNA data of Money I and III were not as expected, since these are the cases with the highest number of actors involved in the change process. However, we are convinced that the countermeasures that we applied and the fact that the SNA data generally support our interview <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that our SNA data are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently valid. Most importantly, however, the SNA data were not our primary data source, but only used complementarily for methodological triangulation (Yin, 2009). Hence, we are convinced that this limitation does not bias our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

In addition, in analyzing the IT competence of actors as a rival theory, we did not explicitly analyze the general capabilities of involved actors such as management and coordination skills. However, no interviewee raised a lack of these skills when openly asked for reasons for low application change effectiveness. Hence, we are convinced that this speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor does not overshadow our results.

## Generalizability of findings

In terms of generalizability, we follow Auerbach and Silverstein (2003) and argue that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are transferable to settings outside our frame of analysis (the application change process). First, we propose that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are valid across industries because industry speci<sup>fi</sup>cs do not strongly in<sup>fl</sup>uence IT processes, in general (Tiwana and Konsynski, 2010), and the IT change process, in particular.

Second, we also argue that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are transferable to other operational business/IT interface processes that require business and IT to work together, to interact and to transfer knowledge. In other processes like incident processes or when identifying problems (Cabinet-Of<sup>fi</sup>ce, 2011), business and IT employees need to exchange tacit knowledge about how the application should work. Thus, our theoretical arguments about the success-determining socio-structural determinants remain valid.

Finally, the generalizability of our results to other types of software applications warrants closer investigation. All seven case studies dealt with applications that support core business operations of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and support many users. Systems with only few users may render interface actors unnecessary because they can better coordinate among each other what they discuss with the IT unit. However, our theoretical argument remains that too many actors from one side contacting the other side will likely result in inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies and con<sup>fl</sup>icts.

## Contributions

This paper has answered the question how social structures between business and IT at the change implementation level outside of project contexts in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which IT applications are maintained and enhanced in line with business requirements in order to achieve IT business value. We developed a theoretical research model and evaluated it using seven comprehensive case studies.

We found that the social aspects that are relevant at the implementation level are similar to those that are relevant at the top management level, namely frequent interaction, crossdomain knowledge, and a trusted partnership. However, we also analyzed structure in addition to relationship contents and substantiated our hypothesis that there need to be individual actors who serve as main contact persons between business and IT units (S0+S1). We also found that the relevant structures within the business and IT units in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which IT applications are maintained and enhanced in line with business requirements: interface actors need to be strongly connected to many of the other actors in their own unit, that is to be central within their own unit (S2).

## Implications for research

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings make three contributions to IS research and one to boundary-spanning theory.

First, we were able to theoretically derive and empirically support the importance of social structures at the change implementation level outside project contexts for alignment between business processes and software applications. While the literature acknowledges the importance of top management business/IT relationships (Feeny and Willcocks, 1998; Reich and Benbasat, 2000) and informal structures within projects (Chan, 2002), it has so far largely ignored implementation-level business/IT social structures along line organizations. Yet, this research clearly highlights that social structures in application change activities can be the reason for poor alignment of business requirements and IT systems, even if the previously researched determinants – such as strategic alignment or IT governance setup – are favorable. Hence, this paper is an important step toward solving the conundrum of why business/IT alignment and the business/IT relationship still rank among the top concerns of CIOs (Luftman et al.,

2013) despite the vast amount of research and management guidelines addressing this issue.

Second, we unified the dyadic and structural views on social IT/business relationships. Due to its focus on top management, most previous IS research has focused on individual dyadic relationships among CIOs and business executives (Preston and Karahanna, 2009), but neglected social structures. Other IS researchers more concerned with project settings and a few who discuss the implementation level have discussed structural properties such as liaisons but have not discussed the content of the relationships between liaisons and their counterparts in detail. We uni<sup>fi</sup>ed both perspectives – social structures and relationship contents – and thus were able to extend previous research on business/IT relationships by identifying which social structures are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial at the implementation level and how they function. Unlike previous research, we did not limit our analysis to the direct business/ IT interface but included structures within units as well. Thus, in addition to showing the importance of social structures, this uni<sup>fi</sup>cation enabled us to theoretically identify and explain precise social structures and also to provide evidence for their effectiveness regarding change implementation processes. Third, we underlined the importance of solidarity as an outcome of strong ties compared to knowledge exchange. Previous IS research has focused on knowledge exchange and largely ignored the importance of solidarity (e.g., Peppard and Ward, 2004; Tiwana and McLean, 2005). This study illustrates that solidarity is also important for a successful business/IT relationship. Even when business and IT are capable of exchanging knowledge, the lack of solidarity might still hamper change implementation effectiveness because of silo orientation and the lack of constructiveness. Solidarity, thus, is a further theoretical explanation for missing alignment of IT applications and business processes.

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also contribute to the boundaryspanning literature. While Levina and Vaast (2006) do not aim at prescribing favorable boundary-spanning practices (community-like vs market-like), our results clearly show that community-like boundary-spanning practices, that is, practices based on embodied relationships characterized by the analyzed relationship contents (cross-domain knowledge, complementary knowledge, and a trusted partnership with common goals), are favorable when tacit knowledge needs to be shared, for example, at the business/IT boundary. In all cases with high application change effectiveness, actors build on these relationship contents, that is, on community-like boundary-spanning practices, to interact (span the boundary) and ensure success. By contrast, market-like boundary-spanning practices do not include social linkages but build on the objecti<sup>fi</sup>ed exchange of information; they lack the relationship contents described above and thus are less capable of spanning this boundary, resulting in lower success (cf. BeSafe II and Money III). Future research can explore additional factors that might make a certain boundary-spanning practice dominant in speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations. We also contribute to boundary-spanning theory by detailing social structures that support communitylike boundary-spanning practices and thus enhance the understanding of such practices. While traditional boundary spanning initially only focuses on the relationship and its contents as such, we also explore its structure. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, in order to ensure community-like boundary spanning, boundary spanners (at least at the business/IT interface) have to be centrally positioned and must have strong relationships with the other unit (S1) and within their own unit (S2) in terms of frequently interacting and sharing goals and trust with other actors. Thus, we provide guidance to those who seek to set up community-like boundary-spanning practices, and we explain that failure to set up effective boundary-spanning practices, for example as part of an effort to drive a cultural change, may be related to introducing boundary spanners who do not possess suf<sup>fi</sup>cient social structures.

A possible area for future research would be to apply our approach to studies investigating outsourced software change activities. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that a large degree of solidarity between those requesting changes and those implementing them is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and that these persons should work toward a common goal. However, in outsourced situations, the goals of the client and the vendor will often be less congruent (e.g., cost or innovativeness focus of client vs pro<sup>fi</sup>tability focus of the vendor). Thus, future research might test the implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to outsourced situations.

## Implications for practice

Practitioners can build on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to increase the degree to which IT applications are changed, enhanced, and maintained in line with business requirements. Knowing that interface actors are important (S0) and that they need to be strongly connected to many other interface actors (S1) and to many of their colleagues in their own unit (S2), practitioners can determine favorable structures, choose appropriate persons for the interface role based on their position in the social network, and apply appropriate actions for re-engineering the social network (Schrott and Beimborn, 2003). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings might also help practitioners who already have liaisons in place understand why the introduction of these liaisons might not have produced the estimated results: the liaison might not be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently well-embedded socially to the other unit (S1) or within his or her own unit (S2).

In conclusion, this paper identi<sup>fi</sup>es important social factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing operational changes of software applications in line with business requirements and explains how these social structures function. Thus, our work contributes to the theoretical understanding of and success of implementation-level business/IT interface processes. We hope that our work will support the effective governance of the business/IT dialog and thus to realize more business value from IT.

## Appendix A

Interview guideline

● Background of interviewee: Personal background? Crossdomain experience? Role in the application change process?

● General network structure: Please draw the general interaction structure as you perceive it in the application change process. Please rate the amount and intensity of interaction between the involved units. Next, describe the relationship structure (i.e., trust, etc.) in the same way.

● Existence of interface actors: Do you interact with the other unit? Are there any actors in the other unit that you interact with in particular?

● Applying laddering: How does this situation (e.g., the existence of a particular counterpart in the IT) in<sup>fl</sup>uence the application change process? Why is it important?

● Relationship contents to the other unit (if person is interface actor): How would you characterize your relationship to the IT unit/business unit?

● Analysis of relationship contents: How does this particular relationship-content help/hinder you in realizing changes? Why is that important? How did this characteristic develop?

● Relationship content within the unit: Characterize the relationship structure within your unit.

● Analysis of relationship contents as described above.

● Success factors: What are the success factors in the application change process? (If these were social structures or relationship contents, apply the same approach as described above.)

● Application change effectiveness: How would you evaluate the IT change process? Are changes implemented in a way that is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for the business? Does IT add value during the IT change process in addition to merely implementing? Are changes implemented on time?

## Laddering

The laddering approach offers a structured interviewing technique that facilitates soliciting knowledge from experts (e.g., Corbridge et al., 1994; Rugg and McGeorge, 1995) or identifying means-ends relationships (Reynolds and Gutman, 1988). It uses a small number of questions and thereby elucidates the relationship between concepts (Corbridge et al., 1994). These questions aim at drilling deeper into and thus building a ladder along the reasoning of the interviewee (Reynolds and Gutman, 1988). This interviewing technique results in more systematic answers, richer insights, and more control by the researcher over the interview even when discussing an unstructured or complex topic (Rugg and McGeorge, 1995). Examples for questions to identify causal relationships used in our interviews are ‘How did this particular relationship content (e.g., trust) help you when dealing with this change?’ or when the answer was that ‘I can come more directly to the point’ a follow-up probe might be ‘Why could you come more directly to your point based on your trusted relationship.
