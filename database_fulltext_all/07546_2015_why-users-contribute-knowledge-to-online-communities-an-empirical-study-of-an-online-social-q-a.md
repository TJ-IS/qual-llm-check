## A B S T R A C T

Based on social capital theory, social exchange theory, and social cognitive theory, this study explored why users continuously contribute knowledge to online social Q&A communities. Empirical panel count data were collected from a popular Chinese online social Q&A community. The results from a negative binomial regression model with user fixed effects indicate that a user’s self-presentation, peer recognition, and social learning have a positive impact on his knowledge-contribution behaviors. The findings can help guide the development and operation of online social Q&A communities.

## 1. Introduction

Knowledge sharing and diffusion are topics of continued interest due to the important role they play in improving human life and heritage. Before the Internet age, most people were knowledge consumers, and they passively received knowledge. However, with the development of information technology, especially the Internet, the volume of knowledge has increased exponentially, with more and more people getting involved in the process of knowledge production and diffusion. Online social Q&A communities augment regular Q&A systems with social networking features that help establish social linkages among users, questions, and topics. In online social Q&A communities, all content is created by registered users and distributed through established networks of users with similar interests. Registered users can post any kind of question and motivate other users with related knowledge to supply answers. Meanwhile, these answers become search results for similar or relevant questions. The quality of the knowledge contributed by members is assessed by other members’ votes or attention. The internal structure of online social Q&A communities is a complex mixture of topics, questions, answers, and users. As on Twitter, users voluntarily follow each other to form social networks in online social Q&A communities [51]. A user can also follow questions and topics, and anything new related to the questions and topics will then be sent to his followers. Thus, users directly or indirectly connect with each other through such social linkages, which provides a way for users to crowdsource their searches for specific knowledge, much of which involves acquiring first-hand answers to specific questions from domain experts [62].

Compared with regular Q&A systems, such as enterprise Q&A systems, users in online social Q&A communities spontaneously construct and maintain social networks based on common interests, show commitment to communal goals, form a sense of belonging, and follow certain norms of interaction. Such community affiliation not only shapes the individual’s opinions and positions on specific issues but also provides the impetus to return to the community in the future. In addition, the social linkages shared by online social Q&A community members have been shown to increase their willingness to contribute knowledge and to commit to the group’s goals [60,65]. Compared with social media, such as Twitter and Facebook, online social Q&A communities focus on knowledge contribution and diffusion rather than information regarding an individual’s current mood, news, or reviews. Social networks in online social Q&A communities are formed by strangers with the same interests, while those in social media are formed by acquaintances. In addition, most regular Q&A systems employ reputation-ranking mechanisms or financial rewards to incentivize knowledge-contribution behaviors, which have been proven to promote users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors [11,12]. However, in online Q&A communities, neither a reputation-ranking mechanism nor any financial reward for users knowledge-contribution behaviors exists, and most participants are independent and have no conflict of interest.

There is no doubt that online social Q&A communities can break through barriers to transfer, replicate, and store knowledge [39]. However, the availability of such communities is no guarantee that knowledge sharing will actually take place [6]. Social dilemmas, which can lead to a ‘‘tragedy of the commons’’ with poor outcomes for the community, are often mentioned in academic and business reports. Increased attention has focused on the mechanisms behind users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors. Based on post-adoption behavior research, He and Wei [30] investigated knowledge-seeking and contribution behaviors and found that contribution intentions and facilitation directly impact users’ continuous knowledge-contribution behaviors. Bock and Kim [11] found that attitudes toward knowledge sharing have positive effects on knowledge-sharing behaviors. Ardichvili et al. [9] indicated that trust would remove barriers to knowledge contribution. Fadel and Durcikova [24] argued that the perceived procedural justice of validation would positively influence users’ quality perceptions and contribution behaviors. In addition to knowledgecontribution behaviors in knowledge management systems, information-contribution behaviors in social media also attract attention. According to Xia et al. [66], there are two types of informationsharing decisions in online social media, start-sharing decisions and continue-sharing decisions. External factors that occur outside the social media, such as economic rewards or recognition, are the main determinants of the start-sharing decision. However, once a user develops information-sharing behavior, internal factors of social media participation, such as sense of self-worth, social norms, and social affiliation, will carry more weight in the user’s decision about whether to continue sharing.

Previous studies have found that the motivation mechanisms for users’ contribution behaviors vary based on the type of online communities—i.e., whether the community is centered on tight social linkages in a small network or on loose social linkages between users in a larger group that shares a common interest [37]. Based on prior studies on knowledge sharing in regular Q&A systems and information sharing in online social media, we focus on the motivation for knowledge-contribution behaviors in online Q&A communities. Panel count data regarding individual activities was collected to explore the relationships between knowledgecontribution behaviors and community identity, the group-size effect, peer recognition, and social learning. We found that community identity, social learning, and peer recognition have positive impacts on users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors. These findings can help system designers develop dynamic, selfadopted, and efficiently managed online social Q&A systems.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the literature on knowledge sharing and propose hypotheses. Section 3 introduces the research data corpus and the variables to test the hypotheses. Section 4 presents the model for data analysis and the analysis results. A discussion of the results, as well as limitations and future research, are in Section 5.

## 2. Theory building and research hypotheses

## 2.1. Related work

Many existing studies have investigated the motivations for online contributions and have suggested a wide range of factors as reasons for online knowledge sharing. In this section, we first summarize motivations that have been proven to impact users online knowledge-contribution behaviors in prior studies. Then, we will describe the research gaps and research questions.

## 2.1.1. Motivation mechanisms for knowledge contribution

Many studies have proven that user-generated contents in social media are of great value to both enterprises and individuals [2,1,25], and numerous techniques have been proposed to analyze these contents [63,69]. However, the availability of social media is no guarantee that user-generated content sharing will actually take place. One of the most difficult challenges of any knowledgecentric online community is sustaining its members’ motivations to share knowledge. We need a clearer understanding of the motivation mechanisms that drive community members to continually contribute, engage, and stay loyal to the community. Many studies have explored why people do or do not contribute content to online communities. The motivations for knowledge contribution can roughly be divided into two categories: organization-based motivations and individual-based motivations.

2.1.1.1. Organization-based motivations. Organization-based motivations include organizational culture and climate, management support, rewards and incentives, and organizational structure. Among many cultural dimensions that are likely to impact users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors, trust has received the most research attention [61]. A culture that emphasizes trust has been found to promote knowledge sharing [32] and to moderate the negative effect of perceived cost [35]. Organizations with cultures emphasizing innovation are more likely to promote knowledge sharing through subjective norms that encourage sharing [56]. In contrast to trust and innovation, a culture that emphasizes competition may have a negative impact on knowledge sharing because competition destroys the atmosphere of teamwork and trust [52]. Studies exploring organizational climate and knowledge contribution have shown mixed results. Taylor and Wright [58] found that a climate that emphasizes learning is positively related to knowledge contribution. However, Hsu [32] failed to find a significant relationship between learning orientation and knowledge contribution. As to management support for knowledge sharing, many studies have proven that management support is positively associated with individuals’ willingness to share knowledge [22]. Lee et al. [40] found that support from the top has a positive impact on both the quantity and quality of knowledge contribution. Based on social exchange and social capital theories, organizational rewards, such as higher salaries, bonuses, and promotion opportunities, have been found to be positively related to knowledge-contribution behaviors [14]. In addition, direct ties and personal relationships rooted in virtual communities have been shown to be positively related to the level of knowledge contribution [51,34]. Individuals who seek to maintain and strengthen social networks ties will more frequently participate in virtual communities and contribute knowledge [16].

2.1.1.2. Individual-based motivations. Individual-based motivations include individual characteristics, a belief in knowledge ownership, perceived benefits and costs, interpersonal trust, and justice. Studies have proven that individual characteristics, such as one’s openness level and curiosity, have a major impact on online knowledge-contribution behaviors. For example, Cabrera and Cabrera [14] found that individuals with higher openness to experiences tend to have higher levels of curiosity, leading to continuous interest in exchanging ideas and insights. Similarly, individuals with more confidence in their ability to spread knowledge are more likely to contribute knowledge to professional communities. Perceived benefits/costs are one of the most studied antecedents of knowledge sharing [61]. Social exchange theory indicates that individuals’ actions depend on their perceived benefits and costs; the benefits are rewards such as respect, reputation, and tangible incentives. Knowledge contribution in online communities is positively related to increased internal satisfaction and professional reputation, enhanced altruism, and self-actualization [19]. Similarly, the theory of reasoned action and the technology acceptance model suggest that people are motivated to contribute knowledge when they believe their useful contribution will enhance relationships [12].

## 2.1.2. Summary

The literature review results indicate that there are numerous studies focusing on users’ knowledge contribution in online communities. However, they have mainly focused on enterprise knowledge management systems, which are significantly different from social Q&A communities in many respects. For example, members of social Q&A communities are voluntarily organized according to their interests, while members of enterprise knowledge management systems are forcibly organized by organizational norms. Social networks formed in social Q&A communities are based on weak ties existing between strangers, while those in enterprise knowledge management systems are mostly strong ties existing between acquaintances. In addition to enterprise knowledge management systems, many studies have focused on online Q&A communities, such as Yahoo Answers [4,49] and Baidu Knows [67]. However, the market share of this type of Q&A communities is rapidly shrinking. Yahoo Answers shows clear signs of stalled user growth and stagnation, with traffic dropping 23% in a span of four months in 2011. The Google Answers service launched in 2001 was shut down by 2006. Conversely, online social Q&A communities, such as Quora<sup>1</sup> and Zhihu,<sup>2</sup> are growing at a brisk pace. Most prior research has employed the self-reported feedback method to collect data to explore users’ contribution intentions. Self-reported approaches are limited by sample size, recall bias, and the bias of social desirability. Recently, data-driven approaches have attracted researchers’ attention because of the easy availability of large amounts of user data, where users’ actual activities and interactions in the community are extracted and used for pragmatic correlations between user needs and behaviors. However, these studies mainly focus on users’ contribution behaviors on social media, such as Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, and Weibo, where the contribution content is information rather than knowledge. The differences between knowledge and information have been widely studied. From the perspective of a user's contribution behavior knowledge-contribution behavior is different from information-contribution behavior in the following two respects. First, a user’s knowledge-contribution behavior is triggered by other users’ questions, while informationsharing behavior occurs when a user wants to let others know him and his opinions. Second, knowledge-contribution behavior is time consuming and requires domain knowledge, while the barriers of information sharing are much lower. In this paper, we focus on knowledge-contribution behavior in online social communities and employ a data-driven approach to explore the mechanisms behind members’ knowledge-contribution behaviors.

## 2.2. Theoretical background

The considerable success of social communities, such as Facebook, Twitter, and Quora, has attracted many scholars to explore the theories behind such success. Social cognitive theory, social capital theory, and social exchange theory have been frequently mentioned in previous studies.

Social cognitive theory, stemming from the social learning theory proposed by Miller and Dollard [46], argues that individual thought, affect, and action may be influenced by observing others within the context of social interactions. Self-efficacy and outcome expectations are two important constructs of social cognitive theory, which refer to an individual’s belief in his ability to successfully perform a behavior and the likelihood that an individual’s behavior may lead to a specific outcome, respectively [7]. Prior information system studies have demonstrated that social cognitive theory can help to explain and predict computer training performance and computer usage [21,20]. In relation to social media, social cognitive theory has been used to analyze how user-generated contents emerge and affect users’ future behaviors [7,18].

Social capital is typically defined as ‘‘the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit’’ [5]. In contrast to other types of capital, social capital is embedded in interpersonal relationships and networks existing in a social context, which can be productive resources. In the social media context, social capital is created through interactions, which are represented by the contact, social ties, gratitude, respect, trust, and friendship that members have toward each other in a social network [64]. Social capital theory has been used to explain a variety of prosocial behaviors, such as crowdsourcing, information sharing, and social achievement, which individual-based capital, for example, human capital or financial capital, is unable to explain [34]. Individual social capital accumulates over time; the more an individual is involved in social media, the more social capital he may possess.

Social exchange theory, combined with economic exchange theory, emphasizes that information sharing is a type of social interaction [42]. Economic exchange theory emphasizes extrinsic benefits, but social exchange theory emphasizes intrinsic rewards. According to economic exchange theory, an individual will behave in a particular way based on the results of his comprehensive consideration of what benefits are received and what sacrifices are made by performing such a behavior. People will take action when the benefits exceed the costs. Prior studies have examined the positive relationship between incentive mechanisms and successful enterprise knowledge management. Employees who believe that they will obtain extrinsic benefits from their knowledge sharing, such as financial rewards, promotions, or educational opportunities, develop a positive attitude about knowledgesharing behavior [11]. Contrary to economic rewards, the benefits involved in social exchange emphasize unspecified obligations that cannot be quantified in terms of a physical medium of exchange. Therefore, social exchange tends to engender feelings of belonging, personal obligation, gratitude, trust, and loyalty. In online social Q&A communities, there are two types of exchange objects, namely, knowledge and attention. People who share knowledge expect to obtain knowledge or attention as their reward— especially attention, which has become a scarce resource in online social media [38].

## 2.3. Drivers of knowledge contribution

Based on social cognitive theory, social capital theory, and social exchange theory, we summarize four aspects that may impact people’s knowledge-contribution behavior: identity communication, peer recognition, the group-size effect, and social learning.

## 2.3.1. Identity communication

As originally formulated by Tajfel and Turner [57], social identity theory introduced the concept of social identity as a way of explaining social behavior. As individuals, we place importance on what makes us unique. The answers to ‘‘Who am I?’’ and ‘‘What am I?’’ form our individual identities, which distinguish us from others. Identity communication reflects an individual’s effort to express and present his identity to others with the goal of achieving a shared understanding. In online communities, users frequently employ other users’ disclosure information to infer their status, interests, and behaviors. We use the term ‘‘selfpresentation’’ to indicate users’ personal information disclosure behaviors. Self-presentation information conveys a user’s personality, experiences, status, and/or social attitude, helping other users understand his social identity (the social stratums that he belongs to) or personal identity (the features that distinguish him from others) [27]. Both social identity and personal identity have been widely investigated for their influences on the formation of users’ beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors [17,50]. Golder and Donath [27] indicated that establishing one’s online identity provides significant motivation for knowledge contributors, not only by helping them enhance their reputations and self-esteem but also by amplifying the possibility of future reciprocation. Ma and Agarwal [43] suggested that self-presentation will enhance a user’s perceived identity verification and then promote his knowledge-contribution behavior. In addition, the amount of personal information disclosed indicates to what extent people trust the website [31]. Numerous marketing studies have proven that the degree to which consumers trust a company positively affects their loyalty to that company [15,54] and that customers’ loyalty is positively related to their continuous participation behaviors [68]. We therefore propose the following:

H1: Individuals who disclose more self-presentation information will contribute more knowledge to online social Q&A communities.

## 2.3.2. Peer recognition

As more information and ideas are freely available on social media, attention has become a scarce resource. The concept of the attention economy, originally introduced by Simon [53], argues that information consumes the attention of its recipients. The openness of social media provides a wealth of information and leads to a poverty of attention [28]. Numerous studies have indicated that the attention economy drives the community, with contributors competing for readers’ attention. The expectation of getting widespread attention motivates video contributions on YouTube [33], and attention from followers inspires Twitter posts [51]. According to social exchange theory, users exchange information and attention in online social knowledge communities. When people provide a solution, they would like to hear other people’s voices about their contribution. Feedback, especially positive feedback from peer members, could lead to future participation behaviors. Theories of reciprocity [29], reinforcement [55], and the need to belong [10] all suggest that feedback from other users will have an impact on users’ future participation behaviors. Brzozowski et al. [13] indicated that newcomers who receive feedback are more likely to continue to contribute content in the future. Macy [44] argued that users whose first comment receives positive numeric ratings will return significantly faster to an online news community to post a second comment. Rui and Whinston [51] investigated Twitter to explore users’ behaviors. They found that people participate in Twitter to obtain information and attention. Their contribution behaviors are largely decided by the amount of attention that they will receive from other participants. Knowledge and attention are two main activities of online social Q&A communities. People who share knowledge are rewarded with knowledge and/or attention. In this paper, we use peer recognition to represent other members’ positive feedback on a member’s contribution behavior. We therefore propose the following:

H2: Individuals who receive more positive feedback will contribute more knowledge to online social Q&A communities.

## 2.3.3. Group-size effects

According to social capital theory, social capital is created through social interactions and the expectation of future social resources that they create. The most important facet of social capital in online social media is the presence or absence of social interaction linkages among users [47]. The degree to which an individual connects with others is a multifaceted phenomenon that reflects his attraction to the group and his interactions with group members [36]. Numerous studies have proven that contributors to the public good receive private benefits as a result of social effects when the group size is sufficiently large, and individual behavior is influenced by the presence of others. In online social Q&A communities, individuals attract other members’ attention and maintain relationships by sharing knowledge. The more users a user can get involved with his knowledge, the wider his audience reach will be. According to social cognitive theory, individuals are more likely to engage in behavior that they expect will result in favorable consequences. One of the favorable consequences of knowledge contribution is social effects. Zhang and Zhu [70] indicated that social effects indeed dominate freeriding incentives and that an individual with a larger group size will contribute more to Wikipedia. Andreoni [8] suggested that a contributor’s utility in contributing may depend on the number of receipts. We therefore propose the following:

H3: Individuals with a larger group size will contribute more knowledge to online social Q&A communities.

## 2.3.4. Social learning

According to social cognitive theory, a person’s behavior is largely shaped and controlled by the influences of his cognition and social network [7]. Social learning is a form of vicarious learning rather than direct learning, which occurs as a result of observing both the behavior of others and the environmental outcomes of certain behaviors. There are three necessary steps in the learning process: attention, i.e., learning the behavior without distraction; retention, i.e., remembering the behavior; and reproduction, i.e., reproducing what has been learned. Social Q&A communities provide all the necessary steps for social learning to occur. Members’ attention is evoked and retention is reinforced by followees’ actions, which are aggregated in a content feed. Existing learning literature has shown that learning creates a growing stock of knowledge and skills that can be applied in the future to improve productivity [23,48]. On the other hand, individuals will respond to other peers’ actions in online communities according to the norms of reciprocity. Therefore, online Q&A communities not only provide a learning environment for knowledge accumulation but also enhance reciprocal behaviors by detailing every member’s contribution. We therefore propose the following:

H4: Individuals with more social learning opportunities will contribute more knowledge to online social Q&A communities.

## 3. Data collection and description

## 3.1. Data corpus

Zhihu is one of the most popular online social Q&A communities in China, where questions are created, answered, edited, and organized by registered users. We randomly selected 1500 members of Zhihu and followed them from March 15 to June 22, 2014. Each member’s behaviors, such as questioning, answering, and commenting, were tracked and recorded using an automatic crawling Java program for a specified time interval. The interval could not be too short because it would not truly reflect the change in user behaviors. It also could not be too long because it would combine too many unrelated activities [66]. In this paper, we set the interval at seven days. To ensure that the users in our study were still ‘‘alive’’, we required that any observable or recordable activities of the qualified user take place at least once during the observation period. Four hundred ninety-four users did not meet this requirement and were removed from the data corpus. The final data corpus included 1006 members and their dynamic behaviors over 15 time periods.

Fig. 1 presents a snapshot of a Zhihu member’s homepage. There are three types of information: personal information, participation information, and feedback information. Personal information, such as education, work, location, and career, was provided by the member. Although members were encouraged to complete their information, it was not required. Personal information is mostly static; it does not change over a short period. Participation information refers to members’ behaviors on Zhihu, including the number of questions asked, answers provided, edits made, and members followed. This information changes as members participate in Zhihu activities. Feedback information refers other members’ responses to a member’s behaviors, such as votes of usefulness or thanks for answers.

## 3.2. Variables description and measurement

As stated in Section 2, there is one dependent variable, knowledge contribution, and four latent independent variables, identity communication, peer recognition, group-size effect, and social learning. Based on the data collected from Zhihu, each variable will be measured as follows.

## 3.2.1. Dependent variable

In Zhihu, users have two primary roles, namely, solvers and questioners. In their roles as solvers, registered users can freely contribute solutions to any questions. Therefore, the user’s number of answers is the most visible variable to measure his contribution to the community. In this paper, we employ the number of answers provided by a member in a given period to represent his knowledge contribution.

## 3.2.2. Independent variables

Identity communication. Most social media communities encourage members to provide personal information, which distinguishes members from one another. In Zhihu, each member owns and maintains a homepage that shows his personal information. There are 11 personal-information items that a user can disclose in Zhihu: gender, Sina Weibo link, Tencent Weibo link, current status, personal description, business, address, company name, position, university, and major. We adopt a self-presentation score to represent a user’s identity communication, which is the total number of disclosed personal-information items. Each completed item is worth a self-presentation score of 1. Therefore, if a member discloses all of his personal information, then his selfpresentation score is 11; if he does not disclose any information about himself, then his self-presentation score is 0.

Peer Recognition. Attention economics indicate that attention is a scarce resource in online social media. Members usually use knowledge or information to receive attention from other members. When a user posts an answer, he expects responses, especially positive responses. In Zhihu, members who post answers may receive votes of usefulness and thanks from other members. There is high correlation between votes of usefulness and thanking; people who offer thanks for an answer always vote for usefulness as well. We therefore employ the number of usefulness votes to measure the scale of positive feedback received by a member during a given period.

Group-size effect. Social networks aim to assemble members with similar interests and to improve the efficiency of knowledge diffusion. In Zhihu, knowledge is spread among members through a network formed by users, topics, and questions. The most intuitive and sensitive spreading channel is members’ followers. When a member posts a new question or answer, it will be fed to his followers. Thus, a member’s number of followers indicates his audience size. We therefore employ the number of followers to represent the group size of knowledge contributors in Zhihu.

Social learning. Social media have changed people’s roles in the knowledge-seeking process. In the past, people have generally passively received knowledge or information in whatever form it was available. In online social media, people have the autonomy to decide what type of information to receive, the volume of information to receive, and when to read the subscribed information. In Zhihu, registered members can subscribe to topics, questions, and members that they are interested in (without the approval of the followee), and then anything new about these topics, questions, or members is fed to them. We therefore employ the number of followees to represent the member’s opportunities for social learning.

## 4. Methodology and results

## 4.1. Regression model for count data

The dependent variable, knowledge contribution, is the number of answers provided by a user during a given period. All the dependent variables are non-negative integers. Poisson regression models and negative binomial regression models aim to analyze count data, although they differ in their assumptions of the conditional mean and variance of the dependent variable. Poisson regression models assume that the conditional mean is equal to the distribution. Negative binomial regression models do not assume an equal mean and variance and introduce a parameter to correct for over-dispersion when the variance is greater than the conditional mean in the data [26]. As is shown in Table 3, the mean and variance of the dependent variable are quite different. Therefore, we adopt the negative binomial regression model to explore users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors in online social Q&A communities. The negative binomial probability function is as follows:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} (Y = y _ {i t} | \lambda , \theta) = \frac {\Gamma (y _ {i t} + \theta)}{\Gamma (y _ {i t} + 1) \Gamma (\theta)} \left(\frac {\theta}{\theta + \lambda}\right) ^ {\theta} \left(\frac {\lambda}{\theta + \lambda}\right) ^ {y _ {i t}}\tag{1}
$$

The negative binomial distribution has two parameters: u and l d. Parameter u captures over-dispersion in the data. When $\theta = 0 ,$ , the negative distribution is the same as the Poisson distribution. Parameter l is the expected value of the distribution. Considering that unobservable individual intrinsic characteristics will influence individual knowledge-contribution behaviors, we employ user fixed effects to represent all unobserved heterogeneity. Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) results of both regression models. According to the AIC and BIC values, negative binary regression is clearly superior to Poisson regression for our count panel data. Thus, the observed count data, the number of answers provided by a user over a given time period, have an over-dispersion problem. The model results for knowledge-contribution behaviors are consistent with prior studies that argue that negative binomial regression is preferable to count data when over-dispersion exists [34,45].

Similar to the linear regression model, the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable is determined by the regression coefficient. The positive (negative) sign of the regression coefficient indicates that the independent variable has a positive (negative) impact on the dependent variable. Because count regression techniques model the log of incident counts, the coefficients can be interpreted as follows: for a one-unit change in the independent variable, the log of the dependent variable is expected to change by the value of the regression coefficient. As is shown under the coefficient column of the negative binomial regression results in Table 4, selfPresentation, log-transformed answersBeenVoted, and log-transformed followees are positively correlated to users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors at different significance levels.

Hypothesis 1 investigates the impact of self-presentation on knowledge-contribution behaviors. Users who disclose more personal information will contribute more content to online social Q&A communities. SelfPresentation represents the number of personal-information items published on a user’s homepage. The regression results indicate that the coefficient for logtransformed selfPresentation is positive and significant (b = 0.01821, p < 0.05). Therefore, hypothesis 1 is supported; the more personal information a user discloses, the more knowledge he will contribute to the online social Q&A community.

Hypothesis 2 investigates the effect of peer recognition on knowledge-contribution behaviors. In online social media, social responses refer to other users’ feedback on a user’s knowledge contribution. We argue that the more positive feedback a user’s knowledge contribution receives, the more likely he is to contribute content to online social Q&A communities. Answers-BeenVoted represents other members’ responses to a user’s knowledge contribution. The regression results indicate that the coefficient for log-transformed answersBeenVoted is positive and significant (b = 0.20589, p < 0.001). Therefore, hypothesis 2 is supported; others members’ feedback will positively affect members’ contribution behaviors.

Hypothesis 3 investigates the impact of the group-size effect on knowledge-contribution behaviors. Group size is defined as the scale of the population that will receive a member’s updated information. We argue that users who contribute knowledge to online social Q&A communities receive private benefits as a result of social effects when the group size is large. In this paper, the number of followers is employed to represents a user’s group size in online Q&A communities. Contrary to our expectations, the empirical results indicate that the coefficient for logtransformed followers is not significant. Therefore, hypothesis 3 is rejected.

Hypothesis 4 investigates the effect of social learning on user’s knowledge-contribution behaviors. Social learning in online Q&A communities refers to a user’s accumulation of knowledge through his observation of the knowledge contributed by other members. The more knowledge he obtains, the more likely he is to contribute to the online social Q&A community. In this paper, the total number of followees is adopted to represent the likelihood that a user will learn from others. The empirical results indicate that the coefficient for log-transformed followees is positive and significant (b = 0.12089, p < 0. 01). Therefore, hypothesis 4 is supported; social learning behavior has a positive effect on knowledgecontribution behaviors.

## 5. Conclusions and future research

Based on social capital theory, social exchange theory, and social learning theory, this study reveals that members’ selfpresentation, other members’ recognition, and social learning opportunities play important roles in knowledge-contribution behaviors. The empirical data were collected from a popular Chinese online social Q&A community, and negative binomial regression was employed to test the proposed hypotheses. The empirical results indicate that a user’s self-presentation, other members’ recognition, and social learning opportunities have a positive impact on knowledge-contribution behaviors.

Self-presentation refers to two signals of a user’s attitudes toward an online social Q&A community. The first is trust; people who disclose more personal information reflect a higher level of trust in the website. When the website operates in a way that builds users’ trust, the perceived risk of personal-information leaks and abuse is likely decreased, which increases the users’ confidence and influences future behaviors [3]. Trust also leads to loyalty by affecting people’s perceptions of congruent values within the community, which enhances their involvement and promotes reciprocity. The second self-presentation signal is intention; disclosing more personal information reflects a higher level of intention to participate in the website. Many studies have shown that intention has a positive impact on behavior. Therefore, we can expect that people who disclose more personal information are likely to maintain a long-term relationship with the community and consistently contribute content.

In this paper, we explore the impact of two benefits of knowledge-contribution behavior that a user can obtain from social networks. The first involves social learning opportunities. One of the main functions of online social Q&A communities is spreading knowledge. Registered users can follow any members without permission, and anything new about the followed members will be fed to them. The more knowledge a user learns from others, the more likely it is that he will contribute knowledge to others. In addition, according to social cognition theory, users will positively respond to other members’ knowledge contributions. The second benefit is the group-size effect. Contrary to prior studies [51], our empirical results indicate that the relationship between group size and knowledge contribution is not significant. There are two reasons for this ‘‘unexpected’’ result. First, in online social Q&A communities, users contribute knowledge, while on online social media, such as Twitter, users contribute information about, among other things, their current mood, the things that they are doing, or recent news. Knowledge contribution is time consuming and requires related experience or domain knowledge. Second, as the number of followers increases, users’ influence also expands. The quality of knowledge contributed to the community has a substantial impact on one’s reputation. Negative word of mouth from followers will greatly decrease one’s reputation. Therefore, users will contribute knowledge more conservatively as their number of followers increases.

Regarding peer recognition, the empirical results indicate that users respond positively to other members’ recognition. Attention economics argue that attention is a scarce resource in online social media. However, we believe that online social Q&A communities can provide a successful platform for sharing information and being acknowledged. Because no financial rewards are available, social rewards, such as approval, status, and respect, become the main motivating factors for knowledge contribution. Users are willing to maintain their reputation and other users’ recognition by continuously contributing knowledge to the community.

In addition, contrary to survey-based data, which are widely used to explore people’s motivations for participating in online social media [18,17,41], this study employs panel count data on individual activities to explore what motivates users’ knowledgecontribution behaviors, which allows us to restore individual online activities, thus enabling us to explore users’ knowledgecontribution decision processes.

## 5.1. Theoretical and practical implications

Previous research on motivators of social media participation has suggested that individuals continuously participate in social media based on a set of identified factors, but potential mechanisms of knowledge contribution in online social Q&A communities have never been explicitly studied. Furthermore, most existing studies on knowledge contribution have been conducted in enterprises’ internal knowledge management systems [30] or enterprises’ customer fora [34], and these studies usually employ survey-based data [30,64]. Few studies focus on online social Q&A communities and employ panel data collected from individual activities in the community.

This study may advance our knowledge in third respects. First, we extend social exchange theory into online social Q&A communities. Contrary to enterprise knowledge management communities, people who contribute knowledge to online social Q&A communities do not receive any financial rewards or promotion opportunities; they do not know each other and are not stakeholders in the real world. As a type of social reward, attention is the main motivator of people’s continuous contributions in online social Q&A media. Social Q&A communities successfully maintain an efficient market for the exchange of knowledge and attention. Second, social learning is an important motivator for knowledge-contribution behaviors. On the one hand, people will learn from other members with the same interests. On the other hand, other members’ knowledge-contribution behaviors reinforces members’ knowledge-contribution intentions. Third, members’ participation intentions will positively impact their knowledge-contribution behaviors. In this paper, we use the extent of personal information disclosure to measure members’ participation intentions and trust in the communities, and the empirical results are consistent with those of previous studies [43].

For practitioners, understanding the motivational mechanisms of knowledge contribution is critical to stimulate contribution behaviors in online social Q&A communities. The findings of this study provide several insights into the complexity of users’ beliefs on knowledge-contribution behaviors in online social Q&A communities. First, online social Q&A communities should be designed in a way that facilitates their role as a marketplace connecting people’s needs for attention and knowledge. Without financial rewards or career promotion opportunities, people exchange knowledge to receive attention. The operators of online social Q&A communities should explore response mechanisms for users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors and introduce new features that would visualize free-riders’ acknowledgment of knowledge contributors. Second, online social Q&A communities should be designed in a way that increases users’ learning opportunities. Most users in online social Q&A communities face problems of either knowledge shortages or knowledge overload. Recently, machine learning approaches have been introduced to match a user’s needs and the content and volume of knowledge contributed by other users. Finally, online social Q&A communities should keep their promises regarding users’ information security and increase users’ sense of belonging. Members who have a sense of belonging are more likely to disclose personal information to online social Q&A communities and to maintain a long-term relationship with such communities. Such personal information is a valuable resource for recommending other people who share similar interests, thereby increasing their learning and social exchange opportunities.

## 5.2. Limitations and future research

Due to the dynamics of online communities, we cannot accurately measure some of the proposed constructs. In this paper, we used an automatic java program to capture users’ behaviors in Zhihu. However, as the website does not record or display all behaviors for each user, some important cues are missing. For example, we use the number of followees to measure users’ social learning opportunities; however, different users have different followees, and a larger number of followees does not necessarily indicate that the user has more social learning opportunities. For example, user A and user B both have the same number of followees. However, user A’s followees are more active than those of user B; thus, user A acquires more social learning opportunities. In the future, we should obtain more data on the behaviors of users’ followees and obtain more accurate data on the social learning that users obtain from followees. In addition, users’ knowledge-seeking behaviors are not considered in this study, which may impact users’ knowledge-contribution behaviors, according to social exchange theory. In the future, we will capture more user behaviors in the community to accurately measure the proposed constructs and to explore the relationship between users’ knowledge-seeking behaviors and knowledge-contribution behaviors.
