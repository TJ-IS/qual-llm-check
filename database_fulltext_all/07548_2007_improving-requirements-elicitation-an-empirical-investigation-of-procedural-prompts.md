# Improving requirements elicitation: an empirical investigation of procedural prompts

Mitzi G Pitts\* & Glenn J Browne<sup>†</sup>

\*Client Services, Cook Systems International, Inc., Memphis, TN, USA, email: mpitts@cooksys.com, and <sup>†</sup>Area of ISQS, Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX, USA, email: glenn.browne@ttu.edu

Abstract. Information system success is dependent upon the effectiveness of requirements elicitation. Requirements elicitation is subject to numerous challenges, however, including cognitive limitations of analysts. In the present study, we test prompts designed to overcome the cognitive limitations that cause analysts to gather inadequate and inaccurate requirements. The study’s results indicate that providing analysts with procedural prompts to aid their requirements elicitation can result in the acquisition of additional meaningful requirements. In addition, the procedural prompts designed to mitigate cognitive challenges were significantly more effective than simple interrogatory prompts. Theoretical implications for the elicitation of requirements and practical implications for the training and practice of systems analysts are discussed.

Keywords: information systems development, requirements determination, elicitation methods, prompting techniques, cognitive challenges

## INTRODUCTION

Improving requirements determination is a critical goal in information systems development. It is generally accepted that poor requirements determination is a primary cause of system development failures (Davis, 1982; Wetherbe, 1991; Byrd et al., 1992; Vessey & Conger, 1993; Hickey & Davis, 2004). Elicitation of requirements from key stakeholders is vital to system success, but unfortunately is a major stumbling block in the systems development process. Users and other stakeholders often are unsure about their needs and/or are unable to articulate them clearly, requirements typically change during the course of a project, and analysts are often poorly trained in information gathering techniques (Boland, 1978; Marakas & Elam, 1998; Hickey & Davis, 2004; Pan, 2005). Furthermore, there are limited initiatives (in research or practice) aimed at providing methods or tools for improving requirements elicitation (Zahedi, 1999). Without proper attention to the task, analysts are likely to ‘short-cut’ requirements elicitation, resulting in incomplete and inaccurate requirements (Pitts & Browne, 2004). Therefore, research aimed at identifying methods and tools to improve the efficiency and the effectiveness of requirements elicitation can have a significant impact on the quality of systems development efforts.

The most common method of requirements elicitation continues to be the interview (Davis, 1982; Agarwal & Tanniru, 1990; Watson & Frolick, 1993; Browne & Rogich, 2001). Regrettably, however, analysts are given little direction regarding the methods of inquiry that are most effective (Marakas & Elam, 1998; Moody et al., 1998). The use of specific information prompts is recommended for aiding analysts in eliciting requirements (e.g. Kepner & Tregoe, 1981; Davis, 1982; Wetherbe, 1991). A few prompting techniques have been shown to be effective in eliciting information from people in several business domains (e.g. Fischhoff, 1989; Browne et al., 1997; Browne & Rogich, 2001). However, there is limited research on either the creation or the outcome of such prompts. The link between requirements gathering and system success and the lack of empirical evidence concerning elicitation efforts makes requirements elicitation one of the most important, yet least understood, activities of systems development.

The current research introduces procedural prompts to improve the completeness and accuracy of information gathered during requirements elicitation. The prompts are based on specific strategies identified for overcoming the cognitive limitations that bring about insufficiency during requirements elicitation. The strategies are rooted in theory and empirical findings from decision-making and cognitive psychology. There is prior experimental evidence to indicate that procedural prompts are effective in eliciting requirements from users (Browne & Rogich, 2001). The present research demonstrates the effectiveness of procedural prompts used by analysts during requirements elicitation, illustrates that analysts are easily trained in the use of this type of prompting strategy, and shows that significant positive outcomes can result from the use of such prompts.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, we detail theoretical background relevant to the requirements determination process, cognitive problems that occur during the process, and prompting strategies that can help overcome these problems. Next, we specify the hypotheses for the study and discuss the methodology utilized. We then present the results of the study and conclude with a discussion of the findings and implications for theory and practice.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Requirements elicitation

Information requirements determination (IRD) is commonly considered the most important activity in information systems development (Davis, 1982; Vessey & Conger, 1993; Watson & Frolick, 1993; Ocker et al., 1998; Hickey & Davis, 2004). This is true regardless of whether the systems development environment utilizes a traditional structured methodology, such as the waterfall model, or a contemporary iterative methodology, such as the ‘agile’ development model. Nearly every identified methodology contains the general activities of analysis, design, code and test (Erickson et al., 2005), with requirements determination taking place within the analysis activity. In the waterfall model, requirements are assumed to be relatively stable and requirements determination occurs early in the development process (Boehm & Turner, 2004). In agile development, changing requirements are assumed (Turk et al., 2005) and requirements determination is often viewed as continuous (Hickey & Davis, 2004). In agile development methods, it is estimated that approximately 10% of requirements are uncovered early in the development process and 90% evolve over several iterations (Larman, 2004). Iterative development philosophies such as Rapid Application Development and Short Cycle Time Development assume vague and ‘fuzzy’ requirements that are made clear during the course of development (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2004). However, iterative or agile methods do not signify a lack of detailed requirements (Larman, 2004). Regardless of methodology, a significant amount of time is devoted to finding and understanding the evolving requirements (Beynon-Davies et al., 2000).

During IRD, analysts spend considerable time interacting with stakeholders and examining process artefacts to identify the requirements of the proposed system. Three stages of requirements determination are: (1) information gathering, during which the analyst elicits requirements from stakeholders about needs for the proposed system; (2) representation, during which the requirements are represented in a physical form; and (3) verification, during which the requirements are verified with stakeholders (Larsen & Naumann, 1992; Vitalari, 1992). The present research is concerned with information gathering, or what is often referred to as requirements elicitation. Techniques for eliciting requirements include inquiring about and/or observing work, examining documents related to the proposed system, and inferring user needs (Davis, 1982; Browne & Ramesh, 2002). We focus our attention on the technique of inquiry.

Inquiry in requirements elicitation requires significant analyst and user interaction (Lauer et al., 1992). The success of this interaction lies in creating an environment that supports communication with stakeholders (Coughlan et al., 2003; Gallivan & Keil, 2003). Typically, the analyst guides the inquiry activities (Tan, 1994) and is responsible for identifying relevant information. The analyst can facilitate successful inquiry by deliberately creating an open communication environment (Gallivan & Keil, 2003).

The ability to elicit requirements effectively depends in part on familiarity with a domain (Walz et al., 1993; Marakas & Elam, 1998). Strong problem-solving skills are also critical for successful requirements elicitation (Cerveny et al., 1990; Mumford, 2000; Vessey & Conger, 1993). These skills and domain knowledge assist the analyst in structuring the problem, acquiring and organizing information, and transforming that information into a complete and accurate set of system requirements (Vitalari & Dickson, 1983). However, even sufficient domain knowledge and strong problem-solving skills do not guarantee requirements elicitation success. Analysts are subject to cognitive limitations that can hamper or prevent adequate requirements elicitation. We therefore turn next to these cognitive limitations.

## Cognitive challenges of requirements elicitation

Requirements elicitation is subject to three fundamental cognitive challenges: (1) limitations of humans as information processors; (2) the complex nature of requirements; and (3) the obstacles encountered in user and analyst interaction (Davis, 1982). This research focuses on the first challenge, i.e. human information processing limitations and their influence on requirements elicitation. The limitations on information processing underlie all human behaviour, and thus the behaviour of analysts and users alike. Table 1 lists the limitations recognized as detrimentally affecting requirements elicitation. It is known that constraints on working and longterm memory affect the information processing capabilities of analysts (see Browne & Ramesh, 2002). In addition, cognitive heuristics and resulting biases can also influence analysts’ information processing (Davis, 1982; Stacy & Macmillan, 1995). We now discuss these limitations in some detail.

## Memory constraints

Working memory. The term ‘working memory’ is used to describe people’s immediate processing of information; it is what we call ‘thinking’. The processing capability of working memory is constrained, having a capacity of seven plus or minus two items of information for most people (Miller, 1956). For requirements elicitation, this means that the analyst and user can only focus on small amounts of information at a time. Consequently, the analyst may only consider a narrow set of sources of information. Analysts can reduce the risk of inadequate requirements by using prompting techniques that increase the chances that the user will think of more relevant information (Lauer et al., 1992; Couger, 1996; Marakas & Elam, 1998).

Associated with the constraints of working memory is the notion of bounded rationality. As a result of working memory constraints, people cannot consider all possible alternatives to a problem and must construct simplified models for solving the problem. Thus, human rationality is bounded during problem solving and decision-making. A consequence of this behaviour is the tendency for problem solvers to ‘satisfice’, that is, to be satisfied with a suboptimal solution that fits their simplified models (Simon, 1957). The use of prompts that challenge an analyst’s simplistic model and offer increased structure for the problem can assist in obtaining a more complete and accurate set of requirements.

Long-term memory. The phrase ‘long-term memory’ describes the cognitive structures that facilitate the storage and retrieval of information not active in working memory. While the storage capacity of long-term memory is virtually boundless, people have difficulties in the recall and use of information from long-term memory (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Reisberg, 2005). As a result, requirements tend to be overly focused on the analyst’s knowledge of current procedures, prior experiences, or recently encountered information (Davis, 1982). In addition, research has shown that in committing information to long-term memory, people generally store only the ‘gist’ of the information (Brainerd & Reyna, 1992). To recall the information, an analyst must reconstruct the knowledge from the general picture that is stored. Unfortunately, this reconstruction may lead to an inexact recollection of knowledge. Analysts can improve requirements elicitation by using prompts intended to overcome the limitations of long-term memory recall and reconstruction.

## Heuristics and biases

Availability. The availability heuristic refers to the tendency of people to rely on the ease with which information can be remembered or visualized when assessing the likelihood of events (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973; Schwarz & Vaughn, 2002). Although this heuristic works wel much of the time, its use invokes biases resulting from the recency or vividness of an event or simply from information that is easily recalled. For example, an analyst may perform requirements elicitation based on memories of his or her most recent analysis project. Unfortunately, this may lead the analyst to limit the breadth and depth of requirements exploration. Prompts designed to overcome the biases of the availability heuristic can aid the analyst in exploring requirements fully.

Anchoring and adjustment. The second heuristic that can affect requirements determination is anchoring and adjustment. Anchoring on a problem structure usually occurs when some piece of information has been offered early in the problem-solving process (Spetzler & Stael von Holstein, 1975). Subsequent information presented may result in adjustments of the initial problem structure (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Epley & Gilovich, 2006). Typically, adjustments made from an anchor are less than if the adjustment had been made from another basis (Slovic & Lichtenstein, 1971). This is called the insufficient adjustment bias. Analysts frequently anchor on existing processes during requirements elicitation (Davis, 1982). The insufficient adjustments made from that anchor result in slight changes to the process. As a result, the analyst has difficulty exploring alternative paths of requirements elicitation. Prompts that lessen the possibility of anchoring should encourage more complete requirements elicitation.

Another bias resulting from the anchoring and adjustment heuristic is overconfidence. People consistently exhibit overconfidence in knowledge across a wide range of tasks and difficulty levels (Koriat et al., 1980; Browne et al., 1999; Klayman et al., 1999). Unfortunately, even when people have less knowledge about a particular subject area, their level of confidence remains unreasonably high (Nickerson & McGoldrick, 1965; Klayman et al., 1999). The overconfidence bias poses the greatest threat in areas outside the decision-maker’s domain. An analyst is required to perform requirements elicitation in various functional areas of an organization, possibly including some in which he has little or no substantive knowledge. Evidence suggests that the analyst may approach requirements elicitation with overconfidence in his understanding of the situation. Prompts that provide the analyst with feedback about the correctness of his knowledge and opportunities to test his judgements can be effective in reducing the effects of overconfidence (Koriat et al., 1980; Lichtenstein et al., 1982).

Representativeness. The representativeness heuristic is defined as the degree to which an event or item is representative of the characteristics of the population from which it came (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Kahneman & Frederick, 2002). Use of this heuristic results in judgements of similarity. People often categorize a problem based on initial cues (Simon, 1978) and take what is known about this category to complete the formulation of the problem. There is strong indication that analysts often use this type of problem solving by similarity, or representativeness, when eliciting requirements. Previously developed information systems judged to be similar to the proposed system are used as the basis for identifying requirements (Adelson & Soloway, 1985; Jeffries et al., 1981). Unfortunately, there is also evidence of problem solvers’ tendencies to force similarity when it is inappropriate (Wason & Johnson-Laird, 1972).

One bias that results from application of the representativeness heuristic is insensitivity to sample size. Typically, people do not give appropriate consideration to sample size and draw faulty conclusions based on small samples (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Frequently, systems analysts model requirements based on low occurrences of information. Elicitation prompts that test and validate requirements can increase the analyst’s awareness of the prevalence of an issue and encourage her to examine the requirements more fully.

A final important cognitive bias is the confirmation bias. Experimental evidence reveals that people possess a strong tendency to seek only confirmatory evidence and fail to consider alternative hypotheses (Wason, 1971; Mynatt et al., 1977; 1978; Klayman & Ha, 1987). The effect of the confirmation bias is so strong that even when instructed to look for information to falsify hypotheses, there is little or no difference in behaviour (Mynatt et al., 1978). However, it has been shown that people will use disconfirming data once it is presented to them (Mynatt et al., 1977). Providing the analyst with specific prompts for seeking contradictory evidence should minimize the effect of the confirmation bias.

If not recognized and attended to, these problems caused by limitations of human information processing (working memory capacity, bounded rationality, long-term memory recall and reconstruction, cognitive heuristics and resulting biases) can confound the analyst’s ability to elicit requirements successfully. Past research has shown that these types of cognitive challenges cause the analyst to develop a false sense of completeness of the requirements, resulting in insufficient requirements elicitation (Pitts & Browne, 2004). Requirements elicitation methods and tools should recognize the threats of these limitations and provide mechanisms for overcoming their consequences.

## Using information prompts to improve requirements elicitation

Analysts commonly make the fundamental mistake of asking the wrong questions during requirements elicitation (Wetherbe, 1991). The analyst cannot simply ask the user ‘What would you like the system to do?’ Questions of this type reveal that analysts often do not know what questions to ask (Moody et al., 1998). The analyst must ask questions that cause the user to reason and generate arguments (Johnson et al., 1991; Browne & Rogich, 2001), which stimulate cognition. The questions can be context-dependent or context-independent (Browne et al., 1997). Context-independent questions are particularly useful because they can be used in many different situations (Browne & Ramesh, 2002).

Several context-independent strategies for requirements elicitation have been proposed in the literature. Wetherbe (1991) introduced a technique for requirements elicitation in which three different methods are combined to assist the analyst in obtaining a complete set of requirements: Business Systems Planning, Critical Success Factors and Ends/Means Analysis. Each method provides a set of generic questions that can be modified to suit the analyst’s circumstances. Couger (1996) recommended a questioning technique for gathering requirements based on interrogatories, e.g. who, what, where, when, why and how. However, although both of these strategies have context-independent elements, both require domain knowledge and creativity on the part of the analyst to generate meaningful questions. Furthermore, both have received limited empirical testing.

Lauer et al. (1992) introduced and tested a context-independent questioning technique, termed the ‘semantic method’, in which questions are classified as events, conditions, actions, agents, or goals (see also Lehnert, 1978; Graesser et al., 1992). These question classifications are intended to assist analysts’ organization of knowledge. In an exploratory analysis, Lauer et al. (1992) found evidence of a relationship between these question types and the accuracy of Data Flow Diagrams (DFDs). More recently Marakas & Elam (1998) tested this relationship empirically and found that particular types of questions had a significant effect on the accuracy of DFDs, and that improvements in accuracy were independent of analyst experience. However, the results of this research are limited because the elicited requirements were not evaluated, so it is not completely clear what actually contributed to the construction of the DFDs.

Continuing this line of research, Browne & Rogich (2001) developed a questioning strategy they termed the ‘task characteristics technique’. This technique has the explicit goals of generalizability (independent of system development context) and power (dependent on similarities within system development projects). There are two types of prompts included in the task characteristics technique: substantive and procedural. Substantive prompts are expected to elicit particular types of requirements, while procedural prompts are intended to overcome cognitive challenges faced by the analyst and user. The authors tested the effectiveness of interrogatories and semantic prompts against the task characteristics technique, measuring the quantity and nature of information requirements elicited. Results indicated that the task characteristics technique elicited significantly more requirements than either the interrogatories technique or the semantic technique.

In the present study, we continue this line of research by developing new context-independent procedural prompts and testing their value in requirements elicitation efforts (some of the prompts are adapted from Browne & Rogich, 2001). In particular, we aim to provide evidence that procedural prompts can mitigate the effects of insufficient requirements elicitation. We next turn to a discussion of these procedural prompts.

## Procedural prompts

The procedural prompts developed for this research are grounded in cognitive theory and derived from argument strategies. Argument strategies are generally present in human decision-making, although there is often no conscious effort to use them (Kuhn, 1991). Typical argument strategies include scenario building, elaboration and counterargument. Prior research has shown that context-independent prompts based on argument strategies elicit information from people that they might not otherwise evoke (Browne et al., 1997). ## Summarization and feedback

Summarization and feedback are effective for overcoming working memory limitations and the satisficing behaviour caused by bounded rationality (Browne & Rogich, 2001). For information to remain active in working memory, it must be rehearsed (Baddeley et al., 1975) and revisited (Conrad, 1964). An analyst may ask the user to summarize what he has said or provide a summary to the user for his consideration. This summarization of information serves as a form of rehearsal. Likewise, offering feedback allows the user to think through the information again, causing the information to remain active in working memory. Prompting strategies that provide an opportunity for the analyst and user to re-evaluate acquired information can help both of them to attend to more information in working memory (Simon, 1957). This should result in a more complete and more accurate set of requirements.

## Repetition and rephrasing

Problems associated with long-term memory can be diminished through the use of repetition and rephrasing. Repetition and rephrasing triggers the user to repeatedly return to long-term memory, thereby increasing the likelihood of improved recall and reconstruction. This provides a means for the analyst to question the validity of the information with which she is operating (Tan, 1994). Both the recency and confirmation biases restrict the search of long-term memory. By revisiting the requirements through repetition and rephrasing, the analyst is able to validate the requirements, and perhaps to identify points at which the requirements are insufficient. In this way she may open the inquiry to additional paths of elicitation (Koriat et al., 1980), resulting in a deeper and more accurate set of requirements.

## Scenario building and elaboration

One scenario building technique utilizes mental imagery. When using mental imagery, the analyst presents the user with a situation and encourages him to visualize the circumstances and consequences rather than simply remember them (Zmud et al., 1993). This type of visualization is likely to elicit unique or contradictory requirements. Elaboration asks a user to place himself in a situation expected to take place in the future and then explain what might happen (Boland, 1984). The use of elaboration and similar strategies by the analyst results in the development of a richer representation of the requirements. The technique of scenario analysis has been shown in the past to be useful in acquiring and validating requirements (Potts et al., 1994). Scenario building and elaboration strategies can be used to enhance recall from long-term memory, assist with the reconstruction of memories, and reduce the confirmation bias (Kahneman & Tversky, 1982; Anderson, 1990). The scenario building and elaboration strategies may cause the analyst to elicit information from different or unusual perspectives; such information may challenge the analyst’s representation and force him to investigate disconfirming evidence.

## Counterargument

The use of counterarguments is expected to overcome the insufficient anchor adjustment and overconfidence biases of the anchoring heuristic, the recency and ease of recall biases of the availability heuristic, the small sample size bias of the representativeness heuristic, and the confirmation bias. Invoking counterarguments is effective in preventing people from anchoring on current procedures and will force the analyst to consider different or unique viewpoints in the requirements (Perkins et al., 1983). Similarly, this technique reduces the impact of recency and recall biases because counterargument generation has the effect of increasing the number of associations in the analyst’s memory (Perkins et al., 1983). In addition, counterarguments may force the individual to re-evaluate the validity and justification of his beliefs (Koriat et al., 1980), thereby generating new or disconfirming evidence. Information prompts that utilize a counterargument strategy should increase the likelihood of eliciting additional information and improve the completeness of the resulting set of requirements.

## METHODOLOGY

## Sample

Participants were voluntarily recruited from organizations in a variety of industry segments in a major metropolitan area. A total of 54 practising systems analysts participated in the study. The sample consisted of 64.8% men and 35.2% women, with an average age of 42.3 years. The data in the present study were collected and coded in conjunction with research published in Pitts & Browne (2004). This study is an extension of that work.

## Procedure

The experiment utilized a case scenario for the development of an online grocery shopping information system. The scenario appears in the Appendix. This scenario has been used successfully in past research (Browne & Rogich, 2001; Pitts & Browne, 2004). The familiarity of the grocery shopping task increased the likelihood of similar domain knowledge across all participants, while providing enough realism to stimulate and challenge the analysts. None of the participants had experience in the retail grocery industry.

A person blind to the purposes of the study was selected as the ‘user’. The ‘user’ assumed the role of store manager in the scenario and was provided with detailed background concerning requirements for the system. To ensure consistency and control in the experiment, the same user met with all participants. The user received thorough instruction and was trained to a predefined set of requirements (the set of acceptable requirements was developed in earlier research by Browne & Rogich, 2001), which guaranteed a level of uniformity in requirements statement presentation. In addition, 20 practice interviews were conducted with analysts who were not part of the final sample. Consistency of response by the user was observed prior to the completion of the trial information gathering sessions. Furthermore, the consistency of the user’s responses was maintained during the entire data collection process through the use of periodic consistency tests.

Each participant was tested individually and each session was tape recorded. The participants (analysts) were provided with instructions concerning the task and then told to elicit requirements from the user for the system. The analysts were not given any other information about the systems development process (that is, they were not asked to assume anything except that they were to elicit requirements from this user). The participant made inquiries and the user responded with information from the set of requirements. The participant gathered requirements from the user until he felt that he had sufficient information to continue with the design of the system.

Upon completion of this initial requirements elicitation, the participant was randomly offered one of two strategies for eliciting additional requirements: interrogatories prompts or procedural prompts. The participant was tutored in example prompts for the technique and told that he may use as many or as few of these questions as he wished. Furthermore, he was encouraged to generate his own prompts utilizing the strategies exhibited by the technique. The participant was asked to continue with the requirements elicitation task using the assigned technique, and stopped the elicitation when he no longer had any additional questions to ask the user. In the present research, only the impact of the prompts on the requirements elicitation efforts was of interest.

## Measures

The requirements elicited by each participant were identified by examining the protocol of each requirements elicitation session. The protocols were transcribed, parsed and coded into categories determined a priori. The method of parsing used is known as ‘context space’ parsing (Reichman-Adar, 1984; Curley et al., 1995). The categories used in our study are based on a taxonomy proposed by Byrd et al. (1992) and expanded upon by Rogich (1997). Four generic requirements categories of Goal, Process, Task and Information were defined. These categories were further refined, resulting in 28 distinct subcategories. ## RESULTS

All participants reported at least 2 years of experience as an analyst, with a mean of 11.07 years. We felt confident that our sample of analysts had adequate experience to have well-developed strategies for requirements elicitation. On average, participants spent 48.31 min on the elicitation task with no statistically significant difference between the amount of time spent on the task by each treatment group (P = 0.257). Therefore, we concluded that time at task had no effect on the results.

## Requirements elicited by prompting technique

Next, the data were analysed to determine whether participants using the procedural prompts obtained significantly more requirements than participants using the interrogatories prompts. Table 7 presents the means and standard deviations for the quantity, breadth and depth of requirements elicited by participants utilizing the two types of prompts. Independent sample ttests were used to analyse the data, and the results are summarized in Table 8. This analysis reveals that, while all participants elicited more requirements, those using the procedural prompts obtained significantly greater quantity, breadth and depth of requirements than those using the interrogatories prompts. These results provide support for hypotheses 3 and 4.

This extends the findings of the previous analyses, which revealed that the general use of additional prompting caused the participants to move beyond the point at which they would have ended their usual information acquisition. The procedural prompts used here were specifically designed to overcome the cognitive obstacles shown to lead to insufficient requirements elicitation. Our results show that the use of procedural prompts results in the elicitation of a fuller set of requirements.

## DISCUSSION

This research contributes to the developing cognitive research surrounding IRD. Past studies have investigated and tested the use of context-independent prompts for increasing knowledge elicitation (e.g. Marakas & Elam, 1998; Browne & Rogich, 2001). In the present work, we have extended these findings by empirically testing the usefulness of context-independent procedural prompts within the requirements elicitation task. Simply asking analysts to continue with requirements elicitation, and providing some prompting recommendations, results in additional information elicited. However, our results also show that it is relatively simple to instruct analysts in the use of procedural prompts and that these prompts, designed in particular to stimulate the cognition of systems analysts, can motivate them to acquire additional meaningfu information. Furthermore, this study has demonstrated that the use of procedural prompts generated significantly greater quantity and completeness of requirements than the use of interrogatory prompts.

From a research perspective, we have shown that procedural prompts improve the communication between analysts and users in one-on-one settings. It is likely that this type of prompting would also be effective in other requirements elicitation settings involving groups of analysts and users (e.g. JAD sessions). Because the cognitive limitations discussed here are present in all individuals, groups are also likely to arrive at insufficient requirements. Procedural prompts that encourage the examination of alternative sources of information and seek disconfirming evidence should lessen some of the difficulties associated with group work, such as groupthink and political or social dominance, which might bring about inadequate requirements elicitation. For example, repetition and rephrasing prompts increase the likelihood of improved long-term memory recall and therefore tend to produce new requirements (Koriat et al., 1980). In addition, counterargument prompts should be effective in group situations, as they are effective in bringing to light unique viewpoints (Perkins et al., 1983) and reducing overconfidence (Koriat et al., 1980).

From a practical standpoint, prior research has shown that providing focus and structure to requirements gathering results in more accurate and complete outcomes (Agarwal & Tanniru, 1990; Marakas & Elam, 1998). Introducing context-independent procedural prompts into requirements elicitation offers additional structure to the analyst’s activities. We have demonstrated that analysts can be trained to utilize the prompts presented here when forming questions for requirements elicitation. However, many analysts are unfamiliar with these strategies, as they are not part of a typical systems analysis program of study. Utilization of specially designed interview templates or question checklists should become as commonplace to analysts as Gantt charts or Use Cases. Providing these types of tools to all analysts as part of their ‘requirements determination toolbox’ should substantially improve requirements elicitation efforts.

Furthermore, effort should be made to incorporate this type of prompting into system development methodologies. Increasingly, system development projects begin without a clear definition of requirements, with the requirements evolving throughout the project (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2004). Analysts want requirements to be fixed and stable, but requirements and the environment in which they exist are volatile (Boehm & Turner, 2004; Larman, 2004). The fact that requirements start out as ambiguous and vague puts additional emphasis on the need to continually question the assumptions in place. Counterarguments and repetition and rephrasing, for example, cause both the analyst and the user to re-evaluate the reliability and validity of the requirements and identify potential insufficiencies (Koriat et al., 1980; Tan, 1994).

In some development contexts, requirements are seen as adjustable, informal stories (Boehm & Turner, 2004). Use cases are examples of situations based on ‘stories’ that involve the interaction of analysts and users for the identification of requirements. The completed use cases become the ‘sign-off’ for the system requirements (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2004). It is therefore critical for the analyst to obtain the best information possible for constructing these use cases. Scenario building and elaboration are particularly good techniques for eliciting rich, detailed requirements as they stimulate consideration of circumstances and consequences in the systems development environment (Zmud et al., 1993). In addition, the use of summarization and feedback during the generation of use cases can help elicit a more complete set of information related to the task because of the rehearsal and revisiting of requirements (Conrad, 1964; Baddeley et al., 1975). The use of the four types of procedural prompts should aid in the proper development of requirements for system development.

In summary, there are several important practical implications to be gained from this research. First, utilization of procedural prompts in requirements elicitation provides much needed structure and focus to the activity. Second, it is clear that procedural prompts can be inserted into existing methodologies to increase the effectiveness of requirements elicitation. Counterarguments and repetition/rephrasing are valuable for validating requirements, and scenario building and summarization/feedback are beneficial for discovery of unique alternatives and information. Finally, these procedural prompts can be used in a variety of contexts, including traditional and contemporary development environments involving individuals or groups. A summary of these practical implications is shown in Table 9.

As with most research, this study has limitations that can be noted. First, to maintain appropriate scientific rigor, we have sacrificed some reality. For example, the analysts conducted their interviews in a one-on-one format with a limited amount of information available. We used this approach to be able to control the individual methods of inquiry. In addition, in this study we did not consider the impact of individual differences on the effectiveness of the prompting tools. This is certainly an important issue to consider in future work in this area.

## FoodCo requirements determination task

FoodCo, Inc. is a regional supermarket chain with stores in several contiguous states. In light of increasing competition in the supermarket industry, FoodCo has developed a strategy for increasing its customer base and, consequently, its sales. One item within this strategy is the implementation of an online grocery shopping system whereby customers can do their grocery shopping from home via computer.

You are a systems analyst for the Information Systems department at FoodCo. You have been working at FoodCo for several years and have a full understanding of the internal processes required for developing an information system at FoodCo. In your tenure at FoodCo, you have been involved in the development of various strategic and operational information systems.

As the first step in the implementation of the online grocery shopping system, FoodCo must identify a set of requirements that will guide the design and development of the system. Because of your analysis experience at FoodCo, you have been given the assignment of defining the requirements for the new online shop at home system.

To assist in the determination of requirements, you will have the opportunity to interview a FoodCo store manager. Store managers at FoodCo will be internal end-users of the online shopping system. Furthermore, they are responsible for all the store’s operations and handle all the day-to-day problems encountered at the store. As a result, they are qualified to provide you with information concerning the needs of online grocery shoppers.

During the interview session, you should ask the store manager any questions you feel will help identify the requirements necessary for the development of FoodCo’s new online grocery shopping system. The IS manager in your department is expecting you to provide an informal report of your findings. Therefore, when you feel you have identified a complete set of requirements, you will need to prepare a diagram that represents the future system. You may use any technique with which you are comfortable (e.g. E-R diagrams, Process Flow diagrams).
