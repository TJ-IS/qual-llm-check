# Reconceptualizing synergy to explain the value of business analytics systems

Ida Someh<sup>1</sup>, Graeme Shanks<sup>2</sup> and Michael Davern<sup>2</sup>

Journal of Information Technology 1–21 © Association for Information Technology Trust 2019 Article reuse guidelines: sagepub.com/journals-permissions https://doi.org/10.1177/026839621881621DOI: 10.1177/0268396218816210 Journals.sagepub.com/jinf ⑤SAGE

## Abstract

How can we use synergy to explain the value created by business analytics systems? In this article, we conceptualize and operationalize two important aspects of synergy: namely, the synergistic relationship and the synergistic outcome. We explore the enablers and mechanisms that are involved in a synergistic relationship between business analytics systems and customer relationship management systems and define it as the ability of systems to work together, span their boundaries and complement each other. Synergistic outcomes are the new business analytics–enabled customer relationship management systems that emerge from the synergistic relationship between business analytics systems and customer relationship management systems. Taking a whole system perspective, business analytics–enabled customer relationship management systems comprise the components and the emergent properties that arise from their interaction (e.g. the ability to cross-sell and up-sell based on advanced computational methods), in which the emergent properties are new because they do not exist in the individual components. We develop a research model that uses Synergistic Relationship and Synergistic Outcomes to explain the business value created by business analytics systems and customer relationship management systems, and we test this model using a survey of 201 managers in Australia and the United States. We find that the synergistic relationship plays a significant role in the creation of business analytics–enabled customer relationship management systems and subsequently business value. Business analytics–enabled customer relationship management systems—comprising business analytics systems, customer relationship management systems and their emergent properties—contribute to transactional, informational and strategic value. This goes beyond the value created by the business analytics and customer relationship management systems individually, as measured through statistical interaction.

## Keywords

Systems approach, synergy, synergistic relationship, emergent properties, business value, business analytics, IT-enabled organizational systems

## Introduction

Business analytics (BA) systems include people, processes and technologies that translate the raw data of organizations into meaningful information and insights. Managers rely on the analytical insights of BA systems to make better decisions and take competitive actions. Industry-based studies have emphasized the importance of these systems to managers ( H.Chen, Chiang, & Storey, 2012; Davenport & Bean, 2018; IBM, 2013; LaValle, Lesser, Shockley, Hopkins, & Kruschwitz, 2011; Wixom & Ross, 2017). The SIM IT Key Issues and Trends study has ranked BA as the number one IT investment for 9years in a row, from 2009 to 2017 (Kappelman et al., 2018).

Despite the strategic importance of BA systems for managers (Hagerty, Sallam, & Richardson, 2012), limited scholarly research has endeavored to explain how BA systems create business value. The business value of IT literature has provided evidence that IT systems generate value when they synergistically interact with other organizational systems (Nevo & Wade, 2010, 2011). When IT and other organizational systems are synergistically related, together they create IT-enabled organizational systems, which, in turn, create business value. BA systems are an important subset of informational IT systems (Aral & Weill, 2007). In recent years, researchers have proposed several theoretical models to explain the business value of BA systems (Elbashir, Collier, & Davern, 2008; Isik, Jones, & Sidorova, 2011; Piccoli & Watson, 2008; Shanks & Bekmamedova, 2012; Shanks, Bekmamedova, & Willcocks, 2012; Trkman, McCormack, De Oliveira, & Ladeira, 2010; Wixom, Yen, & Relich, 2013). However, none of these models consider the synergistic combination of BA systems with other organizational systems. More importantly, there is little IS research that addresses the conceptualization and assessment of synergy (Nevo & Wade, 2010); existing research only considers fragmented aspects of synergy. Hence, we ask, “how do business analytics systems synergistically interact with other organizational systems to create business value?”

To answer the research question, we adopt a systems approach (Burton-Jones, Mclean, & Monod, 2015) to model the interactions between BA and other organizational systems. From a systems perspective, BA systems interact with other organizational systems, and if their relationship is synergistic, together they create BA-enabled organizational systems with emergent properties (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Emergent properties are a source of significant business value. In this article, we focus in particular on customer relationship management (CRM) systems as the “other” organizational system. This enables us to adequately and accurately measure the business impact of BA systems combined with an “other” organizational system. CRM systems are widely used in practice and can potentially benefit significantly from BA systems.

Our objective is to use synergy to theoretically explain the value created by BA systems. To achieve this, we first trace the evolution of the synergy construct and conceptualize two important and distinct aspects of synergy: Synergistic Relationship and Synergistic Outcomes. Second, we develop and test a research model that uses synergistic relationship and synergistic outcome to explain the business value of BA systems. The synergy between BA systems and CRM systems provides the context for our research model. This specific context helps us to (1) measure the impact of synergy in creating value from BA systems and (2) contribute to the broader body of knowledge on quantitative assessment of business value of IT systems.

We provide three main contributions. Theoretically, we develop a novel conceptualization for Synergistic Relationship. We define synergistic relationship as the ability of systems to work together, span their boundaries and complement each other. We conceptualize synergistic relationship in terms of the enablers and mechanisms tha synergistically connect systems and lead to synergistic outcomes. Synergistic outcomes are the higher-order sys tems with emergent properties that arise from the interac tion between systems. This focus extends the current literature on synergy (Ferratt, Prasad, & Enns, 2012; Nevo & Wade, 2010; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) by providing a comprehensive view, including both how synergy can be realized and how it can be measured. It also responds to a recent call for further research on synergy between IT systems as a driver of business value (Schryen, 2013). Empirically, our study is one of the few that provide quantitative and comprehensive evidence for the creation of business value from BA systems. The synergy concept and the systems approach allow us to capture the value of a BA-enabled CRM system as a whole, comprising not just the BA and the CRM components individually, but also the emergent properties. BA-enabled CRM systems contribute value beyond that of the constituent BA and CRM systems through the creation of emergent properties (e.g. the ability to cross-sell and up-sell based on advanced computationa methods). This approach extends the understanding of busi ness value documented in the existing IT literature, which previously has either not measured or underestimated the value of interacting systems and emergent properties. For example, prior studies have operationalized synergistic outcomes, using statistical interaction between variables pertaining to the two interacting systems (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012), or as an enhancement of existing properties rathe than emergent properties (Nevo & Wade, 2011). Practically, the enablers and mechanisms of synergy provide managerial guidance for creating synergies between BA and other organizational systems. Particularly, enablers and mechanisms translate to organizational practices that could be implemented to generate value from BA. While many organizations are pressured to create value from BA, few have achieved progress in changing the fabric of their organizational culture to be data-driven (Davenport & Bean, 2018). We also provide a more comprehensive justification for investments in BA systems

We proceed as follows: we first develop our theoretical conceptualization of synergy. The next section describes our research model. Then, we discuss the research method, and following that, we present results of the empirical tests of the model. We conclude with a discussion of contributions to research, implications for practice, limitations and directions for future research.

## Theoretical development

Our aim is to use synergy to theoretically explain the value created by BA systems. To do so, we first demonstrate the importance of synergy in relation to BA systems. Second, we review the literature on synergy and reconceptualize synergy as both relationship and outcome.

## BA systems

BA systems leverage advanced algorithms to analyze organizational data and deliver actionable information to decision makers (Davenport & Harris, 2007; LaValle et al., 2011). BA systems are an evolution of business intelligence (BI) systems that were focused on business reporting, as well as supporting ad hoc managerial queries. Traditional BI systems provided simple manipulation and visualization of structured data (e.g. financial metrics). With the proliferation of advanced analytics methods, new sources of data and new types of business metrics (e.g. customer sentiment) have become important for organizations. BA as a new area and data scientists as a new community of practice have emerged to handle sophisticated analysis of structured and unstructured data ( H.Chen et al., 2012; Marchand & Peppard, 2013). Digital dashboards play a delivery role for the insights generated by BA systems. For this reason, our definition of BA systems includes BI.

Here, we discuss why synergy between BA and other organizational systems is critical to achieve value. BA people, processes and technologies belong to a particular community of practice (Wenger, 1998) that specializes in analytics and visualization. A community of practice is a group of people with shared interests and similar training, skillset and ways of work (Wenger, 1998). BA systems alone are not able to create value; rather, they interact with other business communities of practice (such as marketing, sales or human resources) and influence their people, processes and technologies as part of developing a data-driven culture (Davenport, 2006; Davenport & Harris, 2007). This is not just providing business people with a number of tools; instead, BA people should (1) influence business people to think, behave and act based on evidence and data; (2) change business processes to include analytics; and (3) embed analytics into transactional systems (Davenport, 2006). BA people are mostly commonly trained in computer science, statistics, mathematics and/or operations research, yet they need to understand the business landscape, work with business people and help them achieve their business goals in their specific business domains. Business communities of practice need to absorb the analytical thinking provided by BA and use analytical outputs to make decisions and also present business problems, decision requirements and feedback to BA teams (Davenport, Harris, & Morison, 2010). Although the two communities need to interact to successfully implement BA initiatives, their languages and ways of working are inherently different. We argue that the concept of synergy and the systems theory approach provides an effective lens for studying these interactions, and the new ways of working that emerge from them.

## Synergy

The term synergy comes from the Greek word “synergos,” meaning “working together.” It is grounded in systems theory, which seeks to examine phenomena as a whole, rather than by piecemeal analysis of the individual parts (Burton-Jones et al., 2015). The whole system equals the sum of its parts plus their interactions (Ackoff, 1971; Churchman, 1968). Synergy thus suggests that the value of the whole is greater than the additive value of the individual parts (Tanriverdi, 2006), where those parts may be viewed as systems (e.g. people, processes and technologies). Although synergy is associated with positive outcomes (Nevo & Wade, 2010), negative synergy can arise when the constituent parts operate as substitutes (Titah & Barki, 2009).

The concept of synergy extends the prior approaches<sup>1</sup> used to model and measure IT business value (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Information system (IS) researchers have often relied on the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Barney, 1991), which views organizations as a bundle of resources (Barney, 1991). While RBV has been successful in explaining the business value of IT, it is limited in modeling interactions between resources and emergent properties that may arise from such interactions (Nevo & Wade, 2010). By contrast, systems theory helps to conceptualize the interaction among organizational systems, which collectively create greater impact. Following Nevo and Wade (2010), we adopt systems theory and argue that a resource from RBV corresponds to a system from the systems approach. This enables us to compare our findings to prior research conducted using RBV.

## Synergy in the information systems literature

Despite many arguments and theoretical explanations about synergy (Evans, 1996; Grover & Kohli, 2012; Kim & Mukhopadhyay, 2011; Melville, Kraemer, & Gurbaxani, 2004; Rai, Pavlou, & Du, 2012; Roberts, Galluch, Dinger, & Grover, 2012; Teo & Ranganathan, 2003), there is limited IS research that addresses the conceptualization and measurement of synergy.<sup>2</sup> The few exceptions in the literature have typically examined only one or two fragmented elements of synergy (Ferratt et al., 2012; Nevo & Wade, 2010; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012). Conceptually, synergy has commonly been defined as new effects that are created by the interaction between two variables and operationalized by a statistical interaction effect. However, measuring synergy as statistical interaction does not capture the emergent properties that arise from interactions between systems in practice. Nevo and Wade (2010, 2011) used systems theory and defined synergy as positive emergent properties that arise from the interaction between IT assets and other organizational systems. However, they focused only on IT assets, and the enhancement of existing properties (Nevo & Wade, 2011) of the constituent systems using a reflective measure, rather than incorporating new properties that emerge from the interaction.

Moreover, prior literature is theoretically and empirically silent on the synergistic relationship from which the synergistic outcomes emerge. While emergent properties are apparent and observable, it is very difficult to model exactly how subsystems interact to constitute a whole (Corning, 2000). Most of the current empirical research has explored synergy enablers (Nevo & Wade, 2010, 2011) or synergy mechanisms (Ferratt et al., 2012; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) in isolation. Enablers indicate the ability of the systems to interact, whereas mechanisms specify the social processes by which emergent properties arise. Consequently, we need a comprehensive and integrated view of synergy that considers synergy as both relationship and outcome and that captures both the enablers and the mechanisms that constitute the synergistic relationship. Examining both enablers and mechanism allows us to understand how synergy is created, and not just assess the potential for synergy or the outcomes of it.

## Reconceptualizing synergy as relationship and outcome

Adopting a wider view of synergy, we focus on defining and operationalizing two constructs: Synergistic Relationship and Synergistic Outcomes. We consider the enablers and mechanisms that describe a synergistic relationship between two systems, and the positive emergent properties (outcomes) that arise from their relationship (Someh & Shanks, 2013). We explore these concepts further below.

## Synergistic relationships

We define a synergistic relationship as one in which systems work together, span their boundaries and complement each other. This is more than just a simple interaction of otherwise independent parts: it is about how the synergistic outcomes are achieved. Drawing on Nan (2011), we use the term synergistic relationship to encapsulate the enablers and mechanisms that are involved in the bottom-up emergence of new properties. We draw on prior literature to identify a comprehensive set of enablers and mechanisms. Enablers of a synergistic relationship, such as compatibility and integration (Nevo & Wade, 2010), are the characteristics of systems (Wand, Storey, & Weber, 1999) that facilitate their synergistic relationship. Although enablers indicate the ability of the systems to interact, they do not specify the processes or social mechanisms by which emergent properties arise (Avgerou, 2013). We extend prior work (Nevo & Wade, 2010) to address mechanisms as well as enablers. The mechanisms we describe below include reinforcement, learning, embeddedness and influence. Together, the enablers and mechanisms form the synergistic relationship.

Our definition of a synergistic relationship—in which systems work together, span their boundaries and complement each other—is grounded in these enablers and mechanisms. Systems can work together if they are compatible and integrated. Systems span their boundaries if they learn from each other, influence each other and become embed ded in each other’s processes and social network. Systems complement each other through reinforcement mechanisms. Table 1 shows a summary of the enablers and mechanisms that underlie a synergistic relationship. We discuss the enablers and mechanisms in detail below.

Enablers of a synergistic relationship. Compatibility refers to the degree to which systems align and fit together and is achieved when systems are able to seamlessly work with each other. Compatible systems have compatible people, processes and technologies and are able to use each other’s functionalities within their own tasks. The compatibility between systems will facilitate their synergistic relationship (Nevo & Wade, 2010, 2011).

Integration effort refers to the effort of management to bring the systems (people, processes and technologies) together and encourage and direct their interaction congru ent with organizational goals (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Management is responsible for enabling the integration of various parts of an organization and planning how the interaction among systems will serve the organization. Related management tasks include assigning decision rights, providing adequate training and changing the organizational structure to ensure that the organizational systems seamlessly work together.

Mechanisms in a synergistic relationship. Mechanisms are a sequence of social processes and events that take place among systems and explain how the whole system with emergent properties can be brought about. We identify four mechanisms from prior literature that underpin our definition of a synergistic relationship. The reinforcement mechanism underpins the complementarity in a synergistic relationship. Learning, embeddedness and influence are mechanisms that underpin the boundary spanning required for a synergistic relationship. These boundary-spanning mechanisms assist in achieving a shared language for collaboration and a shared understanding across different people, processes and technologies.

Reinforcement is a complementarity mechanism and occurs when systems consistently work with each other and add crucial contributions to each other’s capabilities (Ferratt et al., 2012; Wade & Hulland, 2004). For example, collaboration between different systems can help to enhance the functionality of one individual system (Someh, Wixom, Davern, & Shanks, 2017). Complementarity relationships have been frequently identified in the IS literature as a source of synergy ( A.Chen & Edgington, 2005; Grover & Kohli, 2012; Melville et al., 2004; Roberts & Grover, 2012; Tanriverdi, 2006; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011).

Embeddedness occurs when human resources from one system create social ties with their counterparts in other systems, based on familiarity, trust and commitment (Granovetter, 1985). These social ties connect resources from different contexts to collaborate, share knowledge and develop social capital (Evans, 1996). Embeddedness facilitates the crossing of system boundaries and interaction with other communities of practice, leading to synergistic outcomes (Brass, Galaskiewicz, Greve, & Tsai, 2004; Evans, 1996; Granovetter, 1985; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012).

Learning is a boundary-spanning mechanism and is based on social information processing theory and organizational learning theory (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012). The social environment provides an immediate source of information for individuals, who can process and act on the information they collect. Learning is the process by which individuals sense the environment and exploit the opportunities offered to them (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Chellappa, Sambamurthy, & Saraf, 2010; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012). It can also help them to better understand each other’s values and norms and thereby provide a means for the boundary spanning required for a synergistic relationship to develop.

Influence is a mechanism concerned with how dominant systems (people, processes and technologies) encourage or enforce compliance with rules, norms and values (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012). By establishing this consistency and facilitating the development of shared mental models that create awareness of interacting parties’ plans and potential reactions, the influence mechanism contributes to the boundary spanning required for a synergistic relationship (Venkatesh & Bala, 2012).

## Synergistic outcomes

Synergy occurs when a whole system with positive emergent properties arises from the interaction among lowerlevel systems (Nevo & Wade, 2010). Positive emergent properties do not exist within a particular component; rather, they emerge from the interaction between components. For example, “the ability to cross-sell based on customer behavior analysis” is a joint capability and emerges from the interaction between BA and CRM systems. Neither BA systems nor CRM systems alone are able to cross-sell using analytics. Emergent properties help the whole system to create value greater than the sum of the value created by individual systems. The emergent properties cannot be reduced to any individual components; they are by nature new and arise from the relationship between components.

We distinguish between emergent properties and enhanced properties. Enhanced properties are the component’s existing properties that change in value (e.g. increased ability to analyze data using statistical tools) when interacting with other components. Conceptually, the use of a statistical interaction to measure synergistic outcomes is, at best, able to capture only the contributions of enhanced properties, since it does not consider and measure the new emergent properties. Nevo and Wade (2011) employ a reflective construct that captures a broader view of synergistic outcomes, based on their definition of synergy as positive emergent outcomes. However, their measurement items do not examine specific emergent properties, and, conceptually, the items are closer to enhanced properties. Clearly, there is need for a conceptualization and operationalization of synergistic outcomes that incorporates both enhanced and emergent properties. We build on Nevo and Wade (2010, 2011) in providing more comprehensive conceptualizations and operationalizations of Synergistic Relationship (including both enablers and mechanisms) and of the resulting Synergistic Outcomes (including both enhancing and emergent properties) and incorporate these into our research model.

BA-enabled CRM System Quality refers to the synergistic outcomes that emerge from the synergistic relationship between BA and CRM systems. It denotes the degree to which BA-enabled CRM systems are effective in automating customer-facing processes, generating insights from customer data and leveraging the analytical insights in customer-facing processes. BA-enabled CRM system quality is a multi-dimensional construct with three dimensions that cover its domain, including BA systems quality, CRM system quality and emergent properties, described below.

BA system quality refers to the degree to which the BA system succeeds in integrating customer data from multiple sources, ensuring data quality, analyzing data and reporting high-quality insights (Davenport & Harris, 2007). BA systems are an example of informational IT systems (Aral & Weill, 2007). BA System Quality focuses only on the quality of BA systems that support decision-making in customerfacing processes.

CRM system quality refers to the degree to which CRM people use standardized and automated customer-facing processes in the marketing, sales and service areas (Buttle, 2004). CRM systems are organizational systems that are augmented by transactional IT systems in customer-facing operations (Aral & Weill, 2007). Their goal is to create efficiency in the operational processes. These systems are also called operational CRM systems (Buttle, 2004)

Emergent properties refer to the degree to which BA-enabled CRM systems leverage analytical insights provided by BA systems in customer-related decisions and processes. The insights from BA systems support BA-enabled CRM systems in two ways: first, by providing advisory services to CRM managers and helping them in their strategic and unstructured decisions; and second, by providing insights for routine use by CRM people in operational processes (Tamm, Seddon, & Shanks, 2013). CRM managers and staff can leverage these insights to take competitive actions and create business value for organizations. The emergent properties are created by the interactions between BA and CRM systems and thus support both efficient and informed processes. BA-enabled CRM systems are also commonly referred to as analytical CRM systems (Buttle, 2004).

Business value includes the transactional, informational and strategic value created by BA-enabled CRM systems. Transactional value captures the efficiency and effectiveness achieved by automating customer-facing processes (Iriana & Buttle, 2006). Informational value captures the value from fact-based decision-making, real-time decisions and a single version of the truth (Wixom et al., 2013). Strategic value is evidenced through increased revenue, reduced cost, increased revenue from new products and customer retention (Coltman, 2007).

## Hypothesis development

We propose two hypotheses that aim to (1) explain the emergence of BA-enabled CRM systems and (2) explain the creation of value from the BA-enabled CRM systems as a synergistic outcome with emergent properties.

Emergence of BA-enabled CRM systems. From a systems perspective, BA-enabled CRM systems emerge from interactions between BA and CRM systems. We argue that BA-enabled CRM systems will possess positive emergent properties only if there is a synergistic relationship between BA and CRM systems. Emergent properties are seen as positive when they align with organizational goals and help the whole system to achieve more than that of its components. The two systems inherently belong to different communities of practice, and the emergence of a cohesive, analytics-based system of people, processes and technologies depends on the level of Synergistic Relationship between the component systems. The enablers and mechanisms of a synergistic relationship between BA and CRM systems bring isolated systems together and help to realize the potential synergistic outcomes. A synergistic relationship facilitates the creation of emergent properties, by ensuring that BA and CRM systems are able to work together, span their boundaries and complement each other (for details of the relationship, refer to Table 4). The emergent system not only uses CRM to automate customer-facing processes, and BA to generate insights, it also ensures that the insights are leveraged to inform action at the right time. This ability to utilize BA in CRM processes is by nature an emergent property and results from the synergistic relationship between BA and CRM systems. We hypothesize that the Synergistic Relationship between BA and CRM systems positively influences the BA-enabled CRM System Quality.

Hypothesis 1. The greater the synergistic relationship between BA and CRM systems, the greater the BA-enabled CRM system quality.

Creation of business value. Business value will be created from three dimensions of BA-enabled CRM system quality: namely, BA system quality, CRM system quality and emergent properties. CRM and BA systems will help BAenabled CRM systems to achieve transactional (e.g. process efficiencies) and informational (e.g. a single version of the truth) value, respectively. This is consistent with the argument that IT systems will affect business value consistent with their organizational goals (Aral & Weill, 2007). Emergent properties are enabled by the interaction of BA and CRM systems, and they will contribute to business value beyond transactional and informational values. From a systems perspective, BA-enabled CRM systems include emergent properties that BA and CRM systems do not possess individually. These emergent properties will positively influence strategic value in addition to the transactional and informational values enabled by CRM and BA systems in isolation.

Hypothesis 2. BA-enabled CRM system quality has a positive effect on business value.

## Research method

We tested our research model using a cross-sectional survey, targeting BA and CRM managers in large Australian and American organizations. This choice was motivated by two conclusions from our literature review: first, there is a need for empirical studies on the business value of BA systems, and second, managers’ perceptions provide a valid source for collecting data about the value of IT systems (Davern & Wilkin, 2010; Tallon & Kraemer, 2007), thus making these respondents a suitable target population for our research model.

We targeted BA and CRM managers from Australia and the United States who had used BA extensively in customer-facing processes. For the Australian sample, we used a purposive sampling technique to reach the most informed participants from large Australian organizations (Neuman, 2006). Overall, 78% of the people who were contacted eventually participated in the survey. For the US sample, Qualtrics administered the survey using their panel partners to obtain a purposive sample using the same targeting criteria.

## Measures

We adapted scales from the literature where valid instruments existed. Otherwise, we developed new measures in several stages to ensure content validity, construct validity and reliability (Gefen, Straub, & Boudreau, 2000). The instrument development process was based on guidelines recommended in the literature (e.g. Churchill, 1979; MacKenzie, Podsakoff, & Podsakoff, 2011; Moore & Benbasat, 1991). This process (Table 4) involved a qualitative component including conceptualization, item generation, academic interviews, expert interviews, Q-sorting, model specification, pre-testing and a pilot test. Table 9 in Appendix presents our items and the modeling of the various constructs.

Synergistic relationship was operationalized as a second-order formative construct with the various enablers and mechanisms as its first-order indicators, which, in turn, were operationalized as reflective constructs. The initial measures for Compatibility and Integration effort were adapted from Nevo and Wade (2011) to fit the context of our study. Initial items for Reinforcement, Learning, Embeddedness and Influence were generated from the underlying theories (Ferratt et al., 2012; Venkatesh & Bala, 2012) and interview data. Guided by MacKenzie et al. (2011), we conducted 10 interviews with analytics managers and academics to create an initial item pool.

BA-enabled CRM system quality is a formative, secondorder construct that includes three dimensions: CRM system quality, BA system quality and emergent properties.

We developed new items for measuring CRM system quality based on Aral and Weill (2007), Buttle (2004) and Iriana and Buttle (2006). The new items captured the level of standardization and automation in customer-facing processes.

For measuring BA system quality, we developed items based on Davenport and Harris (2007). The new items captured the four dimensions of this construct, including ensuring data quality, performing extract, transform and load (ETL) processes, performing advanced data analysis and reporting and visualizing data.

We adapted measurement items for Emergent properties from the analytical CRM construct of Iriana and Buttle (2006) to fit the context of our study. The items focused on measuring the use of BA to perform customer-facing processes—use that emerges from the interaction between CRM and BA.

We developed new measures for Transactional value based on Iriana and Buttle (2006). A new scale was developed to measure Informational value based on Wixom et al. (2013). For Strategic value, initial items were borrowed from Coltman, Devinney, and Midgley (2010) and were adapted to fit our study’s context.

## Results

We tested the hypotheses using partial least squares (PLS). PLS is a structural equation modeling (SEM) technique, which is recommended for use in studies where the theory is less developed (Gefen, Straub, & Rigdon, 2011). The research model includes formative constructs in cases where new measures are being tested (Gefen et al., 2011; Petter, Straub, & Rai, 2007). We selected PLS-SEM because very few studies have theorized and empirically assessed the impact of synergy in generating business value, and this research model includes first-order and second-order formative constructs. The software package SmartPLS, version 3.2.6, was used for analysis.

## Data screening

We received 236 survey responses. Of these we rejected 35, either because they were incomplete or because the respondent’s answers showed negligible variation: that is, the standard deviation of the responses was less than 0.3, as suggested by Gaskin (2012). During this stage, we also examined missing values, unengaged responses, outliers, skewness and kurtosis (Gaskin, 2012). This resulted in a final sample of 201 complete survey responses. Table 5 summarizes our sample characteristics.

We followed Podsakoff et al.’s (2003) suggestions for reducing common method bias when developing a survey instrument. We developed our instrument in several stages, including expert interviews, Q-sorting, pre-testing and pilot testing to ensure that all of the items were clear, simple, specific and did not confound other items or constructs. We identified and eliminated all of the common words and phrases between the predictor and criterion variables and provided clear definitions of CRM and BA systems in the beginning of the survey. We grouped all of the items relating to one specific construct and separated the predictor and criterion variables. We also included in the survey instrument seven attention filters, the questions for which were general and not related to the constructs. Incorrect answers to these questions were used to screen the survey respondents.

To assess non-response bias, we compared the first and last quartile of participants using a t-test. The t-test results did not find significant differences between the two respondent groups, suggesting non-response bias was not a major issue (Armstrong & Overton, 1977).

## Supplementary analysis

We performed three additional analyses to compare our proposed systems approach to the complementarity approach from the business value of IT literature (Aral & Weill, 2007; Pang, Lee, & DeLone, 2014). These analyses focused on comparing the value explained by BA-enabled CRM Systems Quality (synergistic outcomes from a systems perspective) with the value explained by the statistical interaction between BA System Quality and CRM System Quality (synergistic outcomes from a complementarity perspective). From a systems theory perspective, BA-enabled CRM systems capture the emergent properties that are created from the synergistic interaction between BA and CRM systems. However, the RBV and complementarity literature has used statistical interaction to bring isolated systems together to capture the additional value created from their interaction (Aral & Weill, 2007; Titah & Barki, 2009; Zhu, 2004). In this approach, statistical interaction between variables is captured by their product term (BA

To specify the interaction term between BA System Quality and CRM System Quality, we used a two-staged approach (Hair et al., 2013). In stage one, we estimated the path and measurement model without the interaction term to obtain the latent variable scores. In stage two, a single-item interaction term was created by multiplying the latent variable score of an exogenous variable (BA Systems Quality) and moderator (CRM System Quality).

The assessment of the three models shows that model A, which utilizes Emergent Properties, explains more of the variance in Strategic Value compared to the variance explained in Models B and C. Based on the results for the three models, emergent properties create Strategic Value in addition to the value created by statistical interaction effect.<sup>4</sup> The statistical interaction captures additional value created from the product term of BA System Quality and CRM System Quality. The product term does not capture the emergent properties, and, at best, can only capture enhancements to the value of existing properties when systems interact.

## Discussion

The goal of this study was to understand how synergy between BA and CRM systems leads to business value. We used the concepts of synergistic relationship and synergistic outcomes to explain and empirically assess how business value is created from BA systems.

## Summary of findings

The research model offers three key insights into the creation and measurement of business value from BA-enabled CRM systems. First, the Synergistic Relationship between BA and CRM systems gives rise to Emergent Properties that cannot be reduced to individual BA or CRM systems. Second, with their emergent properties, the BA-enabled CRM systems create Strategic Value, as well as Transactional and Information Value, that is greater than what could be created by the CRM and BA systems individually. Third, Emergent Properties explain the variations in Strategic Value beyond what can be explained by the statistical interaction between the quality of BA and CRM systems. At best, the statistical interaction can only capture the value enhancement of properties that already exist in the underlying individual systems.

## Theoretical contributions

Our study has two main theoretical contributions: (1) developing and operationalizing a conceptual definition for Synergistic Relationship and (2) providing a systems view to conceptualize and test business value of BA systems.

We developed a new conceptual definition for Synergistic Relationship. We defined synergistic relationship as the ability of systems to work together, span their boundaries and complement each other. This definition is based on the enablers and mechanisms that synergistically connect two systems together and lead to the emergence of higher-order systems. Formalizing a relationship using the enabling properties and mechanisms is not only new but also critical to understanding the dynamics and socially complex structures and interdependence among components. The systems approach emphasizes the interactions among components that lead to a whole system (El Sawy, Malhotra, Park, & Pavlou, 2010); however, it does not describe the enablers of the components and the social mechanisms that both underlie the interaction between components and lead to a whole that is greater than the sum of its parts. Our combination of the enablers and mechanisms in the Synergistic Relationship construct extends and integrates (1) the enablers of synergy in Nevo and Wade (2010, 2011); and (2) the mechanisms of synergy discussed by Venkatesh and Bala (2012) and Ferratt et al. (2012). The concept of a synergistic relationship informs the literature on complex adaptive systems (CAS): particularly, the bottom-up IT processes from which new properties and patterns emerge (Nan, 2011). More broadly, it contributes to multi-level approaches to theory and research in organizations (Kozlowski & Klein, 2000) that seek causal explanations for the emergence of higher-order structures and systems in organizational contexts.

We argue that synergistic relationship is an underlying phenomenon that explains the emergence of higher-order IT-enabled organizational systems. Our conceptualization of synergy is developed independent of any particular IT systems context. However, our research model and empirical study are developed within the context of BA systems and CRM systems to enable detailed construct definition and measurement, and explain how BA systems lead to business value. Our choice of BA and CRM systems is deliberate. BA systems and CRM systems are widely used and can readily be applied together for a common organizational purpose: improving the customer experience.

We contribute to the business value of IT literature, by providing a systems view on the value of IT systems. We theorize and empirically assess the business value of an important subset of IT: BA systems. To our knowledge, there is no other assessment of a BA system’s value that is similarly thorough. We conceptualize and measure three dimensions of value that are generated from BA systems,

CRM systems and their emergent properties. In particular, we conceptualize and measure the value of emergent properties that are created in relation to CRM systems. Although RBV theory has been successful in explaining the value of individual IT resources (Aral & Weill, 2007; Mithas, Tafti, Bardhan, & Goh, 2012; Wade & Hulland, 2004), it fails to adequately conceptualize and measure the value that emerges from the interaction between resources. Using the systems approach, an organizational system is more than a bundle of resources; it is what emerges from the interactions among resources as well as the resources themselves. As such, our theory considers not just what a resource contributes to business value in isolation, but what value a resource can generate in light of the other resources in the system as a whole. RBV theory does not formally and adequately capture these interactions and the value related to emergent properties. Hence, RBV theory is unable to fully reflect any value that derives from the synergy between interacting resources (Nevo & Wade, 2010; Schryen, 2013). We demonstrate that the systems approach can augment RBV theory to offer a more comprehensive approach for explaining the business value of BA systems.

We also highlight the power of a latent construct that reflects the emergent properties of the higher-order BA-enabled CRM system and which is created from the interaction between lower-level systems (e.g. BA and CRM systems). While a statistical interaction effect may capture some of the value created by the BA and CRM systems interaction, it is not able to directly conceptualize the emer gent properties and their value. From a systems perspective, a statistical interaction fails to capture the value of emergent properties of a whole system. Emergent properties are, by nature, new (i.e. they do not exist at the component level) and they cannot be captured by lower-level variables and their statistical interaction. Conceptually, a statistical interaction is, at best, only able to capture the value enhancement of properties that exist at the component level. Traditional IS research perspectives, which emphasize analytical traceability, have conceptualized IT systems in isolation and the examination of their joint effects via statistical interaction. Following Nan (2011), we argue the need to go beyond these traditional IS research perspectives, to model the interconnections between systems and understand the emergence of IT-enabled organizational systems. While our study is contextualized for synergy between BA and CRM systems, our findings could be theoretically generalized (Seddon & Scheepers, 2012) to the relationship between BA and other organizational systems such as supply chain, human resources (HR) and finance.

## Implications for practice

Becoming data-driven has become a priority for many organizations, particularly traditional companies established before the digital era. Data-driven transformation is not only about investing in analytical tools or hiring data scientists; rather, it requires businesses to re-engineer their business processes to include analytics, and to educate their employees to think and act based on data. To achieve this, some organizations build Centers of Excellence to provide organization-wide BA services, and others embed their BA groups in various business functions (Davenport et al., 2010). Still, without sufficient guidelines for change, many organizations continue to struggle during their data transformation journeys. We argue that to achieve value from BA, managers need to focus on building synergistic relationships between their isolated systems, rather than just investing in tools and skills. In this regard, the concept of a synergistic relationship provides a managerial guide for the combination of organizational systems (people, process and technology). The specific enablers and mechanisms provide a systematic means through which managers can combine BA systems with CRM systems, create synergies and achieve business value.

We define synergistic relationship as a relationship in which systems work together, span their boundaries and complement each other. Here, we exemplify how the concept of synergistic relationship can help organizations achieve value from BA systems. The specific enablers and mechanisms of synergy are associated with practices that organizations could deploy to achieve desired outcomes (Someh et al., 2017). Systems work together when they are compatible and integrated. Compatibility translates to developing a common language, common goals and a shared understanding between BA and business systems so that they can work together. Compatibility does not occur naturally in organizations. Rather it requires integration effort in which it is the responsibility of management to bring BA and business systems together and guide them in line with organizational goals. This translates to sponsorship and support of BA initiatives as well as communicating BA value by the leadership team. In terms of spanning boundaries, embeddedness focuses on how individuals from BA and CRM systems could connect. Through the concept of embeddedness, organizations can define new organizational structures and relationships to connect BA and business people. These connections can be based on formal reporting structures or processes (e.g. co-location of BA and business teams, establishing shared services for BA or assigning a role to span boundaries), or informal (such as connecting BA and business communities through physical (e.g. conferences) and virtual communities (e.g. Yammer)). The connections between BA and business systems should be leveraged to transfer BA and analytics knowledge or moti vate change. The learning mechanism ensures that knowl edge is transferred by BA people and acquired by CRM people. This could be done by providing educational programs and training courses or establishing physical or virtual knowledge-sharing communities for peer learning. In terms of influence, organizations can motivate or mandate use of BA for decision-making. Influence can help with the adoption and pervasive use of BA tools. Organizations can utilize marketing techniques to market and sell analytics to internal employees. Otherwise, they can use power to mandate the use of BA. Finally, in a synergistic relationship, BA and business systems should complement each other. This means that BA and business systems together can build cross-functional teams, in which they can solve a business task together by adding to each other’s capabilities and complementing each other. Organizations should consider that realizing synergies between BA and business systems will hinge on the implementation of practices related to all enablers and mechanisms. For example, if one organization decides to invest in informal networks to connect BA and business systems, they need to make sure that they incentivize social learning and peer influence.

This study informs managerial decision-making about IT investment and the implementation of BA projects. Practitioners struggle to justify the considerable investments in BA systems (Mithas et al., 2012; Wixom et al., 2013). This study provides a basis for a more comprehensive justification of such investments. BA systems create business value by (1) directly creating informational benefits; (2) indirectly creating transactional value from automated analytical insights; and (3) transforming other systems into BA-enabled systems, which generate business value. These pathways can help managers link their investment in BA systems to business value. Furthermore, we provide evidence that two classes of IT systems (informational and transactional) complement and reinforce each other. Organizations may exhaust gains in efficiency from transactional systems (Mithas et al., 2012). However, combining transactional with other advanced IT systems can generate a new source of business value for organizations. Managers should consider the potential synergy between their new investments and the existing systems in the organization.

## Limitations and future research

Although the systems approach provides a useful means for studying interacting systems (Burton-Jones et al., 2015), concepts that study the link between interacting parts and whole systems are limited. Further exploration of the synergistic relationship concept, including elaborating on its enablers and mechanisms, is an important direction for future research. In particular, mapping these enablers and mechanisms to concrete organizational practices, structures and processes would have significant practical value. This can be followed up in subsequent qualitative research in future research attempts. In terms of synergistic outcomes, different types of emergent properties can be studied. For example, studying the unintended properties (Kozlowski & Klein, 2000) that emerge from the interaction between components can be further explored. CAS (Nan, 2011) provides a useful lens through which to study the coevolution of systems and can enrich the concepts of synergistic relationship and emergent properties. The use of CAS can also help to model the temporal dimension of emergence.

Our notion of emergent properties—that is, the actions or abilities that are enabled by two lower-level systems— provides a new type of fit, in addition to the types of fit discussed in Venkatraman (1989): particularly, fit as moderation. Fit as moderation uses the product term of two variables to measure the level of fit between two underlying concepts. We believe that future research can utilize the emergent construct to represent a systems conceptualization of fit as moderation, emerging from a relationship.

By highlighting the importance of the synergistic relationship in developing higher-order BA-enabled CRM systems and contributing to business value, our study informs future research on the business value of IT. Future research could contextualize the synergistic relationship and its emergent properties to the relationship between BA systems and organizational systems other than CRM systems. Such research would advance our knowledge of how BA-enabled organizational systems in general emerge and help to generalize the synergistic relationship and synergistic outcome constructs.

1. Three main approaches have been used to examine IT Value: a direct link, a process-oriented perspective and a complementary approach. The first approach establishes a direct link between IT resources and value, but frequently reports mixed results for the value of IT investments (Chae et al., 2014; Sabherwal & Jeyaraj, 2015). The second approach adopts the process-oriented perspective (Melville et al., 2004; Rai et al., 2006; Tallon et al., 2000) and establishes an indirect relationship between IT resources and value. Their findings show that IT augments other organizational resources to develop IT-enabled organizational resources that indirectly create value (Davern & Wilkin, 2010; Wade & Hulland, 2004). In the complementary approach, IT resources create business value in conjunction with other organizational resources (Pang et al., 2014). This approach has found that IT resources moderate the impact of organizational resources on performance (Bharadwaj et al., 2007).

2. To understand what synergy is and how it can be conceptualized, we performed a systemic literature search on the concept. The literature search covered Information Systems Basket of Eight Journals. We also conducted an exploratory search on Google scholar to find highly cited papers in the broader management literature.

3. In our analysis, we controlled for four other variables that may influence the value creation from business analytics (BA) systems. Since the sample included participants from Australia and the United States, we controlled for the difference between two countries. Second, we controlled for the differences in responses from BA and customer relationship management (CRM) managers that participated in the survey. Third, participants were asked whether their organizations had a BA unit (e.g. competency center or center of excellence) or not. Responses to this question enabled a comparison of the results between organizations with and without BA units. The fourth control variable was the level of BA use in the organizations, so that organizations with extensive use of BA could be compared with organizations with low/medium use of BA. None of the control variables were significant.
