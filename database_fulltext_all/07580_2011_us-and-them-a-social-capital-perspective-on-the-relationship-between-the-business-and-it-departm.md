# Us and them: a social capital perspective on the relationship between the business and IT departments

Bart van den Hooff<sup>1</sup> and Maarten de Winter <sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>VU University Amsterdam, Amsterdam, the Netherlands; <sup>2</sup>Accenture Netherlands, Amsterdam, the Netherlands

Correspondence: Bart van den Hooff, Faculty of Economics and Business Administration, Knowledge, Information and Networks Research Group, VU University Amsterdam, De Boelelaan 1105, Amsterdam 1081 HV, the Netherlands. Tel: þ 31 020 598 6062

## Abstract

In this paper, a social capital perspective is applied to the relationship between the IT department and the Business organization. IT and Business are conceptualized as different occupational communities, with different understandings of their work. Our focus is on the level of social capital and the process of knowledge sharing between these occupational communities. We analyze the role that these factors play in reaching a mutual understanding within the process of IS development, and the influence this has on the perceived performance of the IT organization. Our study, combining qualitative and quantitative methods, points out that a lack of social capital (structural, relational as well as cognitive) can serve as an explanation for the often problematic relationship between these communities. Our analyses also show that social capital is especially relevant for the Business organization’s perception of IT performance, whereas the IT department’s perception is that performance is primarily dependent on the exchange of information. European Journal of Information Systems (2011) 20, 255–266.

Keywords: IT–business relationship; social capital; knowledge sharing; occupational communitie

## Introduction

The relationship of Information Technology (or IT) professionals with other employees (the ‘Business’) is often described in problematic terms. Day (2007), for instance, sees IT professionals and the Business as ‘strangers on a train’, who may travel together but never get to know one another, whereas Ward & Peppard (1996) see the relationship between IT and Business professionals as ‘a troubled marriage in need of counseling’. Where the IT department is often seen by the Business as having a focus on technology rather than the interests of the organization as a whole, IT professionals often regard Business employees as technologically inept and insufficiently aware of the importance and complexity of IT. The troubled relationship between IT and Business is an important issue, since it provides an explanation for the fact that organizations often fail to derive business value from IT (Poulymenakou & Holmes, 1996; Peppard & Ward, 1999; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007).

In this paper, we analyze the relationship between the IT department and the Business from a social capital perspective, with a focus on the social dynamics and processes of knowledge sharing between the two groups. We investigate how social capital and knowledge sharing influence the perceived performance of the IT department – as perceived by both the IT department itself and their Business counterparts. The two groups are defined as follows: the IT department consists of employees that make IT work, and the Business consists of any function that is not IT (Coughlan et al., 2005; Day, 2007; Nord et al., 2007). Social capital between these groups consists of the value of the relationships between them, in terms of information, influence and resources derived from these relationships (Cameron et al., 2004).

By focusing on the social capital between IT and Business professionals, we highlight the importance of the social dynamics between the people developing and managing IT (in the IT department) or working with IT (in the Business), rather than the strategic alignment of IT and Business as perceived by their managers (which is the focus of much literature addressing the relationship between Business and IT). These social dynamics have been explored before, but to a very limited degree. Reich & Benbasat (2000), for instance, point out that positive communication and connections between the Business and IT departments tend to contribute positively to IT–Business alignment. Hatzakis et al. (2005) propose that positive social dynamics between Business managers and IT professionals play a role in improved collaboration between both parts of the organization. However, previous research lacks a systematic empirical analysis of the role of social capital in the relationship between Business and IT. Our study does provide such an analysis.

Furthermore, we focus on the process of knowledge sharing between IT and Business: to what extent do both groups actually share their insights, requirements, experiences? The role of shared domain knowledge in the Business–IT relationship has been researched before (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Basselier et al., 2003; Kearns & Lederer, 2003), but mainly in terms of an object or an outcome: the knowledge both parties have (often at a management level) of what the other does, which is often found to be positively related to Business–IT alignment. Previous studies, however, have not addressed the process of sharing knowledge between IT and Business professionals. This process is the explicit focus of our study. In conclusion, we contribute to the literature on the relationship between Business and IT by applying a social capital, process-oriented perspective instead of a strategically oriented, objectbased perspective.

In the next section of this paper, we present our theoretical model. This model was tested in a multimethod study within an insurance firm. A combination of qualitative and quantitative data yielded results that lead to a number of conclusions regarding the relationship between Business and IT departments. Based on these conclusions, implications for theory, practice and future research are discussed in the closing section of this paper.

## Social capital between business and IT departments

As noted above, the troubled relationship between Business and IT departments can explain the fact that organizations often fail to realize the full business value of IT (Byrd et al., 2005; Ashurst et al., 2008; Kasi et al., 2008; Chen, 2010). An important reason for the troubled character of the relationship between both groups is that they are different occupational communities (Dougherty, 1992; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995), with different work domains and knowledge regimes (Howard-Grenville & Carlile, 2006). This means that they are likely to have different understandings of a similar situation (Bechky, 2003) – for instance, of the nature of a problem, its importance, possible solutions, etc. These differences in understanding are associated with lower levels of social capital between these communities (Wenger, 2000). In other words, differences between people (their expertise, their preferences, their attitudes, their interests, etc.) who work in Business or IT are likely to negatively influence the relationship between these people, and thus the level of social capital between them. Lower levels of social capital can negatively affect perceived performance, both directly and via knowledge sharing, as will be further discussed below.

## Social capital

Nahapiet & Ghoshal (1998) make a useful distinction between three different dimensions of social capital. We will use these three dimensions to further explore the relationship between Business and IT:

\- The structural dimension refers to the overall pattern of connections between actors – the people you know and how you reach them – as well as to knowing what others know. Relevant aspects of this dimension are the presence or the absence of network ties between actors, and an identifiable pattern of linkages.

\- The relational dimension refers to the quality of personal relationships people have, such as levels of respect and friendship, that influence their behavior. Key aspects are trust and trustworthiness, norms and sanctions, obligations and expectations, and identity and identification.

\- The cognitive dimension of social capital refers to shared representation, interpretation, and systems of meaning among parties, such as shared language and codes and shared narratives.

## Social capital and perceived performance

Research relating social capital to performance finds that higher levels of social capital (on all three dimensions distinguished above) tend to reduce transaction costs, facilitate collaboration, enhance mutual commitment, strengthen relationships and involvement, and ultimately enhance performance (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Adler & Kwon, 2002; Cameron et al., 2004). More specifically, social capital is found to be related to the perceived performance of groups since high levels of social capital will motivate group members to prioritize group interests over individual interests, and adopt high performance work practices (Aquino & Serva, 2005). In other words, a higher level of social capital within a group will cause that group to focus on collective interests, and thus to perform better as a group. Consequently, the higher the level of social capital shared between the Business and IT departments, the better they will perform and the more positively they will judge each other’s performance. Focusing on the performance of the IT department, this means that higher levels of social capital between Business and IT will lead to more positive perceptions of the IT department’s performance by both groups:

Hypothesis 1: The perceived performance of the IT department is positively influenced by the level of (a) structural, (b) relational and (c) cognitive social capital between the IT department and the Business organization.

## Social capital and knowledge sharing

Higher levels of social capital are found to coincide with higher levels of knowledge sharing (Hansen et al., 2005; Smith et al., 2005). Social capital can positively affect the sharing of knowledge through each of the dimensions distinguished above (Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998; Van den Hooff & Huysman, 2009). Through the structural dimension, social capital provides access to people with relevant knowledge, needs and questions. The relational dimension provides a common interest, and an atmosphere of mutual trust and appreciation of the value of the knowledge of others. Finally, the cognitive dimension creates a common ability that helps in understanding, interpreting and valuing other people’s knowledge. Consequently, all three dimensions of social capital between IT and Business are likely to increase knowledge sharing between these occupational communities:

Hypothesis 2: Knowledge sharing between the IT department and the Business organization is positively influenced by the level of (a) structural, (b) relational and (c) cognitive social capital between these groups.

## Knowledge sharing and perceived performance

Knowledge sharing between the IT and Business communities within an organization positively influences the perceived performance of the IT department (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Kearns & Sabherwal, 2007). As members of different occupational communities, IT and Business professionals possess different frames of reference, leading to diverging understandings of their work (Bechky, 2003). For Business and IT to be aligned, however, they must have a common frame of reference, a shared understanding of the requirements and possibilities regarding IT. There is evidence that knowledge sharing has a positive influence on the establishment of such a shared understanding. which in turn is found to influence perceptions of performance (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996). This leads to our third hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: The perceived performance of the IT department is positively influenced by the level of knowledge sharing between the Business organization and the IT department.

## Interrelatedness of social capital dimensions

Tsai & Ghoshal (1998) find that the three dimensions of social capital influence each other. The presence of ties (structural dimension) positively influences mutual trust and obligations (relational dimension), and shapes a common set of goals and values (cognitive dimension). Furthermore, shared meanings, goals and values (cognitive) are a basis for mutual trust and obligations (relational), as network members can expect that ‘they all work for collective goals and will not be hurt by any other member’s pursuit of self-interest’ (Tsai & Ghoshal, 1998, p. 466). This leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 4: The level of relational social capital between the IT department and the Business organization is positively influenced by the level of (a) structural and (b) cognitive social capital between these groups.

Hypothesis 5: The level of cognitive social capital between the IT department and the Business organization is positively influenced by the level of structural social capital between these groups.

## Business and IT at DIC – interviews

Our study took place at a large insurance company in the Netherlands, for which we will use the pseudonym Dutch Insurance Company (or DIC) in this study. DIC offers life insurances, loss insurances, pension insurances, labor insurances, mortgages and banking services. Over the last few years, DIC has grown tremendously as it aggressively made some large acquisitions. One activity that had suffered because of this high growth rate was the IS development process, also known as ISD. The organization was interested in finding out how this process could be improved, which was the point of departure of our study. The first step in this study was getting a detailed insight into how people in different positions in the organization regarded the ISD process, and what their explanations were for any problems regarding this process. For this purpose, 28 interviews were conducted.

## Methodology

The interviews were semi-structured, with the variables from the theoretical model determining the topic list. Each respondent came from one of these three categories:

\- The Business organization: managers and lower employees involved in the primary processes of the organization, such as product development, policy administration, claims management, investment management, reinsurance, actuarial valuation, marketing and sales, etc.

\- The IT department: managers and lower employees involved in IT processes, such as IT strategy, development, implementation and maintenance.

\- Application specialists: employees from the Business organization who were appointed as representatives of the Business organization to the IT department. They acted as intermediaries, translating user needs to the IT department, and IT department suggestions to the users. Although Application specialists were formally part of the Business organization, they were given this specific function, and thus constituted a separate category of respondents.

Of the 28 interviews, seven were conducted with members of the IT department, 18 with members of the Business organization and three with Application specialists. The numbers of interviewees from the Business organization and the IT department roughly reflect the representation of both parts of the organization in the population, while Application specialists are overrepresented. This is due to the fact that there was only a relatively low number of Application Specialists in the organization, and their intermediary role makes them important actors in the relationship between Business and IT. Each interview lasted about an hour.

The interviews were transcribed and then analyzed. We sent the transcripts to the interviewees, so they could confirm the story as reported. Through thematic analysis of the interview transcripts (Miles & Huberman, 1994), we codified the transcripts and organized the results into general conceptual themes. The primary labels for these themes were the variables in our theoretical model: the three dimensions of social capital (structural, cognitive and relational), knowledge sharing between IT and Business, and perceived IT performance. No additional, relevant issues emerged from the data that did not fit into these predefined themes, so these labels could be used to cover all the relevant statements in the interviews. Table 1 provides an overview of these themes: how they are defined, exemplary quotes (both positive and negative ones), and how often the theme was mentioned by each of the three categories of respondents. The final columns of Table 1 show how often a theme was mentioned (followed by the number of respondents mentioning it), which is then split up into the number of quotes containing a positive judgment (þ) or a negative judgment (). Some quotes contain no such judgment, for instance when a respondent describes how they share knowledge. Based on this overview, we will discuss the findings of the interviews in more detail.

Several interviewees made it clear that information technology is crucial for supporting DIC’s value activities. IS development (or ISD) was seen as a crucial process, since the majority of information systems in this organization were developed in house. All interviewees had in some way been involved in ISD projects. Most of these projects concerned the development of information systems that were designed to support a specific set of business processes in the organization. In the interviews, we did not focus on specific projects, but more generally on the collaboration between IT and Business across different projects.

## Interview findings

First, structural social capital is not mentioned very often in the interviews. When it is mentioned, this is primarily in terms of getting the right people into project teams, which requires knowing where the relevant expertise is located in the organization. Most respondents indicate that there is a sufficient level of connectedness in the organization, and that at least they themselves know where to find relevant knowledge. As Table 1 shows, respondents from the IT department tend to be a little more positive about the level of structural social capital than those from the Business. One respondent from the Business organization does mention a relevant risk in terms of structural social capital for the IT department:

At IT, knowledge is insufficiently diffused. If one of them is out of the running, you immediately have a problem.

On the whole, however, structural social capital is not much of an issue in this organization – some problems are mentioned, but it does not emerge as a very significant theme from the data.

Although cognitive social capital is not mentioned very frequently either (at least, compared to the other themes), this appears to be a more important issue than structural social capital. Cognitive social capital was especially mentioned frequently (nine times across all three respondents) by Application specialists, which is in line with their primary role: ‘translating’ between the worlds of IT and Business. Application specialists’ judgments on this theme are quite evenly divided in terms of being positive, negative or neutral. As one Application specialist notes:

As an Application specialist, you translate the business’ needs to IT. We don’t always give the business what they ask, but we do give them what they need. We help the business in specifying its needs and the required solution.

In the other two categories (Business and IT), cognitive social capital is primarily referred to in terms of problems, especially by respondents from the Business organization, who never mention this in positive terms. Business feels that IT insufficiently understands their (the Business’) practices and interests. IT generally agrees that they should be more aware of the interests of the Business, although some IT respondents report significant progress in this area and thus are more positive about the current level of cognitive social capital between Business and IT.

The third dimension of social capital, relational social capital, clearly is an issue in the organization. Both Business (38 times across 13 respondents) and IT (13 times across six respondents) mention this concept quite often. both in terms of problems (lack of trust. the fact that they are two different ‘worlds’) and in terms of solutions (bringing people together, more interactive ways of developing IS). The Business organization mainly sees problems in this relationship, with 24 quotes of the total of 38 being explicitly negative. For the IT department the findings are mixed: some interviewees are quite satisfied with the relationship between IT and Business (saying the two groups are ‘partners’ and relate quite well in concrete projects), whereas others speak in terms of a ‘client-supplier’ relationship and emphasize that both groups focus on their own responsibilities instead of a shared responsibility. Relational social capital also seemed to be quite closely related to cognitive social capital, as many statements addressed both dimensions of social capital together.

The theme of knowledge sharing is also very prominent. This is mentioned 35 times in 14 interviews with the Business, 18 times in seven interviews with IT and 10 times in three interviews with Application specialists. A clear gap between the Business organization and the IT department emerges in terms of how they define knowledge sharing. The IT department considers knowledge sharing to be limited to exchanging documents and experiences regarding the functional design. This is also reflected in the fact that knowledge sharing between Business and IT is dominated by the use of documents and IT applications, with only little face-to-face interaction. The Business organization, on the other hand, emphasizes the importance of face-to-face interaction and actual collaboration. Respondents from the Business organization frequently emphasized the importance of working together on ISD in establishing knowledge sharing with the IT department. This difference is reflected by the different judgments from Business (19 quotes being explicitly negative) and IT (with a majority of six quotes being positive): in the view of the IT department, knowledge is being exchanged (i.e., information is transferred between Business and IT), whereas in the view of the Business organization, it hardly is (i.e., there is a lack of face-to-face interaction and collaboration). Knowledge sharing is also frequently explicitly related to relational social capital by respondents from the Business, emphasizing the interrelatedness of these concepts in their perception. This is illustrated by the following quote from a Business employee:

ISD is more like a journey, one which is full of uncertainties. As a manager, user or developer, you do not exactly know what the system should look like and how it must work in the end. This knowledge is created during ISD when interacting with one another.

This feeling is mentioned by a number of other respondents from the Business as well: rather than IT just explaining what they are doing, the Business would like to be more involved in the actual process of IS development, building up a relationship characterized by relational and cognitive social capital. Application specialists seem to side with the IT department on this issue: like IT, they are generally positive about knowledge sharing between Business and IT.

Finally, the theme of perceived IT performance is an important one as well, but the numbers indicate that this is primarily an issue for the Business organization (with 23 quotes in 16 interviews). It is less of an issue for IT (seven quotes in seven interviews) and Application specialists (two quotes in three interviews). Statements from Business respondents primarily contain negative judgements (11 quotes), although there are some positive remarks as well (8). The quotes from IT people are either positive (2), negative (2) or neutral, and both Application specialists mentioning this issue have very neutral judgements – which may be due to their intermediary role between Business and IT. A further analysis of these quotes shows that the importance of both knowledge sharing and relational social capital for the IT department’s perceived performance is stressed by interviewees from both the Business and the IT side. Remarks made about IT performance are most frequently accompanied by remarks about (lack of) knowledge sharing and (problematic) relationships.

In conclusion, the interviews point towards a lack of cognitive and especially relational social capital, coupled with a lack of knowledge sharing between Business and IT, as likely causes of problems in the relationship between Business and IT. Respondents (from all categories) with a more positive perception of the relationship between both parts of the organization are also more positive about the IT department’s performance. As for knowledge sharing, this is often mentioned as crucial for ISD success – primarily in terms of the exchange of necessary facts by IT respondents, but also in terms of actively collaborating in ISD by Business respondents. We also see some interesting differences between the three categories of respondents here: cognitive social capital is primarily an issue for Application specialists, whereas respondents from the Business organization and the IT department focus more strongly on relational social capital. Also, Business respondents tend to have a more negative view of the level of social capital and knowledge sharing between Business and IT, as well as of the performance of the IT department, than IT respondents and Application specialists. Knowledge sharing is a central issue for all three categories.

For a more detailed analysis of the relationships between social capital, knowledge sharing and perceived IT performance, and statistical tests of the hypotheses concerning these relationships, we will now turn to the survey data.

## Social capital, knowledge sharing and perceived IT performance – survey results

An online survey was conducted among employees of the Business organization who were involved in IT projects (including Application specialists), and employees of the IT department. The purpose of this survey was to enable the quantitative testing of the theoretical model presented before. The total sample of the survey consisted of 236 participants, which included 93 members from the IT department (a 32% response rate) and 143 members from the Business organization (38% response). Participants were managers as well as lower-level employees, who were contacted via the divisional or departmental managers. All participants were sent an email which included a link to the online survey. Respondents were promised anonymity, and participation in the study was voluntary. In general, the items that were used to measure the relevant variables were the same for both groups, but the IT people were asked about their relationships and knowledge sharing with the Business organization, whereas respondents from the Business organization answered these questions with a focus on the IT department.

## Operationalization of the measurement scales

Perceived IT performance was based on Subramani et al.’s (1999) 7-point Likert scale, consisting of statements judging the IT department’s contribution on a number of criteria, judging from ‘non-existent’ to ‘extremely strong’. The remaining variables (the three dimensions of social capital, and knowledge sharing) were measured using scales developed by Van den Hooff & Huysman (2009), all consisting of statements to be answered on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’. SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al., 2005) was used to analyze both the psychometric properties of these measures (measurement model) and the structural relations between them (structural model), following recommended two-stage analytical procedures (Hair et al., 2006). We chose to use PLS instead of a covariance-based Structural Equation Modeling technique (such as AMOS) since our model is relatively exploratory in nature and our main aim is to explain endogenous consructs. Also, PLS avoids problems with smaller sample sizes and non-normality of data, both of which are an issue in the current study (Henseler et al., 2009).

Discriminant validity was confirmed by comparing the within-construct item factor loadings to acrossconstruct item loadings. Since all within-construct item loadings were high (all exceeding 0.60, and except for one even exceeding 0.70), and clearly higher than the cross-loadings, discriminant validity could be assumed. Finally, to assess discriminant validity, the square root of the AVE value of each of the theoretical constructs was compared to the correlations with the other constructs. For discriminant validity, a construct should share more variance with its measures than with other constructs in the model (Chin, 1998), a criterion which was met by these scales: all AVE square roots exceeded the values of the correlations among the constructs in the corresponding rows and columns, see Table 3. As such, discriminant validity was confirmed (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

One final note on the measurement model: we conceptualized all our latent variables as reflective measures (i.e., the items we measured are seen as indicators for an underlying concept), whereas it could be argued that some of these measures have a more formative character (i.e., the concept is seen as being caused by the indicators). The scale for performance (and, to a lesser extent, knowledge sharing) could be argued to be a formative one, but we think it is defendable to consider the items on IT’s contributions and abilities as reflective indicators. However, to test the robustness of our results, we also tested an alternative version of the structural model for the Business organization (Figure 2), in which both perceived IT performance and knowledge sharing were included as formative measures. This did not cause significant changes in the model: paths that were significant with the reflective measures were significant with the formative measures as well, no new significant paths emerged, and no significant paths changed in sign. Based on these results, we can conclude that the results are not an artifact of our modeling choices (Chwelos et al., 2001).

## Structural model

Having this adequate measurement model, we could proceed to analyze the paths between the constructs, in order to test our hypotheses. Although our analysis in SmartPLS 2.0 did show significant paths for most of our hypotheses, the explained variance of perceived IT performance was a mere 0.18. As Henseler et al. (2009) state, the R-square of endogenous variables is an important criterion for assessing a structural model, and a value of 0.18 is considered to be weak (Chin, 1998). Therefore, it was decided to reject the model for the joint sample (Business and IT together) and subsequently split the sample and run separate models for Business and IT.

This decision was not only based on the low proportion of explained variance for the joint sample. First of all, as reported above, the interviews pointed towards some interesting differences between the Business organization and the IT department which justified making separate structural models for each group. Secondly, although we formulated the hypotheses on an integrated level (i.e., treating IT and Business as one group, assessing their mutual relationships) there are also arguments to consider them separately – after all, IT answered questions about their perception of their relationship with Business, and vice versa.

Based on these considerations, separate models were constructed for both groups, yielding the results in Figure 2 for the Business organization, and in Figure 3 for the IT department.

Although these models point to some interesting differences, dividing the sample also created a problem: the sample size of 93 for the IT department is too small in relation to the number of indicators in the model. According to Mitchell (1993), a rule of thumb regarding sample size in structural equation modeling is that there should be 10 to 20 times as many cases as variables. In our model, that would mean a minimum of 150 cases. Furthermore. the model in Figure 3 explains a mere 8% of the variance in perceived IT performance, which makes the model indicative at best. Consequently, our main focus in discussing the results of these analyses will be on the model for the Business organization (Figure 3), which is acceptable in terms of sample size (143 being sufficiently close to 150) and explained variance $\textstyle ( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 1 )$ . In spite of the statistical shortcomings of the model for the IT department, we do draw some indicative comparisons between the two models. These comparisons are based on a Multigroup Analysis (MGA) as outlined by Henseler et al. (2009), to compare the path coefficients for both models. These results can be summarized as follows:

## Discussion

The results of our study provide insight into the relationship between the different occupational communities of IT and Business. We find that a lack of structural social capital (connections between employees and awareness of what others know), cognitive social capital (shared frame of reference) and relational social capital (mutual trust and shared norms) can serve as an explanation for the often problematic relationship between these communities. Social capital influences perceived IT performance directly, as well as indirectly via knowledge sharing. In the interviews, a lack of (cognitive and relational) social capital and knowledge sharing between Business and IT were identified as likely causes of problems in the relationship between these groups. In spite of the fact that the model for the IT department does not fully meet the criteria, the quantitative results seem to be in line with the findings from the interviews. Both qualitative and quantitative results indicate that the emphasis is different for the Business organization and the IT department: in the perception of the IT department itself, the process of exchanging factual information improves their perceived importance (although to a limited extent), whereas the Business organization emphasizes the influence of mutual relationships. For the Business organization, higher levels of cognitive and relational social capital lead to mutual understanding and identification, and thus to a more positive perception of how the IT department performs. This sheds new light on the troubled relationship between two parts of the organization: where IT thinks it should suffice to explain to the Business what they are doing (and vice versa), it seems that the Business would be more interested in being more actively involved in the whole process of IS development, building and maintaining strong relationships with the IT department characterized by mutual understanding of the others’ practices and interests.

These conclusions are based on a multimethod approach, combining 28 interviews and 236 questionnaires, which provides us with an in-depth insight into the relationship between Business and IT. On the other hand, we do acknowledge that these findings may be typical for this particular organization. Therefore, it would be very valuable to replicate this approach in other organizations, to be able to enhance the external validity of findings.

Also, our study examined the relationship between IT and Business at a single point in time. Since our focus is very much on social dynamics between these occupational communities, a more dynamic perspective could be valuable. Therefore, a longitudinal study of such relationships, in which collaboration, knowledge sharing and social capital between the occupational communities of IT and Business would be followed across a longer period of time, would certainly provide valuable information – for instance, in terms of causality, or in terms of the relative importance of certain management interventions.

## Conclusion

The findings presented in this paper offer a contribution to theory in two areas: (1) theory on the relationship between business and IT and (2) theory on collaboration between different occupational communities. Theory on the relationship between Business and IT tends to focus on strategic aspects (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Lockamy & Smith, 1997; Sabherwal & Chan, 2001; Willcoxson & Chatham, 2004), and if it does pay attention to knowledge sharing, it is in a static sense, with ‘shared domain knowledge’ seen as an object that both parties can possess (Nelson & Cooprider, 1996; Reich & Benbasat, 2000; Basselier et al., 2003; Kearns & Lederer, 2003). Based on our findings, we can conclude that a more ‘practice based perspective’ (Brown & Duguid, 2001; Hislop, 2002) on the collaboration between IT and Business could be very valuable. According to this perspective, knowledge creation and sharing take place when practices are shared in a rich and meaningful way (Hislop, 2002), that is, when people work together. The important role of social capital emphasizes this: when IT and Business actively collaborate, they create a shared practice, and through this, shared social capital and shared knowledge. Both these variables will positively contribute to perceived IT performance. Thus, this perspective seems to be valuable since it focuses on the actual processes of collaboration and knowledge sharing between IT and Business. Measuring shared domain knowledge in terms of being informed about each other’s area of work is only a very limited conceptualization of knowledge sharing, one that is actually in line with how the IT department in our study thinks they can influence perceptions of their performance: the other party may be aware of the facts, but since they have not actually had the ability to work together and really learn from each other by collectively elaborating on shared information, there is no real shared understanding. This is supported by the fact that in our interviews, relational and cognitive social capital and knowledge sharing are seen as intimately related and together influence the IT department’s perceived performance.

The second contribution of these findings directly relates to this: considering Business and IT as different occupational communities further emphasizes the importance of actual collaboration between them, in order to create a shared understanding. A collaborative approach towards IS Development creates the possibility to collaborate on the development of an IS and thus make the knowledge regimes of Business and IT meet (Howard-Grenville & Carlile, 2006). This also creates a shared practice between the two occupational communities (Brown & Duguid, 2001), a common goal around which they can be united, helping them find common ways to express their interests and understand those of others (Bechky, 2003). Thus, our findings offer a very concrete insight into the importance of the process of collaboratively creating IS in integrating the knowledge of different occupational communities.
