RESEARCH ARTICLE

# The mediating role of psychological contract breach in IS outsourcing: inter-firm governance perspective

Hyung Jin Kim<sup>1</sup>, Bongsik Shin<sup>2</sup> and Hogeun Lee<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>National University of Singapore, Singapore; <sup>2</sup>San Diego State University, CA, U.S.A; <sup>3</sup>Yonsei University, Seoul, Korea

Correspondence: Hogeun Lee, School of Business, Yonsei University, 50 Yonsei-ro, Seodaemun-gu, Seoul 120-749, Korea. Tel: 82 2 2123 4470; Fax: 82 2 373 7430

## Abstract

A substantial body of previous research on the client–vendor relationship has identified inter-organizational partnerships and formal contracts as important governance mechanisms for outsourcing performance. Successful IS development through outsourcing, however, may be more dependent on the people who execute the project in the field than on inter-firm relationships and agreedupon formalities. Among individual-level variables, the special importance of psychological contracts has recently been noted in IS literature. This study investigates the mediating role of psychological contract breach between these two firm-level governance factors and outsourcing performance. By analyzing matched responses from project managers, vendor participants, and system users, we found that the effects of explicit legal contracts and partnership quality on outsourcing outcome are fully mediated by the client’s perception of breach by the vendor. This study offers an extended theoretical perspective on the governance of firm-level collaboration, especially revealing that the benefits of formal contracts and inter-organizational partnerships eventually translate into satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users through each party’s perception of breach on the individual level. Moreover, discrepancy was observed in this study between the client and vendor regarding the impact of legal contracts on individuals’ psychological contract while that of partnership was prominent in both sides.

## Introduction

Outsourcing of IS development is defined as the subcontracting of some or all of the development and processing activities of an organization to an outside vendor (Richmond et al, 1992). The inter-organizational partnership in which consultants from a service provider with relevant expertise guide the internal development team to implement a system is considered to be a prevalent form of IS outsourcing.

Having witnessed the growth of the outsourcing phenomenon as evidenced from recent industry statistics (Pohlmann et al, 2006), IS research has largely attempted to identify factors influencing outsourcing performance (Grover et al, 1996; Lacity & Willcocks, 1998). IS outsourcing deals are actually fraught with difficulties and high rates of failure (Gurbaxani, 2005). As a means to avoid the risk of failure, the importance of various contracting practices including contract design (Chen & Bharadwaj, 2009;

Dey et al, 2010; Susarla et al, 2010), contract types (Gopal & Sivaramakrishnan, 2008), and control mode (Choudhury & Sabherwal, 2003) have constantly been underscored by IS scholars. On the other hand, relational exchange or partnerships between the client and vendor (Holmstro¨m Olsson et al, 2008; Vlaar et al, 2008; Rai et al, 2009) has also been regarded as a key to satisfactory outsourcing outcomes. Prior research on these so-called governance mechanisms has used well-known theoretical angles (i.e., economic perspective, social exchange perspective) that primarily focus on inter-firm interactions between the client and vendor.

Specifically, extant IS studies on the client–vendor relationship (e.g., Lee & Kim, 1999; Goo et al, 2009; Rai et al, 2009) have primarily stressed the roles of the legal contract and inter-firm partnership in predicting outsourcing success. From the principal–agent perspective, crafting an explicit legal contract (ELC), which clearly defines the roles and liabilities of all parties, has been recommended to reduce the potential opportunistic behavior of a service firm (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993). An ELC involves a certain level of detail to which roles, responsibilities, expected performance, and handling of unplanned events and conflicts are specified (Wuyts & Geyskens, 2005). On the other hand, as the nature of outsourcing relationships has become less contractual and more relational (Lee, 2001), ensuring a sturdy interfirm partnership (PART) has become a strategic means for outsourcing firms to manage client–vendor relationships on long-term base (Lee et al, 2003). Partnership refers to an inter-organizational relationship formed in order to achieve the shared goals of the service receiver and provider (Grover et al, 1996; Lee & Kim, 1999). These two governance mechanisms are often used together and complement each other in achieving expected outcomes in most cases of IS outsourcing (Saunders et al, 1997; Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Goo et al, 2009).

Despite the well-established theoretical evidence for the existence of these two success factors, some scholars have still noted that how the quality of these firm-level factors (i.e., ELC, PART) translates into outsourcing performance is not clear (Nam et al, 1996). For example, system implementation falls to project workers (from both sides), and its success after all depends on their workmanship and cooperation on an individual level. Even though the client and vendor firms sign a partnership contract, the everyday working out of the contractual relationship may be affected by project members’ subjective interpretations of mutual obligations and fulfillment of those obligations. Outsourcing performance may thus be the eventual result of both firm- and individual-level factors.

Intriguingly, recent IS studies (e.g., Koh et al, 2004; Kim et al, 2007a, b; A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk & Fitzgarald, 2008) have increasingly used an individual-level perspective, psychological contract perspective, to illuminate individual-level implicit dynamics such as perceptions of mutual obligations and fulfillment of those obligations. Moreover, the way individual project members interpret their mutual obligations in a formal contract can affect outsourcing success (Koh et al, 2004). The finding attracts attention from IS scholars who have mainly focused on firm-level success factors, and calls for research that considers the individual-level perspective as well to better explain outsourcing performance.

In general, the psychological contract is defined as an individual’s mental beliefs about his or her mutual obligations in a contractual relationship (Rousseau, 1995). To elucidate the employment relationship, prior studies (e.g., Morrison & Robinson, 1997; Tekleab & Taylor, 2003) have paid considerable attention to the psychological contract for two primary reasons. First, it is not possible to address all facets of a relationship in a formal, written contract; a psychological contract fills the gap as it affects one’s understanding of mutual obligations and behaviors. In fact, it exists from the moment when a party starts to believe that promises have been made (Rousseau, 1995; Anderson & Schalk, 1998). Second, psychological contracts can be breached. A psychological contract breach (PCB) has negative implications about attitudes (e.g., commitment to the organization, job satisfaction) and behaviors (e.g., in-role work performance, organizational citizenship behavior). PCB is thus ultimately detrimental to performance outcomes (Dulac et al, 2008). These previous findings suggest that the psychological contract perspective is useful to deeply understand a contractual relationship.

Given its three underlying principles (i.e., mutuality, psychological obligations, and individual level of analysis), the psychological contract can be defined, in the context of inter-organizational outsourcing, as the contractual parties’ mental beliefs and expectations about their mutual obligations, which are perceived at the individual level (Koh et al, 2004). A project member’s perception of promises made by the other party falls into this category (Henderson et al, 2008). The psychological contract resides in the mind of the project member during the duration of an IS outsourcing project. Therefore, it may be involved in many of the operational details of a project. Given the fact that it is impractical to specify all possible project details in a formal outsourcing contract (Brandon & Halvey, 1990), the psychological contract may cover many of the details associated with the governance of inter-firm cooperation, possibly influencing the course and outcome of outsourcing efforts.

However, the client and vendor are likely to hold somewhat different and possibly unique beliefs about their mutual obligations (Koh et al, 2004). They may falsely believe that these obligations are mutually understood, thereby causing a perception of contract breach. This belief is usually evident at the individual level (Koh et al, 2004). There may be several factors influencing a project participant’s psychological contract, such as the natural tendency to present a favorable image during the attraction process, obvious or perceived promises, interpreted patterns of past exchanges, indirect learning, and other factors that may be taken for granted (Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Chen et al, 2008). However, little is known about what factors affect a project member’s psychological contract and perception of breach in the client–vendor relationship of IS outsourcing.

In this study, we endeavored to afford a better understanding on the effectiveness of governance mechanisms in IS outsourcing by investigating a mediating role of an individual-level subjective factor (i.e., PCB) between the firm-level governance factors (i.e., the ELC and PART) and outsourcing performance. Our research model explains that the benefits of formal contracts and inter-organizational partnerships eventually translate into satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users through each party’s perception of contract breach on the individual level. Understanding the dynamics of key individual- and firm-level attributes of IS outsourcing in a holistic manner is important (Lacity & Willcocks, 2000), and this study is intended to serve that purpose.

## Literature review

## Psychological contract

A psychological contract is a collection of beliefs about what each party is obliged to deliver based on perceived promises. It resides ‘in the eyes of the beholder’ (Robinson & Rousseau, 1994, p. 246). Various researchers have argued that different parties in a relationship are likely to hold unequal beliefs about mutual obligations due to the inherently subjective nature of psychological contracts. The psychological component is an inevitable aspect of virtually all contracts (Rousseau & McLean Parks, 1993). In fact, examples abound of implicit or ambiguous promises resulting from vague statements or actions made by contracting parties. For instance, a recruiter’s offhand statement that ‘people in this area are likely to get promoted in three years’ can be interpreted as a promise of rapid promotion (Robinson & Morrison, 2000). As an example in the context of IS outsourcing, a vendor’s utterance of how responsibly it behaved in its previous project with another client firm may lead the current client to perceive a promise that the vendor will be ‘taking charge of the outsourcing project’, while the vendor actually expects the client to ‘own the outsourcing project’ (Koh et al, 2004, p. 361). This construal process leads both parties to perceive the same event differently (Robinson & Morrison, 2000). Subjectivity exists in any contractual relationship and can exert a powerful influence on what each party believes and his or her ensuing behavior. Therefore, psychological contracts reflect what contracting parties really perceive about mutual obligations (Koh et al, 2004; Pavlou & Gefen, 2005).

In the context of IS outsourcing, existing theories focusing on inter-firm interactions (e.g., agency theory, social exchange theory) cannot fully capture what individual project members actually believe about mutual obligations. For example, although agency theory highlights the importance of a tight contract, the written agreement cannot always be distributed to all individuals (Lacity & Willcocks, 2001). In this situation, agency theory alone does not assure the benefits of formal control on expected outsourcing outcomes as individual project members’ understanding of mutual obligations can be different. Agency theory does not address this reality although individual-level perceptions of mutual obligations are critical to outsourcing performance (Koh et al, 2004). Social exchange theory has also applied to inter-firm long-term relationships between the client and vendor; therefore, it cannot fully explain the individual level dynamics regarding perceptions of mutual obligations. In sum, the psychological contract perspective allows for an interesting investigation of individual-level understanding of mutual obligations, especially in the situation in which firm-level governance efforts (e.g., legal contracts, partnerships) are at play.

## PCB

Central to psychological contract theory is the perception of breach. PCB is a cognition that arises when one party perceives another party’s failure to fulfill promised obligations within a psychological contract (Morrison & Robinson, 1997). Although PCB has been the focus of considerable prior research, some scholars have not treated the conceptual distinction between PCB and psychological contract violation cautiously. For example, some scholars (e.g., Tekleab & Taylor, 2003) defined psychological contract violation as cognition of breach. However. Morrison & Robinson (1997) made a distinction between breach and violation in terms of cognition vs emotion, outlining how affective reactions (i.e., psychological contract violation) are developed through cognition of breach. This perspective has been adopted in many other subsequent studies (e.g., Robinson & Morrison, 2000; Dulac et al, 2008).

PCB has received much attention from researchers studying contractual relationships such as those found in the workplace because of the unfavorable attitudes and behaviors it generates, such as reduced job satisfaction, reduced organizational commitment, and increased turnover intention (Robinson & Rousseau, 1994). An inverse relationship has been identified between PCB and workrelated outcomes, including in-role performance and organizational citizenship behaviors (Turnley & Feldman, 2000; Coyle-Shapiro, 2002).

According to prior studies (e.g., Robinson & Morrison, 2000), PCB occurs due to reneging and incongruence. Reneging indicates a situation in which one party recognizes an obligation, but fails to fulfill it due to an inability (unable to do) or unwillingness (unwilling to do) (Robinson & Morrison, 2000). On the other hand, incongruence refers to the situation in which the opposite party is unlikely to believe the obligation exists, or has a different understanding of the nature of the obligation or promise made between the two parties in an exchange relationship (Coyle-Shapiro & Kessler, 2000; Robinson & Morrison. 2o00). In both conditions, an individual is likely to perceive a PCB. These two primary causes of PCB are thus critical to addressing theoretical questions about what can reduce PCB in a contractual relationship.

## Psychological contract perspectives in IS studies

IS scholars have increasingly provided evidence that psychological contract theory offers a unique perspective in understanding IS phenomena such as online buyer– seller relationships and the client–vendor relationship in IS outsourcing. For example, Pavlou & Gefen (2005) applied it to buyer–seller relationships in the online marketplace. They examined several direct and moderating impacts of psychological contract violation on trust, perceived risk, transaction intentions, and price premiums. One of their primary contributions was to extend the literature on the psychological contract into the one-tomany context $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ the community of sellers). Another recent example is A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk & Fitzgerald’s (2008) work, in which psychological contract theory was adopted into the open sourcing context, and customer and community psychological obligations were identified.

More relevant to the context of our study, Koh et al (2004) identified a set of mutual obligations perceived by client and vendor project managers and found that fulfillment of these obligations has a positive impact on outsourcing success. The results stressed the importance of individual-level perceptions of mutual obligations. In addition, the research provided significant insight into governance mechanisms that also deal with what each party is supposed to offer but on the firm level. A related curiosity is, for example, how the legal contract and inter-firm partnership affect project members’ perceptions of mutual obligations. The psychological contract perspective, therefore, facilitates research of the dynamics of IS outsourcing relationships on multiple levels. Combining the individual-level perspective with ideas from the existing literature focusing on inter-organizational relationship management (e.g., inter-firm governance mechanisms) can build a comprehensive understanding on how the firm-level efforts such as ELC and PART eventually translate into outsourcing performance.

To develop a theoretical link between firm- and individual-level success factors, PCB acted as the individual-level variable in this study. Some IS research using the psychological contract perspective has left some to be desired, especially in distinguishing PCB and psychological contract violation. For example, Pavlou & Gefen (2005) used psychological contract violation in their model; however, the affective state of the betrayed party was not considered in the conceptualizations and the measurement items of this variable, thus leading to confusion. Following Morrison & Robinson’s (1997) differentiation between cognition of breach (i.e., PCB) and feelings of betrayal (i.e., psychological contract violation), the present study adopted the concept of PCB for the several reasons.

First, in IS outsourcing, PCB can be defined as an individual’s cognition that the contracting party has failed to meet obligations that he or she believed to be promised within his or her psychological contract. We thus believe that PCB is more likely to be associated with cognitive factors of an outsourcing project, such as the terms of exchange specified in the ELC and interfirm relational norms. Second, prior research on the nature of PCB and its causal associations with organizational support and relationship quality (Dulac et al, 2008) provides insight that firm-level factors such as formal contracts and partnership quality may have an impact on PCB. Finally, PCB is believed to affect outsourcing outcomes more directly than do factors on the inter-firm level, because PCB affects an individual’s reactions to the contract breach (Dulac et al, 2008), thus affecting the quality of collaboration between parties and outsourcing outcomes.

## Research model and hypotheses

As shown in Figure 1, our research model encompasses both firm- and individual-level factors and proposes two primary relationships. We specifically investigate the impacts of the ELC and PART on PCB. We also propose a role of PCB in leading to successful outsourcing outcomes for end-users. The mediating role of the individual-level factor (i.e., PCB) between the firm-level governance efforts (i.e., ELC and PART) and satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users is the main focus of this investigation. The dependent variable (DV) is individual-level impact of the outsourced system (IOS), which reflects the degree to which end-users make use of the system, and the satisfaction, dependence, and individual performance effects of the outsourced system.

## The effect of ELC on PCB

According to psychological contract theory, one of the root causes of PCB, incongruence regarding mutual obligations, arises for three primary reasons: dissimilar cognitive schemata for obligations between the parties involved, ambiguity of perceived obligations, and inadequate communications (Morrison & Robinson, 1997). Any two people may have very different schemata for what a relationship should entail (e.g., mutual obligations) based on their own experience. Disparate perceptions of obligations can grow more distant when the obligations are ambiguous (e.g., when a contract is implicit or incomplete). Part of the solution is to ensure accurate and precise communication about expected obligations.

The results of prior studies emphasizing the importance of a well-crafted formal contract and identifying its principal elements in IS outsourcing allow us to argue that when terms of exchange are well specified in a legal contract, incongruence between the client and vendor may be minimized; in other words, PCB may be less likely to occur. From the economic perspective (e.g., transaction cost economics, agency theory), a significant body of IS outsourcing studies (e.g., Loh & Venkatraman, 1995) has emphasized the importance of a detailed contract for the prevention and resolution of possible conflicts. Both client and vendor usually take part in the negotiation process, discussing what each party is expected to offer during the project duration (Lee, 1996; Goo et al, 2009). Adequate communication is vital at this stage because mutual expectations are jointly developed in the contracting or negotiation process (Goo et al, 2009). If communication regarding mutual obligations is insufficient, perceived promises between the two parties are more likely to be divergent, leading to the potential problem of incongruence. Either of the parties may be unlikely to believe that an obligation exists, or may understand the nature of the obligation differently.

An explicit, well-documented formal contract is an end product or reflection of adequate communication during the contracting process (Lee, 1996). Therefore, the client and/or vendor are more likely to experience PCB in a project where terms of exchange in a legal contract are not well documented. In addition, an ELC may curtail another source of incongruence: ambiguity of perceived obligations. When terms of exchange are clearly specified in a legal contract, greater agreement on each party’s obligations may be more likely (Koh et al, 2004). The consensus between two parties may in turn reduce PCB. In this regard, we hypothesize that:

H1a: The explicitness of the legal contract (ELC) will have a negative impact on the client’s perception of contract breach by the vendor (CPBV).

H1b: The explicitness of the legal contract (ELC) will have a negative impact on the vendor’s perception of contract breach by the client (VPBC).

The effect of PART on PCB

Incongruence regarding mutual obligations may be mitigated in an environment where norms and values are shared. Shared norms and values are the underlying characteristics of relational governance in IS outsourcing (Rai et al, 2009). Three types of norms are commonly found in the IS outsourcing literature: solidarity, information exchange, and flexibility (Goo et al, 2009). The solidarity norm emphasizes the value of joint relationship, while the information exchange norm encourages the active bilateral provision of useful information. The flexibility norm stresses necessary adaptations to changing circumstances.

These unwritten rules that pervade a close inter-firm partnership help the implicated parties better understand what behavior is expected or appropriate in their various contexts of interaction (Kern & Blois, 2002) and how the cooperative work should be accomplished (Rai et al, 2009). Construal processes may thus be diminished, whereby individual project members rely on other cues and prior experience to interpret their perceptions about the other party’s obligation fulfillment (Morrison & Robinson, 1997). As a result, ambiguity of perceived obligations, one of the main causes of incongruence, may be minimized accordingly. Incongruence resulting from other sources, such as dissimilar cognitive schemata regarding mutual obligations, may also be reduced when norms and values are shared between the client and vendor. An individual’s schemata of how the world works is basically shaped by his or her values in life (Bartunek & Moch, 1987). Likewise, shared values between contracting parties may help them develop similar cognitive schemata for what the contractual relationship should entail. Thus, when the spirit of partnership is strong between client and vendor, incongruence regarding mutual obligations may be minimized as they demonstrate values that are important to both parties throughout the IS project.

Reneging, another fundamental cause of PCB, may be reduced by reciprocal exchange in a partnership-style relationship. Reciprocity is not explicitly bargained in social exchanges (Balu, 1964), but emerges when a party that is indebted for a period of time feels an obligation to reciprocate in order to achieve balance (Dulac et al, 2008). A constructive cycle may emerge when both parties reciprocate to each other by positively responding to each other’s expectations (Kamdar & Dyne, 2007). Such reciprocity in a relational exchange may promote selfenforcement of obligations and reduce unwillingness to fulfill promised obligations (i.e., reneging). Relational governance is characterized by self-enforcement of obligations through trust and social identification (Goo et al, 2009). In this regard, we propose that:

H2a: Partnership quality (PART) will have a negative impact on the client’s perception of contract breach by the vendor (CPBV).

H2b: Partnership quality (PART) will have a negative impact on the vendor’s perception of contract breach by the client (VPBC).

## The effect of PCB on IOS

Many previous studies in the employment literature (e.g., Chen et al, 2008; Henderson et al, 2008) have examined the detrimental effects of PCB on important work-related outcomes (e.g., an employee’s in-role work performance, organizational citizenship behavior). For example, Chen et al (2008) found that an employee’s perception of breach by the organization has a negative impact on his or her work performance, objectively measured using salary data for the most recent 3 months in a ‘base plus merit’ pay system. Other studies noted that PCB negatively affected other individual-level reactions, such as job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and intention to continue a job (Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Coyle-Shapiro & Kessler, 2000; Turnley et al, 2003).

These findings indicate that a party’s PCB may have an unfavorable effect on the client–vendor relationship and, in turn, performance outcome in IS outsourcing. When a project member perceives that a promise has been broken and codes of conduct have been ignored by the other partner, his or her response may be negative (Rousseau, 1989). The cognition of breach leads to the perception of balance impairment in the exchange relationship (Henderson et al, 2008); the victim may be motivated to restore the balance by reducing his or her own contribution to the project (Chen et al, 2008). As a result, collaboration may be jeopardized, inversely affecting the quality of the procedures required to complete the IS project. Ultimately, the resulting product may not be satisfactory to end-users, because it is less likely to meet initial requirements when it is developed under these conditions. Usage and work performance for users of the product may then decline. Therefore, it is hypothesized that:

H3a: The client’s perception of contract breach by the vendor (CPBV) will have a negative impact onthe individuallevel impact of the outsourced system (IOS).

H3b: The vendor’s perception of contract breach by the client (VPBC) will have a negative impact onthe individuallevel impact of the outsourced system (IOS).

## The effect of ELC on IOS

Prior studies underline the importance of a tight contract based on the argument that vendors do not always share the same profit motives as their clients (Lee, 1996). They consider ELC to be the most critical element in successful outsourcing (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993). Transaction cost economics supports this standpoint, suggesting that outsourcing firms write detailed, complete contracts to cope with all possible contingencies that might be caused by bounded rationality and uncertainty (Hart, 1988). An ideal outsourcing contract should address a number of issues. including service level, transfer of assets, staffing, pricing and payment, warranty and liability, timeliness of delivery, dispute resolution, contract termination, intellectual property, and information security (Lee, 1996). A wellwritten contract encompassing these critical elements can thus serve as a significant governance mechanism by defining as many details as possible and protecting the client–vendor relationship from fatal conflicts that would compromise the project and ultimately the quality of the product. Thus, the ELC may lead to satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users.

H4: The explicitness of the legal contract (ELC) will have a positive impact on the individual-level impact of the outsourced system (IOS).

## The effect of PART on IOS

Relational governance has been described as another key predictor of successful IS outsourcing (Kern & Willcocks, 2002). Given the complexity of outsourcing arrangements, reliance on the legal contract alone is not enough for outsourcing firms to achieve expected outcomes (Koh et al, 2004). The nature of outsourcing has, in fact, gradually evolved from a contractual to a partnershipstyle relationship that has been thoroughly addressed by social exchange theory (Lee et al, 2003). Inter-firm partnerships can improve outsourcing performance (Sabherwal, 1999), as it hinges on trust, shared benefits and risks, business understanding, and other important relational factors, rather than a simple give-and-take philosophy (Fitzgerald & Willcocks, 1994; Lee & Kim, 1999). This stream of research leads to the expectation that high-quality inter-firm partnerships may lead to satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users.

H5: Partnership quality (PART) will have a positive impact on the individual-level impact of the outsourced system (IOS).

## The effect of ELC on PART

One recent IS study, that of Goo et al (2009), provided an in-depth analysis revealing that principal elements of a well-structured formal contract promote primary attributes of relational governance in the IS outsourcing context. For example, if foundational components of a service-level agreement are well documented, such as a statement of business objectives, expectations, and process ownership roles, the primary components of the partnership, such as relational norms, conflict resolution, and mutual dependence (e.g., joint efforts), are likely to grow between the client and vendor. One of the theoretical contributions of this line of research is the finding that legal contracts have an impact on partnership-style relationships, and that investigations of the unidirectional relationship between the two can yield theoretical and practical implications. For example, Goo et al (2009) provided significant insight into how partnerships can be improved through well-structured legal contracts in the IS outsourcing context. Thus, we expect that:

H6: The explicitness of the legal contract (ELC) will have a positive impact on partnership quality (PART).

## Project duration

Extant studies examining psychological contracts in employment relationships have considered job tenure to be a critical control variable. They argued that if an employee works long enough at one organization, a convergence of perspectives may occur as to the mutual obligations between the two parties (Tekleab & Taylor, 2003). In other words, job tenure has a negative impact on the incongruence that leads to PCB (Robinson, 1996; Suazo et al, 2005). Drawing upon previous work, we thus adopted project duration as a control variable to aid in the examination of the influences of our focal variables on PCB. Project duration denotes the period of engagement between the client and vendor in an outsourcing project. In the IS outsourcing literature (e.g., Tiwana, 2003; Koh et al, 2004), project duration has also been employed as a control for other forces toward the outcome variable.

## Research method

## Data collection

Survey responses were collected using questionnaires customized for three different groups of respondents. The sample of this study was drawn from organizations that had recently outsourced IS developments and maintenance in South Korea. The list was obtained from the periodical White Papers of the Korean IS Market, published by the Knowledge Research Group. We gathered a matched data set in which each data point consisted of three responses: one from the project manager of a client firm, one from a vendor’s consultant with whom the project manager cooperated, and one from a client firm’s end-user of the delivered system. Both the client’s project manager and the vendor partner answered questions regarding ELC, PART, and either CPBV or VPBC, as appropriate. An end-user from the business department of the client firm answered questions on IOS. Each matching response set was complete when all three survey responses on a particular IS project were compiled.

During the data gathering process, special care was taken to avoid common method bias in the responses. First, three different questionnaires (A, B, and C) were sent to the project managers of client firms, asking them to complete the first questionnaire (A) regarding a particular recently finished outsourcing project. Each project manager was also asked to forward the second questionnaire (B) to a project partner of the vendor firm who had been involved in the project, and the third questionnaire (C) to a system user of the client firm.

All completed questionnaires were returned directly to the authors by the individual respondents. The direct return of questionnaire B (by vendors) and C (by system users) was necessary to ensure that their responses would not be visible to the project manager. Emails and reminder letters containing information about our research project were sent out several times to project managers. Responses returned to the authors were identified by a confidential ID number to facilitate the compiling of complete sets (Tekleab & Taylor, 2003). Additional questions (e.g., project name, duration, system type) were also included in the questionnaires to double-check that each response in a set referred to the same outsourcing project. In total, 67 matched response sets were gathered. Among them, 13 response sets were incomplete, leaving 54 response sets (162 total responses) available for further analysis.

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the surveyed projects in terms of system type, duration, and size. While the ERP system and a project that took less than 2 years were the largest among the surveyed projects, various kinds of outsourced system development projects were included in the sample. These data diminish any potential concerns about the respondents’ working experience that may affect incongruence regarding mutual obligations.

## Measures

Survey measures were borrowed from existing studies (see Appendix A). Those for ELC were borrowed from Wuyts & Geyskens (2005), who introduced the construct detailed contract drafting to describe the level of detail of the ELC. For PART, a parsimonious measure was used, drawing from existing literature. Despite the rich body of IS research on inter-firm partnerships, only a few studies have empirically measured this construct. In the literature, multiple sub-dimensions of partnership were usually defined. However, why certain sub-dimensions were adopted and others were not is unclear. For example, drawing upon nine sub-constructs presented by Anderson & Narus (1990), Grover et al (1996, p. 99) adopted only four simply ‘because of their relevance to outsourcing practice’. Although Lee & Kim (1999) distinguished sub-dimensions of partnership from its determinants, little theoretical explanation was offered about the commonalities among these sub-dimensions. In fact, the subdimensions in Lee & Kim (1999) differ from those in Grover et al (1996).

To measure PART, we first developed an appropriate conception of partnership in the context of IS outsourcing. As PART is a firm-level factor, we utilized Lee & Kim’s (1999) definition that partnership is an interorganizational relationship to achieve shared goals. The sub-dimensions in that study were then evaluated for their appropriateness for our purposes. Some, such as commitment and conflict, were rejected due to confusion in definition or inability to explain the impact of partnership on outsourcing performance. In addition, other plausible sub-dimensions were also drawn from the literature. Communication was then considered, as prior studies (e.g., Grover et al, 1996; Tuten & Urban, 2001) saw that it is a key ingredient in successful partnerships. Finally, four sub-dimensions were selected (i.e., trust, business understanding, benefit/risk sharing, and communication) to measure PART, and interviews were conducted with outsourcing project personnel to verify the semantic clarity of the survey items.

Regarding the relationships of the sub-dimensions to PART and to each other, the rationale was based on a recent commentary by MacKenzie et al (2011). They suggest that ‘constructs are not inherently formative or reflective in nature’, rather ‘the way in which the construct and its indicators are linked depends on the content of the indicator and how the construct is conceptualized by the researcher (p. 302)’. In this study, we consider the four sub-dimensions as manifestations of PART (rather than its defining characteristics). In fact, the notion of outsourcing partnership cannot be seen as a function of only a few sub-dimensions. Rather, it exists separately at a deeper and more embedded level than its sub-dimensions (MacKenzie et al, 2011). Furthermore, as partnership is an overall quality of inter-organizational relationships, it is less likely that ‘a change in the focal construct [will] be associated with a change in only one of the sub-dimensions (but not the others)’ (MacKenzie et al, 2011). For example, when communication alone decreases (but not trust or business understanding), partnership quality cannot be easily judged as declining. Instead, a decrease must be found in all of the subdimensions in order for partnership quality to be affected.

For the PCB measures, the notion of PCB was distinguished from that of inter-firm partnership in this study. The results of prior studies investigating how PCB is affected by the quality of social exchange relationships are supportive of the conceptual differences between PART and PCB in our research. Previous scholars studying employment relationships have pointed out that distinct from constructs that capture the quality of relationships, such as LMX (Leader-Member Exchange), PCB is ‘a global cognitive appraisal of the extent to which prior transactions have not fulfilled the perceived promises of one<sup>0</sup>s psychological contract’ (Dulac et al, 2008, p. 1082). Psychological contract focuses on kept or broken promises while constructs expressing relationship quality do not necessarily reflect whether the exchanges that have occurred within the relationship are or are not perceived to have been promised (Liden et al, 1997; Dulac et al, 2008). Similarly, partnership in this study is defined as the quality of an inter-organizational relationship between the client and vendor, with little consideration of whether the exchanges occurring in the relationship involved promises kept or broken. On the other hand, PCB is defined as a cognition that the contracting party has failed to meet one or more promises or obligations within a psychological contract.

To measure PCB, a set of psychological obligations (six obligations for each side) identified by Koh et al (2004) was adopted because it has been previously validated in the IS outsourcing context. As they were drawn from a Singaporean data set, the significance of these 12 obligations and their corresponding items was first examined by four industry practitioners: two IS outsourcing consultants and two experienced project managers. Evaluation by a panel of judges is a well-known process of determining content validity (Emory & Cooper, 1991). These four interviewees were asked to grade the items reflecting obligations in terms of relevance to their actual experience. The top-rated item for each obligation was then used in the final version of the survey<sup>1</sup>. We also asked two IS scholars to inspect all the items for each obligation to assess their status as either manifestations or distinctive characteristics of the corresponding obligation (MacKenzie et al, 2011). Both scholars agreed that internal consistency was quite well achieved among the items for each obligation. In the end, six indicators were used for each of the two parties: the CPBV and the VPBC. Although a single item was used to reflect each obligation, we chose to limit the number of items in order to increase the response rate as our intention was to collect a matched sample from multiple sources (i.e., project manager, vendor consultant, and end-user). This compromised protocol was utilized in prior research studying psychological contracts with a matched sample (e.g., Tekleab & Taylor, 2003). In addition, our approach was based on the proven reliable and valid measures of Koh et al (2004), and the assertion that removing an item does not alter the nature of the underlying reflective construct (Freeze & Raschke, 2007). Respondents were asked to recall a particular IS project in which they had recently participated and were told that they need to indicate how well their contractual partner fulfilled the obligations on a scale of 1–7 (1 ¼ not fulfilled at all, 7 ¼ fulfilled to the fullest). The responses were then inversely scored to be consistent with the concept of PCB (Tekleab & Taylor, 2003).

## Results

## Measurement validation

The measurement model was assessed for internal consistency and convergent and discriminant validity. For reliability testing, internal consistency indicators including composite reliability scores and Cronbach’s a scores were calculated. As seen in Table 3, both of these indicators provide evidence that the internal consistency of all multi-item variables was acceptable (all values 4 0.8).

The measurement items were more heavily loaded on their intended factors than on other factors (i.e., loadings were higher than cross-loadings), except for one item (VPBC3), which was then excluded from further analysis. As shown in Appendix B, Gefen & Straub’s (2005) suggestion for convergent validity, that all loadings of the items on their respective factor should be an order of magnitude larger than any other loadings, is satisfied in our data.

For adequate discriminant validity, the square root of the average variance extracted (AVE) score of each variable should be greater than its correlations with other latent variables (Gefen & Straub, 2005; Pavlou & Gefen, 2005). The square roots of all AVE scores (diagonal elements in Table 3) were much higher than all other cross-correlations (off-diagonal elements in the same table). In addition, Appendix B provides additional evidence that the loadings of items on their hypothesized factors are higher than the loadings of other items on these latent factors and the loadings of these items on other latent factors.

Harmon’s one-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias in the survey data. A principal component factor analysis was carried out including all the principal variables. Common method bias exists if a single factor accounts for a large percentage of the variance of all factors. No indication of substantial common method bias was observed in our data, as the first factor accounted for only 17.5% of the total variance (73.9%) (see Mithas et al, 2008; Goo et al, 2009). ## Testing of the structural model

PLS (partial least squares) was used to estimate the proposed structural model. Given that this study represents an initial attempt to explore the theoretical relationships between ELC/PART and PCB, and that the sample size was relatively small, the use of PLS was deemed appropriate (Gefen et al, 2000; Chin et al, 2003). To obtain an unbiased view of the degree of influence of ELC and PART, responses from both parties (i.e., client and vendor) were averaged (Rustagi et al, 2008).

Bootstrap resampling was performed on the structural model to determine path significance. Figure 2 shows the coefficients of each hypothesized path as well as the amount of variance explained (R<sup>2</sup>) by exogenous variables. In terms of explanatory power, 58.3 and 42.0% of variations in CPBV and VPBC were explained by ELC and PART, indicating that PCB in the client–vendor relationship is fairly well explained by the two firm-level governance factors. Intriguingly, CPBV alone accounted for 22.3% of the variations in IOS. Finally, 39.6% of the variation in PART was explained bv ELC.

As hypothesized, the results indicated a significant inverse relationship between ELC and CPBV (b ¼ -0.456, t ¼ 4.447, H1a: supported), while ELC had no significant impact on VPBC (b ¼ -0.024, t ¼ 0.155, H1b: not supported). This finding demonstrates that the effect of a well-specified contract on an individual’s cognition of contract breach is not straightforward and may vary between two parties. In contrast, PART was found to influence both parties’ perceptions of contract breach (b ¼ -0.360, t ¼ 3.171, H2a: supported; b ¼ -0.635, t ¼ 4.569, H2b: supported). These findings further our understanding of the roles of ELC and PART in reducing PCB, which is dependent on each party’s innate roles and characteristics, such as principal vs agent.

In support of H5, CPBV was shown to decrease IOS (b ¼ -0.485, t ¼ 2.243, H3a: supported); however, the effect of VPBC on IOS was not significant (b ¼ -0.135, t ¼ 0.933, H3b: not supported). This finding is partly incompatible with the results of prior studies (e.g., Koh et al, 2004).

No significant relationships were found between ELC/ PART and IOS (b ¼ -0.099, t ¼ 0.588, H4: not supported; b ¼ -0.016, t ¼ 0.080, H5: not supported). Given the supportive effect of CPBV on IOS (H5), this finding indicates that PCB fully mediates the influence of ELC and PART on IOS. Further analysis of this mediation effect yielded the following results. First, consistent with the approach from previous research (e.g., Rai et al, 2006), the estimations of our model were compared with those of a competing model (Chin et al, 2003; Subramani, 2004). In the model of this study, direct paths from ELC and PART to IOS were observed, indicating a partially mediating role of PCB. The competing model is thus a fullmediation model that excludes the direct paths. As a result, the competing model was found to be slightly lower in the amount of variance explained $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 1 9 )$ than our model $( R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 2 3 )$ . ## Discussion

## Key findings

Our data analysis presents four primary findings of interest. First, ELC has a significant curtailing effect on CPBV, but not on VPBC. This provokes an interesting question: What might have caused this discrepancy in the effectiveness of a well-crafted contract? We expected the impact of ELC on PCB because a wellstructured formal contract can reduce incongruence regarding mutual obligations between two parties. For example, sufficient discussion at the contract negotiation stage as to what each party is expected to offer can help both parties better understand each other’s expected roles and responsibilities. The more details are hammered out and expectations communicated, the tighter the contract. Joint acknowledgement of mutual expectations, roles, and responsibilities through effective communication is one of the fundamentals of formal contracting (Goo et al, 2009).

However, our results suggest that the theoretical explanation that mutual obligations can be communicated adequately in the process of drawing up the ELC does not always hold in reality. Parties may not always benefit to the same degree. Instead, some vendors may be plagued by the practice of client-centered contracting in the negotiation process, which inhibits the establishment of common ground between the two parties about client’s obligations (i.e., high incongruence). According to the agency theory and transaction cost economics, a formal contract is an essential means of precise control and management of the outsourcing relationship to avoid future conflicts (Poppo & Zenger, 2002). The importance of a legal contract has thus been emphasized from the client’s perspective. Outsourcing firms are often keen to clarify the vendor firm’s obligations to mitigate uncertainty. However, our results shed light on the less commonly made point that if the process of contracting goes through with a heavy focus on the details of service provision, the effectiveness of the ELC may be limited to the client only. The vendor’s perceptions of the client’s obligations may remain distinct from what the client actually believes. As a result, the vendor may be more likely to perceive a contract breach during the course of an IS project.

Further to this finding, our results provide intriguing evidence that VPBC is affected by PART (although not by ELC). This demonstrates that the fundamental elements of inter-firm partnership, such as shared norms and reciprocity, can affect the vendor’s beliefs about the client’s obligations. In other words, PART allows vendor participants to assess the client’s obligations and the degree to which the client lives up to them. Moreover, compared with the legal contract, the spirit of inter-firm partnership is more likely to help the vendor understand what the client is supposed to offer. PART will thus be more effective in the context of client-centered contracting, in which PCB on the part of the vendor occurs often due to incongruence regarding the client’s obligations. In our results, PART was shown to influence both CPBV and VPBC, but the magnitude of the effect was much greater in VPBC.

In addition to these divergent influences of ELC and PART on PCB, a significant path was observed between ELC and PART. ELC may in fact be important to VPBC, affecting it indirectly via PART. This finding reveals that while relational governance in IS outsourcing has an important impact on the psychological contract, legal contracts are still fundamental at the firm level and able to affect the dynamic psychological processes of project participants (e.g., PCB) indirectly through the partnership-style relationship. An indirect effect of ELC on PCB was also found on the client side (i.e., on CPBV).

The second important finding involves the impact of PCB on IOS. Our results show that CPBV has a negative impact on IOS, while no significant influence of VPBC on IOS was observed. This finding raises doubts about the efficacy of psychological contract theory in explaining outsourcing outcomes. However, even a discrepancy can provide a deeper understanding of psychological contract theory in the IS outsourcing context. Specifically, the insignificant impact of VPBC on IOS can be explained from the perspective of power asymmetry, which has been considered an important factor by other scholars who investigate psychological contracts in employer–employee relationships. Power is one party’s ability to influence the other party to act as desired. The party with power can suppress the voice of the other party, force them to do something against their will, can take part in political activities to negotiate for private interests and so forth (Johnson & Cooper, 2009). IS researchers have extensively examined the roles and characteristics of power on various relationship levels, such as the group, project, organizational, and inter-organizational levels (Jasperson et al, 2002). In the systems development context, power is multi-dimensional and has many labels, including zero-sum, procedural, organizational, structurally constrained, and socially shaped power (Sillince & Mouakket, 1997).

Extant studies on power imbalance in contracting relationships demonstrate that asymmetric power between the client and vendor in IS outsourcing can attenuate the impact of VPBC on IOS. In the employment literature, for example, power asymmetry is an important factor that moderates an employee’s reaction to the contract breach by his or her organization (Chen et al, 2008). An individual who recognizes the power inequality between his organization and himself is more likely to be tolerant when he perceives the organization’s contract breach than an individual who continues to seek a balance in power. Reduction in contributions to the company are thus less likely due in part to the danger of dismissal (Chen et al, 2008).

Some outsourcing vendors who provide services for a powerful client firm may be unable to react against a contract breach because they need to retain the relationship with the client (i.e., for constraint-based reasons). Therefore, they may be unlikely to reduce their contributions to the IS project. However, this tendency may not apply to all outsourcing vendors. Some vendors may exert relative power over clients via political strategies, relying on their technical expertise (Bloomfield & Danieli, 1995). A follow-up interview provided evidence supporting a speculation about some powerless vendors included in our sample. Two experienced vendor consultants were interviewed, both of whom highlighted the power inequality between their firms and the clients’ firms, saying that they usually need to make a good impression on their clients to improve their chances to create more revenue from the relationships. Future research may take into account perceived power asymmetry as a moderating factor to investigate the impact of each party’s PCB on collaboration efforts and outsourcing outcome.

Another possible explanation for the discrepancy between the effects of CPBV and VPBC on IOS is that compared with vendor consultants, project managers usually share many things in common with end-users. As an employee of the client firm, the project manager must understand necessary system capabilities better than vendor firm partners do. In other words, the project manager’s expectations of the delivered system are better aligned with the expectations of systems users, for obvious reasons (e.g., they are employees of the same company, they share common goals, and they have more opportunity for formal and informal interactions and communication). In this situation, breach perceptions felt by the project manager with regard to the vendor’s obligations affecting system performance may be correlated with end-users’ perceptions toward the outsourced system.

Third, the analysis showed that the direct paths from ELC and PART to IOS were not significant. This lack of influence of PART on outsourcing outcome contradicts the results of other studies (e.g., Lee & Kim, 1999) for several possible reasons, including the mediation effect of PCB and the potential impacts of other control variables, such as project complexity. To elucidate this finding from the perspective of mediation, we further analyzed the direct effects of these two firm-level variables on IOS by dropping the PCB variables. As a result, PART was shown to have a positive relationship with IOS $( \beta = 0 . 2 5 6 , t = 1 . 7 2 9 )$ ; however, ELC was still not influential $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 7 , t = 0 . 7 4 1 )$ . Given the mixed findings in previous research of the effectiveness of formal contracts (Aubert et al, 2003), the insignificant effect of ELC observed in this study is acceptable. The analysis of the alternative model therefore underlines the mediating role of PCB between ELC/ PART and IOS. PCB is a critical intermediate variable that eventually translates the benefits of firm-level ELC and PART into satisfactory outsourcing outcomes for system users.

Organizational-level arrangements between two firms, such as contract drafting and partnership acknowledgement, may not directly guarantee satisfactory end product. End-users’ reactions toward the system (e.g., usage, satisfaction, dependence) may be too distant from interorganizational formal agreements and partnership quality. Critical intermediate incidents and processes that are more likely to have an immediate effect on outsourcing performance are thus necessary. Our research indicates that a project manager’s cognition of how well the vendor fulfills promised obligations (cf. CPBV) is one of the important intermediate processes that are affected by the firm-level efforts and take place at the individual level.

Fourth, our results found that project duration, a proxy for the amount of time two parties work together on an outsourcing project, is inversely related to CPBV, but not to VPBC. According to Tekleab & Taylor (2003), employees and managers who work together for a long time have a tendency to agree on employees’ obligations only, not necessarily on their organization’s obligations. This finding provides insight into the insignificant relationship between VPBC and project duration. Agreement on mutual obligations serves as a critical precondition of PCB (Tekleab & Taylor, 2003) because it means that less incongruence is evident. Therefore, our results indicate that in the IS outsourcing context, the client and vendor are likely to agree primarily on the vendor’s obligations as the project progresses. In turn, CPBV tends to be reduced in longer IS projects.

This finding implies that opportunities for vendor representatives to discuss the client’s obligations may be lacking, even in a long-term outsourcing project. The spirit of inter-firm partnership alone (e.g., the norm of reciprocity) may serve as an effective yardstick for individual vendor consultants to determine what the client is obliged to do and judge how well those obligations are fulfilled. In contrast, more opportunities exist for project members from the client firm to confirm the vendor’s obligations, such as the contracting process, the underlying principles of partnership, and long-term interactions.

## Conclusion

The objective of this study was to examine the importance of PCB in IS outsourcing by investigating its theoretical relationships with two firm-level governance mechanisms (i.e., ELC and PART) and outsourcing performance (i.e., IOS). Our results from an analysis of matched survey responses show that CPBV has a negative effect on IOS, and that it fully mediates the influences of ELC and PART on IOS. These findings, in general, indicate that the psychological exchange that operates on the individual, project member level is a stronger indicator of the quality of outsourcing outcome than firm-level factors. Moreover, this study provides new evidence that making concerted efforts to specify terms of exchange in a legal contract and nurture inter-firm partnerships is an important precondition of successful IS outsourcing. According to our findings, the two firm-level factors studied here alleviate the negative effects of PCB that impair outsourcing performance.

## Theoretical implications

Our work in this paper contributes to the IS literature in several ways. First, extending the initial work of IS scholars (e.g., Koh et al, 2004), this study attempted to advance the understanding of psychological contract theory from a different theoretical angle (i.e., inter-firm governance). Although a growing body of IS research has adopted the psychological contract perspective to elucidate IS phenomena, fewer attempts have been made to establish theoretical links between the new perspective and existing perspectives. Drawing upon recent research which indicates that psychological contract fulfillment plays a critical role in leading to outsourcing success (Koh et al, 2004), our study introduced a focal construct from psychological contract theory (i.e., PCB) to the IS community so as to afford a deeper understanding of that theory in the context of IS outsourcing. Then, we endeavored to build a theoretical bridge between extant IS knowledge regarding the inter-firm governance mechanism and new knowledge of PCB. Specifically, our theoretical elaborations connect these two distinct research streams that focus on firm-level success factors (i.e., legal contract, partnership) and individual-level psychological contracts. Theoretical arguments addressed how the fundamental causes of PCB (i.e., reneging and incongruence) can be reduced by several aspects of ELC and PART, providing new insights as to how project members’ perceptions of mutual obligations and contract breach are affected by firm-level governance efforts.

Second, the mediation analysis in our study contributes to the governance literature by improving existing knowledge on how inter-organizational governance mechanisms lead to satisfactory outsourcing outcomes. Given the mixed findings in prior research regarding the direct impact of firm-level factors (i.e., ELC and PART) on outsourcing success (Aubert et al, 2003), investigation of more dynamic mediating variables was warranted. Our study responded to this need by focusing on the individual-level subjective factor PCB, providing empirical evidence that the advantages of ELC and PART translate into improved outsourcing performance as the two firm-level factors lead project members to perceive low levels of breach by the other party. Our findings, therefore, have significant implications for the understanding of the nomological network of the two governance factors. The effectiveness of legal contracts and partnerships is, on one hand, well established in the literature. However, on the other, better theoretical explanations are still required.

Third, this study furthers understanding of PCB in the IS outsourcing context by examining its relationships with the other study variables within a dual-perspective model in which two separate PCB variables (i.e., CPBV and VPBC) are at play. The dual-perspective model revealed some intriguing discrepancies between PCB by both client and vendor, which may be the subject of future IS research. For example, our findings show that while VPBC does not affect outsourcing outcome, CPBV has a significant impact on the outcome variable. In an attempt to reconcile these findings with existing knowledge, further theoretical efforts were made, yielding a deeper understanding of PCB in the context of IS outsourcing. For example, power asymmetry between the two parties in an exchange relationship may moderate the impact of PCB on outsourcing performance, such that vendors who recognize the power inequality in the relationship may be less likely to react negatively to their client’s failure to keep promises. Various characteristics of the outsourcing relationship must be examined further to elucidate the impact of PCB on outsourcing performance.

Another theoretical finding from the dual-perspective model presented here is that the impact of the ELC on PCB differs between the client and vendor, while the effectiveness of PART on PCB is consistent between the two parties. Specifically, ELC has no impact on VPBC. One possible explanation for this is that in some IS projects, the client’s obligations may not be as clearly spelled out in the contracting process as the vendor’s obligations (i.e., client-centered contracting). Accordingly, the vendor participants’ understanding of the client’s obligations may differ from that of the client project members. These findings and related theoretical speculations suggest that the mixed results of prior studies as to the effectiveness of legal contracts may be attributable to their inability to influence project members’ individual-level perception of mutual obligations and breach. In a similar vein, our results provide evidence that inter-firm partnership is a critical governance mechanism that prevents incongruence regarding mutual obligations between project members from both sides.

Fourth, this study contributes to the literature by identifying another role of project duration as an IS variable. Studies of systems development have often employed project duration as a control variable for corresponding DVs (e.g., outsourcing success, project success). We used the construct more purposefully in our model. Project duration, a proxy of the time period in which the client and vendor work together on an IS project, was found to have a negative effect on PCB, especially CPBV. This finding indicates that in a long-term project, incongruence about the vendor’s obligations tends to decrease, but incongruence about the client’s obligations does not. Thus, project duration can be used as an important variable in future research that focuses on perceptions of breach either in projects of different sizes or at different stages of an IS project.

## Practical implications

This study also has implications for practitioners. First, our findings suggest that both client and vendor make concerted efforts to achieve congruence about expectations (i.e., mutual obligations) between project members from both sides. On one hand, vendors should acknowl edge that they may be unaware of certain obligations, but that client project members may expect those obligations to be fulfilled. Unspoken obligations may lead to subsequent PCB, resulting in discontent on the part of client project members and possible undervaluing of the vendor’s other contributions. Negative impressions can be further exacerbated when client project members encounter the dissatisfaction of endusers with the delivered system. According to the results of this study, CPBV can lead to a disappointing end product. Therefore, vendors must make efforts to understand client project members’ actual understanding of mutual obligations.

For their part, client project members must make their beliefs about the vendor’s obligations explicit. In this study, when client project members (e.g., project managers) perceive the vendor’s failure to fulfill promised obligations, the goals of the project are less likely to be met. Therefore, client project members are advised to communicate clearly with vendors about what they as individuals actually understand and believe about vendor obligations in order to minimize incongruence between the two parties. Another lesson emerging from our results is that although no effect of VPBC was shown on outsourcing performance, not all vendors bear PCB on the part of the client. Vendors with fewer constraints in the relationship may reduce their contributions when they perceive that the client has failed to keep promises. As a result, outsourcing performance can be impaired.

Hence, sufficient communication is of paramount importance for both parties so as to minimize ambiguity during joint development of a two-way expectations (Morrison & Robinson, 1997). Communication practices such as sense-giving and sense-demanding may be useful in realizing that objective (see Vlaar et al, 2008).

Second, our findings using the dual-perspective model provide practical implications as to how PCB can be regulated by governance mechanisms. Agreement on mutual obligations is much more difficult to achieve in real settings. Our results demonstrate that both formal contracts and relational governance are useful in minimizing CPBV. Vendors are thus advised to draft terms of exchange carefully, because based on the formal contract and communications during the contract negotiation process, the client’s project members tend to develop beliefs about the vendor’s obligations, using them to assess the degree of breach. In addition, vendors should note that the client’s project members are also likely to rely on the underlying principles of inter-firm partnerships such as shared norms (e.g., solidarity, information exchange, flexibility; see Goo et al, 2009) to judge what the vendor should do as a partner. Vendors must understand the client project members’ expectations from the perspective of these attributes of inter-firm partnership.

In addition to these implications for vendors, the results of this study provide implications for clients as well. In this study, inter-firm partnership was found to have advantages over the legal contract in curtailing VPBC. This finding suggests that clients bear two points in mind. First, the insignificant impact of ELC on VPBC may be a reflection of the inefficacy of the contracting process. Some clients may need to look back at this process to see if it defined their own expectations more than those of the vendors. If so, they may experience an unsatisfying outsourcing outcome possibly because of an increase in VPBC. Client-centered contracting can in fact result in VPBC, as opportunities may be lacking to improve understanding of the client’s beliefs about their own obligations, and the beliefs about those obligations may remain different between parties. The resulting incongruence may lead to negative consequences during the course of the project, such as impaired collaboration. The insignificant effect of ELC on VPBC observed in this study may thus have important implications for clients in the IS outsourcing context.

We also encourage clients to communicate in the spirit of partnership and think about the attributes of a good partnership when establishing mutual obligations with vendors. According to our results, PART alone is an obvious foundation on which vendor participants’ beliefs about the client’s obligations are formed. Vendors are less likely to perceive a breach when relational exchange is sufficient between the two parties. Vendors may use the attributes of inter-firm partnership, such as benefit/risk sharing and reciprocity, as an important yardstick to judge fulfillment of expected obligations on the part of the client.

## Limitations and future research

This study has some limitations and points to some future research opportunities. First, the limited sample size should be noted. The study design, which required each data point to have three matching responses representing different interest groups, made it difficult to gather complete sets of responses. However, the sample size in this study satisfied the requirement that the sample size should be more than 10 times the largest number of structural paths directed at a particular construct (Chin et al, 2003).

Second, this research was performed on a crosssectional data set. PCB in our data represents the average cognition of individual project participants within their individual psychological contracts, which may evolve during the course of an IS project. A longitudinal research design may be able to provide new insight into the relationships among the study variables, as PCB may change over the project duration (Robinson et al, 1994). For example, using the disconfirmation of expectations theory, future research may examine if the impacts of firm-level governance factors measured at the initial stage of an IS project are reinforced or attenuated by PCB measured in the middle of the project. To examine the possibly cyclical relationship between ELC and PART (Goo et al, 2009, p. 138), a long-term point of view is also required; this may be the subject of a future study.

Third, the relatively weak explanatory power of our model indicates that the expected role of PCB in IOS may be better explained when other factors (e.g., collaboration quality, system quality) are considered, which are expected to affect the end-users’ responses to the outsourced system more directly. Future research that might extend this study and consider those factors may be able to demonstrate a stronger casual relationship in explaining the impact of PCB on IOS.

Finally, we suggest a unit of analysis that may make an interesting future research design. Future research models may consider PCB as a shared unit property or a configural unit property. Theoretical arguments may be made regarding the impact of PCB on individual-, group-, or firm-level outcome variables. For example, if the construct is defined as a configural unit property (i.e., one that captures the pattern of individual characteristics within a unit and is not shared among individual members) rather than a shared unit property (Klein & Kozlowski, 2000), an individual-level outcome variable is more appropriate to include in the model, rather than group- or firmlevel variables. However, if the model treats PCB as a configural unit property, but the researchers want to investigate its impact on group- or firm-level outcomes, then intervening variables must measure the diffusion of PCB in a group or organization. If the construct is defined as a shared property, then the hypothesized effects of PCB on group- or firm-level outcomes will be more convincing.<sup>5</sup>
