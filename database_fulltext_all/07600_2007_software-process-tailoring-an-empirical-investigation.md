# Software Process Tailoring: An Empirical Investigation

PENG XU AND BALASUBRAMANIAM RAMESH

PENG XU is an Assistant Professor at the Department of Management Science and Information Systems of the University of Massachusetts, Boston. She received her Ph.D. in Computer Information Systems from Georgia State University in 2004. Her research areas are software process, software engineering, knowledge management, and process agility. Her work has appeared in several journals and conferences, including Requirements Engineering Journal, Communications of the ACM, Information and Management, and the Workshop on Information Technologies and Systems (WITS) and Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS).

BALASUBRAMANIAM RAMESH is a Professor of Computer Information Systems at Georgia State University. His research interests include supporting complex organizational processes such as requirements management, agile software development, and business process management with decision support and knowledge management systems and methods. His research has appeared in several leading journals, including the IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, MIS Quarterly, Journal of the AIS, Annals of Software Engineering, Communications of the ACM, Requirements Engineering Journal, Decision Support Systems, Information Systems Journal, IEEE Computer, IEEE Software, IEEE Internet Computing, IEEE IT Professional, and IEEE Intelligent Systems. His work has been funded by several leading government and private industry sources such as the National Science Foundation, Defense Advance Projects Agency, Office of Naval Research, Army Research Laboratory, and Accenture and has been incorporated in several computer-aided software engineering tools.

ABSTRACT: A well-defined software process is critical for success in software projects. Software process tailoring refers to the activity of tuning a standardized process to meet the needs of a specific project. We conducted two case studies that address the research question: How is a software process tailored to suit its context? This study identifies process tailoring as a key mechanism to address the challenges faced by a project and develops a model that describes how a process is tailored to resolve these challenges. The model identifies a set of environmental factors, challenges, project goals, process tailoring strategies, and their influences on each other. Specifically, the findings demonstrate the duality of the software process, showing how the project context (i.e., project goals, environmental factors, and challenges) and tailoring decisions dynamically interact with each other and construct the context in which the project is developed and the process is tailored.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: duality of software process, software development, software process, software process tailoring, software project management, software projects.

A SIGNIFICANT PROPORTION OF SOFTWARE PROJECTS fail to deliver on time, do not satisfy quality expectations, and run over budget [50]. One of the major reasons for so many failures is the lack of a disciplined approach to managing software projects [5, 30]. A widely recognized approach for avoiding failures and increasing software development productivity and quality is to focus on the quality and appropriateness of software development processes [11, 19, 36]. A software process defines the activities to be performed, interdependence among them, their inputs and outputs, entry and exit criteria for each, and the roles of various stakeholders involved in each [37]. It provides a road map that helps create a timely and high-quality software product.

Constructing a software process for a project is a knowledge-intensive, timeconsuming activity in which success largely depends on practitioners’ prior experience [18]. Building processes from scratch each time is risky and involves high overhead. Therefore, they are often created by adapting existing processes and standards. Several software process standards or reference models such as ISO/IEC 12207 [48], IEEE/EIA 12207 [22], and Rational Unified Process<sup>®</sup> (RUP<sup>®</sup>) [23] provide excellent foundation for process construction. These models specify the phases (e.g., requirements gathering, system analysis and design, implementation, deployment, etc.) in a software development process, the activities (e.g., modeling business processes, identifying user requirements, designing user interfaces, etc.) that need to be performed in each phase, the sequences of the phases and activities, the roles of various stakeholders (e.g., system analysts, system architect, etc.) involved in each phase and activity, and the artifacts (e.g., business model, use case model, etc.) used or produced by the activities in each phase. Although process reference models are based on industry best practices, they cannot be applied directly without significant tailoring. Process tailoring refers to the activity of “adjusting the definitions and/or particularizing the terms of general description to derive a description applicable to an alternate (less general) environment” [14, p. 3]. In process tailoring, the formality, frequency, granularity, scope of process elements such as activities, documents, artifacts, roles, phases, and so on may be changed. For example, process tailoring may involve modifying stakeholder roles, removing artifacts, using less formal artifacts, and dropping an activity. Each software development project is unique in terms of its business domain, customer requirements, technology, and so on. Adjustments to standard software processes are necessary to make them suitable for the specific project environment [4]. This view is supported by organizational contingency theory that suggests that no business process is equally effective under all conditions, and that each organization needs to adjust its structure and processes to suit its internal and external environments [53].

A detailed review of process standards and current literature, however, suggests that the current understanding of how a software process is tailored and how various environmental factors (i.e., the factors that characterize a project and its environment such as project size, team size, application type, stakeholders involved in the project, etc.) affect process tailoring is very limited. Standards and reference models such as ISO/IEC 12207, IEEE/EIA 12207, and RUP<sup>®</sup> only provide tailoring guidelines that can be used to perform first-level tailoring—that is, tailoring the standards for systems in diverse domains such as aviation, nuclear power generation, medical informatics, and defense. However, no detailed guidelines on second-level tailoring—that is, tailoring the standards for a specific project—are provided in these standards.

Prior research has identified various organizational, project, team, and personnelrelated factors that impact software development processes [1, 3, 25, 35]. However, there are several issues that have not received much attention in this emerging literature. First, although most studies have focused on the various factors that characterize a project environment, research on how these factors shape software processes is very limited. Second, although the use of high-level project management strategies (e.g., coordination strategy [1, 35], the role of formal planning and internal integration [3], and decision-making structure [25], etc.) have been investigated, strategies that may be used to tailor software processes have not been studied in detail. Third, current literature also lacks a systematic framework that can be used to understand and explain the process of software process tailoring.

In summary, though the importance of process tailoring is well recognized, current literature and industry standards fail to adequately address the important research question: How is a software process tailored to suit its context? Our research develops a model that addresses this question using case studies in two organizations.

## Related Research

## Standard Software Processes

PROCESS REFERENCE MODELS AND GUIDELINES have been developed to describe software development processes at varying levels of details. For example, several software life cycle models such as the waterfall model, the evolutionary model, and the spiral model have been developed to organize software production activities [5, 44]. The life cycle models define phases such as requirements analysis, systems design, implementation, testing, and deployment. The life cycle models also define the dependencies between the phases, and the criteria for starting or ending a phase.

The life cycle models are too coarse-grained to be of help to practitioners in constructing or adopting a process for a specific project. This shortcoming motivated the development of process standards or reference models such as the ISO/IEC 12207 [48], IEEE/EIA 12207 [22], and RUP<sup>®</sup> [23]. These complement the life cycle models by specifying roles, responsibilities, tasks, activities, and so on. For example, ISO/ IEC 12207 and IEEE/EIA 12207, which share the same process model but provide different guidelines, are composed of three hierarchies consisting of processes, activities, and tasks. These models include primary, supporting, and organizational processes [47]. The primary processes serve the key parties involved in the acquisition, supply, development, operation, and maintenance of software; the supporting processes include document management, configuration management, quality assurance, verification, validation, joint review, audit, and problem resolution; and the organizational processes help in establishing, controlling, and improving processes by defining the processes for management, infrastructure, improvement, and training. RUP<sup>®</sup> [23], a popular commercial process model, adopts an iterative life cycle approach. RUP<sup>®</sup> consists of four phases—inception, elaboration, construction, and transition. Each phase includes disciplines such as requirements gathering, analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, project management, and configuration management with varying levels of emphasis. For example, in the inception phase of a project, though the team may need to gather requirements, analyze and design the system, and even implement a small part of the system, the focus of the effort is on requirements gathering. In the elaboration phase, even though the team may still need to finish requirements gathering, it should focus much of the effort on design. In the construction phase, the team will continue to finalize requirements and system design, but should mainly concentrate on the implementation of the system. The tasks and activities in each phase are described by workflow diagrams and activity diagrams. RUP<sup>®</sup> follows an iterative development process in which each phase can consist of multiple iterations.

The software process standards explicitly recognize that they need to be tailored before use in any specific project. ISO/IEC 12207 [48], IEEE/EIA 12207 [22], and RUP<sup>®</sup> [23] include sections dedicated to explaining the importance of process tailoring and encourage users to document their tailoring activities.

## Process Tailoring

The tailored objects in the context of software process include activities, work products, artifacts, roles, and so on. Process tailoring entails establishing a relationship between different levels of process definitions or between process requirements and process definitions. In process tailoring, the way in which an object is altered can be specified by its attributes such as [14]

• formality: the degree of attention to formal rules, procedures, and standards with which various process objects such as activities and artifacts are performed,

• frequency: the number of times activities are performed,

• granularity: the level of detail with which process objects are defined, and

• scope: the area that software processes address.

Organizations continually adapt development approaches to their environment; methods and processes must be necessarily unique and distinct for each information requirement that confronts them [4, 6]. Practitioners need to evaluate the specific needs and requirements of the project and tailor processes accordingly [23].

Case studies on process tailoring (such as tailoring ISO/IEC 12207 for instructional software development in a small software development organization [12] and maintenance [43]) suggest that it is a challenging activity that involves intensive knowledge about the appropriateness of different processes in different contexts. These case studies, as well as the recommendations included in the standards themselves, highlight that the guidance provided by standard process models is far from sufficient to support process tailoring. A review of literature on software project management (discussed below) also suggests the need for further research to systematically understand software process tailoring.

## Software Project Management

Contingency theory suggests that business processes and strategies are contingent on a business’s internal and external environment and that a clear understanding of the factors that characterize the organizational environment and their influence on business processes is critical for achieving organizational goals [53]. Software processes need to be customized to different types of products and business strategies [49]. To tailor a process for a project, it is essential to understand how various environmental factors affect the application of the process [6]. These factors include the following: characteristics of the customers, development team, technology, and products, as well as the business conditions in which the products will operate. Studies in software project and process management also identify a set of risk factors that can impact management strategies. Table 1 summarizes this literature stream by identifying various factors that influence software development and the recommended software process strategies (such as process goals and the specific software process activities or tasks that can be used to meet these goals).

However, much of this literature has focused on high-level project management goals (such as obtaining user participation, using formal planning tools, adopting evolutionary approaches, and encouraging integrations within teams), rather than on providing detailed guidelines on specific process strategies that may be adopted to achieve these goals. Also, these studies mainly concentrate on a few factors or risks, but fail to provide a comprehensive theoretical framework that can be used to identify process tailoring strategies that can be used under the wide variety of contexts faced by projects that are executed in dynamic environments.

## Amethodical View of Software Development

Current standard process models and much of the current literature on software project management and software processes are based on the methodical view of software development. The following assumptions are typical in this view [20, 21, 54]:

• software development is a well-managed process,

• the factors that affect a process can be predicted,

• the strategies for managing and tailoring the process can be determined at early stages of development,

• a software process is repeatable,

• predetermined processes need to be followed to control the progress of a project, and

• the predetermined goals of a project can be achieved by a tailored process.

Many process models described in the literature adopt this perspective [21]. Many contingency approaches described in the literature also adopt this methodical view by assuming that the factors that affect a project and strategies used in process tailoring remain the same throughout the project [1, 3, 35]. The methodical view emphasizes that software development should follow a disciplined approach and it is necessary to plan and stabilize software processes as early as possible.

Recent research on the amethodical view of software development challenges the methodical view and suggests that software development processes are far more dynamic; accidents that drive software development sometimes are usually unpredictable, therefore, software development is a random, opportunistic process that needs to be adapted and tailored constantly during development [54]. The amethodical view also suggests that “information systems development is pasted together ‘on-the-fly,’ in a manner that is interactive and negotiated with the organizational actors and the rest of the environment of the systems development project” [54, p. 70].

Although the amethodical view challenges the dominant methodical view, little empirical research has been conducted to examine how software processes are managed and tailored during software development. Our review of current literature on software process and project management highlights the lack of a model to facilitate the understanding of how software processes evolve in dynamic project environments. Our research seeks to address this research gap.

## Research Methodology

BECAUSE THE FOCUS OF OUR RESEARCH IS TO EXAMINE how a software process is tailored for a project, and as our approach is exploratory rather than confirmatory, we use a case study design [58]. The unit of analysis is the project. Our study seeks to develop a theoretical model that can explain software process tailoring from these case studies [13]. We adopted the grounded theory approach [51, 52] to collect and analyze our data.

Seaman [46] argues that the blend of technical and human behavioral aspects of software engineering lends itself to qualitative study. Use of qualitative methods enables a researcher to delve into the complexity of software engineering problems, obtaining richer and more informative conclusions. The case study approach has been used to study different information technology (IT)-related phenomena such as case tool adoption [39], IT outsourcing [28], and IT project failure [33].

The lack of a systematic framework to guide software process tailoring motivates the use of the case study approach to inductively develop such a model. Successful process tailoring activities need to take into account various factors and interactions among them that are not well understood. As a relative “uncharted land” [15], it is appropriate to adopt the case study approach to investigate software process tailoring within real-life contexts [58].

## Site Selection

Our research questions guided the choice of the case study sites. Using theoretical sampling [41], we selected two organizations that used tailored versions of standard software processes for their projects. The first site is an e-business systems development division in a public organization (referred to as EBIZCO).<sup>1</sup> The second site (referred to as UTILCO) is a large information systems (IS) development organization in a utility company. The study sites provided access to various stakeholders who had rich experiences in software process tailoring. These stakeholders had both positive and negative experiences with process tailoring, which provided rich data for the development of our model.

To strengthen the generalizability of our findings and get access to sufficient data, we used a replication strategy [58]. The purpose of multiple cases is to replicate the findings across cases [13, 32, 58]. These two sites are similar in the use of tailored software processes in their projects. Both had developed standard software process models and had instituted relatively formal procedures for software process tailoring. They develop software systems of varying levels of complexity (small, medium, and large projects) for their clients, and face similar pressures to tailor their processes to suit the evolving needs of their projects. Multiple cases offer deeper understanding of software process tailoring and improve the confidence in our findings [32]. Such a sampling strategy has been successfully used in a wide variety of contexts in IS research [8, 26, 27].

## Data Collection

Qualitative data were collected from the two sites progressively via on-site interviews and the review of documentation. Data collection and analysis were conducted in an iterative fashion, as is commonly done with the use of a grounded theory approach [51, 52]. Data collection was done over a period of two years.

We discussed our research goals with the key contact persons who were senior managers in the two organizations. They helped identify participants who were intensively involved in software process tailoring. We conducted semistructured interviews with these participants and sought their help in identifying additional participants. When necessary, we interviewed each participant two or three times to seek clarifications and obtain additional insights. Data collection was concluded when theoretical saturation was reached during data analysis.

In EBIZCO, 11 participants were interviewed. The interviews focused on a recent project that tailored the standard process, RUP<sup>®</sup>. Each participant had received formal training in RUP<sup>®</sup> and was experienced in creating tailored versions of it. Further, each participant had participated in software process tailoring efforts in the IEP project, which is the focal project in this study, as well as other projects. They had an average of 12 years of work experience in the IT field. Because different roles of stakeholders in a software development process may have different perspectives on software process tailoring, we specifically included participants who represented these varied perspectives. Table 2 provides details on the participants.

The second case study was conducted in UTILCO. We followed the same data collection procedure that was used in EBIZCO. The interviews mainly focused on a recently completed project. Semistructured interviews were conducted with the goal to validate the concepts and relationships derived from the analysis of the data collected at EBIZCO and to discover any additional concepts and relationships. Eleven subjects who represent different stakeholders in software development participated in the study. They were trained in the standard process used in UTILCO as well as in tailoring this process. They had participated in the creation of multiple versions of the tailored process that had been successfully used in several projects. They had an average of 14 years of work experience in the IT field (see Table 2 for details).

Data collection focused on eliciting information on the goals that trigger process tailoring, the factors considered in the process, the specific approach taken to identify candidate strategies, and the challenges in specifying and maintaining a software development process that is aligned with dynamic project needs. An interview guide was constructed based on review of the literature (see Table 3 for an excerpt). Each subject participated in one to three interviews. Each interview lasted from one to two hours. A total of 43 interviews were conducted. All of the interviews were taperecorded and transcribed.

Further, secondary data in the form of process documentation from both the sites helped develop a comprehensive understanding of the projects studied. This information included a review of the standard processes, tailored versions of the processes and supporting documentation, as well as training materials.

As is common in grounded theory method, data collection was tightly interwoven with data analysis. Indeed, both activities occurred simultaneously. As interviews were conducted, the transcripts were analyzed. The preliminary results guided the selection of additional participants and helped refine the interview questions [52]. New data were used to refine or modify prior concepts and guide the next round of data collection. Data collection (using interviews as well as observations) and coding were done until “theoretical saturation” was reached, when additional data did not add to the concepts and categories developed [13].

## Data Analysis

Transcripts of interviews constitute the primary data in this study. The Atlas-TI<sup>®</sup> tool was used to organize and code the data. We first conducted within-case analysis for each of the cases and then did cross-case analysis to compare the cases. For withincase analysis, the data analysis focused on identifying key concepts, categories, and relationships for each investigated project. Initially, two researchers separately analyzed the qualitative data from the two sites. Then the coding schema was debated and modified until the two researchers reached agreement.

Following the coding procedure for grounded theory recommended by Strauss [51], the data analysis on EBIZCO started with open coding where the concepts and events were labeled. Concepts that represent the factors that influence process tailoring, strategies used in tailoring a process, and actions taken in tailoring were identified and labeled. Based on similarities and differences among the concepts, labels were modified, revised, and merged to better represent the underlying meaning. These concepts were further validated or clarified by data from subsequent interviews. New data sometimes suggested new coding schema, which led to new iteration of open coding.

After several iterations of open coding, axial coding was conducted in which the concepts were grouped into categories and subcategories and the relationships among them were identified. Open and axial coding were done iteratively. Finally, five categories—environmental factors, challenges, project goals, tailoring strategy, and tailorable process elements—were identified as central to the phenomenon. Axial coding generated a set of categories, associated concepts, and relationships.

The concepts and categories yielded from the first site guided the data analysis effort in UTILCO, the second site, allowing “the process of data collection, coding, and analysis to be more targeted” [39, p. 314]. The data from UTILCO were compared with those in EBIZCO and sorted into the concepts and categories developed for EBIZCO. At the same time, the researchers also actively searched for any new concepts and relationships that emerged from this site.

The final stage in the process involved selective coding in which a model that describes how a process is tailored (presented in the next section) was constructed. In this stage, we examined the categories of the concepts and the links among them. Each tailoring practice was revisited and compared to discover the relationships and the common themes. Cross-case analysis was conducted by comparing and contrasting the data and the models from the case studies. The findings from the two cases conformed to each other. The theoretical concepts and relationships were discovered in both of the cases and were included in the final model presented in the next section.

Concepts identified are validated by their presence in several interviews. However, no attempt is made to statistically evaluate the strength of a concept [51]. As long as the concept is not contradicted by the data, it is considered to be valid. As Strauss and Corbin [51] suggest, theoretical relevance of concepts is indicated by their repeated presence or notable absence when comparing incidents. Although the findings from such analyses using grounded theory method are detailed and particularistic, they can be used to develop a general explanation of the results [13, 39] through analytic generalization [58].

The research model, which includes project goals, environmental factors, challenges, tailoring strategies, and their relationships, was presented to key informants in both the sites. On the basis of their feedback, minor adjustments were made to the terminology used in the model.

## Research Results

## Case Study Site 1: EBIZCO

EBIZCO IS RESPONSIBLE FOR SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT, system maintenance, and technical and methodology support for over 60 agencies in the public sector. Two years before our study, EBIZCO introduced a standard process (RUP<sup>®</sup>) that is widely used in IS practice. The adoption of a formal process (i.e., RUP<sup>®</sup>) was a part of EBIZCO’s ongoing effort to improve its systems development process capabilities. Before then, each project team developed its own ad hoc process. This approach not only involved unnecessary overhead but also resulted in a chaotic environment in which the development processes across projects varied widely. To quickly implement the standard process in the organization, EBIZCO offered its employees several training sessions on RUP<sup>®</sup>. In addition, EBIZCO took several other measures to ensure the successful adoption of a standard process. First, it hired several new team members who were experts on RUP<sup>®</sup>. A process engineer with extensive experience with tailoring RUP<sup>®</sup> in several other organizations was hired to serve as a process tailoring “master” to guide project teams. Second, a consulting company was hired to facilitate software process tailoring in a project that used a tailored version of RUP<sup>®</sup>. Encouraged by the successful experience in this project, several new projects adopted a tailored version of RUP<sup>®</sup>. In fact, EBIZCO also developed a customized version of RUP<sup>®</sup> that can be used by teams as a baseline. Our study mainly focused on a large project (called IEP). Although it experienced some difficulties, especially at the beginning, the project was considered successful by the organization. Valuable lessons were learned from both positive and negative experiences gained during the use of a tailored process in this project.

## IEP Project

A major goal of the IEP project was to develop an interenterprise portal that provided the customers a uniform experience when dealing with multiple agencies. The introduction of a standardized process (i.e., RUP<sup>®</sup>) in the organization was also an additional goal of the software development organization. Before the IEP project, different agencies developed and maintained their own systems, which were isolated from one another. Because there was no single point of entry to access various services, it was difficult for users to access these services. The IEP project created the infrastructure that connected the various existing systems and new applications. It also provided a single point of entry to online services provided by various agencies. Thus, the project involved both application and infrastructure development. The first release of the software was delivered in one year, and involved a team of about 50 persons. The first phase of the IEP project focused on the development of a core interenterprise infrastructure that created a general interoperable platform for different agency services. The second phase developed an application that demonstrated and leveraged the new capabilities provided by this infrastructure. The second phase involved about 20 persons and lasted four months. In both phases, tailored versions of RUP<sup>®</sup> were used. Because the goal of our research is the development of a model to understand and explain process tailoring, the discussion below focuses on only details related to process tailoring in the IEP project. Appendix A provides specific examples of the software process tailoring done in this project. Specifically, we describe how the artifacts produced, activities performed, and roles played by various stakeholders are affected by process tailoring decisions. For each category, we provide several examples of tailoring decisions (such as adopting, changing, dropping, and replacing artifacts or their representation; adopting, changing, and dropping activities, or their inputs/outputs; redefining and adding roles of stakeholders involved).

## Phase I

Setting Project Goals and Initial Process Tailoring. The initial tailoring effort was guided by the goals of the IEP project. The project team was charged with creating the necessary deliverables and also introducing a tailored version of a standardized process within the organization (“both the delivery of a high-quality product and the introduction of the new process—a tailored RUP<sup>®</sup>”).<sup>2</sup> A process engineer in EBIZCO acknowledges the strong influence of these goals in process tailoring decisions as follows: “If you don’t start with a goal, you can’t keep track of where you going, you don’t know when you got there. It shows what it is [that] you are trying to accomplish. . . . It also helps you negotiate [with stakeholders].”

After a careful examination of the available resources, managers at EBIZCO realized that meeting both the product and process goals would be very challenging. They recognized that the project team had to not only manage a technically challenging project but also implement a new process. After extensive discussions, they decided to provide brief, mandatory training on tailoring RUP<sup>®</sup> to all the members of the development team, and also hire a consulting company that had extensive experience with both infrastructure development and process tailoring to help the team.

The project teams decided to follow RUP<sup>®</sup> as closely as possible and adopt the four development phases proposed by RUP<sup>®</sup> (inception, elaboration, construction, and transition phases). However, due to time pressure, instead of using multiple itera tions in each phase as proposed by RUP<sup>®</sup>, they decided to use only a single iteration in each phase. The project manager acknowledged that this was a difficult, but the only viable, decision, given the resource constraints. “We kept asking ourselves the question—what can we accomplish. We have to make sure to meet the budget. . . . We have to . . . meet the deadline. . . . Ideally, we probably should use multiple iterations [for some phases], but we have deadline.” As the project moved forward, the development team faced or anticipated a number of challenges. They used process tailoring as a way to address these challenges.

Challenge I: Requirements Management Challenges. The IEP project was initially driven by technology, rather than by business needs. The vision was to integrate the diverse set of systems that were used by the agencies to provide services. However, when the project was initiated, the client groups were not clearly identified. As a result, it was initially difficult for the project personnel to clearly understand the project requirements. Different project team members worked with different assumptions about critical project requirements, and they struggled hard to achieve consensus on the scope and specific requirements for the system. A project manager explained this problem as follows: “The problem was that . . . it was a technology-driven project. We really didn’t have customers. . . . No agencies gave us requirements. . . . We didn’t know exactly what we should build.”

The absence of a clearly articulated scope and vision for the project also contributed to ambiguous and unstable requirements. The inception phase, during which the project scope and requirements were finalized, grew from the original estimate of two months to four months. Meetings were held almost every day to discuss the scope of the project and specify detailed requirements.

Process Tailoring to Address Requirements Management Challenges. Recognizing the challenges posed by ambiguous and unstable requirements, the project team decided to tailor the process in several ways. First, the team decided to use more formal documents (e.g., vision, requirements attributes, analysis model, software architecture, etc.) in the inception and early elaboration phases so that these could be reexamined when changes were requested later. The team also decided to create a record of critical design decisions made during development so that the evolution of the system could be tracked and managed. A designer commented on these efforts as follows: “There were a lot of changes coming. We tried to identify all the possible changes that could come. A lot of things were put on design [models] based on the features of the project. Since we expected many changes, the design documents had more details.”

The project team also considered the possibility of using shorter, but a larger number of, iterations in which clearly defined requirements could be developed. “If we could break the huge project into smaller [pieces], I guess we could better understand those requirements, because you are more narrowly focused on one thing at a time.”

However, the project team decided not to follow this strategy due to resource challenges. Incorporating more iterations was not acceptable because the project was already behind schedule.

Challenge II: Resource Challenges. As a result of the challenges faced with requirements, the inception phase took a lot more time and personnel than initially planned. Due to the ill-defined and evolving requirements, the design team had to spend more effort in specifying design models. This situation was a cause for serious concerns because the project was already behind schedule and had no budget to recruit more team members.

Process Tailoring to Address the Resource Challenges. In order to deliver the system on time and within budget, instead of closely following RUP<sup>®</sup> as planned at the beginning, the team had to significantly alter the process for the construction and deployment phases. First, the team decided to use a less formal process when compared to the inception and elaboration phases. Several formal documents, such as the iteration assessment and the deployment plan, were eliminated. Also, instead of elaborate descriptions, much simpler templates were used for some of the key documents, such as iteration plans and test plans. Second, numerous activities were dropped or simplified. For example, certain test steps were dropped—test details were not defined and recorded, no effort was made to define and structure test plans before the tests started, and the testing strategy was not carefully evaluated. Instead of using formal test summary reports, test results were documented informally. Third, instead of using formal documents, the team used mostly face-to-face communication whenever feasible. A test engineer recalled this dramatic change in the process as follows: “The process was just evolving . . . they were trying to follow RUP<sup>®</sup> . . . they insisted on RUP<sup>®</sup>. . . . At the beginning, there was a [formal] process. But once moving to construction phase, I don’t think they followed any formal process . . . running out of time, you know.”

To address the resource challenges, the product goals were also dramatically changed. With the approaching deadline for the completion of the project, and the political pressure to deliver key functionalities on time, the scope of the project was narrowed down. Some functionalities that were originally planned in the middleware layer were dropped from the release.

Challenge III: Communication Challenges. During the first phase, a consulting team worked on-site with EBIZCO teams. The development team had about 50 members who were collocated. This provided easy access and face-to-face interaction with the various stakeholders involved in the development. However, the large size of the project and the project team, as well as its long duration, posed challenges to effective communication. Because the team included several new hires and customers from various client agencies and consultants, lack of trust and low team cohesion were obvious at the beginning. Members were reluctant to actively share information, ideas, and opinions. The team also faced the difficulties in sharing relevant project information with a large number of participants, often resulting in miscommunication and misunderstanding. The team also faced communication challenges due to its long duration. Some team members predicted that the team might lose track of critical decisions and other information over time. “After six months of working on the project, I myself might have forgotten what I have done,” exclaimed a designer.

Process Tailoring to Address the Communication Challenges. The communication challenges described above led to several tailoring decisions, including the use of frequent meetings among team members and adopting a more formal, detailed format for deliverables such as meeting minutes, a vision statement, and an architectural proofof-concept document. Instead of relying on face-to-face communication, documents became an effective way to convey ideas and keep track of decisions. As a project manager said: “If you have too many people on the team, you can’t keep conversation, so you need more formal documents.”

Several of the documents that were created during the inception phase were discarded in the later stages of the project due to the time pressure. Also, as a result of the trust that the team members had established among themselves and with the customers, they were more agreeable to using informal face-to-face communication.

Challenge IV: Technical Challenges. The first phase of the project included both application and infrastructure development. Even though the project team was experienced in application development, it lacked experience in the design of the architecture for the infrastructure. Although multiple iterations could be used to iteratively build up the required skills and expertise, the nature of the infrastructure project that involved telecommunications, hardware and data centers made it very difficult to “break down [the project into] very small chunks for multiple iterations . . . than, say, doing a software application.” Also, the project involved the use of new tools (such as a testing workbench) with which EBIZCO did not have much experience.

Process Tailoring to Address the Technical Challenges. In anticipation of these technical challenges in design and implementation tasks, the project manager decided to hire a consulting team that had expertise with infrastructure design and implementation as well as the new tools. During the design of the infrastructure, the consulting team also developed new design templates that were more appropriate than the RUP<sup>®</sup> templates.

The complex nature of the project and schedule pressure during the construction phase made it difficult to adopt multiple iterations as recommended by RUP<sup>®</sup>. Therefore, the process was tailored to include only one iteration. The availability of additional resources and help from external experts helped address technical challenges and implement the tailored process.

## Phase II

Setting Project Goals and Initial Process Tailoring. The second phase of the project involved the development of an application that supported the renewal of a service provided by a client organization. This application also helped highlight the capabilities of the interenterprise infrastructure. This phase involved multiple internal divisions of EBIZCO such as a data center, telecommunication division, and development division, as well as the consulting team. Encouraged by the valuable lessons on process tailoring learned from the first phase, EBIZCO managers decided to continue to tailor their process during the second phase. Although, technically, it was the second phase of the project, the majority of participants within EBIZCO actually considered this development as a separate project because it went through the full development life cycle. Even though this project was relatively smoother when compared to the first phase and the product was released on time, it also faced several challenges that necessitated several process tailoring decisions.

Challenge I: Requirements Management Challenges. As a result of the challenges faced in the first phase, an elaborate inception phase was planned. During this phase, business needs and the vision for the application were specified in detail. These helped in the process of requirements analysis. However, a primary challenge faced in this phase was the inability of some customers to articulate their requirements in detail.

Process tailoring to address the requirements management challenges. To address this new challenge, the project team tailored the process to include “frequent meetings with customers” and “several short iterations during the requirements analysis phase.” Also, the roles of the analysts were redefined to allow them to play a more active role in defining the requirements in consultation with client representatives. An analyst recalled this strategy as follows: “[Sometimes] the client couldn’t [explain] exactly what they wanted. Our analysts had to guide them through the steps . . . like ‘ok you may want this, you may want that’ and they would say yes or no.”

Challenge II: Political Challenges. Initially, the project did not enjoy the support of senior management. Later, the project attracted a lot of attention within EBIZCO, as it was viewed as a pilot project that could demonstrate the feasibility of several future efforts. During the project, political considerations played a major role in several critical decisions. When the development team was still discussing the scope and the deliverables of the project with the client, a deadline was externally imposed by the top management so that funding for the project could be secured from the state legislature. The development team had only four months to complete the project and deliver the application. With this tight schedule, the project managers realized that they could not meet the original product and process goals.

Process Tailoring to Address Political Challenges. Only minimally required documents such as use case models, design models, and test cases were used and all other documents were dropped. The templates of these adopted documents were dramatically simplified to provide only minimal information required by the stakeholders. Also, several planned meetings were dropped. Support activities such as design and requirement reviews were curtailed as well. All of these efforts were focused on meeting the politically imposed deadline: “so it became a less matter of how we created this cutting-edge [technology] and used the process. The goal became how we met the deadline, get something out there before the deadline and continued the funding,” said a project manager. A designer reflected upon what happened: “I think people just threw their up hands and said, ‘let’s cut the lines of communication; let’s cut the involvement in meetings; just focus on our core team that is able to deliver the core functionality.’” The product goals were changed to include only critical functionality that could be delivered by the imposed deadline. Several noncritical functionalities were dropped from the release, but the system was released on time.

## Case Study Site 2: UTILCO

UTILCO is a very large IS development organization that employs over 2,000 IT professionals. The organization is responsible for the development and maintenance of software for a large utility company. The organization has developed a customized software process which was derived from a standard process used by a major consulting company.

Three years before our study, UTILCO embarked on a process improvement program with the objective of improving its processes on the Software Engineering Institute’s Capability Maturity Model for Software (SW-CMM) scale. As a part of the effort, the organization decided to adopt standardized processes across the organization. It engaged a major consulting company to help define and implement a process framework. The consulting company developed a process standard for the organization. However, the senior managers recognized that the wide variation in the nature of the systems that were being developed (ranging from systems that control and monitor services provided to millions of customers to Web-based front-end applications that provided fast and easy access to data) required customized processes. Therefore, project managers were authorized to tailor the standardized process to suit the specific needs of the project. Experts from the consulting company helped individual project teams in tailoring the standardized process and provided training on process tailoring. At the time of our study, the organization had successfully completed several projects with this approach and was close to obtaining Level 3 certification in the SW-CMM.

## PUC Project

The study was conducted in a large development group that was engaged in a variety of projects. Many of these projects involved the development of systems that were mandated by the state public utility commission and federal regulations. These mandates and the resulting system requirements were changing frequently. Also, UTILCO was engaged in a major acquisition. Thus, the organization had to develop systems that had to meet not only changing government mandates but also the business needs of the organization, which was operating in a dynamic market. Our study focused on the PUC project, which was designed to produce a variety of tools that supported the exchange of information between UTILCO and other utility providers who were sharing the common physical infrastructure for delivering services to end users.

Setting Project Goals and Initial Process Tailoring. Operating in a rapidly changing market and regulatory environment, UTILCO had to tailor its processes as the project needs evolved during development. In addition, UTILCO’s software process improvement initiative, which had the objective of enforcing a process discipline across projects, produced a process standard. Meeting the goals of the process improvement initiative was considered important by the top management of UTILCO.

The development process was adjusted frequently to meet the evolving needs of the PUC project. For example, a project manager mentioned that frequently he had to “relax project goals by dropping functionalities or quality requirements” during some iterations/development cycles because it was impossible to achieve them with the available resources and meet the delivery schedule mandated by the regulatory agencies. In addition, the project manager had to ensure that the process did not deviate too much from the standard process so that the process improvement goals were not sacrificed. A project manager in the PUC project commented that “we need to continuously make sure that the process is . . . compatible with our goal of reaching a maturity certification.”

The PUC project faced several challenges, which are described below.

Challenge 1: Requirements Management Challenges. The requirements for the system were often mandated by the public utilities commission in the state in which the industry had just been deregulated. As the deregulation process was just unfolding, system requirements evolved over the duration of the PUC project. For example, every month a regulatory agency issued new directives that specified how UTILCO should support the needs of other utility companies that were sharing the common utility network maintained by UTILCO. Even though some of these requirements were not well developed and fully articulated, the PUC project was required to satisfy them to the extent possible. A project manager lamented that “we are working on the third iteration of this requirement. Each time the commission gives us somewhat contradicting requirements. We can’t complain to them because it is in the long-term interest of the company to keep the commissioners happy—even if some of the mandates are not logical. We won’t know for sure whether what we build now will stay stable in future iterations.”

The industry in which UTILCO was operating was also undergoing major changes in the form of mergers and acquisitions. In fact, the PUC project was significantly affected by the acquisition of a competitor by UTILCO. The project had to integrate the system being developed with systems used in the acquired entity. Because the acquired entity was operating in an unstable market, its systems were also evolving. Therefore, the integration efforts in the PUC project proved very challenging.

Process Tailoring to Address Requirements Management Challenges. To deal with the uncertainty in the requirements, the project team decided to adopt an evolutionary, iterative life cycle approach. Rather than focusing on the robust implementation of all the known requirements, the team decided to deliver the functionalities in successively refined versions. The project team also decided to add a new role that was not defined in the standard process: a product manager, acting as a surrogate customer, was tasked to interpret the government regulations and provide detailed requirements specifications. This helped shield the developers from dealing with the ambiguities in the requirements. “We don’t have to constantly argue over how to interpret the rules and regulations that come from the commission. We can rely on the product manager who is more familiar with that environment and jargon to translate it for us. He also understands the problems and concerns of the development teams and can hopefully represent them to the commission when they consider making more changes.” Encouraged by the success of this approach, the PUC project also assigned a product manager to manage the requirements concerned with the integration of the PUC system with the systems used by the acquired company. This surrogate manager was the “go-between the teams. All requests came through her and we channeled all the unresolved issues through her so that their system could be modified to integrate better with our system.” Further, “we also scheduled weekly meetings to iron out the major concerns so that both the projects had a clear definition of the requirements and work products. We also used these meetings to develop schedules for implementing the critical features.”

UTILCO also instituted a more formal process to deal with the numerous and frequent changes to requirements. “Adopting a formal process to control requirements changes” and “using formal documents to track requirements and decisions and creating intermediate documentation” were found to be helpful in UTILCO.

Challenge II: Resource Challenges. The PUC project had to constantly deal with the lack of “schedule, budget, personnel, and tools that are needed to meet the current goals.” A major challenge to the PUC project was the inability to develop a clear understanding of the scope of the project because of the changing landscape of regulations imposed by external agencies. The PUC project managers were often required to assess and adjust resources allocated to the project as well as project goals to strike a manageable balance between the two. A project manager explained the problem as follows: “my project requirements are often mandated; the delivery scheduled is fixed to meet statutory regulations. I have only two options to meet both. Either get additional resources to meet the goals or change the process to work with what I have.”

Process Tailoring to Address Resource Challenges. A project manager elaborated on some of the strategies that were used to tailor his project to address resource challenges. “We just can’t bring in more people, even if the senior managers are willing to provide them. It will be disruptive in the beginning to assimilate all the new personnel and it may in fact end up delaying my project. Instead, I have been able to negotiate with my superiors some flexibility in the way I run individual development cycles. For example, if I am really under a lot of schedule pressure to meet some critical deadlines, we fall back to a ‘fast track’ process. This process has fewer formal deliverables, some quick peer reviews by experienced developers replace a formalized review process, and the formalized QA [quality assurance] process is restricted to critical functionalities.” The project manager also clarified that such process tailoring (in which activities and deliverables were simplified or dropped) could span multiple development cycles. “Whenever we adopted this fast track process, we also made sure that in the next development cycle, we address any concerns about the deliverables that may not have been resolved in the current cycle. For example, we start the QA process quite early to address the inadequate QA in the previous phase. We also updated design documents that were not completed in the previous cycle due to time pressure.”

Challenge III: Communication Challenges. The lack of a readily available customer was a major challenge in the PUC project. Even though the use of a product manager as a surrogate customer was successful to a large extent, there were problems with this approach due to the heavy demands placed on him. “Initially, the customer representative was readily accessible and we could get clarifications on the requirements by informally talking to him. But this changed during the course of the project and the customer representative was no longer easily accessible. This created a lot of misunderstanding and miscommunication between the team and the customer representative about the acceptance tests.”

Other communication challenges faced during the PUC project were due to the turnover of personnel and the need to bring in additional personnel to meet the enlarged scope of the project. The development organizations faced difficulties in assimilating new team members when critical knowledge about the development was lost due to turnover. “This is a serious problem in this large, complex project as well as in projects where there is not much documentation of important deliverables like requirements specifications and designs.”

Process Tailoring to Address the Communication Challenges. Among the strategies used to manage this challenge was the use of frequent meetings to discuss critical aspects of the development. These meetings helped the team members develop a shared understanding of project goals and requirements. Also, the project tailored its documents to suit the needs of different types of stakeholders. “Designers need detailed knowledge to understand where the development is and where it is headed. To help the team and any new hires you may have to bring on board, you need detailed design documents. We created templates for these documents so that everyone understood these clearly. . . . But we had to develop much simpler templates for documents that were provided to the customers. They were more interested in high level descriptions rather than specific details of the implementation,” a design engineer recalled.

Although face-to-face communication was preferred for quick exchanges, the PUC project also developed several templates for commonly used documents. For example, formal templates for documenting acceptance criteria and customer approval were developed.

Challenge IV: Technical Challenges. The introduction of new technologies, tools, or methodology created additional challenges in the PUC project. An example is the introduction of the new process standard for the organization. Because this process deviated significantly from the prior practices in some key process areas such as requirements management, it created a lot of anxiety and resistance in the beginning.

Process Tailoring Strategies to Address Technical Challenges. Due to the introduction of a new process, the PUC project had to “compromise on the functionality delivered in the first three iterations of the project until the team became proficient” in the process. “Our choices were clear. Either deliver the product on time, or learn to use the new process. We decided to go with a compromise. It was important for the team to learn the new process so that we could use it in several other projects without going through the learning curve all over again. The customer understood that and was willing to relax the functionalities that we had agreed to deliver until that time. It took us almost three development cycles to get comfortable with the process,” explains a project a manager.

The PUC project also used another process tailoring strategy to successfully mitigate technical challenges: it used multiple iterations so that these challenges could be tackled iteratively and the development team could slowly “build up the required skills and expertise.”

Challenge V: Political Challenges. Political challenges faced by the PUC project mainly related to unexpected events and decisions made by upper-level management that dramatically affected the direction and execution of the project. These challenges include changing priorities, project drivers, and project goals.

Process Tailoring to Address Political Challenges. The PUC project was susceptible to serious challenges due to “changes to the priority assigned to major requirements and changes to project goals.” The senior mangers, however, recognized that it was difficult for the project team to control or adequately handle this challenge without their support. There was recognition that “clear identification of the business drivers of a project,” “commitment of senior management,” and “agreement on project priority and project goals” could help manage this challenge.

## Influence of Software Process Tailoring on Project Context

Our study shows that a software process is tailored to manage the challenges faced by a project. Our study also suggests that project goals, environmental factors, and the execution of the tailored process dynamically construct the context of the project, which affects process tailoring. Project goals, environmental factors, and challenges affect each other during process tailoring. For example, both in EBIZCO and UTILCO, the challenges such as unstable and evolving requirements and lack of resources forced the team to reevaluate product and process goals. Some functionalities that were originally planned were dropped from the release or development iterations. Also, resource challenges such as schedule pressure and lack of personnel contributed to tailoring the standard process by adopting only the minimally required process steps. While software process tailoring needs to take into account the influences of the context (i.e., challenges, project goals, and environmental factors), the tailored process also influences its context. For example, in EBIZCO, the requirements management challenges forced the team to adopt more formal documents and artifacts in the requirements analysis stage. These tailoring decisions, in turn, changed the available resources and therefore resource challenges. Similarly, communication challenges were mitigated by the incorporation of frequent meetings and additional documents. These decisions helped establish close interpersonal ties among team members and, as a result, reduced communication challenges. Also, in UTILCO, the lack of experience with a new process standard first led to the adoption of multiple iterations with fewer requirements. The resulting process helped build up the required skills and expertise. These observations demonstrate the ongoing interactions between project goals, environmental factors, challenges, and tailoring strategies that construct the dynamic and complex project context.

## A Model of Software Process Tailoring

Process tailoring often starts with the project team setting the goals for the project, which include product goals and process goals. Product goals are concerned with achieving desired levels of quality in the products produced and other related deliverables. Process goals are concerned with improving or changing processes to achieve or maintain a process maturity level. Whereas the former is the most common concern in typical projects, the two organizations studied were also concerned about process goals due to their interest in maintaining and improving process maturity. These goals are initially set to be consistent with both the nature of the products produced and the development process. Project goals specify the criteria with which success is measured. Therefore, satisfying project goals is a primary consideration when tailoring a process. When tailoring a process for a project, decision makers prioritize project goals, allocate resources, and adjust tailoring strategies.

In order to meet project goals, project managers need to assess and often adjust environmental factors that influence the process used (arrow 1a in Figure 1). In fact, this assessment may as well suggest reevaluation or adjustment of the goals initially identified (arrow 1b). Environmental factors, characterizing the project, team, external stakeholders, and the organization, along with project goals, create challenges (both observed and anticipated) (arrows 2a and 3a). Specifically, an assessment of the environmental factors helps in the evaluation of various types of challenges (such as resource, communication, requirements management, political, and technical challenges) faced by the project (arrow 2a). Similarly, project goals are also an important input in the evaluation of challenges associated with the project (arrow 3a). On the other hand, on the basis of the assessment of the challenges involved, project goals may get revised (arrow 3b). In addition, further adjustments to the environmental factors that are under the control of the project team may be necessary to ensure that the project goals can be achieved in the current environment (arrow 2b). This assessment and adjustment of goals, environmental factors, and challenges happen in an iterative fashion. Software processes are tailored to meet project goals and manage project challenges (arrows 4 and 5). The implementation of process tailoring strategies may help the software development team recalibrate project goals (arrow 6) and adjust environmental factors (arrow 7). Further, the execution of the process may, in fact, change the nature and intensity of the challenges faced by the project (arrow 8). The feedback from the execution of tailoring strategies initiates another cycle of tailoring activities. These complex interactions are repeated as the project proceeds through various phases and iterations.

## Software Process Tailoring

A key step in software process tailoring is to evaluate the challenges faced and anticipated by a project. Drawing from the data, we classify the challenges into five categories:

1. Requirements management challenges: Requirements management challenges include the need to deal with ill-defined, unstable, and uncertain requirements.

Customer requirements that result from vaguely defined requirements and vision for the project, limited access to the appropriate customers, and customers who have insufficient knowledge and experience with the business domain or system severely affect the successful execution of a project.

2. Resource challenges: Resource challenges are concerned with the lack of critical resources such as schedule, budget, personnel, and tools that are needed to meet the current goals of a project.

3. Communication challenges: Communication challenges refer to the degree of difficulty in achieving effective communication among the members of the development team and with external stakeholders. Some of the factors that cause this challenge include large project teams in which effective communication is problematic, conflicting needs among diverse or a large number of stakeholders, high turnover among development personnel, insufficient access to critical stakeholders, product complexity, and low cohesion among team members due to diversity of experience and culture.

4. Technical challenges: The introduction of new technologies, tools, or methodology is a common source of technical challenges. The challenge results from the lack of experience of the development team with the tools, technologies, and methodologies.

5. Political challenges: Political challenges are mainly related to unexpected events and decisions made by upper-level management that dramatically affect project’s execution and direction. The major political challenges faced by a development team include changing priority of projects, project drivers, and project goals.

Our empirical study suggests that project goals, environmental factors, and various challenges encountered by a project influence each other and the software process tailoring strategies used. Although it may seem elegant for the factors to map nicely to the challenges in a one-on-one alignment, the real world of software development is too complex to support such a crisp mapping. Various environmental factors that are involved in process tailoring are identified in Appendix B. Similarly, the mapping between process tailoring strategies and challenges is also many-to-many. In fact, each group of strategies, to varying degrees, addresses each of the challenges. ## Theoretical Implications

## Factors Influencing Software Process Tailoring

PRIOR STUDIES HAVE INVESTIGATED various environmental factors and risks and their roles in shaping software process and project management strategies. Table 1 summarizes prior literature. Our study suggests that the factors that influence software process tailoring fall into three groups—project goals, environmental factors, and challenges. Project goals define the expectations of a project; environmental factors such as project size, application type, team size, and so on characterize the environment in which the project is executed; challenges refer to problems currently faced or anticipated by a project. While some prior studies have categorized some environmental factors such as project size as risks (potential losses) [2, 7, 24, 29, 45], we differentiate these two concepts in our study. Although prior studies have suggested that environmental factors affect the software process, our study enhances this literature by identifying the challenges that result from these factors, which in turn, are addressed by process tailoring.

## Duality of Software Process

Our study develops a model that illustrates how a software process is tailored to suit the needs of a project. The field study provides insights into the software process tailoring phenomenon. The results of our study demonstrate the duality of software process, a dynamic interaction between software process and the context faced by projects.

Software processes are tailored to the context of a project. During process tailoring, various actors interpret and assess goals, factors, and challenges and make tailoring decisions. When the tailored process is executed, it changes the context faced by the project. As a result of the execution of the tailored process, resources are consumed and reallocated, the project team gains valuable experience, more information about the project is collected, and some challenges are mitigated while others emerge or become more severe. The new challenges that emerge require new tailoring strategies, leading to a new tailoring cycle.

Thus, the software process is not only the outcome of the project context but also is a critical force that gradually reshapes the context of the project. The software process can be considered as a modifiable artifact through its existence [38]. At the same time, this artifact continually affects its context. We call this reciprocal interaction between software processes and the context of the project the duality of software process, following the concept of duality of technology [38, 40]. Our study shows that neither software process nor the project environment remains fixed due to this duality.

Our empirical study shows that the ongoing, recursively dynamic interaction between the process and the context of a project leads to an emergent process and an emergent environment. Software process is a critical success factor in software development [23]. The dominant methodical view suggests that software development needs to plan and stabilize software processes as early as possible to make software development more predictable and measurable [54]. However, our study shows that the process for a software project is dynamically constructed as the project unfolds. Anticipated and unanticipated events force project teams to constantly scan their environment and evaluate the context of the project. During this process, the challenges faced by the project influence the software process tailoring strategies used. Thus, the tailored process is the product of ongoing negotiations among conflicting demands and challenges. Our study shows that it is essential to continuously monitor and maintain this fit when operating in dynamic contexts. Processes are tuned and refined as projects unfold and the attendant needs evolve. In this sense, software development processes emerge during a project’s life cycle. Thus, our study conforms to the amethodical view of software development, suggesting that software processes are emergent and dynamic.

The amethodical view proposes that a software development process is the product of ongoing negotiation, but this literature does not adequately investigate the emergent nature of the project and its context. Complementing the amethodical view, our study demonstrates that the project along its context is also a product of constant social negotiation and consensus building. In other words, the project and its context are also emergent during development. Prior research suggests that software processes are influenced by environmental factors and risks that are stable (see Table 1 for a summary). However, our study recognizes that the project context cannot be fully defined at the beginning. Due to the duality and emergent nature of software processes, tailoring strategies and the execution of tailored processes will gradually redefine project goals, reallocate resources, and reshape the challenges faced by the project. Therefore, the context of a project is also a product of a continuous process of negotiation and evolution during software development.

## Conclusions

OUR RESEARCH DEVELOPS A THEORETICAL MODEL to understand how a software process is tailored to suit its context. The results provide interesting insights into the role of various project goals, factors, challenges, and their impact on process tailoring.

## Contribution to Theory

In sum, the study contributes to the literature on software process management by providing a systematic model to help understand and guide software process management and process tailoring. By proposing the duality of software process, the model challenges the methodical view and complements the amethodical view of software development. The model identifies a set of environmental factors, challenges, project goals, process tailoring strategies, and their influences on each other. These findings represent a comprehensive view of process tailoring beyond what is reported in the literature. This study identifies process tailoring as a key mechanism to address challenges faced by a project and develops a model that describes how a process is tailored to resolve these challenges. The findings also demonstrate the duality of software process, showing how a project context and tailoring decisions dynamically interact with each other and reshape the context in which the project is executed and the process is tailored.

## Contribution to Practice

Practitioners can use the model (shown in Figure 1) to guide their process tailoring efforts. Our study suggests that both the development process and the environment need to be responsive and flexible. Therefore, practitioners need to continuously monitor and adjust both process tailoring strategies and a project’s environment to achieve success. The findings provide guidance on the environmental factors that may be considered, challenges that need to be assessed, and tailoring strategies that may be used in specific contexts. Table 4, which summarizes the key tailoring strategies that can be used to address various challenges, is expected to be of significant interest to practitioners.

## Limitations and Future Work

This research adopts the grounded theory approach to understand the phenomenon of software process tailoring. Although the study sites and participants are well suited to the purpose of this study, generalizability and completeness of the findings are undermined by the unique context of the study such as limited access to end users, significant technical novelty and complexity faced by the project, and the redefinition of project scope and process steps as the project evolved. More empirical studies are needed to generalize our understanding of the software process and the interrelationships between the software process and the project environment.

The study highlights the boundary-spanning role of stakeholders who help with the integration of knowledge from several domains in software process tailoring. Further investigation of boundary-spanning activities in the context of process tailoring is an interesting topic for future work.

The study participants were experienced in tailoring heavyweight software processes. The increasing popularity of agile software processes suggests the need to examine software process tailoring in this new setting as well. Further research to examine whether agile software processes face similar challenges and whether agile software processes follow different tailoring principles to mitigate these challenges is needed. The examination of the applicability of the model developed in this research to product development processes in general (beyond software development) is another interesting avenue for future research.
