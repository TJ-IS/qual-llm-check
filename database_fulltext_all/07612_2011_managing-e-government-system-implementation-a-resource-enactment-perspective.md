RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Managing e-Government system implementation: a resource enactment perspective

Calvin M.L. Chan<sup>1</sup>, Ray Hackney<sup>2</sup>, Shan L. Pan<sup>3</sup> and Tzu-Chuan Chou<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>SIM University, Singapore; <sup>2</sup>Brunel University, U.K.; <sup>3</sup>National University of Singapore, Singapore; <sup>4</sup>National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, Taiwan

Correspondence: R Hackney, Brunel University, Brunel Business School, Kingston Lane, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, U.K. E-mail: ray.hackney@brunel.ac.uk

## Abstract

The research presents a theoretical and empirical analysis of an e-Government system implementation. The resource-based view and the enactment concept were leveraged as a theoretical sense-making lens to study the system through its planning, development and operation phases. Consequently, a process model of resource enactment was developed to theorize how organizational resources were mobilized for successful implementation. It was found that the environmental climate at each phase gave rise to a particular focal capability. This was developed through the symbiotic enactment of a focal resource in conjunction with other complementary resources. Specifically, knowledge, social and leadership resources were found to be pertinently enacted in developing the focal capabilities. When observed across the phases, such symbiotic enactment of complementary resources followed a co-evolutionary path. The empirical research was conducted through a qualitative case analysis. This research would therefore be of interest to both academics and practitioners as it contributes to cumulative theoretical development and provides practical grounded insights to inform and advance e-Government system implementation.

## Introduction

e-Government may be broadly defined as the use of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) to facilitate government service delivery (UN & ASPA, 2002) by enabling greater customer orientation, efficiency, effectiveness, citizen satisfaction, reduced bureaucracy and national development (West, 2004; Yildiz, 2007; Chan et al., 2008). In the pursuit of these benefits, many countries around the world invested significant public resources to implement various e-Government systems (Grant & Chau, 2005). However, many of these implementations failed to achieve their intended objectives (Heeks, 2006; Baumgarten & Chui, 2009). Scholars also observed a lack of cumulative theoretical development in e-Government research (Heeks & Bailur, 2007). Hence, there is a need for research that not only enhances e-Government systems implementation success, but also adds to the cumulative theoretical development of e-Government research. This substantive investigation aimed to address this need through analysing a successful e-Government system implementation in Singapore.

The resource-based view (RBV) (Barney, 1991, 2001) and the enactment concept (Orlikowski, 2000; Weick, 2001; Weick et al., 2005) were adopted as theoretical sense-making lens (Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Langley, 1999). RBV has often been posited by scholars as an effectual theory for studying ICT enabled organizational enhancements and business improvements (Melville et al., 2004; Peppard & Ward, 2004; Wade & Hulland, 2004; Piccoli & Ives, 2005). However, RBV is an innately static perspective (Barney, 2001; Priem & Butler, 2001). The enactment concept was therefore introduced as an additional theoretical lens to bring in elements of process dynamism and human agency. This corresponded with the dynamic nature of the system implementation process that evolves from one implementation phase to another through the intervention of human agents (Walsham, 1993; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001).

## Theoretical foundations

## RBV: from resources to capabilities

RBV is widely acknowledged to be rooted upon the seminal work of Penrose (1959), who argued that organizations are essentially composed of a collection of organization-specific resources. It is the ability of the organization’s management in combining these resources to exploit market opportunities that determines their performance (Collis & Montgomery, 1995). Resources are the most basic unit of analysis in the production process of any organization (Grant, 1991). They can exist in either tangible or intangible forms (Wernerfelt, 1984; Collis & Montgomery, 1995). A common example of tangible resource is financial resource, while knowledge and social capital are examples of intangible resources (Tanriverdi, 2005; Hitt et al., 2006). Despite the conventional tendency to overlook the importance of intangible resources (Grant, 1991), studies suggested that intangible resources are more likely to generate advantageous outcomes (Carmeli & Tishler, 2004).

While early research on RBV emphasized the possession of strategic resources with certain characteristics in determining organizational performance (Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1986; Diedrickx & Cool, 1989), recent works have increasingly emphasized the need for organizations to develop apposite capabilities (Teece et al., 1997; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Winter, 2003). Indeed, this progression of focus in RBV from strategic resource possession to capability development has advanced the RBV to address the dynamicity of organizational performance. Notably, the focus on capabilities offered an enhanced explanation where improved organizational performance may be experienced by firms that can perform an activity (be it static, dynamic or creative) more effectively than their competitors with otherwise similar resources (Collis, 1994). Capabilities are constantly extended, modified and created in response to changing environmental climate (Teece et al. 1997; Wheeler, 2002). The development of capabilities is analogous to a dynamic longitudinal process (Teece et al., 1997; Santhanam & Hartono, 2003) that allows for a variety of possible strategic options (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Barney, 2001; Bowman, 2001) in accordance with the environmental climate.

## Towards a resource enactment view in e-Government systems implementation

Enactment is a key constituent process of organizing (Weick, 1979, 2001; Weick et al., 2005). The underlying theme is human agency (Weick, 2001; Boudreau & Robey, 2005), emphasizing the action of humans in fulfilling certain emergent outcomes. Orlikowski (2000, p. 425) noted that the notion of enactment conveys the sense of ‘to constitute, actuate, perform’ or ‘to represent in or translate into action’. It embodies ‘the central point that when people act, they bring events and structures into existence and set them in motion’ (Weick, 1988, p. 306). Enactment focuses on social practices as embedded, embodied and materialized aspects of human agency in constituting particular outcomes (Orlikowski & Yates, 2002). As human actions are highly dynamic, the enactment process is neither predictable nor easy to control (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). In essence, enactment can be understood as a set of material and symbolic actions (Smircich & Stubbart, 1985) that transform organizations (Walsham, 1993: Feldman, 2004).

Enactment is coupled with the contextual environment (Weick, 1979, 2001) as human agents act in response to various environmental stimuli (Fountain, 2001; Daneels, 2003). It is through enactment that human agents intrusively construct their environment (Rindova et al., 2004). Thus, both the enactment process and the contextual environment interrelate with each other (Reed, 1997; Pawlowski & Robey, 2004), resulting in a path-dependent and self-limiting process (Daneels, 2003) as depicted in Figure 1.

The development of capability corresponds with the enactment process. The environmental climate renders certain capability to be more salient (Teece et al., 1997) and present them as focal capability. Efforts will hence be directed towards the development of the focal capability. Capabilities are developed through a process that manipulates resources, ‘specifically the processes to integrate, reconfigure, gain, and release resources – to match and even create market change y (and) achieve new resource configurations’ (Wheeler, 2002, p. 127). The use of resources would invariably involve human agents to act upon the resources to generate the focal capability (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). In turn, the actions of the human agents in manipulating the resources give rise to new environmental climate (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000; Winter, 2003; Feldman, 2004). Such a process is termed as resource enactment, where resources are enacted to bring forth a focal capability that matches existing environmental climate, and at the same time, gave rise to new environmental climate.

The enactment concept has also been incorporated into the study of ICT systems in general and e-Government systems implementation in particular. Boudreau & Robey (2005) and Orlikowski (2000) have demonstrated that the enacted usage of ICT systems may not necessarily comply with their original design or intended application. Every ‘engagement with a technology is temporally and contextually provisional, and thus there is, in every use, always the possibility of a different structure being enacted’ (Orlikowski, 2000, p. 412). Furthermore, research that applied the enactment concept to study ICT systems affirmed the role of these systems in enabling organizational transformation and change. The potential of ICT systems to effect change is contingent on how they are enacted by human agents within the organizational context (Walsham, 1993; Orlikowski & Barley, 2001). Such enactment does not necessitate the human agent to exercise total ‘free will’ as they may be ‘transformed into an agent of the socially constructed world of the organization’ (Lee, 1994, p. 151). Furthermore, Boudreau & Robey (2005) demonstrated that such enactment is arbitrated by resources such as knowledge and social capital through social construction.

Fountain (2001) noted that the enactment of ICT systems in government agencies ‘is the result of various cognitive, cultural, structural and political “embeddedness” ’ that reside in the contextual environment. According to her technology enactment framework, institutional changes that occur during e-Government systems implementation is mediated through the enactment of ‘objective’ ICT systems (i.e. IT artefact) in conjunction with institutional arrangements (e.g. organizational forms and bureaucracy networks) inherent in the contextual environment. This framework is widely recognized as a seminal piece among e-Government researchers (Garson, 2003; West, 2004; Yildiz, 2007) as it presents a ‘key arrays of variables together into a single conceptualization that is based on an impressively broad set of classic and contemporary theoretical domains’ (Danziger, 2004, p. 108). In response to critiques that Fountain’s framework is inadequate to address the pertinent presence of political influence (Yildiz, 2007), Cordella & Iannacci (2010) added an emphasis on the salient influence of prevailing e-Government policy in the contextual environment to develop the e-Government enactment framework.

## Research method

This research was conducted as a qualitative case analysis (Eisenhardt, 1989) of an e-Government system implementation in Singapore. Santhanam & Hartono (2003) stressed the importance of selecting recognized leaders when investigating ICT-related capabilities. Singapore has been consistently ranked by different international e-Government league tables to be among the most successful in the world (Rutgers, 2010; Waseda, 2010). Moreover, the specific system in this case has won multiple awards for being an effective technological innovation that brought about strategic improvements. For reason of confidentiality, the identity of the government agency and system studied are, respectively, assigned pseudonyms of Alpha Agency (AA) and e-File System.

## Data collection

Data were collected from three main sources (i.e. via interviews, observations as well as documents and records), allowing data triangulation to be achieved (Yin, 1994). Three groups of people who were involved in the implementation of the e-File System were interviewed. These included officers from AA, system developers from the vendor firm and external users of the e-File System. In particular, the interviewed AA officers form a broad spectrum across the hierarchy of the agency; ranging from the CEO to frontline customer service personnel who frequently interacted with external users. All interviews were recorded with the consent of the interviewees and were subsequently transcribed. A summary of the number and duration of the interviews across the three groups of interviewees is illustrated in Table 1.

In addition, observations on the actual operations of the e-File System were conducted. For instance, observations were made on how external users used the selfservice kiosks located in AA and also how officers used the system. Consequently, the researcher generated a rich contextual understanding of how the e-File System actually operated. This source of data also complemented and affirmed claims made during the interviews. For example, an interviewee highlighted that the e-File System incorporated certain business rules to automate some basic processes. This was confirmed by the observational data and served as valuable insights on the effectiveness of the system.

Moreover, relevant documents and records were collated and classified into two categories. The first category comprised of official documents, which included project documents, plans and minutes of meetings. As these official documents were deemed to be sensitive and protected under the Singaporean legislation, their disclosure and sharing were at the discretion of AA. Official documents that were shared included the project plan, contingency plan and reports from overseas study trips. The second category of documentary data were collated from the mass media and the Internet. Being a public organization, news articles on AA and the e-File System implementation were published in the mass media. Searches for relevant articles and reports in the major local newspapers (e.g. The Straits Times and The Business Times) were undertaken through the Factiva and LexisNexis databases as well as news websites (e.g. Channel News Asia website). This source of case data also provided an appreciation of the general perception of AA and the e-File System implementation.

## Data analysis

An interpretive approach for the data analysis was adopted (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), with the RBV and enactment concept employed as the conceptual sensemaking lens (Walsham, 1995; Lee & Baskerville, 2003). Literatures on e-Government as well as ICT systems implementation were also referenced to inform the analysis. As the implementation of the e-File System was viewed as a process, a temporal bracketing technique (Langley, 1999) was employed to organize the data into three conceptual phases of ‘planning’, ‘developing’ and ‘operating’. This provided descriptive utility where the phases served as, ‘y simply, a way of structuring the description of events’ and may not necessarily have exact theoretical significance (Langley, 1999, p. 703). Notwithstanding, it is worth highlighting that temporal bracketing afforded an understanding of the e-File System implementation as a dynamic process where the context and situation evolved through the phases.

Open and axial coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) approaches were employed to identify the environmental climate, capabilities and resources involved. This gave rise to the development of resource enactment concept, which can be understood as the behaviour and actions of human agents in constituting organizational resources. Selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) was conducted to improve parsimony and consistency. For example, while numerous types of resources were identified during open and axial coding, these were amalgamated into three pertinent generic types, that is

\- knowledge resource (e.g. procedure knowledge/knowhow, declarative knowledge/know-what and transactive memory/know-who);

\- social resource (e.g. culture, organizational practices/ norms, historical legacy, trust, stakeholder relationship and commitment);

\- leadership resource (e.g. strategic support/champion, leadership) through selective coding.

Although these three approaches of analysis are described sequentially here, the entire analysis occurred as a highly iterative process (Walsham, 1993; Strauss & Corbin, 1998).

## Case description

As with many countries in the world, the application of certain licenses in Singapore used to be an unwieldy process that was typified by the need to submit multiple paper documents and waiting in long queues at the counters. At the AA, at least 2 weeks were needed between the submission of the application and the ultimate issuance of the licenses. Furthermore, as every licensee was required to keep their information up-to-date, AA also had to handle submissions for information updates. As such, applications and submissions were submitted using paper forms, there was voluminous amount of ‘back office’ data entry work. These also raised concerns over the validity of information on the database as there may be considerable latency between the time when a licensee submits a request for information update to the time when the backend database actually got updated. Moreover, with the pressure to clear the data entry backlog, additional temporary clerks were recruited to expedite the data entry. Typographical errors were prone to occur, resulting in instances of data discrepancies. At times, such data discrepancies also resulted in disputes between AA and the licensees as both parties accused each other of either submitting or keying-in incorrect data.

## Planning phase

The planning of the e-File System implementation occurred during a period when external users were demanding greater efficiency. An interviewee from AA described the situation:

We were [already] doing our best to shorten processing time, shorten updating time, extending hours, etc. But there is only so much a human being can do y . We were looking at our timelines and time frames, [we’re] already working at our utmost. y [But] when you looked around the world, our standards were [not impressive] y . The people, the [external users] that we were dealing with [were getting] more demanding and they’re not unreasonable demands.

Indeed, it was possible to understand the intensifying demands from the external users as piles of supporting documents had to be submitted and multiple trips had to be made before a license could finally be issued.

At the same time, the Singapore Government was also launching their first e-Government Action Plan (eGAP), which envisioned to delight customers with convenient and easy to use services by leveraging ICT. The five key thrusts of eGAP are illustrated in Table 2. This vision was promoted throughout the entire Singapore Civil Service through various road-shows and seminars. Moreover, the eGAP was strongly advocated by the Head of Civil Service and was embraced by many senior civil servants throughout the Government. This was perceived by AA as an imperative to introduce more radical and innovative solutions that gave rise to the e-File System. AA also engaged consultants as they learned from their previous

In planning for the e-File System, AA also went on overseas study trips to learn from the experience of other governments. Such practice was an established norm in the Singaporean Government when embarking on initiatives that were deemed to be strategic and innovative. Both Anguilla and Australia had each implemented an electronic filing system. Two separate AA teams were sent to learn from these two countries. A member of AA senior management noted:

When we had some ideas [through these studies], we were able to come up with some blueprint as to what we wanted the [e-File] System to be.

An example of how these studies facilitated the planning of the e-File System was the decision to close the counter services. AA came to appreciate that transformational performance improvement might only be gained if all transactions were conducted online. If part of the transactions remained to be done over the counter, not only would manpower still be needed but the processing of the transactions would also not be efficiently streamlined. Thus, AA was resolute on closing all their counters and have all transactions processed via the e-File System. External users could access the system either through the Internet or at the self-service kiosks set up in AA’s lobby.

However, appreciating that such a radical move would indelibly impact on not just internal AA’s officers, but also the external users. The CEO noted:

What we recognized at the very beginning and tried to establish from the beginning was that this was going to be a radical change for everyone. Not just our staff, but all those people out there who have been doing business with us all these years. There was a necessity to buy them over.

Consequently, AA engaged the users through dialogue sessions to gather their views and incorporated their requests into the design of the new system where possible. A key group of external users invited to participate in the session were members of an industry guild that had long established working relationships with AA. This group would be among the heavy users of the new system. The senior management of AA took a keen interest in gathering feedback from this group of users and would personally conduct every dialogue sessions. Inputs from these users were incorporated to further enhance the e-File System design.

## Developing phase

After months of planning, the tender for developing the e-File System was called and awarded to a leading ICT solutions vendor. Within a month of awarding the tender, the e-File System project governance structure was established. The structure comprised of a Steering Committee, a Working Committee, as well as seven Sub-Committees. Each Sub-Committee focused on a certain aspect of work in the implementation of the system and staffed by officers who were conversant in the respective area of focus. For instance, the Legislation Sub-Committee was staffed by officers who were legally trained. The names of these seven Sub-Committees are listed in Table 3.

The e-File System was to be developed on a common ICT infrastructure that was newly launched to support the rapid roll-out of e-Government systems across various government agencies in Singapore. It was realized that some of the initial design and requirements of the e-File System could not be achieved on this common ICT infrastructure. AA’s CIO commented:

It’s [a] common infrastructure. They wouldn’t [customize] it just for you. Obviously whatever we asked for must have [sufficient] merit and they [will then] see [if] other agencies [may also] want to use it. Also, [sometimes] the problems that we raised were something that they felt they can’t tackle because it’ll affect other agencies coming in [to use the common infrastructure].

The e-File System was among the first to be developed on the new common ICT infrastructure. The vendor had to rapidly develop an understanding of the features and functions of this common ICT infrastructure. Simultaneously, the vendor also had to quickly familiarize themselves with AA’s requirements. In order to address the situation and ensure a smooth implementation, a number of interventions were undertaken by AA. For instance, AA worked very closely with the vendor to the extent that there were daily meetings held at the height of development. The AA senior management also requested for the vendor to station its e-File System development team within AA premises. Such close proximity and interaction benefited the development process as it has helped to facilitate better communication and mutual understanding.

With the development of the e-File System progressing, some of the officers in AA started to worry about their job security and began to feel apprehensive. This was especially the case among officers working at the frontline counters as these would be closed once the system was completely implemented. In allaying their worries, the senior management made it clear that no permanent staff would be retrenched. The team-lead of the front-line unit in AA revealed:

[It] was made clear from the very beginning [that] permanent officers don’t [have to] worry [as] we’re not going to retrench [them]. But there would be changes in the way they work. And the benefits of e-filing were emphasized again and again.

During this period, the CEO took the initiative to organize a series of ‘viewpoint’ sessions with the line officers in the absence of middle managers so that she can get a better sense of the morale as well as the challenges of her officers. At these sessions, she would consider the concerns of the staff and also take the opportunity to inform them about any future plans. A front-line officer spoke about the concern and assurance provided by their senior management to address their concern and boost their morale:

The top management, I can see that they were concerned about the staff [as their] first [priority] and [they] were very supportive and they tried their best [to help us].

As the development of the e-File System approached completion, user acceptance tests (UAT) were conducted. In addition to having AA officers to carry out the UAT, external users were also involved. As the UAT could only accommodate selected external users, a number of seminars were conducted to prepare the remaining external users before the system was launched. An AA officer recounted:

Before the launch, we gave public seminars to prepare the users, professional firms, [and] professional bodies y . We told them that this is the new system that will be coming. Roughly what were the changes; changes in [the] law, changes in the system, changes in the way of filing, everything y . We gave [them] advance notice y to prepare themselves for the total conversion.

## Operating phase

On the day of the launch, all counter services were terminated and the only way of transacting with AA was through the e-File System. This resulted in a sudden surge of traffic which caused signs of instability. An external user recalled:

During [those] time, the filing of the information was so slow y . [The system was] not that responsive. You click a button, [then] you had to wait for one, two or three minutes before you [can] see something.

As crowds of disgruntled external users waited in the AA’s lobby to file their transactions at the self-service kiosks, a decision was made to reopen the counters and have the counter officers serve the external users. Nevertheless, the turn-around remained slow and by about 5pm, there were still 40–50 agitated external users waiting. Moreover, the frontline officers were exasperated by the angry external users and started to feel demoralized and lose confidence in the e-File System.

Sensing the ominous situation, the CEO of AA together with other members of the senior management team immediately intervened and joined their front-line officer in placating the external users. The CEO explained her actions:

This is something which is initiated by senior management; we are imposing this huge change on our officers. We need to be there, seen to be part of that whole process.

Such an act was positively received by officers and boosted their morale as they sensed that the senior management was in solidarity with them. One officer commented:

Our CEO was very encouraging y she gave us a lot of encouragement and she was also involved y . She is not a CEO who is an armchair [leader], sit in her room and that's it.

Besides addressing the concerns of the staff and the external users, the senior management also stepped in to deal with the technical instability. Post-mortem meetings were called every evening where the senior management from AA, the vendor and the common ICT infrastructure project team were all represented. The focus of these meetings was directed at resolving the system instability. The CEO explained how she approached these postmortem meetings:

I made it clear to the vendor that this was not a witch-hunt. Our purpose of working on a steering committee, and our purpose of working together was to deal with the issues and solve them y . The idea was that we both wanted this to work. That’s the common [goal] we both had. It’s a vision we both wanted [to realize]. So if that’s our vision, then we should be working together to get it sorted out.

The Deputy CEO also mentioned that it was beneficial to have the senior management of all the various parties present at the post-mortem meetings as this provided the much needed clout to propel the project forward:

We got the top people, [the senior] management y . [We] sat down with them and told them [that] these were the problems [and explored] what were the possibilities [in resolving them] y . I think you needed to have senior management involved in this, so that things can moveyif it’s just the working level, then there’ll be no clout.

The respective senior management teams from the various parties often brought along staffs with relevant area of expertise to these meetings so that mutually agreeable remedial actions could be devised immediately. With the continuous endeavor of AA to perfect the e-File System in concert with the collaborative effort of the vendor and the common ICT infrastructure team, the system soon stabilized.

With the implementation of the system AA achieved significant operations improvement. For example, a transaction that used to take 5 working days is now reduced to a mere 15 minutes and the result would be available on the Internet without the need to visit AA’s office. Moreover, potential disputes resulting from data entry errors were also greatly reduced as data entry was entirely done by the external users. The amount of backend data entry work was significantly reduced, which in turn eliminated the need to employ temporary data entry clerks. The level of external users’ satisfaction also improved as the number of complaint letters received by AA reduced considerably. In an interview, an external user noted succinctly:

It was user friendly y . For the e-filing that I did, it’s pretty straightforward y . [Previously] we have to make a few trips to [AA] and it may take a few days. Now it’s immediate y [and] we get a response from [the] Internet.

The implementation of the e-File System also brought about a transformation in the nature and focus of work in AA. On a daily basis, hundreds to thousands of transactions were filed with AA. This could reach up to ten of thousands during the annual peak period. Prior to the e-File System, every transaction had to be manually processed. Hence, the operational focus of AA was centred on ensuring the timely processing of these transactions. Since the implementation of the e-File System, the nature and focus shifted to greater value adding activities. An AA officer described:

Right now we’re more focused on how we can provide more value added services to [external users] to meet their needs y we’re more focused on how we can improve our current services and to think of how we can provide more valueadded, like doing research right, looking at other countries [for good practices] y and see whether we’re able to do likewise.

As a further affirmation to the improvements achieved by AA the e-File System had won numerous awards for being an effective technological innovation that brought about strategic changes and significant improvement.

## Case analysis

This study aimed to generate insights to advance the research and practice of e-Government system implementation. RBV and the enactment concept were employed as a theoretical sense-making lens to analyse the case of e-File System implementation in AA. The case evidences were analysed across the three implementation phases of Planning, Developing and Operating. As shown in Table 4, Table 5 and Table 6, the analysis revealed that each implementation phase was marked by the presence of a predominant environmental climate and a focal capability. Moreover, the focal capability for each phase was developed through the symbiotic enactment of social, leadership and knowledge resources.

## A focal capability at each phase

Through the case analysis, it was observed that the environmental climate backed the development of a particular focal capability at each phase of the e-Government system implementation.

In the planning phase, the e-File System implementation was considered to be in an imperative environment. The escalating demands from external users together with the launch and strategic advocacy of the eGAP served as impetuses for the e-File System implementa tion. The imperative environment was defined by an apparent and pervasive presence of imperatives for change. Such imperatives created an urgent need for transformative change to avoid undesirable consequences. The novelty of the idea at that time coupled with the absent of pre-existing knowledge in undertaking such a bold and massive Internet-based e-Government initiative required AA to focus on the capability to be innovative. For instance, cognizant of the need to conduct process re-engineering in implementing the e-File System, external expertise was recruited to review existing processes. Also, to generate ideas for the system, overseas study trips were conducted to learn from countries that have implemented similar systems.

Organizations operating within constrained situation often undergo adaptation by making improvisational decisions (Orlikowski & Hofman, 1997). AA was seen to be making adaptive provision to establish working relationship with the new vendor. Moreover, some of the AA officers were anxious about their ability to cope with new technologies and were apprehensive about the risk of being made redundant. Adaptive measures were hence taken to address the officers’ concerns. The senior management assured the officers of their job security and provided training for them to pick up relevant skills for their new job scope. In view of these changes occurring during this phase, the environmental climate was termed as commutative and the corresponding focal capability in AA was observed to be the capability to be adaptive. The commutative environment was defined by the occurrence of pervasive changes where the general mood was one of being in transition. It was marked by the volatility of the status quo. Conflicts might also exist between the conventional and the new and between the institutionalized and the emergent.

As the implementation progressed into the operating phase, the environmental climate was found to be propulsive as there was a high degree of volatility. The propulsive environment was defined by an intense level of capriciousness and ambiguity often associated with crisis situation. Such dynamic uncertainty often required acute attention to contain or resolve the situation. The newly launched e-File System did not function as planned but exhibited signs of instability. In addition, both the internal AA officers and the external users were frustrated with the situation. Such propulsive environment necessitated AA to be highly responsive in addressing the various issues. Although the developing phase also demanded AA to react to different challenges that arose from developing the e-File System, the speed and urgency of reaction required during the operating phase was a key differentiator between the two phases. For instance, swift actions were taken to address the de-escalating morale of the officers and the instability of the e-File System. As the central focus of AA during this phase was on being highly responsive to the situation presented by the propulsive environment, the focal capability was termed as the capability to be responsive.

## Focal capability developed through symbiotic enactment of resources

This case analysis also revealed that the focal capability at each phase of the e-Government system implementation process was developed through the symbiotic enactment of resources. Such symbiotic enactment of resources was characterized by the enactment of a focal resource, along with a synergistic enactment of complementary resources to bring forth the focal capability. While the enactment process is known to be initiated by human agents in correspond to the prevalent environmental climate (Weick, 1979, 2001), the actual action of enactment is mediated through organizational resources (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). The AA case showed that enactments of organizational resources were not mere discrete autonomous occurrences, but a symbiotic enactment of complementary resources. When viewed longitudinally across the three phases, the case analysis yielded a Process Model of Resource Enactment in e-Government System Implementation. This is illustrated in Figure 2.

During the planning phase, the development of the focal capability to be innovative was through the enactment of knowledge resource as the focal resource. Knowledge resource is a primary ingredient for innovation (Leonard & Sensiper, 1998; Quinn et al., 2005). In the case of AA, the enactment of knowledge resource was manifested as AA called upon the expert knowledge of external process reengineering consultant and conducted overseas study trips to learn from the experience of other countries. Moreover, the enactment of knowledge resource was supported by the enactment of social resource and leadership resource. For instance, social resource was enacted as AA leveraged on their relationships with external users by holding dialogue sessions to tap on their knowledge in planning for the e-File System. Leadership resource was enacted when AA’s senior management exhibited their leadership by personally conducting the dialogue sessions and provided moral and financial endorsement for the overseas study trips.

As the implementation proceeded into the developing phase, the capability to be adaptive was developed through the enactment of the social resource as the focal resource.

People are instinctively change averse, especially when changes are believed to result in distressful or detrimental outcomes (Denrell & March, 2001; Pinsonneault & Kraemer, 2002). Moreover, the inappropriate management of social and relational issues is often highlighted to be a key cause of ICT system implementation failure (Jiang et al., 2006; Chan & Pan, 2008). In this case, effort was made to enact social resource in attaining the capability to be adaptive. For example, many AA officers were uneasy with the e-File System implementation as they may subsequently be made redundant. To address these concerns, social resource was enacted as the senior management reassured the affected officers that nobody would be retrenched. In addition, the enactment of leadership resource and knowledge resource also supported the enactment of social resource. The actions taken by the senior management to reassure affected officers as well as their direct involvement in leading the Steering Committee were instances of such supportive enactment of the leadership resource. The enactment of knowledge resource was most prevalent in the form of knowledge resource known as ‘transactive memory’ (Austin, 2003), which can be simply defined as the knowledge of who knows what. This was most evidently enacted in the relationship between AA and the vendor. The vendor co-located at AA’s premises throughout the development of the e-File System. This helped the vendor to quickly build up transactive memory and contextual knowledge about AA’s operations through close and constant interaction. Such enactment of knowledge resource supported the cultivating of social resource as it reinforced trust and mutual understanding between the two parties.

In the operating phase, the capability to be responsive was achieved through the enactment of leadership as the focal resource. The importance of the leadership resource is often highlighted in a propulsive environment (Ireland & Hitt, 2005; Huang et al., 2010). The senior management in AA took on a direct and personal interest in dealing with the various exigencies that arose during this phase. For example, leadership resource was enacted when they choose to personally deal with the agitated external users at the lobby instead of remotely supervising the situation. Furthermore, in contrast to the earlier phases where the enactment of the focal resource was supported by the other two resources, the operating phase showed that the focal leadership resource actually drove the enactment of knowledge resource and social resource. For instance, the enactment of knowledge resource was found to be driven by the enactment of leadership resource. This was clearly visible when the senior management brought the various parties together to resolve the instability of the e-File System as unique knowledge resource residing within AA, the vendor and the common ICT infrastructure team were evoked and shared. The leadership resource also drove the enactment of social resource as the move by the senior management to join the front-line officers in handling the discontented external users boosted the morale of the officers.

When observed across the three phases, it appeared that the symbiotic enactment of resources followed a co-evolutionary process where the resources were enacted in a manner to develop the focal capability. At the same time, such symbiotic enactment of resources would give rise to a new environmental climate, which in turn required a new focal capability to be established. Hence, the symbiotic enactment of resources would be correspondingly adapted to generate the new focal capability as the cycle repeats.

## Discussion

In recent years, scholars have observed a lack of cumulative theoretical development in e-Government research despite the rapid growth of interest and publication in this area (Heeks & Bailur, 2007). Moreover, recognizing the danger of underestimating the complexity of e-Government as simple technological artefacts and outcomes, appeals were made for more research to examine the implementation process (Yang, 2003; Yildiz, 2007). Fountain’s (2001) technology-enactment framework is a seminal piece that explains the institutional changes that occurs with the implementation of e-Government systems. According to Fountain (2001), the enactment of ICT system in government agencies ‘is the result of various cognitive, cultural, structural and political “embeddedness” ’ that reside in the contextual environment. Cordella & Iannacci (2010) built on Fountain (2001) to develop the e-Government enactment framework. Highlighting the influence of government policy, Cordella & Iannacci (2010) argued that the implementation of e-Government systems is part of a policymaking process and an enactment of prevailing e-Government policy, bureaucracy networks and organizational forms.

Our research augments this trail of cumulative theoretical development by offering further insights into the enactment process. Specifically, the contextual environment accentuates a focal capability at each implementation phase. To develop the focal capability at each phase, resources are enacted symbiotically such that a focal resource is enacted along with other complementary resources in a synergistic manner. Consequently, our study also answered the appeals (Yang, 2003; Yildiz, 2007) to examine e-Government implementation as a dynamic and complex process and undergird the danger of over-simplifying e-Government systems implementation as mere introduction of technology artefacts into government agencies.

Aligned with the emerging concerns for more pragmatic investigations (Baskerville & Myers, 2004; Agerfalk, 2010), our study also has an applied research emphasis where value lies in its practical usefulness and utility to induce informed change. This is perhaps even more pertinent in the area of e-Government given the incongruence between research and practice (Heeks & Bailur, 2007). Practical insights are therefore drawn to inform and enhance the practice of e-Government system implementation.

First, the realization that there is a focal capability highlights the importance for practitioners to contextually develop the focal capability at each phase of the implementation. This is in contrast to the usual practice of laying out all activities in advance and ensuring that all activities are executed according to plan. The complex and dynamic nature of e-Government systems make it implausible to develop a highly detailed and enduring plan with no adaptation throughout the entire implementation. Hence, instead of strictly executing the plethora of activities mapped out in an implementation plan, it is proposed that practitioners should ascertain the focal capability that is pertinent at each phase and adapt their plans accordingly.

Second, our research also revealed that the focal capability is developed through a symbiotic enactment of complementary resource. This suggests that upon identifying the capability pertaining to a particular implementation phase, government officials should

Third, the saliency of knowledge, social capital and leadership in e-Government systems implementation indicates that the management of knowledge, the cultivation of social capital and the exemplification of leadership are important for successful implementation. Hence, e-Government practitioners should put in place corresponding knowledge and social capital management strategies in conjunction with the implementation of an e-Government system. In addition, the senior management team of government agencies should also play a direct and conscientious role in leading similar implementation within their agencies. This will enable them to be cognizant about the status of the implementation and dedicate necessary attention and resources to address any potential challenges in a timely manner. Their championship will help foster the success of the implementation as it also demonstrates the strategic importance attributed to the system.

In terms of future research, there is a need to further develop both e-Government research and practice by examining the political and policy aspects of systems implementation (Yildiz, 2007; Cordella & Iannacci, 2010; Scott & Robbins, 2010). While our study did not explicitly address this need, it has laid a foundation that future research may build upon. Specifically, future investigations can explore the enactment of the three complementary resources as the interplay of politics can often be observed through the manner in which knowledge, social and leadership resources are enacted (Foucault, 1972; Howell & Higgins, 1990; Burt, 1997). Such research may uncover more detailed resource enactment routines and mechanisms in e-Government systems implementation and adds to further cumulative theoretical development.

## Conclusion

Through identifying the need to improve research and practice for e-Government an investigation of an awardwinning system in Singapore was conducted. This resulted in the development of a process model of resource enactment, which theorized how organizational resources might be mobilized for successful systems implementation. Specifically, the process model of resource enactment construed that the environmental climate at each development phase gave rise to a particular focal capability. Furthermore, the development of the focal capability was through the symbiotic enactment of resources. The research is believed to generate theoretical and empirical insights and consequently has beneficial implications for both academics and practitioners engaged in e-Government systems implementation.
