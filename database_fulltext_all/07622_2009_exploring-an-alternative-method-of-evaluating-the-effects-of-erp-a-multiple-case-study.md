## Abstract

Previous research has already established that compared to other types of investments, information technology (IT) investments are insufficiently or not at all evaluated. This can be partly explained by the lack of adequate IT evaluation methods and tools. In the case of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems whose effects on organizational processes and performance are intrinsically profound and wide-ranging compared to those of traditional IT limited to some spheres of organization, evaluation activities may be an issue of great concern. This study thus aims to propose and test an alternative evaluation method adaptable to the organizational context, making it possible to measure the contribution of an ERP system to organizational performance in all its aspects. Combining a process-based model and a scorecard model, the proposed method was first designed from a review of information systems evaluation literature. It has then been validated and refined through a multi-case study of manufacturing firms: an in-depth pilot case study was conducted, and thereafter the study was replicated on two other cases. Results show that the method proposed here enables organizations to determine the extent to which the firm’s operational and overall performance has been impacted by the adoption and use of ERP systems, through the automational, informational, and transformational effects of ERP on their business processes. From a practical point of view, three contributions must be mentioned: the proposed method allows for a strong contextualization of its application, it is action-oriented, and it allows comparison across organizations even though organizational contexts may totally differ.

## Introduction

he adoption of enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems is a general trend in organizations, be they large or small, in the private or public sector. These systems have in fact become the de facto standard for managing the core operational and functional processes of large and medium-sized business enterprises (Peslak, 2006). The efforts required to implement ERP are considerable in financial and organizational terms, yet enterprises make them in the hope of improving their performance. Thus, measuring the contribution of ERP systems to organizational performance is a preoccupation of the firms that have adopted them.

Evaluating the contribution of information technology (IT) to organizational performance has always been a major problem for both the information systems (IS) professional and research communities. If this problem existed when IT was generally confined to one organizational function, or one business process, it is even more of a preoccupation for

ERP systems, given that this technology integrates many if not all functions and processes. Practitioners have thus been called upon to review their evaluation methods with regard to ERP (Elbanna, 2006).

While importance is accorded to the IT evaluation problem, it is well known that compared to other types of investments, IT projects are insufficiently or not at all evaluated (Ballantine and Stray, 1999; Seddon et al., 2002). This can be partly explained by inadequate IT evaluation methods and tools (Ward et al., 1996; Kohli and Grover, 2008). From a theoretical point of view, previous studies suggest that one explanation of the now famous ‘productivity paradox’ lies in the inadequacy of measurement methods and tools (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Pinsonneault and Rivard, 1998). It thus seems that the key to answer the persistent question of IT’s contribution to organizational performance is to be found in the measurement issue (Barua et al., 1995). Moreover, the mitigated and sometimes contradictory results of IT impact studies have been attributed to the fact that these technologies are studied without accounting for the organization context within which they are deployed (Pinsonneault and Rivard, 1998). One can add that in many studies, organizational performance is limited to a small number of financial ratios (e.g., Hitt et al., 2002; Hunton et al., 2003; Nicolaou et al., 2003; Nicolaou, 2004), whereas the expected benefits of complex systems such as ERP are multiple and varied (Shang and Seddon, 2000). There are also a number of such studies that do not clearly distinguish between organizational or overall firm performance and operational or business process performance (Wieder et al., 2006).

This study thus aims to propose an alternative method for evaluating the contribution of an ERP system to organizational performance. This method takes into account the organizational context in which the system is implemented and the multiple aspects of performance, both operational and organizational. It was developed first from the literature on the ex post evaluation of IT, notably on evaluation problems, models, and techniques. The literature review was followed by a multiple case study, that is, a pilot case and two other cases.

## Theoretical background

## IT impact evaluation models

As found in the literature, IT impact evaluation models can be regrouped under four categories: causal models, contingency models, process models, and scorecard models. Causal models (e.g., Byrd and Marshall, 1997; Sircar et al., 2000; Thatcher and Oliver, 2001; Hitt et al., 2002; Hendricks et al., 2007; Shin, 2006), also known as variance models, attempt to establish a cause–effect relationship between IT investments on the one hand, and organizational performance on the other. In contingency models (e.g., Bergeron et al., 2000; Somers and Nelson, 2001; Gattiker and Goodhue, 2005; Ragowsky et al., 2005), it is assumed that the impact of IT on organizational performance depends not on IT as such but rather on the alignment or ‘fit’ of IT with other dimensions of the organization such as its strategy, structure, and business processes. Process models (e.g., Mooney et al., 1995; Soh and Markus,

1995; Wieder et al., 2006) conceptualize the contribution of IT to performance through a temporal series of interlinked effects. This process is then one of converting the potential value of IT into realized value for the enterprise (Davern and Kauffman, 2000). Scorecard models highlight the multiple dimensions of performance, and their use to evaluate the impacts of IT in general (Wright et al., 1999) and of ERP systems in particular (Rosemann and Wiese, 1999; Chand et al., 2005) seems well indicated, given that IT, and ERP even more so, are deemed to affect the organization in many different ways. The evaluation approach based on the concept of ‘IS business value’ is similar to the scorecard approach to the extent that this concept is broken down into multiple aspects of performance (Kraemer et al., 1994).

The literature review on the ex post evaluation of IT led us to develop an ERP evaluation model that combines both the process approach and the scorecard approach, as presented in Figure 1. A process model was chosen for its ability to relate the ERP system to organizational performance, through the automational, informational, and transformational effects of the system upon both operational and managerial processes (Mooney et al., 1995). It was, however, found necessary to add a scorecard model in order to account for the intrinsically multidimensional character of organizational performance (Markus et al., 2000), and for the multiplicity of potential effects of an ERP system (Shang and Seddon, 2000).

Quantitative/qualitative approaches to the evaluation of IT impacts Reviews of IS/IT impact studies conclude that quantitative methodological approaches are still dominant (Chan, 2000; Sircar et al., 2000; Chan and Horner Reich, 2007). This would be explained on the one hand by the fact that researchers have mostly viewed the performance of business enterprises from an economic/financial perspective, and have mostly used causal models on the other hand with the aim of establishing statistical correlations between IS/IT investments and economic/financial performance measures. A number of IS researchers have, however, noted the limitations of solely using such measures (Rosemann and Wiese, 1999; Wright et al., 1999; Sedera et al., 2001; Stefanou, 2001), and in so doing have proposed alternative types of measures, more qualitative in nature. In general, the criticism formulated against quantitative methods concerns ‘context stripping,’ loss of significance and of object, disjunction between all-encompassing theories and local context (e.g., the ‘emic/etic’ debate, Headland et al., 1990), inapplicability of general data to individual cases (the nomologic/idiographic disjunction), and exclusion of ‘surprise’ or ‘discovery’ in research (Daft, 1983; Guba and Lincoln, 1994).

Qualitative methods can also be the object of criticism. The advantages obtained from the methodological richness of qualitative research are counterbalanced by difficulties associated with using qualitative approaches in all their diversity while meeting the requirements of quality, validity, and rigor (Carrol and Swatman, 2000). The main weaknesses of these approaches have been identified as follows (Gable, 1994): (a) incapacity to manipulate independent variables, (b) risk of erroneous interpretations, and (c) absence of random sampling capability.

Given the respective limitations of quantitative and qualitative methods, an ideal research design should combine both. Such a hybrid approach is, in fact, strongly recommended in IS research (Byrd and Marshall, 1997; Mingers, 2001). We have, however, initially opted for a research design that essentially favors qualitative methods. Given that quantitative methods dominate in the IS evaluation research domain, as mentioned previously, there is a growing conviction that qualitative methods may bring new light to this domain. Also, given that the phenomenon under study, namely ERP systems use, is complex and that one requires a deeper understanding of it in its actual context, a qualitative methodology is more appropriate (Bourlakis and Bourlakis, 2006). Finally, given the literature on the subject, we expect many of the most important effects of ERP systems to be intangible and thus relatively difficult if not impossible to quantify.

## Methods for evaluating IT impacts

Methods to evaluate IT impacts ex post can be regrouped under four categories: (a) methods of an economic and/or financial nature, (b) strategic analysis methods, (c) general analysis methods, and (d) integrated methods.

In the IS project management literature, methods based on economic/financial indicators such as payback, internal rate of return, net present value, and return on investment abound. The two main arguments in favor of such standard financial techniques (Milis and Mercken, 2004) are that: (a) they subscribe to the traditional business performance logic of performance based on maximizing shareholder wealth and (b) compared to other techniques, they are well known and better understood. The success of these techniques would even have a perverse effect in that they can be used in a ‘ritual’ manner (Milis and Mercken, 2004), that is even if they bring no added value to the decision process.

Strategic analysis methods are based on the strategic nature of IT investments, and thus attempt to measure their contribution in this regard. In so doing, these methods confront one of the main problems of the evaluation of IT, that is, the difficulty in quantifying the strategic advantages obtained from this technology (Giaglis et al., 1999), to the extent that such advantages imply dynamic interactions with customers, suppliers, and competitors (Post et al., 1995), and would explain why this evaluation is often shirked.

General analysis methods comprise mathematical methods such as data envelopment analysis (Shafer and Byrd, 2000; Chen et al., 2006) and those based on the attribution of scores such as importance-performance maps (Skok et al., 2001).

Integrated methods combine different approaches, both objective and subjective; different dimensions, both financial and non-financial; different factors, both tangible and intangible; and different considerations, both operational and strategic (Kaplan and Norton, 1996). These methods aim to span the complexity and variety of benefits that an organization can obtain from its use of IT (Wright et al., 1999). Multi-attribute or multi-criteria methods and scorecards or dashboards are typical of this evaluation logic (Chand et al., 2005).

From the literature, one can observe that the choice of an appropriate IT evaluation method would depend on one of the following criteria: the object of the IT investments (Hochstrasser, 1990; Fitzgerald, 1998), the extent to which the IT benefits are tangible (Remenyi and Sherwood-Smith, 2001), or the characteristics of the IT project and of the implementation context (Farbey et al., 1992, 1994). The choice itself or the order of priority of the chosen performance measures will also depend on the IS structure (Heo and Han, 2003), as the intrinsic nature of these measures can provide indications as to what method is more appropriate. IT project classifications have thus been identified in the preceding studies on the basis of these criteria. For instance, referring to Hochstrasser’s (1990) classification, traditional economic methods using costbenefit analysis and financial ratios would be appropriate for ‘cost replacement projects’ that provide gains in efficiency, but would be inappropriate for ‘new technology projects’ whose aim is more strategic and whose benefits are more intangible (Irani and Love, 2000). In the latter case, risk analysis based on the judgement of experts would be more appropriate (Hochstrasser, op. cit.).

Using the preceding choice criteria, it would suffice to classify an IT project into one category or the other in order to identify and select one or more corresponding evaluation methods. The problem with ERP systems, however, is that they are difficult to classify under any one of the categories proposed. In effect, what distinguishes ERP from other IT is its integrative or ‘unifying’ ambition, that is, to regroup within its perimeter all of the organization’s productive and administrative activities (Bazet and Maye\`re, 2004). Moreover, in referring to Farbey et al.’s (1994) classification, Hedman and Borell (2004) conclude that ERP systems have an impact potential under all eight IT categories and cumulate the uncertainties associated to each category. This last remark also applies to all other classifications, including that of Hochstrasser (1990) mentioned previously.

While it is not easy to qualify a particular IT evaluation method as being appropriate for ERP, given the preceding considerations, one can be, however, relatively certain that the method chosen or developed cannot limit itself to one dimension of performance, be it financial, strategic, or operational. Rather, as is done in this study, a combination of methods would seem necessary.

## Comparison of methods for evaluating ERP impacts

In order to better illustrate the specific nature and advantages of the method proposed, it can be compared to other such methods that appear in implicit or explicit fashion in a number of recent studies of the ex post impact of ERP systems (Chand et al., 2005; Gattiker and Goodhue, 2005; Ragowsky et al., 2005; Hendricks et al., 2007; Shin, 2006; Wieder et al., 2006), as presented in Table 1.

One may first note that, with the notable exception of Chand et al.’s (2005) single case study, the previous studies adopted a survey or secondary data-based deductive approach to test hypotheses with samples whose size varies from 106 to 525 firms. These studies provide most interesting and relevant results from a macroeconomic point of view, allowing us to better understand the global ex post impacts of ERP systems. But case studies are more appropriate to provide concrete and significant lessons for ERP implementation and exploitation strategies based on the experiences of other firms (Shin, 2006). The evaluation methods used by the survey studies are not apt to be adopted by a particular business enterprise. In contrast, through a multiple case study, we illustrate in concrete fashion the manner by which the proposed method is to be used in specific enterprises.

The studies analyzed here include all four models identified in the literature on the ex post evaluation of IT in general, and of ERP in particular, and that are used as a conceptual framework by the evaluators. One may note, however, the preponderance of causal models in these studies (Ragowsky et al., 2005; Hendricks et al., 2007; Shin, 2006; Wieder et al., 2006). Some combine a causal model with a process model (Wieder et al., 2006) or a contingency model (Ragowsky et al., 2005) for a better comprehension of ERP impacts. For their part, Chand et al. (2005) use the balanced scorecard exclusively, whereas Gattiker and Goodhue (2005) combine a contingency model with a process model. In this study, the ERP evaluation method is conceptually founded on a process model that is combined with a balanced scorecard model. This emphasizes the conversion of ERP investments into added value for the organization, while taking into account the multiple aspects or dimensions of this added value.

Moreover, the method proposed here distinguishes itself by its multi-level nature that includes both local and global levels of analysis. First, this allows us to identify ERP system impacts that manifest themselves at the operational or process level (local), but whose trace is lost at the organizational level (global) due to aggregation effects (Peaucelle, 2002) or to the presence of ‘noise’ in the performance measures (Barua et al., 1995). Combining local and global levels also allows us to widen the perspective on the ERP system’s contribution. By limiting oneself to the local level, one has a better chance, without a doubt, to identify significant ERP effects, but given the strategic and financial importance of ERP investments, is this sufficient for managers whose main interest lies in their firm’s global performance? Third, a multi-level evaluation model also helps us in identifying the global effect of ERP that results from the dynamic interrelationships between local effects (Peaucelle, 2002).

It is worth noting that this study conceptualizes performance in an extended manner, both at the local and global level, when compared to most of the other studies, with the exception of Wieder et al.’s (2006) study. Moreover, in contrast to the implicit or explicit evaluation methods used in research with which this study is compared, prescriptive rather than contextual or situation-specific performance measures are used. With the exception of Chand et al.’s (2005) single case study, all other studies use performance measures originating in the literature and chosen by the researchers rather than measures specific to the organization studied and chosen by its managers. Prescriptive measures allow comparison across enterprises, but contextual measures are advantageous in that they are the ones that are actually used and valued by the firm. The method proposed in this study provides a practical mean of comparing ERP impacts, notwithstanding the use of different performance measures from one enterprise to the next.

## Research methodology

The research strategy chosen is a multiple case study, that is, three cases chosen to allow the exploration and initial validation of an alternative method for evaluating the effects of an ERP system. Following Yin’s (2003: 78-81) prescriptions, a first case, namely the pilot case, was studied in depth and the results of this first case were used to better structure the subsequent cases. Case studies, in general, are appropriate for the analysis of a contemporary phenomenon in their actual context, notably when the frontier between the phenomenon and its context cannot be clearly delineated (Yin, 2003: 13), which generally seems the case for IT in organizations (Alter, 1999), and for ERP systems in particular (Ross and Vitale, 2000). Case studies have an important role in evaluation studies, in that they can explain presumed causal links in real-world phenomena that are too complex to be explained by survey or experimental research strategies (Yin, 2003: 15). And, in particular, case studies reveal the insights gained by applying an evaluation method to a real-world IT application (Silverman, 1992).

Here, the choice of a multiple case study is justified by the fact that the evaluation of ERP is a phenomenon that is in relative emergence. Whereas the evaluation of IT has been a longstanding problem, the relative novelty of ERP systems, their ‘radical’ character, and the complexity of the mechanisms by which these systems affect organizational performance constitute a challenge to the evaluation practices developed previously. This methodological choice is also justified by the need to develop an ERP evaluation method that takes the context of use into account, that is, if one wants to overcome the incoherencies that have characterized the study of the IT-performance relationship (Pinsonneault and Rivard, 1998).

These are named Alpha (pilot case), Beta, and Gamma in this study, that is, fictitious names used to preserve their anonymity as wished by these enterprises.

## Data collection and analysis

Data collection, mainly through semi-structured interviews, was made over a period of 20 months, starting in December 2005 and ending in July 2007. As shown in Table 2, there were 13 interviews with nine individuals at Alpha (pilot case) for a total of 12 h, six interviews with three individuals at Beta for a total of 8 h, and six interviews with four individuals at Gamma for a total of 7.5 h.

For those individuals who were interviewed twice, the second interview was always held after analyzing the data obtained from the first. For the others, cross-verifications were done and when apparent contradictions emerged, further clarification was obtained either by telephone or email. Moreover, Alpha provided us with rich documentation on the decision process that led to the adoption of the ERP system, and on the system/supplier adoption and selection process. Document summary forms were used to exploit this documentation effectively, as proposed by Miles and Huberman (2003). As the production process is at the core of this study, a guided tour of the plant in each case allowed us to better understand the explanations provided on this process, and especially the use made of the ERP system within the production process.

To analyze the collected data, document summary forms, interview recordings, and interview notes were used, as well as notes taken during the guided tours of the plans. The transcriptions of the recorded interviews (and other data in electronic form) were processed with the Atlas.ti qualitative data analysis software, whose development follows the principles of grounded theory-based research. Open coding of the data was used, with pre-defined codes based on the literature and corresponding to the principal categories included in the evaluation model. The coding system was refined, however, during the analysis, allowing for a further breakdown of these categories, notably with regard to ERP effects. The three cases were first analyzed individually, before proceeding to a cross-case analysis.

## Methodological rigor of the multiple case study

As it is fundamentally context-driven in nature, this research adopts a constructivist rather than a positivist epistemological posture, wherein researchers are viewed as ‘craftsmen, as toolmakers who are part of a network that creates knowledge, and ultimately guides practice’ (Mir and Watson, 2000: 941). The methodological quality of the multiple case study may thus be judged in terms of credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Guba and Lincoln, 1994; Berthon et al., 2002) rather than in terms of internal validity, external validity, reliability, and objectivity.

First, certain measures were adopted to insure credibility, that is, to insure that we have an authentic portrait of what was observed (Miles and Huberman, 2003: 504). Thus, case results were validated by the respondents, data collection methods and sources were triangulated, and rival explanations were considered. Transferability was increased in three ways, that is, by a rich description of the case, by confronting results to existing theories, and by the use of a generic approach (Miles and Huberman, 2003). Rich description not only allows a comparison between the studied context and the transfer context, but it also renders the method proposed intelligible and reproducible. We have also determined whether our results agree with those of prior studies, and discussed of possible explanations when they do not. Given that the approach used to develop and test the ERP evaluation model and tool does not depend upon a specific organizational context, our results are also sufficiently generic in nature to be applicable in other contexts (i.e., in key business processes other than production and/or in other manufacturing firms), and even in contexts of a different nature (e.g., in hospitals) (Miles and Huberman, 2003: 506). Dependability is insured by fully documenting the research process and results (Halldo´rsson and Aastrup, 2003). In this regard, an interview guide, a case documentation, and a data analysis protocol were elaborated. And in insuring that the multiple case study’s conclusions accurately reflect the results obtained and are free from the researchers’ bias, that is, confirmability, care was taken to anchor these results in the underlying data (Halldo´rsson and Aastrup, 2003). Previously denoted measures of credibility and transferability also contribute to confirmability, namely validating results with respondents, considering rival explanations, and confronting the results to existing theories (Miles and Huberman, 2003: 503).

## Research results

## Presentation of the cases

A comparative description of the global organizational context of the three firms studied is presented in Table 3. The pilot case is a medium-sized enterprise (Alpha with 300–350 employees and a turnover of 54 million \$ CAD), one is a small enterprise (Beta with 60 employees and a turnover of 10.5million), and another is a large enterprise (Gamma with 600 employees and a turnover of approximately 300 million).

Alpha and Gamma operate in the same market but they are not direct competitors and their products are quite distinct: door windows and PVC doors for Alpha, and lumber and its derived products for Gamma. Beta is a subcontractor for large wholesalers in the furniture industry (chairs and tables). Alpha and Gamma export on the American market, whereas Beta’s market is limited to Canada.

The production system’s main challenges reflect the ERP system adopted by each firm. ## Links of ERP effects with operational and organizational performance

It is important to note at the outset that in the three cases, very few of the ERP effects studied could be taken for the usual indicators of operational or organizational performance. In other words, it is rare that an ERP effect is at the same time a performance indicator (PI) used by the firm to evaluate and control its activities. For example, in Alpha’s case, managers indicate an ERP effect to be the ‘standardization of information,’ whereas this effect does not constitute one of Alpha’s usual PIs. Hence it is necessary to relate such effects to the indicators affect by them in one form or another. Tables 5–7 show this relation for Alpha, Beta, and Gamma, respectively. The measure developed here takes into account the importance of the indicator (from 1 ¼ unimportant to 5 ¼ very important), and the extent to which a variation in the indicator is attributable to the ERP effect (none (0), weak (1), average (2), strong (3), with a minus sign if the variation is negative).

The ERP effects were identified through the interviews; it was asked of the individuals responsible for the various sub-processes to describe the changes brought about by the ERP system. It was then asked of the respondent to estimate the impact of each of the ERP effects identified on each of the PIs used by the managers to evaluate and control their activities. Thus, one (a b) product is obtained for each (ERP effect–PI) couple. The global score is obtained by summing the $( a _ { i } { * } b _ { i } )$ products. ## Contributions to theory

The evaluation method proposed here enables organizations to link their adoption and use of IT to their PIs, through the effects of IT on their business processes. Most IT evaluation studies, even when they use process models, have limited themselves to identifying benefits obtained from IT use, without ascertaining the extent to which these benefits affect organizational performance as perceived and conceived by management. For instance, the studies that have focused on ‘IT business value’ are of this type (Kraemer et al., 1994; Tallon and Kraemer, 2006). And sometimes, IT effects are put into the same basket as measures of organizational performance (e.g., Rajagopal and Tyler, 2000). However, the effects of IT on processes do not necessarily correspond to the PIs used by those who manage these processes, nor do the organizational benefits of IT necessarily correspond to the organizational PIs used by managers. The method we propose steps further, by systematically seeking to link IT effects to the ‘in-use’ performance logic of the firm. In evaluating the contribution of IT to performance, a change in perspective is thus advocated here. Instead of saying, as implicitly assumed by most previous studies, ‘here is what IT brings to the organization,’ whereas this is not what the organization really needs, we propose saying ‘here are the organization’s PIs and the extent to which IT affects them.’ In so doing, we follow the perspective taken by Luftman and Muller (2005) in their Total Value of Ownership model, that is, one of IT alignment with the firm’s strategic priorities.

The literature has echoed a number of difficulties, both conceptual and methodological, with regard to the evaluation of IT’s contribution to organizational performance. The approach proposed here resolves at least two of these: (a) interference of exogenous variables and (b) anchoring bias related to the initial motivations for the adoption of an ERP system.

Organizational performance is determined by a multitude of factors and, for IS researchers, separating IT determinants from the other determinants of performance constitutes a major problem. ## Contributions to practice

From a practical point of view, three contributions of this research must be mentioned: (a) the proposed method is contextualized, (b) it is action-oriented, and (c) it allows comparison across organizations even though organizational contexts may totally differ.

It is of the utmost importance for managers and researchers to be able to measure ‘in context’ the value realized from an investment in IT (Davern and Kauffman, 2000). The contextualization of the method proposed lies in the fact that it is more or less aligned with the PIs (both at the process and at the global level) used by the organization. Previous studies have generally used identical measures for all organizations sampled, such as ROA or market share at the organizational level (Barua et al., 1995; Byrd and Davidson, 2006), and throughput or inventory turnover at the intermediate level (Barua et al., 1995), which is convenient because firms can thus be compared on the same basis. However, if these measures are not within the range of PIs used by a firm, or if they are of secondary importance in its business context, evaluation results are less significant for that firm. The opposite is true of the method proposed in this study as it is meant to ‘fit’ each organization that uses it. Depending upon the level of detail and complexity of the organization’s usual PIs, the approach itself will be more or less complex. Moreover, this method takes into account the relative importance of each PI.

Being action-oriented, the proposed method identifies the areas of operational and organizational performance that are unaffected or weakly affected by the ERP system. This allows managers to determine the corrective actions necessary, say adding new ERP software modules, investing in complementary assets, or actions aimed at improving the use of modules already implemented.

Finally, this ERP evaluation method offers a basis for comparing business firms of a diverse nature (size, sector, technology, etc.) without sacrificing its relevancy and usefulness for each and every firm. Contextualization makes it possible to account for each firm’s specific situation, whereas normalized scores make it possible compare firms. This becomes even more important, as benchmarking practices are judged necessary for improving the evaluation of IT investments (Skok et al., 2001; Cragg, 2002).

## Limitations and future research

This study is based on the analysis of the ‘production and delivery’ process in three manufacturing enterprises. Not only would constraints of time and means have precluded the analysis of all business processes, but such an analysis would not have been necessary to develop an ERP evaluation method as the approach undertaken in the production process can be taken for other processes if need be. Moreover, the choice of the production process is justified by the central role that it occupies in manufacturing, as the operations that provide the greatest added value reside within this process. It is also at this level that an ERP system should have a major impact. By limiting ourselves to three cases and concentrating our efforts on one key process in each case, we were able to probe deeper than would have been possible if more cases or more processes had been studied. The imperative answered here is one of increased understanding of a complex phenomenon in its natural context, rather than one of generalization in the statistical sense.

Another limitation resides in the use of perceptual measures of ERP system impacts on the PIs. While such measures are common in IT evaluation research (Chang, 2000; Tallon et al., 2000), they are still a subject of debate when compared to objective economic and financial measures, even though it has been demonstrated that they constitute valid proxies for IT business value (Tallon et al., 2000).

Having said this, a fairly obvious avenue for further research lies in the application of the method developed and initially validated in this study, but by extending the range of investigation to all processes affected by the ERP system, and on a larger sample of firms whose characteristics are more varied. Such research would further test the method and lead to refinements if needed. With a larger number of cases, cluster analyses could be used to categorize organizations depending upon the level of impact of ERP systems, thus identifying the typical attributes of each category. For instance, one could ascertain if firm size or other contextual factors have a certain incidence on the contribution of ERP to performance, given that with these systems, the potential value added depends as much on the characteristics of the organization as on the characteristic of the application itself (Ragowsky et al., 2005).

In general manner, with these further studies one could identify the ‘conversion contingencies’ in the process by which an investment in ERP is converted into realized value for the firm (Davern and Kauffman, 2000: 124). One could also study the relationship between the initial motivations for the adoption of the ERP system and its contribution to performance. As the types of ERP implementation and use are influenced if not determined by these motivations, be they technological, operational or strategic in nature, one may expect the performance outcomes of ERP to vary correspondingly.

## Conclusion

Evaluating the contribution of IT to organizational performance has always been and may always be a major preoccupation for IS research. While significant research efforts have been made in this regard, existing evaluation methods do not seem effective enough in the eyes of managers and IS professionals. As a consequence, IT investments are less evaluated than other types of investments notwithstanding their increasing weight in the budget of most private and public organizations. Also, changes in the organizational and technological landscapes make it necessary to develop new methods. This applies to ERP systems in particular, as their complexity is such that one cannot assume that IT evaluation results obtained by studying much simpler applications are transferable to an ERP context (Amoako-Gyampah and Salam, 2004). Hence, in such a context, new evaluation approaches are necessary, that is, approaches such as the one proposed in this study that accounts for greater integration of functions and processes, both intra-organizational and inter-organizational, for both strategic and operational aspects, and for both local and global levels of performance measurement.

This study has presented an ERP evaluation method that is founded upon a process model combined to a scorecard model. Following this method, an organization is able to relate its use of an ERP system to its usual indicators or operational and organizational performance, by identifying the system’s automational, informational, and transformational effects on operational and managerial processes. PIs were not defined by the researchers, as is usually the case in IT evaluation research, but were rather the ones actually used by the three organizations studied. This method also includes multiple aspects of performance, thus rendering a clearer picture and providing a deeper understanding of the contribution of ERP.
