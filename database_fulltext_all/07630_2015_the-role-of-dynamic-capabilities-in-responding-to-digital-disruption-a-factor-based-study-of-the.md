# The Role of Dynamic Capabilities in Responding to Digital Disruption: A Factor-Based Study of the Newspaper Industry

JAHANGIR KARIMI AND ZHIPING WALTER

JAHANGIR KARIMI (corresponding author) received a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Arizona. He is director of the Information Systems Program at the Business School, University of Colorado Denver. His research interests include disruptive innovations, digital innovations, digitization, digital Infrastructure, digitally enabled business transformation and new business models, ERP implementation, and corporate and strategic entrepreneurship. He has published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, Decision Sciences, and other journals and books. He is a member of the Editorial Board of International Journal of Electronic Commerce.

ZHIPING WALTER received a Ph.D. in business administration, specializing in computer information systems from the Simon School of Business, University of Rochester. She is an associate professor of management information systems at the Business School, University of Colorado Denver. Her research interests are in the areas of online information seeking, online consumer behavior, and disruptive innovations. Her research has appeared in Communications of the ACM, Decision Support Systems, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction, and European Journal of Operational Research, among others.

ABSTRACT: Internet and digitization are fundamentally changing and disrupting newspaper companies’ traditional operating models. Disruptive innovation theory offers explanations for why companies succeed or fail to respond to disruptive innovations. This study builds on disruptive innovation theory by ascertaining the role of dynamic capabilities in the performance of response to digital disruption. Empirical results suggest that first-order dynamic capabilities that are created by changing, extending, or adapting a firm’s existing resources, processes, and values are positively associated with building digital platform capabilities, and that these capabilities impact the performance of response to digital disruption. For information systems (IS) researchers, this study clarifies the role of first-order dynamic capabilities in responding to digital disruption. For IS practice, it helps managers to focus on the most promising factors for creating first-order dynamic capabilities, for building digital platform capabilities, and for reinventing their core functions to accelerate digitization.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: autonomous growth group, common language, digital disruption, digital platforms, digital transformation, digitization, disruptive innovation, innovative culture, multimedia mindset, organizational capabilities, resources-processes-values (RPV) framework, response performance, staged resource allocation.

Internet and digitization are changing the newspaper industry at an extraordinary pace. They have made technologies for e-commerce, social networking, finding and compar ing choices, and entertainment easy and powerful. Internet information is now cheap and abundant; as information grows people become more comfortable and willing to spend more time using the technologies. The Internet’s open standards have also facilitated open distribution platforms, which have further resulted in more flexibility for consumers to access digital content from any provider. As more content distribution moves to the web, readers are moving online to read news and share articles. At the same time, aggregative news services are becoming more important intermediaries in the dynamics of newspaper circulation [98]. Furthermore, the convergence of inexpensive digital information goods and computing and communication devices are changing not only the newspaper industry but also business and society as a whole through five digital forces: globalization, millenialization, prosumerization, business virtualization, and platformization [53].<sup>1</sup> Nowhere are the effects of these digital forces more disruptive than in the communications, information, media, and entertainment (CIME) industries. In response to Internet and those digital forces, major CIME companies in both print and broadcasting have established significant online news services and are moving fast in (1) deploying digital platforms to create new ways of attracting and engaging audiences, (2) offering digital solutions and very different approaches to news, information, social networking, and entertainment, and (3) implementing a wide variety of online revenue models that draw away more and more newspapers’ advertising dollars [5]. At the same time, those same digital forces that are changing CIME companies in general are radically changing newspaper companies in particular by changing their value chain [53, 98]. By enabling community collaboration and citizen journalism, Web 2.0 platforms and digitization have facilitated the production of user- or community-generated digital content by nonjournalist readers, resulting in virtualizing news-gathering processes. Readers now have access to journalists’ blogs and tweets about the news as it happens and can comment on articles and make suggestions on both online and print versions. Since digital news and information content can now be produced by amateurs in addition to professionals, Internet and digitization are competence-destroying and disruptive for the newspaper industry. Consequently, newspaper companies are seeing their dominance erode and Internet and digitization are fundamentally changing and disrupting their traditional business models. Readers’ new online behavior has pushed newspaper companies to focus on digitization, digital platforms, digital transformation, and digital innovations [52]. Even so, some have raised serious concerns about the future viability of the print newspaper industry [30]. Many industry analysts believe that disruption to the newspaper industry is likely to follow the same pattern seen by disruptive innovations in other industries [6], and are predicting a complete eventual collapse of the century-old newspaper industry because the newspaper industry as a whole has lost tens of thousands of jobs since the digital disruption and its revenue level has returned to that of the 1950s [42, 69, 77].

According to disruptive innovation theory [28], the resources-processes-values (RPV) framework offers explanations for why big companies succeed or fail to respond to disruptive innovations,<sup>2</sup> and helps managers in assessing an incumbent’s organizational strengths (capabilities) as well as its weaknesses (disabilities) in responding to disruptive innovations. Despite drawbacks brought about by a lack of clarity in the terminology used, Christensen’s work on disruptive innovations has received extensive coverage not only in innovation literature [e.g., 10, 32, 35, 39,54, 116] but also in information systems (IS) literature [e.g., 81, 87, 88, 101]. Through categorization and application of dis ruptive innovation theory to diverse disciplines, industries, and topic areas (such as new product development, marketing, strategy, health care, education, orthopedics, psychotherapy, political science, technology, and innovation management) (e.g., [27, 36, 86, 119, 126, 129]), it has made a transition from a descriptive to a normative theory [24]. Despite the ongoing process of theory building [24, 36, 90], disruptive innovation theory is not mentioned as one of the widely used theories in IS research [83]. Not surprisingly, the emphasis in the theory has always been on nontechnology factors that are embedded in the RPV framework, and little attention has been paid to the enabling role of technology in responding to disruptive innovations [134]. Digital technologies, innovations [70, 133], and transformation [15], however, are fundamentally transform ing business processes, products, services, and relationships [16]. The complex patterns of dynamic interdependencies among environmental turbulence, dynamic capabilities, and IT systems have resulted in the phenomenon called “digital ecodynamics” [45]. However, most prior IS studies have not focused on the role of dynamic capabilities in responding to digital disruption. The identification of factors that create dynamic capabilities also aids IS researchers in identifying digital transformation drivers that are necessary for building digital platform capabilities in responding to digital disruption. Consequently, we integrate the literatures on dynamic capabilities, digitization, and digital transformation into the literature on disruptive innovations in order to build on disruptive innovation theory and gain new insights. In other words, we account for the interdependence among RPV factors as clusters of interconnected elements that create first-order dynamic capabilities and that lead to building digital platform capabilities. This will allow us to improve RPV categorization and trigger the cycle of improvement in disruptive innovation theory by identifying anomalies in the theory [24, 36, 134].<sup>3</sup>

In this study, we focus on the newspaper industry as an information-intensive industry to provide an empirical context to describe and measure RPV factors that create first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption. Three research questions are addressed: (1) what are newspaper companies’ key RPV factors that create first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption? (2) what is the role of such capabilities in building digital platform capabilities? and (3) do digital platform capabilities mediate the impact of first-order dynamic capabilities on the performance of newspaper companies’ response to digital disruption? This study, therefore, makes three main contributions. First, it provides definitions, measurements, and data for a firm’s RPV factors that create first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to disruptive innovations. Second, it examines the role of these RPV factors as clusters of interconnected elements in building digital platform capabilities. This is especially important for shaping future research in digital innovations, digital infrastructures, and digital business strategy [15, 16, 120, 133]. Third, it examines how building digital platform capabilities mediates the impact of first-order dynamic capabilities on response performance. By empirically examining the microfoundations of digital platform capabilities, this work also contributes to the extant literature in revealing how first-order dynamic capabilities help in reconfiguring a firm’s resource base for responding to digital disruption [125].

## Theoretical Framework

A technology is considered disruptive when its use generates services or physical products with different performance attributes that may not be valued by a company’s current customer base [40, 47]. Disruption, however, is not an event or immediate phenomenon but a process that may take several years or even decades [28], “sometimes quickly and completely, but other times slowly and incompletely” [129, p. 58]. The literature on discontinuous innovations has often examined incumbents’ resources and capabilities when trying to explain their difficulties in responding to these innovations. Christensen [25] proposed an alternative explanation for the recurrent pattern of incumbent’s failure to respond to disruptive innovations and framed disruption as a theory (see Supplement A for an overview of disruptive innovation theory). Although prior case studies provide rich context for theory-building purposes, “the most promising area for research would be to provide data specifying resources, processes, and values” [36, p. 252]. As a normative (or a formal) theory, disruptive innovation theory can be further improved by “making definitions and measurements more precise, by refining the categories, and by clarifying the mechanism of causality” [25, p. 48], and by an “empirical study which tries to compare precisely which combinations of the enablers are most important” [134, p. 445]. Given that there are no studies on the role of technology in enabling response to disruptive innovations [134], it is clear that the extant literature on disruptive innovations has also largely ignored technology managers, and thus ill serves them with respect to providing insights into key RPV factors that create dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption.

To begin to address this knowledge gap, we build on disruptive innovation theory by drawing on strategic management literature on organizational ordinary and dynamic capabilities [41, 125] and on the recent IS literature on digitization, digital transformation, and dynamics of digital ecosystems [15, 45, 121, 133]. Organizational ordinary capabilities are defined as “high-level routine (or collection of routines) that, together with its implementing input flows, confers upon an organization’s management a set of decision options for producing significant outputs of a particular type” [131, p. 991]. They are the zero-level and “how we earn a living now” capabilities [131]. A radical technological change, however, often creates capability gaps for incumbent firms in the industry because it introduces new technological knowledge and alternatives, new ways of performing organizational activities, and new ways of creating value. A capability gap is the distance between a firm’s existing configuration of capabilities and the most valuable configuration potentially available post the technological change [84]. Such gaps require firms to develop or acquire new sets of capabilities to enter into a new technological subfield, commercialize new products, and incorporate the new technology to create value [84]. The literature has also explored the reasons for incumbents’ reluctant or ineffective responses to such radical technological innovations [13, 14, 61]. Although the choice of a capability reconfiguration mechanism to narrow capability gaps depends on the pace and uncertainties of the technological change [84], the incumbents’ responses are guided by their intrinsic resources, processes, and cultural influences [85, 122].

In contrast to organizational ordinary (operational) capabilities, dynamic capabilities govern the rate of change in organizational ordinary capabilities and involve long-term commitment to specialized resources [41]. They are essential in (1) responding to disruptive technological shifts [114], (2) integrating e-business transformation with the existing operation of the business [15, 34], and (3) connecting with customers and suppliers in response to disruptive innovations [86]. Dynamic capabilities theory is an extension of the resource-based view of the firm [11, 46]. Research on dynamic capabilities has focused on technological innovation, adaptation, and strategic responses as streams of research to differentiate from the organizational (operational innovation) capabilities theory of a firm [125]. It has suggested that managers need to be able to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competencies to address changing environments [125], or “to use resources . . . to match or even to create market change” [46, p. 1107]. Dynamic capabilities are theorized as existing at different levels. The first-order dynamic capabilities “extend, modify, change and/or create ordinary capabilities” [41, p. 255]. In the absence of the firm’s ability to create the first-order dynamic capabilities, ordinary capabilities may well become core rigidities [85]. When deploying first-order dynamic capabilities may not be sufficient, a firm may need to adapt its current approach or adopt a completely new approach to develop second-order dynamic capabilities [33].<sup>4</sup> The second-order (or higher-order) dynamic capabilities enable spontaneous responsiveness in novel situations and in recessionary conditions [33, 131]. In theory, an infinite progression of orders of dynamic capabilities may exist; in practice, dynamic capabilities are unlikely to be sustained much beyond second-order [33, 131].

Organizational ordinary and dynamic capabilities have been shown to influence performance at firm and process levels through various mechanisms [18, 41, 128]. As a matter of normal business practice, firms actively manage their capabilities and, therefore, attempt to limit their downside risks and are likely to abandon or replace capabilities if performance falls below a certain threshold [41]. However, an exogenous change (such as a radical technological change) could at any time tip an existing cost–benefit balance of capability investment in favor of lower-order capabilities. Therefore, a firm (1) may employ a capability that is disconnected entirely from its profit mechanism(s), (2) may not achieve any return from the higher-level capability to cover its costs of usage, or (3) may use a capability that has a negative effect on performance [41, 131]. Our theoretical model investigates the effects of the first-order dynamic capabilities on response performance to digital disruption.

## Newspaper Companies’ Response to Digital Disruption

Recent research on how companies can transform themselves in response to disruptive innovations suggests that such transformation needs to happen as a result of two separate and simultaneous transformation efforts: (1) adapting the core business to the realities of the disrupted marketplace, and (2) establishing a “capabilities exchange” that allows for a new disruptive business to create the next source of growth by sharing resources with the core business without interfering with it [52]. The path forward for the incumbent companies will involve repositioning their core businesses by identifying the JOBS that their customers want them to perform more easily, conveniently, or affordably [129].<sup>5</sup> The above two transformation strategies have been adopted by many newspaper companies as their responses to disruption [5], and some have seen great success in generating new revenue streams [52, 69].

Accordingly, to respond to digital disruption, newspaper industry analysts and task forces have called for newspaper companies (1) to undertake digital transformation and act in a more complex value network (or “value web”) of business-to-consumer and business-to-business so as to better address the needs of the actors in their value network [15]; (2) to collaborate with other content providers, independent Internet companies, and value web partners to offer a variety of digital products, and to organize them into electronic databases and back office content management systems [52]. These will increase the quality and quantity of original digital content on platforms of consumers’ choice, and will go beyond the core products and core functions of reporting news and create a wide range of digital noncore products fulfilling JOBS [6]. The American Press Institute (API) views the newspaper industry’s core products as traditional print products that rely primarily on display, classified, or insert advertising sold by sales representatives to traditional advertisers, and that use traditional production and distribution methods (e.g., paid daily or weekly newspapers, and all other products based on a similar business model) [6]. Specifically, these include (1) print dailies and weeklies, (2) niche publications, either content-focused or advertising-focused, (3) news websites relying mainly on cost per thousand (CPM) (display) advertising and classified [6]. The digital noncore products, however, include (1) niche websites targeting users’ JOBS (such as community bulletin boards like craigslist, social networking portals, and user-ratings engines), (2) digital products that rely on new revenue streams (such as paid search, lead generation, and consumer direct marketing), and (3) digital products sold through nontraditional sales channels (such as self-serve advertising via Google AdWords) [6].

## First-Order Dynamic Capabilities and RPV Framework

Dynamic capabilities are shown to be the source of “variation” for technological innovation and adaptation among firms [32, 125]. Recent research on dynamic capabilities has suggested that first-order dynamic capabilities for new product development [57, 68, 100] or for IS project portfolio management [33] are created by changing, adapting, or extending a firm’s existing RPV. For example, recent research in product innovation capability has suggested that a firm’s organizational culture, structure, innovation process, and senior leadership lead to dynamic capabilities in terms of radical product innovation capability [115]. Disruptive innovation theory also suggests that an incumbent is able to develop new innovative products in responding to disruptive innovations if the development of these new innovative products can be accommodated by the incumbent’s resources (what it has), processes (how it works), and values (what it wants to do) [24]. Understanding how the first-order dynamic capabilities create innovation project management capabilities for building digital platform capabilities requires a process- or a project-level analysis rather than a firm-level analysis [33, 41], since processes are where the resources are deployed and where their first-order effects are expected to be realized [111]. The firm-level analysis, however, fails to provide information regarding how changing, adapting, or extending existing RPV can directly lead to innovation project management capabilities in terms of effective decision-making processes for managing innovation projects [33]. Therefore, we take a process- and project-level perspective and draw on disruptive innovations research and factorbased information technology (IT) innovation research to identify the key constituents of RPV that need to be changed, adapted, or extended to create first-order dynamic capabilities for managing innovation projects. Dedicated financial resources to fund innovation projects for enabling response to digital disruption are especially important because those projects are often seen as unlikely to generate sizable profits. Furthermore, disruptive innovation literature points to the importance of senior management support for innovation projects that are designed to enable response to disruption through not only stressing their strategic importance but also actively participating in those projects. For newspaper companies, this implies that senior management needs to go beyond words, and in addition to leaning back and reviewing improvements in the core products, senior management needs to lean forward and work on growth initiatives for noncore products [8, 63].

According to the RPV framework, processes that govern investment decisions are the most crucial processes in which many organizations’ most serious disabilities reside when attempting to respond to disruptive innovations [28]. Companies that made above-average progress on responding to disruptive innovations had allocated a greater extent of their resources toward business processes for digital transformation efforts than those who made below-average progress [7]. As shown in Table 1, there are two critical processes for enabling response to digital disruption in the context of the newspaper industry. The staged allocation of funding resources allows for committing small amounts of resources to multiple innovation projects, filtering out unpromising innovative ideas, and refining promising ones. In addition, establishing an autonomous growth group, business unit, or “heavyweight” development team is essential for creating new processes or ways of working together because individuals of those groups or teams often do not represent their functional group interests [75].

In the RPV framework, values are defined as standards by which employees make prioritization decisions for judging whether “a particular customer is more important or less important than another, whether an idea for a new product is attractive or marginal” [28, p. 185]. To successfully manage innovation projects, then, requires firms to build and sustain an innovation-supportive culture, which can be nurtured through introducing advantageous culture artifacts [61, 73]. As shown in Table 1, there are three critical cultural artifacts for developing an innovation-supportive culture in the context of the newspaper industry [5, 6, 7, 63]. Innovative culture derives from values and informs an underlying belief structure that reinforces the daily practices [73]. Since enabling response to digital disruption is risky and unpredictable, newspaper companies need to establish or reinforce values for identifying high-growth opportunities [6]. Companies with a common language for disruptive innovations are able to avoid some of the mental traps that make enabling response to digital disruption difficult. These traps include pursuing perfection when “good enough” is often sufficient, overestimating knowledge of new markets, and making big bets when it is better to begin with small ones [7]. For executives and top management teams, however, their mindsets guide their strategic decisions and actions. Newspaper companies, therefore, are increasingly challenged to adopt a multimedia mindset and view their business as a portfolio of products and services with different business models, pricing, and distribution strategies. This will allow them to develop a new mindset that does not favor the status quo and further develops the innovation competence needed to successfully develop and execute strategies to respond [62, 86].

As detailed in the next section, developing digital platform capabilities is a complex process encompassing various components of RPV that need to work in concert. Therefore, a more realistic representation of system development process requires that we consider how project-level RPV factors lead to building digital platform capabilities jointly rather than in isolation. This is consistent with the resource complementarity argument in the strategic management literature, which suggests that the presence of one resource enhances the value of another resource, and one may have little or no value without the other [11]. As such, we posit that first-order dynamic capabilities should be conceptualized holistically in order to account for the complementary combinations of project-level RPV factors for building digital platform capabilities. Our approach benefits from recent research on the dynamics of digital ecosystems in a turbulent environment, which suggests that digital ecodynamics requires a holistic convergence among dynamic capabilities, IT systems, and environmental turbulence [45].

## Digital Platform Capabilities

Developing digital noncore products requires new digital platform capabilities that many newspaper companies do not necessarily possess. Gawer [48] defines platform as the building blocks that act as a foundation for developing complementary products, technologies, or services by an array of firms. Digital platforms organize IT capabilities into software frameworks for integration of transactions across diverse consumers and businesses [16, 59, 92, 120]. Digital platform capabilities provide standards, connectivity, rules, and IT capabilities for mediating the production, search, and delivery of digital content and information goods among users of digital platform ecosystems. Although digital (product) platforms extend the functionality of products, there are a limited set of complementary products that could be developed using the basic features of the platforms, and such extensions are typically regulated by firms owning the platforms [95].<sup>6</sup> Since digital platform users transact with each other at the same time they transact across digital platforms, the focus of value creation shifts from value chain to value networks [94]. This in turn has induced new forms of business and digital strategies [16, 53, 100]. To pursue a digital strategy, newspaper companies need to be “digital first and print last” by building compelling digital noncore products with original content on platforms of their customers’ choice [76]. Digital strategy, however, does not mean web only or discontinuation of print. It means continuous development, and not just development around the schedule of print products, to ensure timeliness of content on websites or mobile platforms throughout the day. Some industry observers now believe that pursuing a digital strategy will result in (1) more audience (crowd) engagement by increasing the quality of shared content, (2) more robust growth of digital platforms and audiences, (3) more efficient cost structure by harnessing “both the cloud and the crowd,” (4) more revenue opportunities, and (5) more profits by allocating resources to cost-effective creation of content and sales and not just to the legacy mode of production [76]. Pursuing such a strategy, however, demands development of a multiplatform or platform-free mindset by considering all forms of content (e.g., video, audio, article, photo, interactive features) when planning a reporting project. It will require developing a digital-first mindset, making digital strategy everyone’s job, cultivating an open culture that embraces digital innovations [1], and ultimately building digital platform capabilities.

To respond to emerging threats and opportunities associated with digital disruption, API’s Newspaper Next (N2) project has recommended that newspaper companies create two new digital platform capabilities. These are platforms to connect to consumers and platforms to connect to businesses. Building these digital platform capabilities is similar to digital transformations that are taking place in many industries [15, 80]. This will allow newspaper companies to create new disruptive businesses and new sources for growth by concentrating on digital value creation and by restructuring them around their core functions when developing digital noncore products [5, 6, 53].

Platforms to connect to consumers: Building digital platform capabilities to unlock the community’s collective wisdom supports those information needs of the local community that require tapping into prior experiences, opinions, and knowledge of people in the community (e.g., finding good home contractors or restaurants) [5, 6]. Besides incorporating user-generated content and citizen journalism on their existing websites, newspaper companies are increasingly challenged to reach new audiences through building digital community platforms (e.g., photo- and video-sharing tools, bulletin boards, wikis, etc.) that allow virtual communities to self-form [5, 12, 52].<sup>7</sup>

Platforms to connect to businesses: Newspaper companies often have extensive local data about institutions, businesses, and local residents (e.g., databases for parks, medical facilities, and restaurant menus). However, most of this information is not organized for easy public access or search. Over time, newspaper companies could develop a range of digital noncore products such as

numerous print and Internet business solutions to reach general and niche audiences; a broad and growing assortment of digital and online marketing solutions including but not limited to targeting, lead generation, promotions, email, paid search, self-serve ad creation, web site optimization, video, audio, mobile and other emerging technologies and media; a range of other services, including customer loyalty programs, back-office tools and platforms, transactional services, product delivery services, public events and other businesssupport services, and whatever else will meet important unsatisfied businesses “JOBS-to-be-done.” [5, p. 9].

These will help them implement new revenue and service models that could have the broadest appeal to businesses in multiple sectors or verticals and could reach large numbers of nonconsumers [12, 52].

## Hypotheses

Creating growth businesses can happen only if companies allocate necessary resources toward innovation projects [8]. A greater synergy among financial and human resources, and senior management support is necessary for setting up the staged allocation of resources and for giving the right autonomy to innovation projects [8, 63, 64]. Having a shared perspective or values on key innovation principles can dramatically change the way organizations think as well as strengthen their ability to identify and develop new ideas [6, 99]. Most innovative organizations have effectively infused a pro-innovation value system throughout their companies’ processes, and their employees behave according to it [99].<sup>8</sup> Resource complementarity implies that project-level RPV should be conceptualized holistically to create first-order dynamic capabilities for managing innovation projects. In the dynamic capability literature, resource-picking mechanism refers to a firm’s capability to create rent by effective selection of resources from the firm’s own pool especially in the right combinations with other resources and to guide decision making. Disruptive innovation theory also suggests that RPV factors collectively determine organizational capabilities or disabilities in responding to disruptive innovations. Dynamic capabilities contribute to performance by reducing costs associated with providing products and services, improving their quality, and increasing their revenue [41]. Therefore, one would expect that the higher a firm’s ability to collectively change, adapt, or extend its existing RPV to create first-order dynamic capabilities for managing innovation projects, the higher its response performance will be in terms of digital revenue generated from all online sources. Therefore, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 1: The greater the extent of the first-order dynamic capabilities, the higher a newspaper company’s response performance will be in terms of digital revenue generated from all online sources.

Dynamic capabilities also contribute to performance by allowing the firm to identify and respond to opportunities through developing new processes, products, and services [41]. By changing a firm’s processes, products and services, and customer relations, these capabilities can have positive contributions to firm performance at both the process and firm levels [41]. First-order dynamic capabilities are created by changing core innovation processes and exploiting resources to search for new opportunities and by cultivating values to promote innovative risk-taking and creative actions. A firm is able to create first-order dynamic capabilities in terms of innovation project management capability by changing, adapting, or extending its existing RPV [33]. Innovation project management capability is necessary for building digital platform capabilities because without developing such capabilities a firm may not be able to improve the speed, effectiveness, and efficiency with which to respond to digital disruption.<sup>9</sup> Therefore, one would expect that the higher a firm’s ability to create first-order dynamic capabilities for managing innovation projects, the higher its ability will be to build digital platform capabilities for responding to digital disruption. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 2: The greater the extent of first-order dynamic capabilities, the higher a newspaper company’s ability will be to build digital platform capabilities in response to digital disruption.

Newspaper companies can build audience by building digital platform capabilities for fulfilling JOBS beyond news [6, 52]. Building relevant databases and platforms for collective conversation and wisdom can open excellent opportunities to help nonconsumers and small and medium-sized businesses get their information JOBS done better than any existing solutions [5, 52]. The nonconsumers and “low end” of the business market represent the largest growth opportunities for newspaper companies in local markets [6]. Digital platform capabilities enable newspaper companies to progress faster toward digital transformations by offering digital products on platforms of a consumers’ choice, by engaging them in new ways, and by better connecting to communities and communicating digitally, which is the way that most conversations are conducted in today’s digital age. It will also enable them to add new services to the core physical products for businesses by offering new digital products and opportunities, and by creating new digital insights, experiences, and revenue streams.<sup>10</sup> Without developing digital platform capabilities, it will not be possible for newspaper companies to lead digital transformation efforts. Thus, we hypothesize that the higher a newspaper company’s ability to build digital platform capabilities, the higher its response performance will be in terms of number of digital noncore products. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 3: The greater the extent of digital platform capabilities, the higher a newspaper company’s response performance will be in terms of number of digital noncore products.

The literature on IT capabilities has emphasized the impact of IT capabilities on new product development for integration with suppliers and customers [102, 110] and on innovation creation process and performance [18, 68, 74, 78, 128]. However, such impacts depend on a firm’s ability to change, adapt, or extend its existing RPV to effectively manage innovation projects. Without this ability, the existing RPV factors may well become core rigidities [85]. For example, financial and human resources affect the number of digital noncore products only to the extent that these resources are used effectively in managing innovation projects for building appropriate digital platform capabilities. Similarly, staged allocation of resources and autonomous growth groups have effects on the number of digital noncore products only to the extent that these processes are adapted effectively to improve decision making for the deployment of digital platform capabilities.<sup>11</sup> First-order dynamic capabilities are created by changing, adapting, or extending existing RPV to create innovation project management capabilities for effectively managing innovation projects. However, to build digital platform capabilities, firms must first develop such capabilities. Therefore, one would expect that the existing RPV may not necessarily be directly related to the number of digital noncore products because developing digital noncore products ultimately depends on a firm’s ability to change, adapt, or extend its existing RPV to create innovation project management capability. This capability is needed for the effective management of innovation projects for building digital platform capabilities. Therefore, one would expect that building digital platform capabilities should mediate the impact of first-order dynamic capabilities on a newspaper company’s response performance in terms of number of digital noncore products. Hence, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 4: The impact of first-order dynamic capabilities on response performance in terms of number of digital noncore products is mediated by a newspaper company’s ability to build digital platform capabilities.

## Research Method

Guided by the literature, we developed our research model as depicted in Figure 1. This model was tested through a web survey. The theoretical model in Figure 1 includes patterns of interaction between dynamic capabilities and the performance of newspaper companies’ response to digital disruption.

## Measures

First-order dynamic capabilities (FODCAP) are measured by assessing the strengths and weaknesses of a newspaper company’s RPV for effectively managing innovation projects for responding to digital disruption. FODCAP is created (formed) by changing, extending, or adapting a firm’s existing RPV. Each of the three components of RPV has been individually studied in the strategic management and IS strategy literature. However, FODCAP for managing innovation projects is complex, structured, and multidimensional. It is, therefore, defined as a multidimensional construct in an aggregate model per multidimensional construct structure rules discussed in Wong, Law, and Huang [132], and measured as a multidimensional construct with three formative subdimensions: RESOURCES, PROCESSES, and VALUES. Formative rather than reflective subdimensions are used because the subdimensions of FODCAP describe different facets of the construct and are not conceptually interchangeable. As such, they do not necessarily correlate highly with each other. Furthermore, removing any of the subdimensions would alter the conceptual meaning of FODCAP.

Resources (RESOURCES) assess the extent to which a newspaper company has dedicated financial resources (DFR), dedicated human resources (DHR), and senior management support (SMS) for developing noncore products. RESOURCES is measured as a second-order reflective construct with three reflective factors: DFR, DHR, and SMS.

Processes (PROCESSES) assess the extent to which a newspaper company has established processes for staged allocation of resources (SAR) for developing noncore products and has autonomous growth group (AGG). It is measured as a second-order reflective construct with two first-order reflective factors: SAR and AGG. SAR assesses the extent to which a newspaper company takes a trial-and-error approach to innovation projects. AGG measures the extent of the growth group’s ability to be self-directed and the extent of its authority to make decisions in developing noncore products.

Values (VALUES) assess the extent to which a newspaper company has established innovative culture (IC), a common language (CL), and a multimedia mindset (MM) necessary for establishing an innovation-supportive culture. It is measured as a second order reflective construct with three first order factors: IC, CL, and MM. IC measures the extent to which a newspaper company encourages experimentation and rewards innovative behavior. CL assesses whether (1) various stakeholders in a newspaper company understand and have a shared perspective on the key innovation principles, (2) they see their roles in it, and (3) the core concepts reflecting the key innovation principles are built into company documents and stakeholders share the same principles. MM evaluates the extent to which a newspaper company has defined its role and its business as delivering a portfolio of products and services to satisfy consumers’ needs for news, information, entertainment, and communication.

Digital platform capabilities (DPCAP) assess the degree to which a newspaper company has built digital platforms to connect to consumers (PCC) and to connect to businesses (PCB). DPCAP is measured as a second-order reflective construct with two first-order reflective factors: PCC and PCB.

Response performance (RSPRF) is measured by assessing a newspaper company’s performance in producing and selling digital noncore products as a response to digital disruption. By introducing a different set of features, performance attributes, and prices, the digital noncore products will allow newspaper companies to serve the needs of a new value network of customers and businesses via digital platform capabilities [4]. Developing digital noncore products to support digital strategy depends on having the freedom to create a unique cost structure and value network to be profitable in selling its earliest products. The response performance, therefore, needs to measure the quantity of digital noncore products and the revenue generated from such products. Therefore, the number of digital noncore products and the digital revenue generated from all online sources are considered to be important indicators of newspaper companies’ response performance to digital disruption.<sup>12</sup> Therefore, we measure the number of digital noncore products on a monthly basis $( \mathsf { R S P R F } _ { 1 } )$ and the percentage of revenue from all online sources $( \mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 } )$ . Because the two indicators of RSPRF are hypothesized to have different antecedents, RSPRF cannot be measured as a reflective construct; it can only be measured as a formative construct [65,103]. The complete final list of items for the ten constructs and for the dependent variable is listed in Supplement B. As discussed in Supplement C, a rigorous construct development and validation process, which included a pilot study, was followed to ensure construct validity and to ward off common method bias [31].

For control variables, firm size (SIZE) and first year of online publishing (YEAR) are used. YEAR values range from 1 (before 1990) to 4 (after 2000). Consequently, the smaller the response, the earlier the newspaper firm started online publishing. YEAR is a control variable for DPCAP. SIZE is measured with five variables that all reflect a firm’s size: weekday circulation, weekend circulation, number of employees exclusively for web operations, total number of employees, and annual revenue. SIZE is a control variable for DPCAP, $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ , and ${ \mathrm { R S P R F } } _ { 2 }$ .

## Data Collection Procedures

We determined that self-reported data from senior executives of newspaper companies would be reliable and free from common method bias. This is because: (1) the questionnaire items address the RPV of newspaper companies; such data can be obtained only from self-report; (2) the dependent variables measure revenue breakdown and number of noncore products; such information can be obtained only from executives of newspaper companies; and (3) both the independent and dependent variables tend to be objective in nature. Respondents were contacted through e-mails and data were collected through a web survey. To collect e-mail addresses, we used a website that had a comprehensive listing of all local newspapers in the United States by state, from very small local newspapers with under 25,000 in circulation to very large local newspapers with over 1,000,000 in circulation. We visited websites of all local newspapers listed and manually collected names, e-mail addresses, and job titles of senior executives of each newspaper, if its website listed such information. The e-mail addresses were divided into four groups according to time zones. Each solicitation was e-mailed according to the receiver’s local time between 8:30 a.m. and 9:30 a.m. on weekdays. To improve response rate, the solicitation e-mail addressed each recipient individually. On the survey, respondents were instructed to answer each question as a representative of the newspaper as opposed to basing replies on purely personal views. The instructions stated that respondents could leave the survey at any time and that their responses would be completely anonymous and confidential. Similar to the pilot study for the measurement validation process (see Supplement C), for control purposes, the web survey recorded IP addresses, date/time survey started, date/time survey submitted, and a session ID for each survey. Respondent job titles and newspapers-working-for information were collected for further data control. A total of 158 responses were collected after three rounds of e-mail solicitations. However, due to a server problem, 10 data points were lost, resulting in a total of 148 responses. No submitted survey took less than five minutes. Using the IP addresses and newspaper names of respondents, we eliminated 10 data points where a respondent had a lower job title than an existing respondent from the same newspaper and 2 data points with no job title or newspaper-workingfor information but the IP address matched an existing respondent’s. This resulted in 136 usable responses, all from different newspapers. This represented approximately 8–9 percent of all U.S. daily newspapers, which numbered 1,500–1,700 [67, 96]. Table 2 lists job titles of respondents for both the pilot study (see Supplement C) and the main study, which shows that respondent job titles are similar between the two data sets. For the main study, 117 out of 136 respondents have titles of publisher, president, chief executive officer (CEO), chief financial officer (CFO), senior vice president of marketing or related functions, or director of marketing or related functions. Comparing combined data of job titles and newspapers-worked-for between the pilot data and the main data showed no overlapping respondents between the two data collections.

## Measurement Mode

The measurement and path models were tested using partial least squares (PLS) structural equation modeling software, SmartPLS 2.0 Beta [113]. Psychometric properties of reflective constructs (all first- and second-order constructs) were assessed by examining internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. SmartPLS calculates all factor loadings and t-statistics, cross-loadings, average variance extracted (AVE), Cronbach’s alphas, and composite reliability scores. Item means and standard deviations for measurement items were obtained using SPSS. Internal consistency was evaluated by examining Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability score. Values higher than 0.70 for both Cronbach’s alphas and composite reliability scores indicate that internal consistency is strong. As seen in Tables 5 and 6 for those constructs, Cronbach’s alpha values range from 0.75 to 0.94 for first-order reflective constructs and from 0.84 to 0.89 for second-order reflective constructs, showing strong internal consistencies. Composite reliability scores range from 0.85 to 0.96 for first-order constructs and from 0.88 to 0.91 for second-order constructs, also indicating high internal consistencies.

Concerning common method bias, statistical remedies have been proposed in the literature and have been used by researchers to rule out common method bias. These remedies include Harman’s single-factor test, partialling out a “marker” variable, and partialling out a common method factor [e.g., 89, 105, 132]. All three remedies were performed and the results passed suggested thresholds for all three. However, recent studies have questioned or discredited the validity of statistical remedies [22, 31, 112, 130]. They have generally proposed or favored the control of common method biases through the design of the study’s procedures, which includes a careful consideration of data or rating source, rigorous construct development process, demonstrated construct validity, and best practice in survey administration such as psychological separation of measurements, anonymity and confidentiality guarantees, and reduction of apprehension [31, 104]. Since, as discussed in the measurement development and data collection sections and as demonstrated by the construct validity and reliability testing results, our research design followed those rigorous processes, we conclude that common method bias is not a concern to the validity of our empirical findings.

## Structural Model

Table 10 lists descriptive statistics of both indicators of the dependent variable, which shows that the majority of newspaper firms reported one to ten noncore products on a monthly basis and 1–10 percent of revenue coming from all online sources.

RSPRF measures a newspaper company’s overall performance in responding to digital disruption in terms of number of digital noncore products $( \mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 } )$ and percentage revenue from all online sources $( \mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 } )$ . As mentioned earlier, $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \mathrm { R S P R F } } _ { 2 }$ are formative indicators of RSPRF. FODCAP affects ${ \mathrm { R S P R F } } _ { 2 }$ directly $\left( \operatorname { H } _ { 1 } \right)$ . FODCAP affects DPCAP positively $\left( \operatorname { H } _ { 2 } \right)$ and DPCAP in turn impacts $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ positively $\left( \operatorname { H } _ { 3 } \right)$ . Furthermore, FODCAP does not impact $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ directly; its impact on $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ is achieved through DPCAP $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 4 } )$ . This means that impacts of FODCAP and DPCAP on $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ are different from those on ${ \mathrm { R S P R F } } _ { 2 }$ . Those hypotheses were tested by using two separate dependent variables: $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 } .$ . Significance tests were done using a bootstrap procedure with $1 { , } 0 0 0$ subsamples for 136 cases. Figure 2 shows the testing results. All path coefficients are positive, indicating positive relationships between the predictor and the dependent variable, as hypothesized. FODCAP has a significant direct impact on $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 } ~ ( \beta = 0 . 1 9$ , one-tailed $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ , therefore $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 }$ is supported. There is a strong and significant effect of FODCAP on DPCAP $( \beta = 0 . 5 2$ , onetailed $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , therefore $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ is supported. The direct effect of DPCAP on $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ $\left( \operatorname { H } _ { 3 } \right)$ is medium in size $( \beta = 0 . 1 9 )$ and statistically significant (one-tailed $p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , therefore $\mathrm { H } _ { 3 }$ is supported. Our model explains 17.8 percent variance in $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ and 15.1 percent variance in ${ \mathrm { R S P R F } } _ { 2 }$ . To see the effect on aggregated response performance, we also ran the model by directly modeling RSPRF with two formative indicators. All paths to either $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 1 }$ or $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 }$ were modeled as paths to RSPRF. This model explains 21.7 percent variance in RSPRF. (Path coefficients of RSPRF → RSPRF and of $\mathrm { R S P R F } _ { 2 }  \mathrm { R S P R F }$ examined for formative construct validity in the Measurement Model section were obtained using this model.) ## Discussion and Implications

Three research questions were examined in this study: (1) What are newspaper companies’ key RPV factors that create first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption? (2) What is the role of such capabilities in building digital platform capabilities? and (3) Do digital platform capabilities mediate the impact of first-order dynamic capabilities on the performance of newspaper companies’ response to digital disruption? Using a sample data from newspaper senior executives, we found direct associations between first-order dynamic capabilities and the revenue generated from digital noncore products $\mathrm { ( H _ { 1 } }$ supported), between first-order dynamic capabilities and building digital platform capabilities $( \mathrm { H } _ { 2 }$ supported), and between building digital platform capabilities and number of digital noncore products $\left( \mathrm { H } _ { 3 } \right.$ supported). The majority (78 percent) of the effect of first-order dynamic capabilities on response performance is achieved indirectly through building digital platform capabilities when it comes to producing digital noncore products $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 4 }$ supported). The same effect is achieved mostly directly when it comes to percentage of revenue coming from all online sources. As such, the number of digital noncore products is more directly impacted by digital platform capabilities than by first-order dynamic capabilities, and the percentage of revenue from online sources is more directly impacted by first-order dynamic capabilities than by building digital platform capabilities.

## Theoretical Implications

This study makes several contributions to IS research and practice. It contributes to disruptive innovation theory by identifying, operationalizing, and measuring key constituents of RPV that need to be changed, extended, or adapted to create firstorder dynamic capabilities and to build digital platform capabilities in response to digital disruption. Although disruptive innovation theory has proved helpful in understanding the pattern of entrant-incumbent dynamics in responding to disruptive innovations in a variety of settings [86], this is the first empirical study that has examined the role of first-order dynamic capabilities in responding to digital disruption in the context of the newspaper industry. By empirically validating the mediating role of digital platform capabilities, this study has identified anomalies in the theory.

The literature on disruptive innovations has not focused on operationalizing the underlying RPV that need to be changed, extended, or adapted in order to build digital platform capabilities in response to digital disruption. By empirically examining the microfoundations of digital platform capabilities, this work contributes to the extant literature. In addition, much of the research on key assertions of the resource-based view of the firms has not taken a holistic approach and has focused on individual resources or capabilities to guide theory building and theory testing [111]. This study contributes to the strategic management and IS literature on dynamic capabilities by establishing that the higher a newspaper company’s ability to collectively reconfigure its resource base by changing, adapting, or extending its existing RPV, the higher its response performance will be to digital disruption. The findings from this study suggest that responding to digital disruption is dependent on holistic combinations of constituents of RPV. Since first-order dynamic capabilities predicates building digital platform capabilities, this study suggests that measuring the first-order dynamic capabilities as a composite construct is not only theoretically but also empirically meaningful. Our research model thus pinpoints how response to digital disruption is affected directly and indirectly by first-order dynamic capabilities. These findings call for future research to investigate whether the relationships between first-order dynamic capabilities and digital platform capabilities vary across contexts. Managing “the flows” between them should be the focus of attention for academics and practitioners alike.

Digital transformation can help various industries by enhancing, extending, and redefining their physical or traditional products and services through digital content, by reshaping the value propositions delivered to their customers, and by creating new revenue streams to ensure their survival [15, 107]. The music industry is one of the first to feel the impact of digital revolution brought about by the forces of mobility, social media, digitization, and the resulting changes in customer expectations. These same forces are disrupting the newspaper industry and pushing it, along with almost all other consumer-serving industries, toward creating more digital content, higher degree of product and service digitization, and deeper digital transformation [15, 120]. The increased fragmentation of the market further demands digital innovations for offering content to consumers, for cocreating services, applications, and content, and for creating new alliances [9, 20]. This study has suggested that by building digital platform capabilities, newspaper companies will be able to develop digital noncore products to enhance and extend their core print products and services. For the newspaper industry, which offers a mix of print and digital products, new revenue streams can result from new digital noncore products [135]. Our results are deemed generalizable to other industries with different degrees of product or service digitization or those with different mixes of physical and digital products. These include those industries with primarily physical products (such as consumer or industrial products), those with mixed digital and physical products (such as consumer electronics, automotive, health care), and those with primarily digital products (such as financial markets, music, software applications) [15]. To capture the value from digital transformation, firms need to go beyond small applications and toward truly digital enterprises. Since virtually all firms use digital technology in some form, no business is immune to digital disruption, and the evolution of digital technology will present unique opportunities and challenges for all business executives [15]. We encourage future researchers to examine and extend our findings to other technological and organizational contexts to further evaluate the generalizability of our results. As more individuals embrace the digita revolution, more and more businesses are now creating and delivering digital content and are undertaking their own digital transformations by offering more digital products, conducting more business operations digitally, and generating deeper digital insights. Future research needs to investigate how to (1) apply crowdsourcing, collective intelligence, and open innovation concepts for developing digital platform capabilities in order to realize new business models and to drive digital innovations, (2) create innovative digital products, platforms, tools, and technologies to respond to digital disruption and enhance the digital ecosystem of value, (3) keep the digital platforms and core IT infrastructure capabilities as a single coherent infrastructure so as to maximize synergies and deliver consistent services to all consumers and nonconsumers within a particular value network, (4) evolve dynamic capabilities to respond to disruptive innovations, coordinate, and bring along an entire ecosystem of customers and businesses on incremental innovations on a large scale, and (5) digitize for agility over efficiency by embracing dynamics of digital ecosystems and connecting to customers and suppliers to rapidly respond to changing conditions. Future research should also investigate how to integrate digitization and digita strategy with business model innovation strategies to (1) bring JOBS focus to all digital products where one does not exist, (2) find out what criteria customers use to evaluate digital products, (3) improve customers’ ability to use these products, and (4) find out what competing technology solutions they consider. This is because (1) disruptive innovations create new opportunities that are almost always associated with new products and services served by new business models, and (2) many incumbent firms have managed to respond to disruptive innovations before being disrupted through developing new business models [21, 52, 61, 86, 129]. For the newspaper companies, the legacy print business model is too costly; print newspaper revenue is shrinking while digital revenue is growing; and the consumers and advertisers prefer digital formats [105]. Consequently, they are struggling to find sustainable business models in an era of digitization, free content, and citizen journalists [52]. In addition, future research should examine management’s efforts through which the individual resources, processes, and values are integrated to impact the extent of their realized synergy and compatibility [97], and the organizational contexts for their interactions.

## Managerial Implications

Responding to digital disruption is not easy, especially because most newspaper companies “have no tradition of innovation, no innovation budgets, no product development processes, and no experience in seeking out new customer needs” [5, p. 78]. Nevertheless, they have tremendous RPV for producing core products, and if they can establish “capabilities exchange” for producing digital noncore products [52], they may be able to become the dominant players in tomorrow’s modern CIME landscape [26]. This study suggests that they will be able to do so through changing, adapting, or extending their existing RPV to create first-order dynamic capabilities and to build digital platform capabilities for producing digital noncore products. Rather than investing in the status quo, resources need to be appropriately allocated to build digital platform capabilities for new markets and value networks for their digital products. They also need to find out whether processes for their core products are appropriate for developing digital noncore products and whether their values give them the priority they need to reconfigure their capabilities. If not, values need to be changed first since values ultimately determine what processes are in place. Changing values, however, can be very difficult. Not only do they need to change their predominant print culture, but they also need to change their news-gathering processes to increase audience engagement and build unique values for their digital products. They can do so by connecting with their communities using a variety of crowdsourcing techniques to develop deeper source networks and by emphasizing industry standards for promoting platform agility for their digital products [52]. They also need to audit their existing digital platform capabilities for core products and develop a plan for addressing identified weaknesses for developing digital products. These steps will allow them to deliver the right information to the right user on the right device and to grow beyond the core business with value-added services. Although integrating digital operations into physical (core print) business can create additional value by providing multichannel capabilities for customers and sharing infrastructure, attracting and retaining digital talent in a traditional culture can be very difficult. Advancing digital agenda may take a lot of management time and attention to develop effective multichannel digital and business strategies and to decide whether to keep digital businesses separate or integrate them with the current nondigital ones.

## Limitations

Like any empirical research effort, this study contains a number of methodological strengths and limitations. The breadth of the sample included in this study suggests that the findings are fairly generalizable to many newspaper companies. However, the findings are limited in some ways. This study was the first attempt to create an instrument to operationalize the RPV concepts based on disruptive innovation theory. To do so, we used an inductive approach in conducting the preliminary research of the relevant academic literature and in reviewing the normative advice given to newspaper company executives by the industry association and the subject matter experts. This approach was essential in examining how the focal constructs have been used in prior research or by practitioners. Although subsequently the instrument was rigorously validated, adaptation is needed for it to be used in other industry contexts. Furthermore, all measures are self-reported and, therefore, are subject to hindsight and other biases. We did not consider the institutional effect of considering a single industry. Another limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the study and the fact that we captured only a snapshot of the newspaper companies RPV and digital platform capabilities.

## Summary and Conclusions

Despite the extensive coverage of disruptive innovation theory in the innovation and IS literature, the emphasis in the theory has always been on nontechnology factors and little attention has been paid to the enabling role of technology in responding to disruptive innovations. This study has synthesized the knowledge from the strategic management literature on dynamic capabilities and IS literature on digitization, digital transformation, and dynamics of digital ecosystems and integrated that knowledge into literature on disruptive innovations in order to build on disruptive innovation theory and gain new insights. In the extant disruptive innovation literature, there is a tendency to offer partial explanations by focusing on the role of each individual element of RPV in responding to disruption. This paper has proposed a more holistic explanation for how changing, adapting, or extending the key constituents of RPV directly creates first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption. Using empirical data, it has demonstrated that (1) first-order dynamic capabilities have a direct association with building digital platform capabilities and with newspaper companies’ response to digital disruption in terms of revenue generated from all online sources, and (2) digital platform capabilities mediate the relationship between first-order dynamic capabilities and newspaper companies’ response performance in terms of number of noncore products. In doing so, it has provided new insights into contributions, limitations, and anomalies of disruptive innovation theory. Given that the effects of first-order dynamic capabilities on responding to disruptive innovations have not been demonstrated in prior research, we hope our study has provided some directions for future research in disruptive innovation theory. By empirically examining the microfoundations of digital platform capabilities, this work has also contributed to the extant IS literature in revealing how reconfiguring a firm’s resource base creates first-order dynamic capabilities for responding to digital disruption. In doing so, this study has provided a foundation for future IS research in digital infrastructures and digital innovations, and in developing digital platform strategies by identifying digital transformation drivers that are necessary for building digital platform capabilities. As digital transformation is taking place, more and more businesses are increasingly being disrupted by digitization. Reinventing the IT function to deliver digital products and services can provide a new competitive advantage for incumbents in every business.
