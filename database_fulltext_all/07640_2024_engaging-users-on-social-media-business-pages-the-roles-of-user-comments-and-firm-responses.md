# ENGAGING USERS ON SOCIAL MEDIA BUSINESS PAGES: THE ROLES OF USER COMMENTS AND FIRM RESPONSES<sup>1</sup>

Xiaoye Cheng Xiaoye Cheng

Department of Accounting and Management Information Systems, Alfred Lerner College of Business and Economics, University of Delaware, Newark, DE, U.S.A. {xycheng@udel.edu}

Hillol Bala

Department of Operations and Decision Technologies, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A. {hbala@indiana.edu}

Mochen Yang Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota – Twin Cities, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {yang3653@umn.edu}

Firms must strategically manage their responses to user comments to keep users engaged on their social media business pages. The question of whether, how, and when a firm should respond to user comments to achieve favorable outcomes is of great interest to researchers and practitioners. We focus on these questions and study the effects of initial user comments and firm responses on subsequent user engagement on social media business pages. In particular, we theorize and examine how two features of initial user comments (i.e., sentiment and controversialness) and two features of firm responses (i.e., uniqueness and timeliness) jointly affect the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments. By analyzing data from the Facebook business pages of multiple U.S. retailers (10,312 firm posts from 37 firms and over 1 million user comments), we found that firms are more likely to respond to negative comments (than positive or neutral comments) but less likely to respond to controversial comments (which evoke diverse opinions and emotions). Further, we found that engaging with negative and controversial comments and promptly responding to comments are linked to an increase in the volume of subsequent user comments but also to a more negative sentiment in these comments. We also found that providing unique responses improves the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments. Our findings offer theoretical and practical insights into firms’ response management on social media.

Keywords: Social media management, firm response, user engagement, firm business page, user comments, sentiment, controversialness

## Introduction

Firms respond to user comments on social media to provide services and enhance customer relationships (Balawi et al., 2023; Gunarathne et al., 2018). People who follow a firm on social media expect attentive and timely responses from the firm on social media platforms. Given that responding to all user comments would be too costly and time-consuming (Yang et al., 2019b), firms need to decide carefully which comments to respond to and how responses should be crafted effectively in a timely manner to increase customer satisfaction and avoid public relations risks. How firms deliberately respond to user comments on their social media business pages could influence how users engage with them. Effective user engagement can yield positive outcomes such as continuous user involvement (Lin & Lu, 2011), improved customer trust and loyalty (Dessart, 2017), enhanced brand relationships (Carlson et al., 2019), and heightened purchase intentions and behaviors (Hollebeek et al., 2014; Prentice et al., 2019). Although firms strive to cultivate positive user engagement and prevent negative bandwagons in engagement, there has been limited understanding in research and practice with respect to firms’ responses to user comments. Therefore, examining the dynamics that underlie the relationships among user comments, firm responses, and user engagement on social media business pages has become imperative.

The findings from prior research focusing on firm responses to online reviews could not be generalized directly to the context of social media business pages because online reviews and social media user comments differ in terms of source, topics, and intended audience (Yang et al., 2019a). Firms may have different objectives and apply distinct response strategies across the two contexts. The extant studies on the impact of social media firm responses on user engagement have mainly focused on firms’ response decisions (e.g., Ma et al., 2015; Mousavi et al., 2020) and response content strategies such as apology and compensation (e.g., Herhausen et al., 2019). Given that other response features such as response uniqueness that could potentially influence the engaging behaviors of subsequent users have largely been overlooked in prior research, we lack a holistic understanding of whether, how, and when a firm should respond to user comments for favorable outcomes. Features of initial user comments that could shape a firm’s response decision and engender subsequent user comments are also underexplored in the literature. These subsequent comments represent an integral part of user engagement in firms’ social media presence. The objective of our research is to provide a comprehensive characterization and understanding of firms’ response strategies on their social media business pages. In particular, we study the impact of user comment features and firm response features on subsequent user comments in tandem to address two research questions:

What types of user comments on firm posts are more likely to receive a firm response?

How do features of initial user comments and firm responses affect the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments? Does the timeliness of a firm’s response matter in influencing subsequent user engagement?

We develop hypotheses based on the attention-based view (ABV) of the firm and the literature on negativity bias, controversy, and user engagement. We characterize user engagement by the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments, two important dimensions of user engagement (Lee et al., 2018). Two features of user comments and two features of firms’ responses are theorized and examined. Comment sentiment and comment controversialness (i.e., the extent to which a user comment evokes diverse sentiments and opinions) may affect a firm’s response decision and subsequent user engagement. Response uniqueness represents the distinct or novel informational value in a firm’s response and response timeliness reflects the speed of a firm’s response, which together indicate response quality that could affect subsequent user engagement.

We analyzed data collected from the Facebook business pages of multiple U.S. retailers using econometrics models, resulting in three major findings. First, negative comments are more likely to be responded to by a firm, whereas controversial comments are less likely to receive a response. Second, responding to negative or controversial comments and responding quickly are associated with more subsequent user comments but a more negative sentiment in these comments. Third, unique responses are associated with both a larger volume and a more positive sentiment in subsequent comments. This study contributes to the literature on firm social media management by examining these comment features and response features in tandem. Moreover, we theorize and quantify two novel responding strategies, responding to controversial comments and response uniqueness, as antecedent factors of user engagement on firms’ social media pages. Finally, we complement the literature that addresses the impact of comment sentiment and response timeliness on user engagement in the context of social media. We offer practical implications with respect to response strategies that firms can leverage to reach their engagement objectives on their social media pages. Our findings provide insights to platforms on improving system design and help social media users better understand firms’ response strategies.

## Related Work

The literature on firm responses (also called management responses or managerial responses) can be divided into two streams: (1) antecedents of firm responses (i.e., how do the comment/review features and commenter/reviewer characteristics influence the likelihood of a user comment/review receiving a firm response?) and (2) the impact of firm responses (i.e., how do the adoption and features of firm responses influence the attitudes and behaviors of subsequent users or firm performance?). These questions have been studied in two contexts: online review and social media. Our study is related to both streams of research. Studies on the antecedents of firm responses have mainly explored the impact of comment (review) features (e.g., comment sentiment) and commenter (reviewer) characteristics (e.g., social status of a commenter). Sentiments have been studied in both social media and online review contexts, with inconsistent findings however (e.g., Gunarathne et al., 2015; Proserpio & Zervas, 2017). That is, as firms may have different response strategies for online review platforms and social media platforms, they could have different priorities in dealing with each sentiment type. More research is thus needed to discern which sentiment type is more likely to be responded to in the context of firms’ social media business pages.

The stream of work addressing the impact of firm responses has primarily focused on the impacts on user engagement, customer satisfaction, and firm financial performance. A few comment, response, and commenter features have been investigated in the social media context, including the adoption of response (e.g., Ma et al., 2015), responding to different comment sentiment types (e.g., Mousavi et al., 2020), response tone (e.g., Gao et al., 2021), response amount and timeliness (e.g., Chung et al., 2020), response content strategies (e.g., Herhausen et al., 2019), and commenter’s social influence (e.g., Gunarathne et al., 2017). As discussed earlier, previous research on comment sentiment and response timeliness in the online review literature cannot be directly transferred to the social media context due to differences in firm management goals and user engagement behaviors. The current literature on comment sentiment or response timeliness in the social media context has mainly focused on the effect of negative comments (e.g., Ma et al., 2015) or the timeliness of firm responses to customer complaints (e.g., Sun et al., 2021). Although Chung et al. (2020) examined how the timeliness of firm responses to positive and negative user comments affects a firm’s financial performance, its impact on user engagement is unclear. Understanding the independent impact of comment sentiment and response timeliness on user engagement is crucial, as they represent key decisions that firms need to make when crafting responses. It is also important to consider response strategies, such as responding to controversial comments and the uniqueness of information in responses, that can affect user engagement behaviors but have been overlooked in social media research. A holistic study of these features is therefore needed to fully understand their impact on user engagement.

## Hypotheses Development

## Which User Comments Are More Likely to Receive a Firm Response?

We first draw on the ABV of the firm to theorize the antecedents of a firm’s response behavior on business pages and associated relationships. The ABV is a widely used theory of firm behavior that explains how and why firms prioritize issues to focus on and allocate their attention to certain issues that can determine strategic agenda, actions (i.e., allocation and deployment of resources), and outcomes (Ocasio, 1997, 2011). Building on the ABV, we theorize that negative and controversial comments on social media platforms are likely to draw managerial attention as they are potentially detrimental to a firm’s reputation. This prompts firms to prioritize responding to such comments on their social media business pages.

Our first hypothesis posits that negative comments are more likely to receive a firm response than positive or neutral comments. The ABV and associated literature suggest that firms attend to issues selectively, with a distinct focus on the time and effort they require, and therefore allocate attention to issues with certain characteristics like negativity, recency, urgency, or novelty (Brielmaier & Friesl, 2023). Negative user comments on social media business pages are typically customer complaints (Yang et al., 2019a). Consistent with the ABV, firms are likely to respond to complaints in order to address and resolve customer issues and retain customers who might otherwise be lost (Fornell & Wernerfelt, 1988). Negative comments could also occur in the form of customer questions or suggestions, which are normally accompanied by an issue they are looking to resolve. Attending to such negative comments selectively, as highlighted in the ABV literature, enables a firm to address a question or acknowledge a suggestion, while at the same time directly assisting the customer with the issue and signaling to other users its ability to respond to such issues.

Further, the negativity bias literature has demonstrated that negative information has a more significant effect on opinions and behaviors than positive or neutral information because it usually indicates the risks or unfavorable outcomes of an activity or event that is important to a focal individual (Baumeister et al., 2001). Firm responses to negative comments are thus likely to improve customer satisfaction and loyalty more effectively than responses to positive ones (Gu & Ye, 2014). Hence, given a firm’s limited resources and attention, it is reasonable to prioritize responses to negative comments to offer more functional and instrumental values to users.

H1: A firm is more likely to respond to a negative user comment than it is to respond to a positive or neutral user comment on its social media business page.

Consistent with the ABV and associated literature, we suggest that a firm is likely to pay more attention to controversial comments on its social media business page. Controversial comments refer to those that can induce heated discussions and evoke diverse emotions and opinions among social media users (Choi et al., 2010). These characteristics of controversial comments are in line with the issue characteristics in the ABV literature that typically heighten the attentional engagement and selectivity of a firm (Brielmaier & Friesl, 2023). This implies that if an initial comment receives reactions of mixed sentiments from other users, the comment likely pertains to a controversial topic, which motivates readers to pay attention, think more deeply, and participate in the discussion (Lunstrum, 1981). It has been shown that the readership of an enterprise blog increases as the blog’s level of controversy increases because controversial blogs can evoke readers emotional engagement and attract discussions with peers (Singh et al., 2014). As controversial comments naturally attract a large audience (Jasser et al., 2022), social media users would expect feedback from firms on these comments. In responding, firms may take the opportunity to clarify misunderstandings or contribute information from their perspectives. In keeping with the ABV, if a firm does not pay attention to a topic or issue that its audience is heatedly debating, it can create the impression that the firm either does not value the opinions of users or does not take responsibility for moderating and managing its online discourse effectively. To avoid potential negative repercussions (e.g., a loss of user trust), a firm may feel obligated to respond to controversial user comments on its social media business page.

## H2a: A firm is more likely to respond to a more controversial user comment than it is to respond to a less controversial user comment on its social media business page.

Nevertheless, we also propose a competing hypothesis that a firm may choose not to respond to controversial comments to avoid potential conflict with users. Controversial comments can be difficult to handle due to the diversity of opinions and emotions involved; agreeing with the opinion of one group may annoy another group holding the opposite opinion. As such, keeping silent in controversial discussions may sometimes be a good choice. Moreover, firms may not be motivated enough to respond to controversial comments. The ABV and its associated literature have suggested that attentional engagement and selectivity are a function of cost, time, and effort considerations (Ocasio, 1997, 2011). Crafting responses to controversial comments can be costly and prone to error because multiple factors need to be taken into consideration. As a result, firms may decide not to pay attention or respond to controversial comments to reduce the risk of making inappropriate statements.

H2b: A firm is less likely to respond to a more controversial user comment than it is to respond to a less controversial user comment on its social media business page.

## How Do User Comments and Firm Responses Affect Subsequent User Engagement?

We suggest that a firm’s response to negative comments rather than positive or neutral comments induces a larger volume of subsequent user comments and a more negative sentiment in these comments. Responding to negative comments can improve the customer service experience by showcasing the solutions to customer problems and/or enhance interactions by answering customer questions or acknowledging customer suggestions. Users will likely be encouraged to generate more subsequent comments after seeing that even negative comments are accepted and responded to by a firm. Further, as negative information naturally draws more attention than positive or neutral information (Rozin & Royzman, 2001), a firm’s responses to negative comments are likely to receive more attention, especially sparking negative subsequent comments. For example, after observing a problem that is addressed/resolved in a response, users having similar problems may be more likely to complain by commenting on the initial comment with the expectation of being noticed by the firm as well. Ma et al. (2015) found that responding to customer complaints on Twitter encourages more negative tweets. Moreover, users who are not satisfied with what they perceive to be a poor or ineffective firm response may generate negative comments.

H3: A firm’s response to a negative user comment rather than a positive or neutral user comment is associated with (a) more subsequent user comments and (b) a more negative sentiment in these comments.

We further hypothesize that a firm’s response to a more controversial user comment can induce more subsequent user comments. According to the controversy literature, controversy affects discussions through two opposing forces: (1) controversial topics are interesting to discuss, but (2) controversial topics are often uncomfortable to talk about, which deters discussion (Chen & Berger, 2013). This means that firm responses may heighten users’ interest in discussing controversial topics while simultaneously alleviating their discomfort regarding the discussions. Specifically, a firm’s response that adds its perspective to a discussion may make the conversation more informed and encourage users to generate more subsequent comments. Further, a firm’s response to controversial comments may send a signal to users that it is willing to take an active moderator role in the interaction (Berge & Collins, 2000). Having a moderated discussion environment in online communities has been found to encourage user participation because people feel safer and more comfortable in presenting their own opinions (Wise et al., 2006).

Moreover, users may be motivated to participate in controversial discussions for social enhancement. Improving social identity is a major reason for online users’ active engagement in brand communities (Gummerus et al., 2012). Social media users are likely to receive more attention and enhance their online social identities when participating in a more controversial discussion involving both a firm and users, compared to a less controversial discussion. Thus, they may comment actively following a firm’s response to a more controversial user comment.

Firm responses to controversial comments, however, could lead to a more negative sentiment in users’ subsequent comments. As extremely satisfied or dissatisfied consumers are more motivated to write online reviews (Anderson, 1998; Hu et al., 2009), social media users who have strong positive or negative emotions are more likely to generate comments. To avoid the risk of negative reactions from users with opposite opinions, users who agree or are very satisfied with a firm’s response to a controversial comment may be less motivated to generate subsequent comments. On the contrary, people who disagree or are very dissatisfied with the firm’s response may be more likely to speak out: they may fight back against the firm’s opinion/explanation expressed in the response and show disappointment or dissatisfaction in their comments. Hence, a firm’s response to a controversial comment is associated with a heightened negative sentiment in the subsequent comments of users.

H4: A firm’s response to a more controversial user comment is associated with (a) more subsequent user comments and (b) a more negative sentiment in these comments.

We further theorize that response uniqueness (i.e., containing distinct or novel content compared to other responses) positively affects user engagement on firm business pages. A unique response typically provides high informational value to recipients.<sup>2</sup> The engagement literature has found that obtaining useful information is a major motivation for user engagement (Gummerus et al., 2012; Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004). Accordingly, social media users may be encouraged by novel firm responses to ask questions or express themselves through comments, with the expectation of receiving similar useful information from the firm. To generate a unique response, a firm normally needs to exert heightened efforts, allowing it to be perceived by users as caring or hardworking and thus leading to a more positive sentiment in subsequent user comments. On the contrary, if a firm’s response is too similar to other responses, users may think that the firm is only making a perfunctory effort to reply to them and thus react negatively. For example, it is common practice for firms to apply templates in responses on their business pages. One response template often used by firms is intended to transfer an ongoing conversation into another channel (e.g., “Please call our customer service team at (XXX)-XXX-XXX”), which has been shown to negatively affect the virality (i.e., likes and comments) of a firm post (Herhausen et al., 2019). We thus propose that unique firm responses could motivate social media users to produce more comments and express a more positive attitude in these comments.

H5: The uniqueness of a firm’s response to a user comment is positively related to (a) the volume of subsequent user comments and (b) the positive sentiment in these comments.

Finally, we propose that response timeliness (i.e., the elapse of time between a user comment and the firm’s response to the comment) has a nonlinear relationship with subsequent user engagement on firm business pages. Quick firm responses to user comments (regardless of comment sentiment) can improve users’ experience with a firm, keep them continuously engaged, and motivate other users who are observing the conversation to enthusiastically participate. In contrast, responding too late (e.g., in a few days) after a comment is posted could easily cause users to lose interest and attention, curtail the generation of follow-up comments, and develop a negative impression of the firm. The service recovery literature has indeed indicated that timely firm responses to customer complaints can increase customer satisfaction (Istanbulluoglu, 2017; Moore & Moore, 2004) and decrease negative word-of-mouth commentary (Davidow 2000). A quick response to a complaint may not solve a customer’s problem directly or immediately, but it at least offers some timely comfort or reduces possible anxiety or dissatisfaction of the focal customer. Responding quickly is especially important in the social media context because it could ameliorate potential economic and social losses caused by the speedy diffusion of customer complaints on social media (Cambra-Fierro et al., 2015).

Nevertheless, it may not be helpful for firms to provide responses extremely quickly (e.g., within a few minutes) after a user comment is posted. Specifically, adding a firm’s opinion/solution too soon may impede the development of organic interactions among users. Users in this case may perceive that a topic is settled by a firm and is not worthy of careful consideration or further discussion. This could cause fewer users to make subsequent comments. At the same time, providing very speedy responses may elevate other users’ expectations for real-time interactions with a firm. These users could thus become easily disappointed if their comments are not responded to at the same speed, leading to a more negative sentiment in subsequent comments. In contrast to prior literature, we thus suggest that neither responding too soon nor too late is a good strategy for firms to keep users engaged on their business pages.

H6: The timeliness of a firm’s response to a user comment has an inverted U-shape relationship with (a) the volume of subsequent user comments and (b) the positive sentiment in these comments.

## Method

## Data and Variables

Data for this study were collected using the Facebook Graph API from the Facebook business pages of 37 firms listed among the top 100 U.S. retailers (ranked based on sales) in 2017.<sup>3</sup> We selected several firms ranked at the top, middle, and bottom of the list to construct a representative sample. Our focus was on the retail industry, where cultivating customer relationships is of great importance and retailers tend to actively interact with customers on social media. We collected all firm posts published in 2017 and all associated user comments and firm responses from the Facebook business pages of these retailers. At the time of data collection, Facebook allowed no more than two levels of comments. We refer to the initial user comments on a firm post as the firstlevel comments. There are usually multiple first-level user comments under a firm post. Users can make subsequent comments on a first-level comment, which are referred to as second-level comments. Firm responses to first-level comments are displayed in parallel with second-level comments. There are 1,079,593 first-level comments in total in our dataset, with 77,932 being responded to by a firm.

Our empirical analyses unfold in two phases, each dedicated to addressing one of the research questions. In Phase 1, we examined the types of first-level comments that received a firm response using the full sample of all first-level comments. In Phase 2, we studied how features of first-level comments and firm responses jointly affected the volume and sentiment of subsequent second-level comments, using a subsample of firstlevel comments that had received at least one firm response and their associated second-level comments. Tables 2 and 3 provide definitions and descriptive statistics of all variables, including the control variables, for the two phases.

Key variables in Phase 1: The dependent variable in Phase 1 is FirmResponse, indicating whether a first-level comment was responded to by a firm or not (yes was coded as 1 and no was coded as 0). The first independent variable, CommentSentiment, is the sentiment expressed in a first-level comment. Consistent with the literature (Deng et al., 2018), we categorized user comments into positive, neutral, and negative, coded as 1, 0, and -1, respectively. We used a supervised text mining method to label sentiment for all comments. We first recruited Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) workers to perform the task of sentiment classification for 11,030 randomly selected comments (about 1% of all first-level comments in our dataset). In each MTurk task, a worker was directed to classify a comment as either positive, neutral, or negative. Five different MTurk workers were assigned to categorize the sentiment of each comment. Based on a majority rule, 96.6% of the comments were labeled without ambiguity and served as the training set.

We then trained and evaluated five popular sentiment classifiers: long short-term memory (LSTM), XGBoost, logistic regression, support vector machine, and Bayes regression. They were ensembled using a weighted voting method, and the weight of each classifier was learned via a stacking technique. The 10-fold cross-validation accuracy for the final ensemble model is 83.85%, which outperforms similar studies (e.g., 70.15% of Deng et al., 2018). Finally, we deployed the final ensemble model to assign a sentiment label to each unlabeled comment.

The second independent variable, CommentControversialness, was measured as the variance of sentiment scores of the secondlevel comments associated with a first-level comment. If the first-level comment was responded to by a firm, its controversialness was measured using all the second-level comments before the first firm response. Missing values were replaced with zero because first-level comments without any second-level comments were considered non-controversial. <sup>4</sup> This measure was adapted from Singh et al. (2014), who measured blog controversialness as the standard deviation of sentiment scores in reader comments. Choi et al. (2010) also detected controversial news articles based on sentiment polarities.

Key variables in Phase 2: There were two dependent variables in the second phase. NumSubseqComments is the number of second-level comments after the first firm response to a firstlevel comment. SentiSubseqComments is the mean of sentiment scores of second-level comments after the first firm response. There were four key independent variables in Phase 2. CommentSentiment and CommentControversialness were measured the same way as in Phase 1. ResponseUniqueness indicates the extent to which a firm’s response provides unique information to the audience. Based on the responses from all firms in our dataset, we first calculated the entropy score for each word and then used the average entropy score of words in a response to measure its uniqueness.<sup>5</sup> Note that the entropy scores were calculated based on the vocabulary of responses in the entire dataset. As such, ResponseUniqueness was less affected by the unobserved factors of certain firms. Finally, we measured the Timeliness of a firm response as the time elapsed (in minutes) between the posting of a first-level comment and the first firm response.

## Estimation Results

In Phase 1, the coefficients for neutral and positive comments are negative and significant (see Table 4), indicating that firms are more likely to respond to negative user comments than neutral or positive comments, supporting H1. Additionally, CommentControversialness is negatively related to the probability of receiving a firm response, supporting H2b.

In Phase 2, the negative binomial regression results for NumSubseqComments suggest that firm responses to negative user comments are associated with more subsequent comments than responses to neutral or positive comments. H3a is thus supported. Moreover, the coefficients of CommentControversialness are positive and significant, where firm responses to more controversial comments are associated with more subsequent comments, thus supporting H4a. Further, the coefficients of ResponseUniqueness are positive and significant, demonstrating that the more unique a firm response is (compared to other responses in the dataset), the more subsequent comments are generated after the response, thereby supporting H5a. Finally, the coefficients of Timeliness and its squared term are negative and significant. As the time variable can only take non-negative values, the effect of Timeliness on the volume of subsequent comments is negative with no curvilinearity. This indicates that a firm’s response speed is positively related to the volume of subsequent user comments; hence, H6a is not supported.

The panel regression results for SentiSubseqComments in Phase 2 indicate that compared to responses to negative comments, firm responses to positive or neutral comments lead to a generally more positive sentiment in subsequent comments. H3b is therefore supported. Furthermore, as the coefficients of CommentControversialness are negative and significant, H4b is supported, which suggests that there is a more negative emotion among subsequent comments after a firm’s response to a more controversial user comment. Additionally, the coefficients of ResponseUniqueness are positive and significant, where higher response uniqueness is related to a more positive sentiment in subsequent comments, supporting H5b. Finally, the coefficients of Timeliness on SentiSubseqComments are positive and significant, suggesting that quicker responses are associated with a more negative overall sentiment in subsequent comments with no curvilinearity (the squared term is not significant). H6b is thus not supported.

## Robustness Checks

## Controlling for More Comment, Response, and Post Features

Comment content features: One potential concern with our Phase 2 analyses is that firm response strategies can be endogenous with respect to the content characteristics of first-level user comments. To alleviate this concern, we employed a number of additional variables in the secondphase models to account for the granular content characteristics of first-level comments. These variables included the use of punctuation, emojis, and the firm name in a first-level comment and the linguistic, stylistic, and inferred psychological features of first-level comments, which were extracted using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software.<sup>6</sup> The LIWC-based content features were measured as the percentage of a specific category of words appearing in a comment.

Response content strategies: The response content strategies might be another source of potentially endogenous problems that needed to be controlled for in Phase 2 analyses. Following Herhausen et al. (2019), we included five response content strategies (i.e., apology, compensation, channel change, emotion, and explanation) as additional control variables in our models. These variables indicate whether the specified content was contained in a response. We also added another response content strategy, appreciation, which indicates whether appreciative words were used in a response.<sup>7</sup>

Time trends: Firms in our data sample may have participated in social activities, such as political and social events, at different levels in 2017. These activities could potentially affect the sentiment and controversialness of the initial first-level user comments on their business pages. To account for the weekly fluctuations in firms’ activities, we incorporated week indicators into the models of Phase 2. Controlling for both time-fixed effects at the weekly level and firm-fixed effects, our models were able to account for potential time-varying and firm-specific variations. Further, it is possible that posts that stay on the platform longer could accumulate more subsequent comments. We thus included PostingDuration in our Phase 2 analyses to control for the time a firm post has remained on Facebook, measured as the time elapsed (in days) between the day a firm post was published and a fixed date, January 1, 2018.

Our main results are robust in terms of including the various content features of user comments and firm responses as well as the posting time controls, as shown in Table 5.

## Alternative Variable Measures

Alternative measures of controversialness: There may be a concern that using the variance of comment sentiment to measure controversialness may not adequately capture opinion diversity. To address this, we constructed an alternative measure calculated based on entropy, inspired by the work of Basile et al. (2017). Specifically, we calculated an entropy score based on the counts of positive, neutral, and negative second-level comments that preceded the first firm response to a first-level comment. Higher entropy scores indicate more controversial first-level comments. Moreover, we tested two additional measures of controversialness: the coefficient of variation (CV) and percentage difference (PD).<sup>8</sup> The estimation results, presented in Table 6, show that our findings remain robust with regard to these three alternative measures of controversialness.

Alternative sentiment classification methods: Due to the large data size, we used a supervised sentiment analysis method to classify the sentiment of user comments. Although the prediction accuracy of our ensemble model is satisfactory, there may be a concern that misclassifications in the sentiment variables could have biased the regression estimations (Yang et al., 2018). To address this issue, we used two alternative sentiment analysis methods: (1) a dictionary-based unsupervised method, SentiStrength<sup>9</sup> and (2) an LSTM classifier trained on comment embeddings generated by BERT (a state-of-the-art pre-trained language model, Devlin et al., 2018).<sup>10</sup> The results in Table 7 indicate that our findings are robust in relation to these alternative sentiment classifiers.

Multiple responses: There is a chance that all firm responses (rather than only the first one) kept users engaged on a firm’s business page, making it difficult for us to validate the effects of the comment and response features hypothesized in this paper. To alleviate this concern, if there was more than one firm response, we reconstructed the two dependent variables as the number and the sentiment mean of second-level comments between the first and second firm responses. Their measurements stayed the same as before if there was only one firm response. In this way, the effect of the initial firm response can be clearly shown. The results presented in Table 8 suggest that our findings are robust with respect to multiple firm responses.

Self-generated subsequent comments: Because some subsequent second-level comments may be made by users who posted initial first-level comments, counting such self-generated comments may inflate the effects of user comments and firm responses. Due to Facebook’s privacy policy, however, we could not identify commenters to remove self-generated comments. To alleviate this concern, we introduced a new dependent variable, NumSubseqComments’ = NumSubseqComments – 1, assuming that users make an average of one second-level comment after a firm responds to their firstlevel comments. The results in Table 8 align with our findings.

## Selection Issue of Comments Receiving Firm Responses

The set of first-level comments that received firm responses may be systematically different from those not receiving firm responses, thereby posing a sample selection issue for Phase 2 analyses. To mitigate this concern, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) analysis, which reduces selection bias in estimations by constructing a matched control group that resembles the treatment group on observable covariates (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). First, we assigned all first-level comments to either the control group (not responded to by a firm) or the treatment group (responded to by a firm). Next, we utilized a logistic regression to estimate the propensity scores for all first-level comments.<sup>11</sup> Using these scores, we employed the nearest-neighbor matching method to match 77,932 first-level comments in the treatment group with an equal number of comments in the control group. We observed high similarity in the distributions of propensity scores between the treatment group and the matched control group, verifying the quality of the matching procedure. We re-ran Phase 2 regressions with the estimated propensity score (i.e., PropensityScore) as a control variable to account for the probability of a first-level comment receiving a firm response in the first place. The regression results in Table 9 confirm the robustness of our findings against the potential selection issue.

## Facebook’s Filtering Algorithms

Facebook’s proprietary content-filtering algorithms (e.g., EdgeRank and Promoted Post<sup>12</sup>), which recommend a firm post to a nonrandom set of users, may have biased our findings. Because the visibility of a firm post is affected by Facebook’s filtering algorithms, the visibility of a first-level user comment under the firm post is also affected. As a result, unobserved variations in the visibility of first-level comments may have driven the effects on subsequent user engagement. While this concern cannot be fully eliminated because the exact algorithms are not available (Lee et al., 2018), we sought to mitigate it by controlling for post-level fixed effects rather than firm-level fixed effects. This involved estimating the effects of comment and response features on subsequent user engagement among the first-level comments within a given firm-generated post (which may have been promoted by Facebook’s filtering algorithms). Our findings are still robust, as shown in Table 10. This robustness check also reduces concerns about bias potentially arising from the heterogeneity of firm posts, such as the semantic meaning of post content.

Finally, we extended our study to account for the potential impact of the degrees of comment positivity and negativity, measured as the percentages of positive and negative words in a comment. We also explored how response uniqueness and timeliness moderated the effects of comment sentiment and controversialness on the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments. The results (available upon request) are highly consistent with our main results.

## Discussion

Our results (summarized in Table 11) show that firms are more likely to respond to negative comments but less likely to respond to controversial comments. Further, responding to negative or controversial comments and responding quickly are all associated with a higher volume of subsequent comments and a more negative sentiment in these comments. Finally, the uniqueness of a firm’s response is positively related to both the volume and sentiment of subsequent comments.

## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes three major theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on firm social media management by examining comment features (usergenerated content) and response features (firm-generated content) in tandem. Prior research has either mainly focused on response features or has only looked at negative user comments. The literature has largely overlooked the significance of initial user comments in shaping a firm’s response decision and in influencing subsequent user engagement behaviors. This study fills this research gap by complementing antecedent variables in the explanation of user engagement, which could potentially influence customer purchasing behaviors and brand loyalty. We highlight a holistic view of firms’ response strategies by demonstrating the dynamics between initial user comments and firm responses in affecting subsequent user engagement.

Second, we add to the literature on firm response strategies by theorizing and quantifying two novel response strategies that affect user engagement on firm social media business pages: responding to controversial comments and response uniqueness. Prior research on firm social media management has examined the impact of firm posts and firm decisions in responding to user engagement. Specific response strategies, which indicate how firms construct a response (such as comment selection strategy, response content strategy, etc.), are important antecedent factors of user engagement but are underexplored, especially on social media business pages.

This study contributes to the current literature by revealing the pros and cons of responding to more controversial comments (i.e., a larger volume of subsequent comments and a more negative sentiment) and the positive effect of unique responses in enhancing user engagement on firms’ business pages.

Third, this study complements the literature on the impact of comment sentiment and response timeliness on user engagement in the context of social media. We provide a new theoretical perspective to look at the impact of response timeliness by proposing and examining a novel nonlinear relationship between response timeliness and subsequent user engagement. Overall, our study extends the literature by shedding light on previously overlooked elements of firm social media management and their effects on user engagement.

## Practical Implications, Limitations, and Future Research

This study provides practical insights to firms and platform owners by showing that a firm’s response strategy should be based on which comments to respond to, at what time, how to respond, and how these decisions affect subsequent user engagement. Our findings can improve practitioners understanding of the nuanced effects of these response strategies, which could help them make more informed response decisions. Firms can strategically guide the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments in a desirable direction by adjusting choices of user comments to respond to (based on comment sentiment and controversialness), response content uniqueness, and response timeliness. For example, if a firm wanted to improve the sentiment of user comments on its business page, it could consider making more responses to positive comments (rather than negative comments), responding to less controversial comments, and not responding too quickly so as to manage users’ expectations. Importantly, firms should always select user comments that could induce a unique response to increase both the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments.

Further, this research provides insights for improving platform design. Our findings may help platforms design creative commenting and responding mechanisms to enhance interactions between firms and social media users. For example, they could create a feature that highlights unique firm responses, making it easier for social media users to identify useful responses. This could also motivate firms to provide novel and useful (rather than repetitive and boilerplate) information in their responses. Finally, our findings could help social media users understand the interplay between firms’ response strategies and user comments. Users could use our findings to craft their comments on firms’ social media business pages in a way that draws attention.

We acknowledge a few limitations that might be addressed in future research. First, because of Facebook’s policies that protect users’ information security and privacy, we could not account for any user-level characteristics (e.g., demographics, browsing history, network, etc.) in our models. Future research could overcome this shortcoming with richer data. Second, our models could not fully account for Facebook’s filtering algorithms that push firm posts to users nonrandomly. Future research could access information on recommendation algorithms directly from firms or social media platforms. Third, the exact social media management goal of each firm is not explicitly available. It is possible that sudden changes in a firm’s strategy may influence its posting and responding behaviors that are not captured by the factors examined in this study. Fourth, we studied the impact of responding to controversial comments on subsequent user engagement only. Future research could examine the impact of different response strategies for controversial comments. Finally, we only focused on the business pages of U.S. retailers on Facebook, a dominant social media platform. Researchers could explore other industries (e.g., healthcare, finance, and education) and other social media platforms (e.g., Twitter and Instagram) in the future to reveal unique patterns of user engagement in different contexts.

## Conclusion

Social media platforms are ubiquitous. Firms maintain their presence on these platforms through business pages where they interact with users (i.e., followers) by posting messages and responding to user comments. In this research, we examine the relationship between firm responses and user comments to understand the features of the responses and comments that may affect subsequent user comments, an important indicator of user engagement on social media business pages. We found that two features of initial user comments (i.e., sentiment and controversialness) and two features of firm responses (i.e., uniqueness and timeliness)

jointly affect the volume and sentiment of subsequent user comments. The theoretical and practical implications of our findings should not only inform and extend the current understanding and practices related to social media business pages, but also bring forth insightful future research and practice directions.
