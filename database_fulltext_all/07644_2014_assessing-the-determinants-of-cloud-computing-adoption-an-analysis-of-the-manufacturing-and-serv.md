## Abstract

A multitude of factors influence the adoption of cloud computing. Organizations must systematically evaluate these factors prior to making the decision to adopt cloud-based solutions. To assess the determinants influencing the adoption of cloud computing, we develop a research model based on the innovation characteristics from the diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory, and the technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework. Data collected from 369 firms in Portugal are used to test the related hypotheses. The study also investigates the determinants of cloud computing adoption in the manufacturing and services sector.

Keywords: Cloud Computing, IT adoption, Diffusion of Innovation (DOI), Technology-Organization-Environment (TOE).

# Assessing the determinants of cloud computing adoption: An analysis of the manufacturing and services sectors

## 1. Introduction

Large and small enterprises are rapidly re-orienting their overall information technology (IT) strategies to include cloud computing. As the strategic emphasis on flexibility, innovation, and economic gains increase, organizations can no longer overlook the advantages of the computational agility, and scalability, provided by a distributed cloud computing environment [1-3]. With the potential to transform business processes, lower IT expenditure, deliver real time applications, access to ubiquitous storage, unlimited computing power, and market information mobilization, organizations look toward cloud-based solutions to achieve business efficiencies [3-7]. Thus, increasing IT expenditure on cloud computing is a strategic direction to which companies are giving serious consideration [8].

Despite the touted advantages of this new technology, evidence suggests that not all companies are rushing to adopt cloud-based solutions [9, 10]. Reasons identified include the disruptive technology not reaching a level of maturity, lack of industry-specific conformity to standards, and high level of related risk and costs [11-13]. In addition, firms in certain sectors may have only a modest investment in technology. Examples include small and medium enterprises (SME) in the supply chain-centric industries such as manufacturing, agriculture, and construction [9]. They are limited in technical capabilities and often rely on smaller groups of IT professionals or contract IT staff for their IT needs. Reluctance to adopt cloud computing solutions, a disruptive technology in the ‘technology trigger’ or the ‘inflated expectations’ phase of the hype-cycle [8, 14] are therefore real and noteworthy.

Clearly, the cloud phenomenon is not the panacea for all organizations. The purpose of this study is to understand the determinants of the adoption of cloud computing and its relative advantage to organizations. Most earlier studies on cloud computing have focused on the technical and operational issues [15]. A few studies have addressed the adoption of cloud computing from an organizational perspective (see Table 1). They mostly assess the direct effects of the innovation characteristics or the contextual factors. No studies have conducted a holistic evaluation of the direct effects and the indirect effects of the determinants on cloud computing adoption. Motivated by these issues, this study seeks to develop a research model that integrates the innovation characteristics [16] of cloud computing and the technologyorganization-environment (TOE) perspectives [17] that underline its adoption. The contribution of the article is twofold. First, to investigate the direct and indirect effects of the innovation characteristics and the TOE context on the adoption of cloud computing, data from 369 firms in

Portugal is used to evaluate the research model. The study thus presents a more holistic assessment of the determinants of cloud computing adoption compared to earlier studies. Second, by investigating the determinants of cloud adoption in two different sectors (manufacturing and services), we contribute to the wider body of scientific knowledge that has so far not studied the adoption of cloud computing in these two sectors. Our study highlights the importance of systematically evaluating the determinants of cloud computing at the industry level.

Through a review of the literature we provide a background on cloud computing and related research. We then describe the theoretical foundations for the research model and propose the hypotheses. The research methodology and the results are presented, followed by a discussion of the major findings. We conclude by highlighting the implications of the findings and summarizing directions for future study.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. Cloud computing

There is a lack of universal or standard definition for cloud computing [5, 18-21], even though it is not a completely new concept. Cloud computing evolved through the recent advancements in hardware, virtualization technology, distributed computing, and service delivery over the Internet. The “cloud” metaphor is a reference to the ubiquitous availability and accessibility to computing resources via Internet technologies [5, 22]. Cloud-based solutions give businesses and users easy access to massive computing power at negligible costs [23]. By moving IT functions such as storage, business applications, and services to the cloud, organizations have the potential to reduce the overall cost of IT [21, 24, 25]. Cloud computing thus offers monetary benefits that businesses can no longer ignore.

Generally speaking, services offered by cloud computing can be classified into three types [26]. Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) is where the basic unit of computing power and storage are cloud-based and available on demand (e.g., Amazon Elastic Compute Cloud (EC2), Rackspace, Amazon Simple Storage Service (S3), and GoGrid). Benefits of this model include pay-per-use and resource elasticity to match the computing demands [27]. Platform as a Service (PaaS) is where the service provider offers an integrated solution stack for creating and deploying applications from the cloud (e.g., Salesforce, Google AppEngine, and Microsoft Azure). An advantage of this model is the ability to provision all aspects of software development (design, testing, version control, maintenance, and hosting) over the Internet [25]. Software as a Service (SaaS) is where the users access the applications centrally hosted on the cloud using a thin client (such as a web browser or a mobile application) instead of installing software on their own computer (e.g., Joyent and SalesForce CRM). Benefits of this model of cloud service include centralized configuration and hosting, software release updates without requiring reinstall, and accelerated feature delivery [28].

Cloud computing represents the convergence of IT efficiency and business agility [29]. IT efficiency results from the use of scalable hardware and software resources [21], improvement in work efficiency and coordination among firms [4], and high availability of services [6]. Business agility of cloud computing is the ability to deploy computational tools rapidly, reduce upfront capital expenditure [21, 30], and respond more quickly to changing market needs [6, 21]. Cloud computing eliminates traditional boundaries between businesses. The capacity to seamlessly deliver IT functions as cloud-based solutions has proven viable and cost effective as evidenced by its growing adoption.

## 2.2. Adoption models

Two theories are commonly used in innovation diffusion and adoption studies in organizations. They are the diffusion of innovation (DOI) theory [16] and the TOE Framework [17]. Other popular theories such as the technology acceptance model (TAM) [31, 32], theory of planned behavior (TPB) [33], and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) [34] are not considered in this research as they pertain to an individual’s choice.

## Diffusion of innovation (DOI)

DOI [16] is a prominent adoption model used in Information Systems (IS) research [35- 40]. It proposes five attributes that explain the adoption of innovation in an organization. They are: 1) relative advantage, the extent to which an innovation is better than the previous generation; 2) compatibility, the degree to which an innovation can be assimilated into the existing business processes, practices, and value systems; 3) complexity, the level of difficulty to use the innovation; 4) observability, extent to which the innovation is visible to others; and 5) trialability, the ease of experimenting with the innovation. DOI is predominantly based on the characteristics of the technology and the users’ perceptions of the innovation. An organization is a more complex entity than an individual. Here, Rogers [16] suggests that innovation is a communication process using the various channels within the social system. Three factors influence the adoption of innovation in organizations. They are individual (leadership attitude toward change), internal organizational structure (centralization, complexity, interconnectedness, number of employees, and the organizational slack), and external characteristics (system openness) of the organization.

## Technology-organization-environment (TOE) framework

Tornatzky and Fleischer [17] proposed the TOE framework to explain the process of innovation in the context of an enterprise. It considers three features of an enterprise that influence the adoption of innovation - technology, organization, and environment contexts. The technology context refers to the internal and external technology relevant to the organization, and the technologies that are available for possible adoption. The organization context refers to the descriptive characteristics of the firm (i.e., organizational structure, firm size, managerial structure, degree of centralization), resources (human and slack resources), and process of communication (formal and informal) among employees. The environment context comprises of the market elements, competitors, and the regulatory environment [17, 41-43].

## 2.3. Related literature on cloud computing adoption

Many studies have addressed the technical and operational issues related to cloud computing. They include topics such as the selection of cloud computing services based on costs and risks [44], audit protocol for secure storage and computation in the cloud [45, 46], analytical models for determining the cost of ownership of cloud computing [47, 48], and issues of security, privacy risks, and information loss [49-51].

Our search of scholarly databases identified only a few published journal articles that evaluate cloud computing adoption from an organizational perspective (see Table 1). Low et al. [4] used the DOI and TOE framework to investigate the adoption of cloud computing in the Taiwanese high-tech industry. Their research model is not expansive, as it does not consider key factors such as cost savings and security concerns that are critical to the firm’s adoption of cloud computing. Furthermore, they evaluate the adoption of cloud computing as a dichotomous dependent variable and not as a continuous process. Lin and Chen [30] conducted a semistructured interview among 19 IT professionals in Taiwan. Their qualitative assessment indicated that IT companies are reluctant to adopt cloud computing until the uncertainties associated with cloud computing (e.g., security and standardization) are reduced, and successful business models have emerged. Trigueros-Preciado et al. [10] used a qualitative and quantitative analysis methodology to identify the barriers to cloud adoption. They surveyed 94 SMEs in Spain and concluded that knowledge about cloud computing was low among the firms, and the company’s ignorance was the main barrier to cloud computing adoption. Nkhoma and Dang [52] used secondary data from the survey of a large services company to study the drivers and barriers to cloud computing adoption. Wu et al. [53] investigated whether the information processing requirements and capacity affect the firm’s intention to adopt cloud computing. They used the DOI theory and information processing view (IPV) to conduct their study in the supply chain domain. Abdollahzadehgan et al. [9] proposed using the TOE framework to evaluate the barriers to cloud computing adoption in SMEs. Their study does not include hypothesis testing or empirical validation. Kshetri [54] used the institutional theory to investigate the perception and security issues based on the context provided by formal and informal institutions; no empirical assessment is provided.

The review of published journal articles indicates that most studies empirically evaluate the direct effects of innovation or contextual factors, or conduct analysis using qualitative methods or secondary data on the adoption of cloud computing. No study has taken a holistic approach to empirically validate the direct and indirect effects of the innovation characteristics and the underlying technology, organization, and environment context. Yang and Tate [15] point out similar concerns by classifying the published journal articles on cloud computing into four research themes (technological, business issues, domains and applications, and conceptualization). Based on a descriptive literature review of 205 refereed journal articles, their study indicates that research on cloud computing is skewed mostly toward technological issues. They highlight the paucity of cumulative research to address the social, organizational, and environmental perspectives of cloud computing. In this study we address this crucial research gap by developing an integrative research model that combines the theoretical perspectives of the diffusion of innovation and the technology, organization, and environment context. We use the model to holistically evaluate the determinants of cloud computing adoption in an organization.

## 2.4. Combining DOI and TOE

Many researchers have called for approaches that combine more than one theoretical perspective to understand IT adoption of innovative new technologies [42, 53, 55, 56]. To better understand the organizational decisions related to the adoption of technological innovation, the context of the study should be comprehensive and the variables tailored to the specificity of the innovation [57]. DOI and TOE have been used extensively in IT adoption studies, and have enjoyed consistent empirical support.

In many ways, the TOE perspectives overlap with the innovation characteristics identified by Roger. Therefore, the value of incorporating the TOE contexts to strengthen the DOI theory is well recognized [42, 53, 57, 58]. The technology context is implicitly the same idea as Rogers’ [16]. DOI’s internal and external organizational characteristics include the same measures as TOE’s organization context [58]. There are also important differences between the two theories. TOE does not specify the role of individual characteristics (e.g., top management support). Here, the DOI theory suggests the inclusion of top management support in the organization context. Similarly, DOI does not consider the impact of environment context. Because of DOI’s shortcomings, the TOE framework helps to provide a more comprehensive perspective for understanding IT adoption by including the technology, organization, and environment context [39]. The theories thus meaningfully complement each other [42].

To identify the constructs of the integrative research model, we used the DOI theory and TOE framework as the basis to conduct an elaborate search of scholarly databases, including EBSCOHost’s Academic Search Complete and Business Source Complete, all databases within Proquest (e.g., ABI/INFORM Complete), databases within PsycNet, and Google Scholar search. We then grouped the well-cited studies to determine the most representative factors evaluated in the published literature on adoption studies. Finally, we examined each construct to determine its applicability to cloud computing adoption. Among the five attributes of DOI, three innovation characteristics are applicable to cloud computing adoption. They are relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility. Trialability and observability are not widely used in IT innovation studies [59]. Thus, following the general guidance of IS research, we also disregard these two attributes as they are not relevant to cloud computing technology. Rogers [16] states that “the nature of the innovation determines the type of relative advantage that is important to the adopter,” and that the relative advantage of the innovation can be “expressed as economic profitability, as conveying social prestige, or in other ways” [16]. In the context of our study we posit that cloud computing may lead to the economic advantage of cost savings [4, 60]. Similarly, security concerns may diminish the relative advantage of cloud computing. We therefore include two additional variables, namely, cost savings and security concerns as antecedents to the relative advantage of cloud computing. They determine whether cloud computing will be relatively advantageous if it provides cost savings, and less advantageous if there are security concerns.

From the TOE framework, the technology context determines whether the technology readiness of the firm will constrain or facilitate the adoption of cloud computing. Factors specific to the organizational context are top management support and firm size. The extent to which environment context may influence the firm’s decision to adopt cloud computing are identified by two variables, competitive pressure and regulatory support.

## 3.1. Hypotheses of innovation characteristics

A security breach is an incident in which a company or a government agency loses information, personal records, or other sensitive data [62]. Cloud computing is the convergence of storage and computing in a shared multi-user environment, which heightens security concerns [63, 64]. It leaves companies unaware and uncertain of potential security risks [65]. In addition, identity management still remains a challenge in the cloud environment. The lack of mature security protocols and identity management standards implies that organizations will be reluctant to adopt a cloud solution. Moving to the cloud adds new layers of complexity for securing data and will influence a firms’ decision to adopt the innovation. Hence,

H1a: Security and privacy concerns will negatively influence the relative advantage of cloud computing.

Cloud computing creates an opportunity for innovation, lower IT expenditure, and reduces the total cost of computing [66]. It enables innovation by allowing companies to focus on the core business than be stifled by changes in technology. By adopting cloud computing a firm can reduce the time devoted to system maintenance and routine upgrades. Cloud computing reduces infrastructure costs, decreases energy consumption, and lowers maintenance expenditure [21]. Vendor specialization enables cloud service providers to offer IT functions at lower costs and pass the cost savings from economies of scale to the end user [65]. As an enabler for the swift adoption of changing technologies, cloud computing offers cost effective ways to transform businesses by re-inventing how goods and services are sold and consumed. Hence,

H1b: Cost savings will positively influence the relative advantage of cloud computing.

Relative advantage is the “degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes” [16]. Innovations that have a clear, unambiguous advantage in creating strategic effectiveness (e.g., increasing sales) and operational effectiveness (e.g., reducing operational costs) have a greater impetus for adoption [67]. If the benefits of the technology (in this case, cloud computing) exceed existing practices and processes [60], the merits will positively influence its adoption. Therefore,

## H1: Relative advantage will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

Complexity is the “degree to which an innovation is perceived to be relatively difficult to understand and use” [16]. The easier it is to integrate the technology in the business operations, the greater is the chance of its adoption. The cloud environment offers the ability to pool resources instantaneously to match the workload. However, adopting a cloud-based solution can be challenging to companies that lack technological expertise and IT specialists. For example, the integration of existing applications to a specialized cloud infrastructure (e.g., Oracle’s Elastic Cloud or HP’s Cloud System) may require a level of expertise that is not readily available within the firm. Challenges also arise in the use of cloud based solutions when welldefined boundaries to secure the business processes and data privacy in a shared multi-tenant environment are not fully refined [68]. Thus,

## H2: Complexity will negatively influence cloud computing adoption.

Rogers [16] defines compatibility as “the degree to which the innovation fits with the potential adopter’s existing values, previous practices, and current needs”. Compatibility is an important determinant of innovation adoption [36, 37, 69-71]. For example, if the purpose of adopting cloud computing is to take advantage of the scalability benefits for applications with low security concerns, then offloading the capability to the cloud infrastructure makes economic sense. Business enablement and compatibility are therefore factors that will determine whether cloud computing will be adopted by an organization. Thus,

H3: Compatibility will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

## 3.2. Hypotheses of the TOE context

## The technology context

The technology context refers to the technological characteristics available in the organization for the adoption of technology. It includes both the structural aspects and the specialized human resources. The structural aspects refer to the platform or the technological infrastructure (e.g., installed network technologies and enterprise systems) within the firm that the cloud computing services can complement or compensate (e.g., implementing a collaborative document sharing solution using cloud-based storage). The specialized human resources are those within the organization that have the knowledge and skill to implement the cloud computing services (e.g., employees with computer skills, IT specialists) [72]. Together they enhance the technology readiness of an organization. Therefore, firms with a higher degree of technology readiness are better positioned for the adoption of cloud computing. Hence,

## H4: Technology Readiness will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

## The organizational context

The organizational context is defined in terms of the resources available to support the adoption of the innovation [73]. It refers to the characteristics of the firm that facilitate or constrain the adoption and implementation of innovation. Multiple factors influence the relationship between organizational structure and adoption of innovation. They include the level of centralization, distribution of power and control, information links, availability of slack resources, lateral communication, firm size, and top management support [4, 17, 74]. Of these, top management support and firm size are the most important factors to assess the adoption of cloud computing [4].

Top management support plays an important role in cloud computing adoption as it provides guidance to the allocation of resources, integration of services, and re-engineering of processes [4]. Top management that recognizes the benefits of cloud computing will likely allocate the necessary resources for its adoption and influence the organizational members to implement the change. Where they fail to recognize the benefits of cloud computing to the business, the management will be opposed to its adoption. Thus,

## H5: Top management support will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

Firm size is the other organizational factor that can influence the adoption of cloud computing. Large firms have an advantage over small ones, as they have more resources and can take greater risks associated with innovation adoption [75, 76]. Studies have shown that small firms, although more versatile, do not readily adopt newer technologies [73]. Thus, firm size is a determinant in the adoption of cloud computing [4]. Hence,

## H6: Firm size will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

## The environment context

The environment context is the setting in which the firm conducts its business, and is influenced by the nature of the industry, the firm’s competitors, access to resources supplied by others, and interactions with the government [73]. Of these, the determinants that have an impact on the cloud adoption are the firm’s competition and the regulatory environment [76].

Competitive pressure has long been recognized in the innovation diffusion literature as an important driver for technology diffusion. It refers to the pressure felt by the firm from industry competitors [4, 41, 77]. Adopting new technology is often a strategic necessity to compete in the market place. By adopting cloud computing, firms can benefit from greater operational efficiency, better market visibility, and more accurate access to real-time data [78]. Hence, H7: Competitive pressure will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

Regulatory support refers to the support given by the government authority to encourage the assimilation of IT innovation by firms [76]. The impact of existing laws and regulations can be critical in the adoption of new technologies. Government regulations can encourage or discourage businesses to adopt cloud computing. For example, legislators in the United States and the European Union member states have specific mandates to protect organizational data. When the government requires businesses to comply with cloud specific standards and protocols, firms will be more willing to adopt cloud computing. Thus,

H8: Regulatory support will positively influence cloud computing adoption.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Measurement

To evaluate the theoretical constructs, a survey was carried out in Portugal covering manufacturing and services industries. A questionnaire was developed by an expert panel composed of experienced researchers in the field of Information Systems. The questionnaire items were based on published literature (see Appendix A). To be consistent with the sources, the constructs (security concerns, cost savings, relative advantage, complexity, compatibility, technological readiness, top management support, competitive pressure, and regulatory support) were measured using a five-point Likert scale on an interval level ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree". Number of employees and the turnover volume (micro, small, medium, and large) are used as measures of the firm size [39, 79, 80]. Since the questionnaire was administered in Portugal, the English version of the instrument was translated into Portuguese and reviewed by a group of five established academic IS researchers and two language experts. To test the instrument, a pilot study was conducted among 30 firms. These firms were not included in the main survey. The results provided evidence that the scales are reliable, valid, and have translation equivalence [81].

## 4.2. Data

An online version of the questionnaire was emailed to qualified individuals (CIOs, directors, and senior IS managers) of 2000 manufacturing and services companies in Portugal. The company and contact data were provided by Dun & Bradstreet, one of the world's leading sources for commercial information and insight on businesses. Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered in two stages from mid-2012 to early 2013. The study utilized

#

the “key informants” approach for data collection [65, 82] to identify respondents in the organization who are most involved and knowledgeable about cloud computing. To target respondents who assume the role of the key informant, we provided a clear description of cloud computing and gave examples. To increase content validity, we indicated that the survey be filled out by the individual in the organization who is most familiar with the organization’s direction with cloud computing. To encourage participation and reduce self-reporting bias, we gave all participants the opportunity to receive the findings of the study and a report on how their firms compare to other firms of similar profile. 249 valid responses were received in the first stage. A follow-up email was sent in the second stage to those who did not respond in the first stage. 120 valid responses were obtained in this stage for a combined total of 369 usable responses. The overall response rate was 18.5%, which is comparable to other studies of similar scale [43, 65]. 62% of the valid responses (229 firms) were received from the services sector, and 38% (140 firms) were received from the manufacturing sector. To test for nonresponse bias, the sample distribution of the early and late respondent groups was compared using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test [83]. The sample distributions of the two groups did not differ statistically, indicating an absence of nonresponse bias [83]. The respondents were qualified individuals (see Table 3), indicating a good quality of the data. The common method bias was examined using Harman’s one-factor test [84]. ## 5. Results

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to empirically assess the research model. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed that none of the measurement items are distributed normally (p<0.001). This allows for the safe use of partial least squares (PLS) for the analysis, as it does not require a normal distribution [85]. For PLS estimation the minimum sample size should satisfy one of the two following conditions: 1) ten times the largest number of formative indicators used to measure one construct; or 2) ten times the largest number of structural paths directed at a particular latent construct in the structural model [23, 86]. Our sample consists of 369 firms, thus meeting the necessary conditions for using PLS. Smart-PLS software [86] was used to assess the reliability and validity of the measurement model before testing the various structural models. A two-fold analysis approach was taken to evaluate the research model. To understand the key determinants of cloud adoption we conducted a quantitative assessment of

#

the full sample. To investigate how the determinants vary across different industries we analyzed the sub-samples of the data for the manufacturing and services sector.

The indirect effect of cost savings for the manufacturing sub-sample is 0.227 $( 0 . 6 4 5 ^ { * } 0 . 3 5 1 )$ ), and is statistically significant (p<0.01), whereas the indirect effect of security concerns is found to be not statistically significant. This indicates that cost savings not only explains relative advantage but also that it indirectly influences cloud computing adoption. The research model explains 36.1% of cloud computing adoption among firms in the manufacturing sector.

For the services sector sub-sample, complexity (H2) $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 )$ , compatibility (H3) $( \mathfrak { p } <$ 0.10), technology readiness (H4) $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { p } } < 0 . 0 1 )$ , top management support (H5) $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ ), and firm size (H6) (p<0.05) are confirmed. The relative advantage (H6), competitive pressure (H7) and regulatory support are not statistically significant. For the services sector, indirect effect of cost savings is 0.053 $( 0 . 6 2 7 ^ { * } 0 . 0 8 5 )$ , and 0.007 $( 0 . 0 7 9 ^ { * } 0 . 0 8 5 )$ for security concerns. The results indicate that cost savings and security concerns are not statistically significant (p>0.10) for the services sector. The research model explains 40.8% of cloud computing adoption among firms in the services sector.

## 6. Discussion

Understanding the determinants of cloud computing is paramount as organizations consider the adoption of cloud computing for collaboration, content management, mobility, business process transformation, and rapid application development. The goal of this study is to assess the determinants of cloud computing adoption by using an integrative lens that combines the innovation characteristics of cloud computing and the technology, organization, and environment perspectives of the organization. The results show that five factors influences the adoption of cloud computing. They are relative advantage, complexity, technology readiness, top management support, and firm size (see Table 7). The results also indicate that the facilitators and inhibitors of cloud computing adoption are different in the manufacturing and services sector (see Table 7).

Innovation characteristics: Of the three innovation characteristics, relative advantage (H1) is found to have a positive influence on cloud computing adoption. This finding is consistent with similar studies reported in the literature [60, 90, 91]. The study confirms that organizations recognize the relative advantage of cloud computing. Advantages identified by the study include improving quality of business operations, performing tasks more quickly, increasing productivity, and providing new business opportunities. In investigating the role of innovation characteristics of cloud computing across the manufacturing and services sector, it is found that relative advantage is more important for firms in manufacturing than for firms in the services industry. On a comparative note, Borgman et al. [12] found relative advantage to be significant among global enterprises, whereas Low et al. [4] and Lin and Chen [30] found it to be an inhibitor of cloud computing adoption by firms in the high-tech industry.

To determine whether cloud computing will be relatively advantageous if it provides cost savings, and less advantageous if there are security concerns, we estimated the full and the manufacturing and service sub-samples with both constructs as predictors of relative advantage. Of the two variables, cost savings (H1b) is confirmed as the important driver to explain the relative advantage of cloud computing. This result is consistent with studies that have found cost savings to be a strong driver for the adoption of cloud based solutions in industries such as technology, manufacturing, finance, logistics, services, and education [7, 65, 92].

In contrast, security concerns (H1a) are not found to be an inhibitor for the adoption of cloud computing in either the full sample or the industry specific subsamples. A possible explanation is the recent advances in privacy enhancing techniques, monitoring mechanisms, and encryption schemes, to ensure confidentiality, integrity, and security of data in the cloud environment [50, 93, 94]. In addition, newly established standards and federal regulations such as the FedRamp [95], and the Federal Information Security Management Act help establish organizational trust and control over data when adopting cloud based solutions. This may explain the lack of concern regarding security and privacy when considering a cloud strategy.

Complexity (H2) is an inhibitor for the adoption of cloud computing in the services sector. The notion of complexity associated with cloud computing is no different than other disruptive technologies and appears to be a significant determinant for cloud adoption. It may be that complexity is associated with the perception of change, which is known to cause discomfort and frustration [96]. Our findings indicate that complexity is not an inhibitor among firms in the manufacturing sector. Complexity was found to be insignificant by Low et al. [4] and determined to be inconclusive by Borgman et al. [12]. Thus, in relation to earlier studies, the results for complexity are mixed and additional research is needed before reaching more definite conclusions.

Similarly, compatibility (H3) is found to be a facilitator for cloud computing adoption in the services sectors but not significant in the manufacturing sector. Its importance in the services industry may be explained by the work style preferences and internet based business operations that prevail among firms in this sector [97]. For manufacturing, the non-significance of compatibility may be attributable to the nature of applications (e.g., prominence of internal software solutions such as resource planning software, and computer controlled machining), and limited requirements for Internet based solutions in the line of business [98, 99]. Compatibility is a facilitator of innovation in some studies [91, 100, 101], but found to be non-significant in other studies [4, 12]. Thus, compared to earlier research, the results for compatibility are also mixed, and more investigation is needed to reach a definitive conclusion.

Technology context: Technology readiness (H4) is a facilitator of cloud computing adoption. The study finds that firms with an established technology infrastructure and workforce with the necessary skills and technical competence are better suited for cloud integration. However, earlier studies have suggested that technology readiness may not necessarily influence cloud computing adoption. For example, Low et al. [4] found technology readiness to be irrelevant for companies in the technology sector. Wu et al. [53] found that firms with greater information processing capacity are less likely to adopt cloud computing. The results of our study indicate otherwise. Implementation of cloud computing can cause possible disruption of services and create management challenges in IT- and non-IT-centric businesses. The finding indicates that organizations must ensure that the technology infrastructure and availability of IT specialists are adequate for the integration of cloud based solutions in the business operations with minimal interruption.

Organization context: Our study provides empirical evidence that top management support (H5) is significant in explaining the adoption of cloud computing. Evidence from the study indicates that top management can influence cloud computing adoption by demonstrating

#

support in the form of committing financial and organizational resources and engaging in the process. This finding is consistent with results from earlier studies on the adoption and use of innovative technologies [60, 99, 102]. However, our findings also indicate that top management support is not significant to firms in the manufacturing sector. Plausible explanations are the unrecognized value of cloud computing in the manufacturing industry, lack of industry specific incurring high costs and risks [91, 102].

Firm size (H6) is a predictor of cloud computing adoption. This finding is consistent with literature suggesting that larger companies have resources to cover the cost and investment risk of an emerging technology [91, 103, 104]. Conversely, smaller firms generally lack the resources needed for the construction of knowledge, and the implementation and testing of cloud computing [75]. In related studies, Low et al. [4] found firm size to be a facilitator of cloud computing, whereas Borgman et al. [12] determined it to be inconclusive.

Environment context: Cloud computing is a relatively recent area of research in the IS discipline, and only a few empirical studies have addressed the importance of the environment context. Low et al. [4] found that competitive pressure pushed firms in the high-tech industry to adopt cloud computing more quickly [4]. Borgman et al. [12], in a study of 24 global enterprises, found contradictory results, suggesting that the legislative environment is not statistically significant in explaining cloud computing adoption. Based on a survey of 214 SMEs, Ifinedo [38] determined that competitive pressure positively impacts the adoption of technologies that support online commerce. He also determined that pressure from customers, business partners, and government support did not play a significant role.

The results of our study indicate that both variables in the environment context (i.e., competitive pressure and regulatory support) are not determinants of cloud computing adoption. It is likely that firms are aware of the benefits of cloud computing, but specific factors of the technology and organization context overshadow the benefits of cloud computing from translating into a competitive advantage. Regulatory support was also found to be not significant to the adoption of cloud computing. This does not necessarily mean that firms disregard prevailing standards and regulations, but that existing legislations protecting the use of cloud computing has not been earnestly embraced by the organizational decision makers. Regulatory processes are essential to instill the sense of trust necessary for firms to convert innovation into business opportunities. Without economic incentives that make good business sense, advances in technology, evolving cloud computing standards, and federal regulations may not be sufficient to overcome the barriers to the adoption of cloud computing. The implications of the study to practice and theory are summarized below.

## 6.1. Managerial and practical implications

Our study results suggest that relative advantage, complexity, technology readiness, top management support, and firm size influence the firm’s adoption of cloud computing. For decision makers in the organization considering cloud-based initiatives, the findings provide a sound basis for gauging the direct and indirect effects of the innovation characteristics of cloud computing, as well as, the literature related to its adoption in varying industries.

We hypothesized that cloud computing offers a relative advantage when there is compatibility with current business operations, and that complexity is an inhibitor to its adoption. However, it appears that the relative advantage of cloud computing is not conclusive across industries. Other studies have identified concerns of hidden costs, human resource requirements for upgrade and maintenance, potential for loss of overall control of resources, and poor quality of operation as factors that impact the relative advantage of cloud computing [4, 30, 105]. Our findings indicate that cloud computing offers the relative advantages of achieving economic benefits as well as environmental responsibility benefits. Economic benefits include cost savings from decreased capital expenditures on IT, lower negotiation costs, and reduced maintenance and energy costs [7, 44]. Benefits of environmental responsibility include reducing environmental impacts and generating brand recognition from the adoption of cloud computing - a type of Green IT solution [106, 107]. With regard to security, privacy and user behavior in a cloud environment, recent technological advances in the cloud computing domain [108, 109] are promising developments that address these organizational concerns and help move a firm’s computing infrastructure to the cloud.

Concerns of complexity are attributable to lack of knowledge about the new technology and resistance to changes in established business processes [110]. However, cloud computing has the potential to reduce the complexity of IT services by introducing commonality across business functions and automation in the management process [11]. As cloud computing matures, compatibility of cloud computing with other IT solutions will present no added difficulties than the routine IT management challenges [12]. To summarize, the innovation characteristics of cloud computing that influence its adoption are the relative advantage from cost savings and concerns of complexity. For the manufacturing sector, complexity is not as much of a concern as it is for the services sector. Compatibility is a greater consideration in the adoption of cloud computing for firms in the services sector.

With regard to technology context, the study finds technology readiness of the firm to be crucial to cloud computing adoption. For successful adoption of cloud solutions, mangers need to factor in realistic expectations of the integration challenges and ramp up a team of experts whose skillsets cross the conventional IT environment and the cloud platform. With regard to the organization context, top management support and firm size are found to be facilitators of cloud computing adoption. Adoption of cloud computing is not a phenomenon limited to large organizations. However, the results of our study are similar to earlier studies [4, 111] that suggest that larger firms tend to invest more readily in cloud based solutions. With regard to the environment context, the results suggest that competitive pressure and regulatory support are not significant drivers of cloud computing adoption. Thus, compared to the high-tech industries [4], gauging the competitor’s cloud strategy and changes in government regulations are not as significant to the adoption of cloud computing in the manufacturing and services sectors.

The findings of this study provide valuable insights for managers to make an informed cloud adoption decision. In evaluating this relatively recent technology, our study highlights the importance of assessing the innovation characteristics against the technology, organization, and environment context of the organization prior to adopting cloud computing solutions. Even in the presence of competitive pressure and supportive regulation, without weighing the technological readiness and top management support within the firm, the relative advantage of cloud computing may fail to translate into economic value for the organization.

## 6.2. Implications for theory

The study makes important contributions to the body of research on the adoption of new technologies and cloud computing. In recent years researchers have called for more holistic approaches that combine more than one theoretical perspective to understand the IT adoption phenomenon involving innovative new technologies [42, 53, 55, 56]. In this research we integrate two theoretical perspectives (the DOI theory and the TOE framework) to develop the research model. The model combines the innovation characteristics of cloud computing and the technology, organization, and environment context of the organization that underlines the adoption of cloud computing. It is different from most other studies on cloud computing that fall short of holistically evaluating the combined effects of the innovation characteristics and the contextual factors (see Table 1). No earlier studies have also empirically validated the indirect effects of the antecedents on the determinants of cloud computing adoption. The instrument used in this study has been verified for reliability, validity, and discriminant tests. It is therefore fair to conclude that the research model and the instrument provide a sound basis for understanding the determinants of cloud computing adoption. The model and the instrument can thus be adopted for future use in other innovation studies.

Based on a large sample (n=369) we evaluate the adoption of cloud computing as a continuous process, unlike Low et al. [4] who use a dichotomous dependent variable. In addition, we use SEM, which is a statistically powerful technique that requires a sample of considerable size to attain good rates of adhesion. Our study thus addresses the research gap between the advancements in cloud computing and its adoption by organizations that have been identified by other researchers as requiring further investigation [5, 15, 112, 113]. Our study also found that manufacturing and services sectors have different drivers of cloud computing adoption. This demonstrates that industry variance cannot be modeled as control variable in cloud computing adoption. For future research, our study thus highlights the need for a systematic evaluation of the determinants of cloud computing at the industry level.

Developing an integrative research model and evaluating it using a large data set represents an important step toward addressing the paucity of research in the comprehensive assessment of the social, organizational, and environmental perspectives of cloud adoption [15]. Our study not only fills this important research gap, but also serves to improve the awareness of firms to the direct and indirect effects of the constructs that influence the adoption of cloud computing in the manufacturing and services industries.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The study is not without limitations. One limitation is that the sample is limited to the country of Portugal, which implies that the study reflects only the Portuguese reality. Another limitation is the possibility of including variables that are not considered in our research model. For example, our analysis revealed different determinants of cloud computing adoption in the two distinct industries that we studied. Thus, our study suggests the need to formulate a model for each industry rather than a comprehensive model that combines the characteristics of innovation as a single control. Strictly speaking, this will involve additional research to develop model estimates similar to those developed for the manufacturing and services sector. Only then can a fair judgment regarding the determinants of cloud computing adoption be compelling. Nonetheless, the model proposed in this research should provide a sound basis for future endeavors in this direction. Another limitation is that our definition of the adoption of cloud computing may be too narrow. We therefore suggest extending the research model to include additional variables to account for factors such as the organization’s information processing requirements, and information processing capacity. Information processing view (IPV) is a good candidate [114] to further enrich the model. The study thus opens possibilities for additional research and refinement of the constructs to elucidate better clarity of organizational cloud computing adoption. Finally, an interesting direction worthy of pursuit would be to apply the proposed model to other countries and industries.

## 7. Conclusion

Cloud computing is an important evolution of IS technology. It boasts attractive properties such as agility, scalability, pay-per-use, and cost efficiency. This study sought to identify the determinants of cloud computing adoption based on innovation characteristics and the technology, organization, and environment context of organizations. A research model was developed that integrates the DOI theory and the TOE framework. The model was empirically evaluated based on a sample of 369 firms from Portugal. It was used to compare the adoption of cloud computing in two distinct sectors, namely manufacturing and services. The results indicated that relative advantage, complexity, technology readiness, top management support, and firm size have a direct effect on the firm’s adoption of cloud computing. The analysis of results validated the direct effect of cost savings on the relative advantage of cloud computing, as well as its indirect effect on cloud computing adoption. Our study also found that the manufacturing and services sectors have different drivers of cloud computing adoption, which underscores the importance of considering the distinct characteristics of cloud computing across different industries. The findings supplement the literature that evaluates cloud computing adoption in areas such as high-tech, finance, supply chain, logistics, and education. The research shows that in assessing the adoption of new technologies such as cloud computing, a scientific approach that takes into consideration the technology, organization, and environment context of the organization along with the innovation characteristics is more holistic and meaningful in providing valuable insights to practitioners and researchers.
