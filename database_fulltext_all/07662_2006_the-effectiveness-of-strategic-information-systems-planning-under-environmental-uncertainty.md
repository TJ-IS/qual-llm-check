# The effectiveness of strategic information systems planning under environmental uncertainty

Henry E. Newkirk <sup>a,\*</sup>, Albert L. Lederer <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business, Department of Decision Sciences, East Carolina University, Greenville, NC 27858-4353, United States <sup>b</sup> C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0034, United States

Received 19 August 2004; received in revised form 1 July 2005; accepted 21 December 2005 Available online 14 February 2006

## Abstract

Researchers have suggested that more extensive strategic information systems planning (SISP) in an uncertain environment produces greater planning success. Managers must decide whether, and if so when, to perform such SISP. Our study tested the effect of SISP phases on planning success in more and less uncertain environments.

A questionnaire assessed SISP in terms of strategic awareness, situation analysis, strategy conception, strategy formulation, and strategy implementation planning phases. It inquired about environmental uncertainty as dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility. Finally, it measured SISP success as a composite of alignment, analysis, cooperation, and capabilities. One hundred and sixty-one IS executives provided data in a postal survey.

More extensive strategy formulation uniformly predicted successful planning in more uncertain environments, whereas strategic awareness generally predicted it in less uncertain ones. Strategy conception predicted it in neither more nor less uncertain environments. More extensive planning is thus not uniformly successful in either environment but depends on the nature of the uncertainty.

## 1. Introduction

Environmental uncertainty can impact organizations detrimentally [20]. In an uncertain environment, customers can reduce their purchases, inventories can rapidly increase, and vendors can demand more prompt payments, causing revenues to plunge, costs to escalate, and profits to vanish.

Organizations perform strategic business planning as an organizational learning process [2,14]. They do so in order to anticipate environmental uncertainty and dampen its detrimental effects. Strategic information systems planning (SISP) help do this. Given today’s global marketplace and increasingly complex economy, SISP has become more and more critical to the survival of many organizations. Not surprisingly, IS and business managers have consistently ranked it among the top IS management issues [9,30].

The purpose of this study was to examine SISP effectiveness in uncertain environments [42]. Researchers have suggested that more extensive planning would be more successful, because it would help planners understand the impact of the environment and better respond to it [41].

On the other hand, the changes produced by a less uncertain environment would be less detrimental and thus more extensive planning would be less imperative. However, such an environment provides a baseline for comparison and contrast with a more uncertain one.

One case study, based on action research, found that an organization more meticulously practicing greater SISP succeeded in its planning endeavor, whereas one acting less meticulously failed in the same, extremely uncertain environment. On the other hand, decision scenario interviews with strategic business planners have shown that comprehensiveness was negatively related to performance in an uncertain environment [18].

An extensive literature review by the authors identified no empirical studies to resolve those contradictory findings. This research makes a first attempt to do so.

## 2. Constructs

## 2.1. The SISP process

SISP has been defined and studied empirically as the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will support an organization’s business plans, thus enabling the organization to align its IS with its business needs and achieve its business goals [40]. SISP is thus intended to help the organization reach its goal of improved competitiveness, operations, and resource management [1,13].

According to Mentzas [31], a process such as SISP ‘‘can be defined as a set of partially ordered steps intended to reach [such] a goal.’’ Partially ordered implies the possibility of some degree of overlap and reiteration of those steps. Mentzas considered each component as a process element, and thus advocated decomposition of SISP in order to study, understand, and improve it.

Other researchers had described SISP as an intricate and complex group of specific, interrelated tasks or activities with well-defined methods and criteria as their basis [17]. In addition, SISP is intended to inspire creativity and innovation [12]. The tasks thus require considerable management coordination [21].

Mentzas described SISP activities as a set of phases and the specific tasks within each. They represent the components of the planning process, with each having its own objectives, participants, preconditions, products, and techniques; e.g., the strategy conception phase involves identifying major IT objectives, identifying opportunities for improvement, evaluating them, and identifying high level IT strategies. Observation of the extent to which an organization carries out each phase and task may be used to assess extent of SISP use. We therefore considered phases and tasks as our basis for assessing the degree of use of SISP in our study: they reflected specific actions and tapped the full range of the effort. ## 2.2. Environmental uncertainty

Environmental uncertainty has been characterized and studied in terms of three dimensions: dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility [34]. These dimensions were extensively assessed and validated by their creators [32,33]. IS researchers have used them in their studies of the contextual factors that facilitate strategic IS applications as well as of the integration of IS and business planning.

 Dynamism is the rate and unpredictability of environmental change. It is especially challenging to managers. Researchers have measured it in terms of the rate of product/service obsolescence, the rate of product/services technology change, unpredictability of competitors’ moves, and unpredictability of product/services demand changes.

 Heterogeneity is the complexity and diversity of external factors. It has been operationalized in terms of diversity in customers’ buying habits, diversity in the nature of competition, and diversity in product lines.

 Hostility involves both the availability of resources and the degree of competition in the external environment. Researchers have measured it in terms of the threats posed by labor and material scarcity, price and product-quality competition, and product differentiation.

These three constructs can be measured in a questionnaire using the evaluation of specific items defined previously by Teo and King [49], as shown in Table 2; they were employed in our study.

## 2.3. SISP success

An early theoretical construct of planning success came from strategic business planning [50]. Its two dimensions were the extent of fulfillment of key objectives and improvement in the capability of the planning system. IS researchers later adapted this framework [39]. Their motivation was based on the importance of good IS planning, the lack of an empirically defined measure of SISP success, and the need for such a measure to guide SISP. Statistical tests and other key measurement criteria provided strong support for the new SISP success measure.

The same two dimensions were used to develop an instrument, but were decomposed into four dimensions [45]. Three, alignment, analysis, and cooperation, represented Venkatraman and Ramanujam’s extent of fulfillment of key objectives. The fourth, capabilities, represented the improvement in the planning system.

Alignment refers to the outcomes of the ties between the IS strategy and business strategy [4,26–28]. It enhances top management’s understanding of the significance of IS, and increases IS management’s understanding of business objectives. It thus encourages top management to provide leadership and financial support for the proposed information systems that would help the firm realize its objectives.

Analysis involves the outcomes of the examination of the firm’s internal operations [7,10,22]. It helps IS planners better understand business processes and procedures, IT, and the organization’s structure in order to discern how the firm can apply new IS to compete using its information architecture and infrastructure.

Cooperation refers to the outcomes of managers’ and users’ agreement about priorities, schedules, and responsibilities [25]. Planners use it to increase the chances that managers and users participate seriously in SISP and its resulting plan. Cooperation can generate a bond between managers, users, and IS developers, and thereby reduce resistance that can impede SISP implementation.

Finally, improvement in capabilities refers to the enhancement of the potential of the planning system. The improvement of the planning process is key to planning effectiveness. The organizational learning provided by SISP should result in greater ability to align IS and business strategies, analyze internal operations, and promote cooperation.

These four constructs formed the basis for the assessment of SISP success in our research. The constructs and their items are shown in Table 3.

## 3. Hypotheses

The achievement of SISP success in uncertain environments is critical to planners. Analysis in uncertain environments might be expected to produce greater knowledge about competitors, resources, customers, and regulators. This would make it possible to understand and predict change and thus to develop plans that are less vulnerable to consequences of that change. This would result in greater top management confidence and commitment, resulting in a better plan with more likelihood of implementation [6].

Gathering information, evaluating potential courses of action, limiting trial-and-error decision making, delaying decisions until all alternatives have been evaluated, and determining a good course of action could produce a successful planning outcome [47]. In a more uncertain environment planning might be expected to be more effective and thus all the more critical.

## 3.1. Strategic awareness phase

Strategic awareness should focus the planning process on obtaining appropriate knowledge about competitors, resources, customers, and regulators. Careful organizing of the teams would result in members more capable of obtaining and understanding that knowledge. Top management commitment provides greater organizational confidence and continued financial support for the process. Hence:

$\mathbf { H 1 _ { h i g h } } .$ . More strategic awareness leads to greater SISP success in high: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

## 3.2. Situation analysis phase

Situation analysis, with careful study of the business, organization, and IS, would produce better knowledge about the organization’s requirements. Study of external business and IT environments would help produce better knowledge about the impact of change and provide a better foundation for the plan, enabling it to produce better results. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathbf { h i g h } } .$ . More situation analysis leads to greater SISP success in high: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

## 3.3. Strategy conception phase

Strategy conception, with identification and evaluation of opportunities, would provide more realistic alternatives. Identification of IT objectives would allow the organization to align future IT and business objectives. Better alternatives and choices would help the plan produce better results. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } 3 _ { \mathbf { h i g h } } .$ . More strategy conception leads to greater SISP success in high: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

## 3.4. Strategy formulation phase

Strategy formulation, with a careful identification of the plan itself (its processes, architectures, and projects), would make it more likely to meet planning objectives. Better prioritization would result in greater likelihood of implementation and greater chance of meeting objectives. Hence:

$\mathbf { H 4 _ { h i g h } }$ . More strategy formulation leads to greater SISP success in high: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

## 3.5. Strategy implementation planning

Finally, strategy implementation planning, with more attention to change management and a better action plan, would be likely to result in good implementation. Better follow-up and control would result in more of the plan being implemented and thus better delivery of planning objectives. Hence:

$\mathbf { H } 5 _ { \mathbf { h i g h } } .$ More strategy implementation planning leads to greater SISP success in high: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

A less uncertain environment provides a baseline for comparison and contrast with the more uncertain one. Therefore, five hypotheses for a less uncertain environment are:

$\mathbf { H 1 _ { l o w } } .$ . More strategic awareness leads to greater SISP success in low: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

$\mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathbf { l o w } } .$ . More situation analysis leads to greater SISP success in low: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

$\mathbf { H } 3 _ { \mathbf { l o w } }$ . More strategy conception leads to greater SISP success in low: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

$\mathbf { H 4 _ { l o w } } .$ . More strategy formulation leads to greater SISP success in low: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

$\mathbf { H } \boldsymbol { 5 } _ { \mathbf { l o w } } .$ . More strategy implementation planning leads to greater SISP success in low: (i) dynamism, (ii) heterogeneity, and (iii) hostility environments.

## 4. Methodology

We developed a field survey for IS executives. The instrument used five-point Likert-scales to operationalize three constructs: the SISP process, environmental uncertainty, and SISP success.

The SISP process construct measured the extent to which the organization conducted the five planning phases and their tasks. The items were derived from Mentzas and previously used in a study of the SISP autonomy of the subsidiaries of multinational corporations [35]. The environmental uncertainty construct measured the extent of dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility in the external environment of the firm. The success construct measured the extent that the organization fulfilled its IS objectives of alignment, analysis, and cooperation, and the extent IS capabilities improved or deteriorated over time. Appendices A–C display the items with their instructions and scales as they appeared in the survey.

## 4.1. Pilot test

Five IS executives were asked to participate in a pilot test. All agreed. Four had the title of Chief Information Officer and one was Director of Information Services. Their experience ranged from 17 to 38 years. They worked in a variety of industries.

Each completed the survey in the presence of the senior author, taking about 17 min. After doing so, they commented on the contents, length, and overall appearance of the instrument. Changes were made to the survey before the next subject was interviewed. The fifth test resulted in no changes.

## 4.2. Data collection

A sample of IS executives in the U.S. was randomly selected from the East and West editions of the Directory of Top Computer Executives [16]. The survey was sent to 1200 people. Data collection was completed in the summer of 2001 and therefore reflected planning for Web-based, enterprise resource planning, supply chain, knowledge management, and other modern applications. In fact, the use of older data is not uncommon in SISP research because they tend to be relatively time invariable even though IT changes rapidly [11,29,44,46]. A total of 220 executives returned the survey for a response rate of 18%. Fiftynine sent only demographic data and stated that they had not participated in an organization’s SISP; thus, the analysis used 161 surveys.

## 5.1. Common method variance and response bias

The CIO is typically seen as the most knowledgeable person to assess SISP, its context, and its outcomes [38]. However, multiple subjects per organization are preferred in order to reduce common method variance, which arises from using one individual and can account for a relationship between similar measures [37]. Harman’s single-factor test is sometimes applied to check for common method variance [5,15,43], and it was used by us. The assumption underlying the test is that if much variance exists in the data, a single factor will emerge from an exploratory factor analysis accounting for most of the variance.

The results of this analysis on our data revealed 15 factors with an Eigenvalue greater than one and no single factor explained most of the variance (i.e., the variances explained ranged from 1.2 to 27%). Such results are consistent with the absence of a significant variance common to the measures.

A time-trend extrapolation test examined nonresponse bias [3]. It assumes that non-respondents resemble late rather than early responders. Using the first 25% as early and the last 25% as non-respondents, a multivariate analysis of variance of all 63 variables indicated no significant differences (Wilks’ Lambda = 0.98; $p = 0 . 1 8 )$ . This finding is consistent with the absence of non-response bias.

## 5.2. Validation of the constructs

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using EQS was performed on the detailed items of the SISP process, environmental uncertainty, and SISP success constructs individually with requirements that the comparative fit index (CFI), robust comparative fit index (RCFI), and non-normed fit index (NNFI) be 0.90 or higher, the Satorra–Bentler chi square divided by degrees of freedom (SB $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . } )$ ratio be 2.0 or lower, the standardized root mean square residual (RMR) be 0.10 or less, and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) be 0.08 or less [8,19,24]. The EQS robust option using the maximum likelihood estimation method was used because it does not make assumptions about the normality of the data. Internal consistency was calculated via Cronbach’s alpha with the minimum requirement of 0.70 [36]. Standardized factor loadings were computed to test the convergent validity for the constructs, and the chisquare difference test and confidence interval test were used to assess the discriminant validity of the constructs. Appendices E–G give detailed explanations of the validation process for each construct.

## 6. Hypothesis testing

Six simultaneous linear regressions examined the relationships between the SISP phases and SISP success. A single regression tested all five phases in each of the six environments: high dynamism, high heterogeneity, high hostility, low dynamism, low heterogeneity, and low hostility. For each regression, subjects were separated into groups of the highest third and lowest third based on dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility. The highest third thus represented the more uncertain and the lowest third represented the less uncertain environment. Using such groupings is consistent with the idea that the effects would hold in extreme environments.

Regression results that provided statistically significant p-values for the highest third supported hypotheses predicting that planning would be more effective in more uncertain environments (the $\mathrm { H _ { h i g h } }$ hypotheses). Those with statistically significant $p \mathrm { - }$ values for the lowest third supported hypotheses predicting that planning would be more effective in less uncertain environments (the $\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ hypotheses). ## 7.1. Strategic awareness

$\mathrm { H } 1 _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ found support only in a hostile environment. $\mathrm { H } 1 _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ was fully supported. Apparently creating strategic awareness has a greater positive impact on planning in a less uncertain environment.

## 7.2. Situation analysis

$\mathrm { H } 2 _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ found no support. Situation analysis is a tedious and time consuming gathering of detailed information.

$\mathrm { H } 2 _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ found partial support. In a low heterogeneity environment (with industry diversity in customer buying habits, the nature of competition, and product lines), situation analysis predicted success.

## 7.3. Strategy conception

$\mathrm { H } 3 _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ and $\mathrm { H } 3 _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ found no support in any of the environments. Strategy conception produces high-level alternative strategies (from which planners choose and develop one particular strategy in the next phase), but more strategy conception does not, apparently, produce a better set of alternatives from which to choose. The discovery that conceiving strategic alternatives did not result in SISP success may be our most surprising finding: the phase had the highest mean (3.99 after dropping one item during validation) showing that it was performed most but had no effect on SISP success!

## 7.4. Strategy formulation

$\mathrm { H } 4 _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ was fully supported, whereas $\mathrm { H } 4 _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ was not.

## 7.5. Strategy implementation planning

$\mathrm { H } 5 _ { \mathrm { h i g h } }$ and $\mathrm { H } 5 _ { \mathrm { l o w } }$ were both partially supported. Strategy implementation planning predicted success in highly dynamic and hostile environments as well as in less heterogeneous and hostile ones.

The strategy implementation planning phase is especially interesting, because implementation is generally seen as the major impediment to success. Plans may be conceived and formulated but are seldom implemented. Our results show that strategy implementation planning predicted success in both high and low hostility environments; that is, regardless of the degree of competition, strategy implementation planning has great impact.

## 7.6. Overall results

Apparently planning is no more effective in either a more or less uncertain environment. The F and $R ^ { 2 }$ values were quite similar in both more and less uncertain environments and six hypotheses were supported in each environment.

Instead, the results suggest that some planning phases are more effective in different environmental dimensions. Perhaps this resolves the previous contradictory findings on planning in an uncertain environment by showing that the success varies by type of planning activity and type of environmental uncertainty.

## 8. Implications for practice

Our work provided implications for planners. First, it can help by making them more aware of the components of SISP success. It confirmed a previous framework that decomposed success into alignment, analysis, cooperation, and capabilities. Understanding those factors may help planners focus their efforts on their objectives and realize greater value from the planning process in their own organization.

Second, it can raise their awareness of the phases of SISP. Planners should be knowledgeable about the five phases, but the individual items in each might suggest overlooked activities. These might impede the organization from achieving its planning objectives and thus from realizing greater value.

Third, it can increase their understanding of environmental uncertainty: the framework of dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility. Consideration of them might make planners more aware of the challenges they face.

Fourth, it can help them understand the role of uncertainty and how they might respond to it. Planners should consider their efforts in each phase carefully and might be more sensitive of the need to vary their planning, depending on the phase and type of uncertainty they are experiencing.

We found that the greatest effort went into the strategic conception phase, which had the weakest predictive power. That phase predicted success in no environmental dimension. Putting effort into conceiving alternative strategies seems to be sensible but the fact that the effort does not affect success suggests that many planners emphasize actions that do not help them achieve their objectives.

Apparently planners currently underemphasize actions that do help them achieve their objectives. Managers might be planning inefficiently and ineffectively. They should pause and reconsider what they need to emphasize and de-emphasize in their endeavors.

## 9. Conclusion

Our research investigated the effects of SISP in both more and less uncertain environments. It contributed by showing that:

 strategy formulation uniformly predicted successful planning in more but not less uncertain ones;

 strategic awareness generally predicted it in less but not more uncertain ones;

 strategy conception did not predict it in either.

It also showed that planners put their greatest effort into strategy conception although it had the least impact on planning effectiveness, while they put their least effort into strategy implementation planning which tied for the greatest impact. Finally, it contributed by validating three existing instruments.

## Appendix G. Validation of the SISP success construct

Thirty items measured the SISP success construct. Eight measured alignment and eight measured analysis. Seven measured cooperation and seven measured capability. Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.79 to 0.87, above the minimally required level.

The NNFI was below the minimum cut-off in the initial CFA. After dropping one item, all fit indices met the criteria. Table G.1 displays the CFA modifications and results, and Fig. G.1 shows the final measurement model.

The standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.50 to 0.76 and the t statistics were significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . All of the loadings thus met or exceeded Hair et al.’s recommended minimum requirement of 0.50. These results supported the convergent validity for the construct.

After setting pair-wise correlations among the constructs to 1, the chi-square differences between the standard measurement model and the revised measurement models were significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ Moreover, the confidence intervals calculated for the construct did not include the value 1.0. Thus, both of these tests supported discriminant validity for the construct.

The results of this validation, with its deletion of only one item, compare favorably to those of Segars and Grover on the same instrument. Those researchers dropped three items due to low reliability and one more due to a cross loading.
