# For whom does a game update? Players' status-contingent gameplay on online games before and after an update

Ji Hyeon Hyeong<sup>a</sup>, Kang Jun Choi<sup>a</sup>, Jae Young Lee<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Tae-Hyung Pyo<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Business, Yonsei University, Seoul, Republic of Korea

<sup>b</sup> College of Business and Economics, University of Idaho, Moscow, ID, United States of America.

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online game Online game update Social status Social motivation Gameplay

## A B S T R A C T

In an online game, multiple players virtually play the game together and are ranked based on their in-game performance through competition. Online games update constantly and continuously (i.e., a modification in the game content after its release), changing the game every time. This research examines the efect that these game updates have on individual gameplay. We compared 9342 players' daily gameplay for a month before and after updates across eighteen updates over seven online games. There are two novel findings. First, middle-ranked players tend to play the game more than high and low-ranked players before an update. Second, contrary to the widespread notion that game updates trigger players to increase their gameplay, online game updates only increase the gameplay of high and low-ranked players. Conclusively, high and low-ranked players tend to play the game more than the middle-ranked players after an update.

## 1. Introduction

In an online game, multiple players virtually play the game together in a game world and are ranked based on their in-game performance through competition. By efectively boosting players' competitive motivation and continuously updating new content, online games have been one of the largest and fastest-growing digital product industries. The online game industry has grown by 8.2% in revenue over the last five years [1] and is estimated to reach a revenue of \$18.2 billion and 931.7 million players in 2020 [2]; in fact, the size of the online game industry has already surpassed film and music industry combined [3]. Furthermore, with the emergence of relevant businesses, such as esports, the online game industry is extending its boundaries to other areas [4], expecting to achieve revenue growth of 1.4% in 2021 [5]. Such growth highlights the significance of the online game market and calls for specialized knowledge that enhances the understanding of online games.

In online games, a game update denotes the process of creating new content and changing the game environment by adding new items, play modes, and ranking systems $[ 6 , 7 ] . ^ { 1 }$ Since game updates provide new stimuli to players and keep them motivated to play the game, they, in turn, increase player retention and overall gameplay [8,9]. Consequently, game updates have become an integral part of online games, and online game companies routinely update their games every three to six months on average [10]. It is important to note that a game update involves various managerial decisions beyond the routine addition of new content, which comprehensively overarch from designing the game elements for the update to promoting the update to individual players. To best use game updates as a means to enhance the popularity and profitability of the game, it is essential to make a series of decisions based on a solid understanding of how updates change individual player behavior.

Although there is a myriad of studies regarding online games [12–15], few studies have examined the efect that updates have on players' behavioral change, despite the apparent necessity [6,8,11,16]. Even the few existing papers are not directly applicable for understanding updates in online games for the following reasons. First, the current studies have overlooked individual diferences in responses to updates but analyzed overall responses to updates at an aggregate level. Given that personalization has become increasingly dominant in online game markets [17], studying updates without individual diferences inevitably ends up lagging behind the market trend and has limited implications for practitioners. Second, past research on updates has missed out on the social element of online game updates. In online games, individual players are social entities who compete with other players to take a higher position in a virtual society. A game update changes the rules of competition and, therefore, will afect players social motivation to pursue a higher rank, as well as their behavior. Past studies have neglected the central drivers of individual gameplay and corresponding changes caused by game updates by failing to consider the social element. Third, there are issues of external validity in the existing studies; since the majority of studies on updates are based on user surveys [11], they lack an analysis of the users' actual behavioral change. Moreover, even the few studies that have analyzed actual gameplay also share the concern of generalizability due to confining the scope of the study to one specific game [6,8].

In this study, we empirically analyzed the actual changes of in dividual players' gameplay before and after updates in online games. Based on the existing theories on social change, social status, and social motivation, we examined, in particular, how players respond difer ently to game updates depending on their game ranking, an explicit social hierarchy in a virtual society. For empirical analysis, we collected 9342 individual player gaming history over eighteen game updates across seven online games. We analyzed individual gameplay during a month before and after the update. There are two novel findings. First, before the game update, middle-ranked players play the game more than high and low-ranked players in a pre-established game environment. Second, game updates do not increase gameplay of all players; they increase the gameplay of only high and low-ranked players but not of middle-ranked players.

Our research has several theoretical implications. First, we examine how updates change individual players' gameplay diferently across the game ranking. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first to investigate individual diferences in response to online game updates. Second, we propose a novel theoretical framework to understand the nature of game updates better. Unlike past research that viewed game updates from a functional perspective only, we see game updates as a social change in online game societies, which can encourage or discourage players' gameplay depending on their game ranking. Third, this research contributes to literature by using a novel data set. Unlike past research on online games that used data from surveys or analyzed only one specific game, we examined individual gaming history of multiple updates across a number of diferent games collected from gaming websites.

Our research also has actionable insights to practitioners in the online game industry. First, we provide insight on how to design the game fundamentals, such as the items or the ranking system. Because high and low-ranked players tend to respond more enthusiastically to game updates, practitioners may want to add new items that favor high and low-ranked players. When updating a game, it would also be essential to design game fundamentals in a way that allows players to feel that they are in high(er) or low(er) ranks than they may be. Second, our findings suggest that practitioners need to customize the promotion of updates depending on the player's ranking. When reaching out to high and low-ranked players, for instance, practitioners may want to emphasize innovative components in the game updates because these players have the motivation to play the game with its new changes. In contrast, such emphasis on innovation should be toned down for middle-ranked players because changes in the in-game environment often demotivate them.

In the following sections, we build our hypotheses on previous research. Next, we describe our research setting, data, and empirical analyses. We then discuss our findings and check their robustness and conclude with the implications and limitations of our research.

## 2. Theoretical framework and hypotheses

## 2.1. An online game as a virtual society

An online game connects multiple individual players via an online network, thus allowing players from diferent locations to play games together [18,19]. Since online game players can observe, compete, cooperate, and interact with each other as they do in the real world [20], online games are the epitome of a virtual society where the action of players influences other players [21]. In this regard, online games are distinguishable from single-player games where only one player plays the game, disconnected from other players.

Online games rank players based on predetermined standards, and such game ranking is often made public to others. For instance, players can observe other players' rankings, level, and-or badges announced on a leaderboard on the game's website or directly from the game interface. This formal and explicit hierarchy facilitates social comparison among players and encourages them to surpass others [22–25]. As a result, a social motivation to pursue a higher ranking, a respected and valued position in the given virtual society, is a crucial determinant of how intensely a player commits their time and efort in a game [26–29]. These characteristics contrast significantly with the player motivation of a single-player game, which is mostly driven by the autonomy and competence achieved by the player during game experience [30,31].

## 2.2. Game update and game ranking

An update in an online game refers to the change in a game environment or game content, such as new policies or new items [8]. In the virtual society of an online game, a game update is a form of social change; for instance, introducing a new scoring system redefines existing rankings, and adding new items provides new ways to compete for a higher game ranking. In this light, a game update is a paradigm shift in online game society; a change in the pre-established rules of competition and performance assessment in an online game facilitates social mobility across diferent rankings. Thus, an update influences players' social motivation to play the given online game.

Then, how will an update afect the social motivation of individual players and their resulting gameplay? According to existing literature, an individual's social motivation is driven by the upward social mobility into their desired status [32–34] and the efort needed to take the chance [35–37]. Given the above, if an update creates a game environment where a player can lift their game ranking with reasonable efort, it will motivate them to play the game.

Yet we expect that an update will not equally motivate the entirety of players. Instead, its influence will vary by the player's game ranking. Previous works have noted that there is a discrepancy in the social motivation that people of diferent social statuses experience due to the difering social mobility they face depending on their social status [38–40]. Such discrepancies require people to commit varying levels of efort to raise their social status [41,42], implying that players in different online game rankings have diferent levels of social motivation and gameplay before and after an update.

## 2.3. Before updates

Before an update, an online game society has pre-established rules of competition and performance assessment. As a result, an in-game society displays the following characteristics: first, players exhibit severe asymmetry in their knowledge and skill of the game; this is analogous to the general phenomenon that a fixed standard in a society exacerbates inequality across the social statuses over time [43]. Under given rules in online games, similarly, experienced players have an accumulated set of skills and knowledge which they have acquired by exploring a myriad of elements of the game. In contrast, novices lack the essential skills and knowledge required for competition [44–47].

Second, online games often design a sophisticated leveling curve that generally leads to faster and easier leveling-up in the lower ranks (e.g., simple quests or lower requirements for experience points) and slower and more dificult leveling-up in the higher ranks (e.g., demanding quests or bigger requirements for experience points) [44]. These game environments are expected to influence the social motivation of player at each game ranking diferently.

High-ranked players have the resources and knowledge to meet the standards for a higher rank; however, every high-ranked player has a comparable level of resources and knowledge. Moreover, the game environment no longer serves the social mobility in the higher ranks as it did in the lower ranks [44]. As a result, a high-ranked player cannot trump another high-ranked player by merely accumulating more re sources and knowledge, and therefore needs to commit an enormous efort for further raising their game ranking [44,48]. Thus, high-ranked players are expected to have comparatively lower social motivation before the update.

With moderate experience in the game, middle-ranked players have adapted to the established social system. They have gained suficient knowledge and skill which they can leverage during their gaming activities. Also, unlike high-ranked players, middle-ranked players are still on their way towards the higher ranking in a comparatively favorable environment, which provides more opportunities to raise rankings [44,48]. This expectancy for growth with reasonable eforts leads them to get more immersed in the gameplay [49]. Therefore, middle-ranked players are expected to have higher social motivation.

Low-ranked players often feel that there is an entry-barrier when starting a game. As new players, not only do they have to collect items from scratch, but they also lack an understanding of the pre-established game environment [44,50]. Furthermore, low-ranked players rarely have access to the know-how on the eficient ways of exploiting their resources to climb up in the social hierarchy [47]. Though low-ranked players encounter a higher chance of upward social mobility, these players may find the required efort grueling, which might socially demotivate low-ranked players.

To sum up, middle-ranked players are expected to have greater social motivation to play the game than high and low-ranked players before an update. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. Before a game update, middle-ranked players play the game more than high and low-ranked players.

## 2.4. Efect of updates

As noted earlier, an update changes the environment of an online game society. Specifically, an update in an online game brings the following changes: first, players generally face uncertainty regarding the renewed rules of competition and performance assessment. An introduction of a new environment, along with new skills and strategies, redefines the value of the established skills and strategies; this, in turn, signifies that players have to forgo the old and explore the newly introduced changes [51,52]. Hence, players, regardless of their gaming history, find that they have to embrace the uncertainty in adjusting their gaming activities to a new game environment directly following an update [53]. Second, social mobility generally is increased by an update; literature in sociology notes that changes in the rules or stan dards of society generally facilitate social mobility [54,55]. Likewise, online game companies intend for an update to motivate all players by giving them a new chance to further develop their competitiveness in the game [8,53].

The aforementioned societal characteristics are expected to influ ence the social motivation of players at each game ranking in the fol lowing way: high-ranked players are expected to have higher motivation. After an update, there has not yet been a clarification made as to how the players will use the newly introduced gaming content. If higher-ranked players dive into exploring and mastering the new content quicker than others, they will be able to dominate the knowl edge and resources which have become valid in the new society, facilitating an environment where they outperform other high-ranked players [32]. With the substantial resources that they have accumulated, high-ranked players will be willing to embrace the underlying uncertainty and explore the new content [47,56,57]. Accordingly, an update will encourage social motivation among high-ranked players.

Despite the increased social mobility, middle-ranked players are less likely to explore unidentified and adventurous content. In general, middle-status people tend to resist change and defend the status quo [58]. Because middle-status people have followed the guaranteed routes to successfully increase their social status in an established system [59–61], they lack the knowledge or resources to eficiently seek out new ways of raising their status when compared to those of higher status. This phenomenon will also apply to the context of online gaming. Updates that entail additions to existing strategies can be especially unfavorable to middle-ranked players who have built their yet unstable skills and resources. Not only will middle-ranked players require extra efort to get used to the new game environment, but they will also find their endeavors undermined, which will frustrate the players [44]. Thus, analogous to literature in sociology, middle-ranked players will be demotivated by updates.

Contrary to middle-ranked players, low-ranked players have had less commitment to the game, as they only possess items and knowledge with little value. In this regard, the change in the environment through updates may not lessen the value of their endeavors in the existing society. However, the increased social mobility triggered as a result of the update will motivate them to play the game to herald a considerable improvement in their game rankings with the same amount of efort [44].

In summary, updates are expected to increase the social motivation of high and low-ranked players, but not middle-ranked players. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. Game updates make high and low-ranked players play the game more, but not middle-ranked players.

## 3. Empirical analyses

In this section, we describe the research setting, data collection, and variables. Then, we build a model for empirical analyses and discuss the results.

## 3.1. Data collection

To address our research question, we compared individual gameplay on online games before and after their feature updates, which accompanied a rule change or introduction of new game elements (e.g., items, maps).<sup>2</sup> Specifically, we collected the data through the following steps.

First, we collected individual gaming data from an online-game social networking site [62,63], gamerDNA (refer to Fig. 1 for the opening page of the website).<sup>3</sup> The website, launched in 2006, connects its service with gaming platforms such as Xfire, Steam, and Xbox Live and automatically posts individual users' complete gameplay history from these platforms on their personal webpage in real-time. Based on the users' gameplay history, the website recommends new games and connects them with other users who play the same game. Aside from the personal webpage of the users, gamerDNA also hosts webpages for individual games as well. On a game webpage, individual users' play history is aggregated and automatically keeps the record of who played the game as well as the date of their gameplay.

We collected the entire gameplay history of all games available at gamerDNA in October 2013; this data set contains information covering 8,434,667 gaming history of 37,494 players of 173 games in total. Specifically, we focused on online games released between 2011 and 2013 for Xbox360, one of the most popular at-home video game consoles developed by Microsoft.<sup>4</sup> This left us with 488,677 observations of 17,139 players of seven online games with a visible and ordinal game ranking system (e.g., experience points, ranking system), which included Battlefields 3, Bulletstorm, Dead Island, Gotham City Impostors, Halo 4, Mass Efect 3, and Minecraft.

Then, we identified all the updates conducted on the focal games during 2011 and 2013, which totals 18 (i.e., two games with five updates, one game with three updates, one game with two updates, and three games with one update). For all 18 updates, we aimed to compare 30 days before and after the update, therefore, setting 60 days as the focal observation period for each update.<sup>5</sup> However, it was not possible to observe the exact time in the day the update took place in the game; hence, we excluded the observations from the day of the update from our analyses.

Out of the 17,139 players of the focal games, we re-identified players who joined each game before the focal observation period (i.e., 30 days before and after each update) and played the game at least once during the last 30 days before this observation period. We followed an online company's practice that regards a player to churn a game if they had not played it longer than 30 days [64]. As a result, we focused on the gameplay of 4677 players and created 9342 player-game-update combinations. The number of player-game-update combinations was higher than the number of focal players because focal players consisted of 3222 players who played a game only during one updating period, as well as 1455 players who played multiple games or who continued to play the game across many updating periods. For the empirical analysis, we used 560,520 observations (=60 days × 9342 player-game-update combinations). Although we analyzed the observations in the focal observation periods only, control variables on an individual's past gameplay were constructed using the entire play history on each game since its release. The details are discussed in the next section.

## 3.2. Variables

## 3.2.1. Key variables

In our empirical analyses, we analyzed a player's gameplay as a focal variable. We defined $y _ { i j k t }$ as an indicator that took the value 1 if a player i played game j on the day t during the observation period of k-th update, and 0 otherwise. As noted earlier, a player's gameplay was automatically recorded in the system of gamerDNA with the exact date. As we had a complete play history since the launch of each game, there was no left-censoring issue.

We posit that game ranking is a crucial determinant of individual gameplay. Unfortunately, gamerDNA did not provide complete information about each player's experience level. According to the past studies, experience points reflect how much time a player has put into the game and are used to rank up higher or collect advanced and rare items and skills, which triggers social comparison among players in a virtual society [65,66]. Thus, we used the relative position of a player's cumulative gameplay in each game as a proxy for the game ranking. Specifically, we computed percentile rank in the cumulative number of gameplays of player i in the k-th update of game j from its launch till day t-1 among the entire game players on gamerDNA. We defined $r a n k _ { i j k t }$ as one minus the percentile rank. By definition, the variable lies in (0,1), and therefore the smaller the variable, the higher the player's game ranking; for instance, $r a n k _ { i j k t } ~ = ~ 0 . 1$ implies the top 10%, equivalently 90% in percentile rank.

In line with our hypotheses, we expected a game update, the focal event of our interest, to afect individual gameplay contingent upon game ranking. Although our findings in the main analysis were consistent with our theoretical predictions, there remain several potential concerns. ## 4.1. Falsification test: update in a single-player game

A single-player game generally refers to a game where players play alone against opponents controlled by the computer [69]. Since a player of a single-player game does not have other players to compare or compete with, single-player gaming is known to be driven by an individual's motivation to achieve autonomy and competence [30,31] and not by social motivation. Otherwise put, the main results will not be replicated in a single-player game if social motivation is, in fact, the driver of the focal efects that online game updates have. We aimed to validate the proposed mechanism by falsifying the main finding in a single-player game.

## 4.2. Robustness check – a diferent proxy of game ranking

In the main analysis, we assumed that a player's cumulative gameplay in a game represented her relative position in a game society based on existing research on game rewards. To validate our findings, we intended to show that the focal efect was replicated when we used a diferent proxy of game ranking. Explicitly, we defined a player's game ranking using Achievements, a unique reward system to Xbox360 games, which gives players acknowledgments when they perform specific tasks in the game [71]. Achievements can be seen as a form of game ranking in that it enables social comparison between players by displaying a collection of the player's Achievements on their personal Xbox360 page. On gamerDNA, every time a player secures Achievements, it is automatically tracked and recorded in the system with the exact date.

In the analysis, we included a new rank variable and used the same seven online games as used in the main analysis. All seven were Xbox360 games, and therefore, we could get Achievements information for these games. No observed players from the main analysis were lost, which resulted in 560,520 gaming histories of 9342 players during the 30 days before and after an update. We analyzed individual gameplay using all the variables constructed in the main model. Note that the only diference here was that $r a n k _ { i j k t }$ was made of the player's cumulative number of Achievements, not their cumulative gameplay. $r a n k _ { i j k t }$ by Achievements was positively correlated with rank<sub>ijkt</sub> by gaming history with $r = 0 . 5 9 _ { \mathrm { : } }$ , which implies that more experienced players tended to be consistently superior in their achievement collection.

Key results are summarized in the column (4), (5), and (6) in Table 3. Even with a diferent operationalization of game ranking, the association between game ranking and gameplay found in the main analysis remained very robust. Before the update, the coeficient of $r a n k _ { i j k t } ^ { 2 }$ was significantly negative $( - 0 . 1 3 0 , \texttt { p } < \ . 0 5 )$ , and middleranked players (i.e., 42% from the top) tended to play the game the most, so game ranking and utility exhibited an inverse U-shaped relationship. Also, the efect of the update had a U-shaped relationship; middle-ranked players (i.e., 55% from the top) were the least afected by game updates, while $r a n k _ { i j k t } ^ { \mathrm { ~ 2 ~ } }$ was significantly positive (0.251, $\mathrm { ~ \tt ~ p ~ } < \mathrm { ~ \ } . 0 5 )$ . After an update, as a consequence, middle-ranked players (i.e., 47% from the top) tended to play a game the least, and the coeficient of $r a n k _ { i j k t } ^ { 2 }$ was significantly positive (0.121, p < .05). All these findings confirmed the validity of our main findings.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical implications

This research has three key theoretical implications. First, this research sheds light on how individual players respond diferently to game updates in online games. Although updates are integral to online games and involve numerous important decisions, scant studies have examined such concepts. Of the few studies that have examined game updates, none have studied how individual players respond diferently to game updates. This research raises and addresses a fundamental question for literature on online games by investigating individual diferences in responses to game updates and improving the knowledge to make decisions associated with game updates. Building upon this research, future research can further investigate other factors that can drive players' diferent responses to game updates.

Second, we propose a novel theoretical framework to understand complex social dynamics caused by game updates. Although past research has narrowly defined an update as a simple marketing ofer [53] or changes in functionality [8,11], game updates in an online game cause the game environment in a virtual society to change. Accordingly, game updates will afect social motivation to compete with others and to be ahead of others; this research views game updates in a novel perspective and documents interesting findings that update increases the gameplay of high and low-ranked players only. Using the proposed framework, future research can relate game behavior with other social needs or motivation that updates can stimulate, such as the need for uniqueness or need for interaction.

Third, our research contributes to literature by using a novel data set. Extant studies on online game updates have used self-reported surveys for analysis [53,72]. Although suficient information, often not directly observable, can be collected through surveys, respondents often sufer from self-report bias [73,74] so that their responses systematically deviate from their actual behaviors. Several studies have examined one specific game [6,8,75], but the findings may be distinctive and hardly be generalizable. We analyzed multiple game updates across multiple games using the data of individual gameplays that were automatically recorded on a gaming social network website. Our empirical approach will be of interest to researchers who aim to analyze individual gameplays in a more rigorous and generalizable way.

## 5.2. Practical mplications

As noted earlier, game updates involve a variety of decisions, including how to design updated content in a game and how to promote updates to players. Our finding that high and low-ranked players positively respond to game updates pinpoints who should be the target of game updates and gives managerially actionable insights to practitioners.

In designing game updates, practitioners may want to add more content that hooks high and low-ranked players. Although updates mainly aim to add a new map, for instance, practitioners can additionally create a new character that requires sophisticated skills to control or new items or new quests that help novice players level up. New content can motivate high and low-ranked players, thus max imizing the efect of game updates in an online game. Also, it may be worth changing the ranking system so that it appears more polarized through the updates as well (i.e., more players perceive themselves to be higher or lower in rank comparatively). For instance, if players are given symbols of high or low-rankings (e.g., badges or colors), more players are likely to perceive themselves as high or low-rank, therefore becoming more enthusiastic about new game updates.

Our findings also have implications in promoting game updates since customization in promotion and targeting has become increasingly prevalent in online game markets. For instance, notifications about updates can be repeatedly sent out to high and low-ranked players because they tend to be motivated to play the game with up dates. On the other hand, such extra efort may not work when it comes to middle-ranked players, since changes in the game environment may demotivate them. Beyond the frequency of exposures to messages about game updates, practitioners can consider sending out diferent mes sages about game updates across diferent game rankings. For instance, messages emphasizing innovative items and novel systems can be sent to high and low-ranked players, and in contrast, messages that tone down on changes while framing an update as maintenance or continuity can be sent to middle-ranked players.

## 6. Conclusion

This research investigated the impact of a game update on online game gameplay. Under the notion that an online game is a virtual so ciety and that an update is the change of the game environment that characterizes that virtual society, we conjecture that individual players respond to an update diferently depending on their game ranking in the given social hierarchy.

Consistent with our conjecture, we have novel empirical findings. First, when the society has established rules of competition and performance assessment before an update, middle-ranked players tend to play the game more than high and low-ranked players do (i.e., the inverse U-shaped relationship between a player's game ranking and the gameplay before an update). Second, an update increases the gameplay of high and low-ranked players by accompanying a paradigm shift in the game society but not the gameplay of middle-ranked players (i.e., the efect of an update on gameplay varies by game ranking, and it is U shaped). As a consequence, high and low-ranked players end up playing the game more than middle-ranked players after an update (i.e., Ushaped relationship between a player's game ranking and the gameplay after an update).

Despite its significant implications, our research has several limitations. First, we investigated only the immediate impact that game updates have on players' gameplay. It would be an attractive future research venue to examine how individual gameplay changes as new updates get established in the long run and investigate how to optimally schedule game updates over product life cycles. Second, we confined our discussion to game updates in an online game. Our theoretical framework can be applied to other contexts where people have social motivation and where there is a change in the rules of competition and performance assessment. For instance, numerous websites have gamification components in their platforms (e.g., badge systems on boardgamegeeks.com or ranking systems on myanime.net), which can potentially trigger social motivation. Future studies can further address if our findings are replicated in other industries where the updates af fect social motivation by changing pre-established rules. Third, we analyzed individual gameplay, not monetary value. It would be interesting to study how game updates change the profitability of online games, including not only gameplay but also behaviors directly converted to monetary value, such as in-game purchases.
