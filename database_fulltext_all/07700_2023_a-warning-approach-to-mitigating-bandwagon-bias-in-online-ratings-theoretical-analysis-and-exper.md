# A Warning Approach to Mitigating Bandwagon Bias in Online Ratings: Theoretical Analysis and Experimental Investigations

Ding Wu,<sup>1</sup> Xunhua Guo,<sup>2</sup> Yuejun Wang,<sup>3</sup> Guoqing Chen<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of International Business and Economics, China, 03129@uibe.edu.cn <sup>2</sup>Tsinghua University, China, guoxh@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn <sup>3</sup>Tsinghua University, China, wangyj5.17@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn <sup>4</sup>Tsinghua University, China, chengq@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn

## Abstract

Current online review systems widely suffer from rating biases. Biased ratings can lead to violations of customer trust and failures of business intelligence. Hence, both practitioners and researchers have directed massive efforts toward curbing rating biases. In this paper, we investigate bandwagon bias, the rating distortion resulting from individuals posting ratings shifted toward the displayed average rating, and propose a bias warning approach to mitigate this bias. Drawing on the flexible correction model, the theory of valuation in behavioral economics, and previous warning research, we design an effective warning strategy in two steps. First, we start with the risk-alert warning strategy, which prior research has widely employed, and rationalize its deficiencies by synthesizing theoretical analysis and extant empirical evidence. Second, considering the deficiencies, we identify a supplementary content design factor—the ranking task—and construct a risk-alert-with-ranking-task warning strategy. We then empirically test the effects of the two warning strategies on individual ratings in cases in which bandwagon bias either occurs or does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments. The results of four controlled experiments indicate that (1) the risk-alert strategy can reduce bandwagon bias in individua ratings but will elicit unwanted rating distortions when bandwagon bias does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments, and (2) the risk-alert-with-ranking-task strategy can mitigate bandwagon bias while avoiding the unwanted rating distortions above and can thus function as an effective warning strategy. Our research contributes to the literature by proposing an effective debiasing solution for bandwagon bias and a bias warning approach for online rating debiasing, which can help increase rating informativeness on online platforms.

Keywords: Bandwagon Bias, Warning Message, Flexible Correction Model, Theory of Valuation, Online Rating Debiasing

## 1 Introduction

In the current e-commerce ecosystem, online review systems serve as a crucial channel for customers to communicate product experience and provide important data inputs for business intelligence systems (Arial, 2014; Adomavicius et al., 2019). However, online review systems suffer from serious rating biases (Hu et al., 2017; Karaman, 2021). For example, using data collected from an online retail store, Moe and Schweidel (2012) provide strong empirical evidence that online ratings are systematically biased. Biased online ratings can mislead product decisions and weaken consumer trust. Hence, platforms such as Amazon and Yelp continue to invest heavily in reducing online rating noises (Kugler, 2014). For example, Amazon introduced a proprietary machine-learning model for filtering and weighting product reviews in 2015 and set up the Vine program to govern incentivized reviews in 2016 (Matsakis, 2019). Accordingly, online rating debiasing has also attracted massive research interests in recent years (e.g., Chen et al., 2016; Burtch et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018; Adomavicius et al., 2019; Karaman, 2021; Garg & Johari, 2021).

Bandwagon bias is a well-known online rating bias, which refers to cases in which individuals post ratings shifted toward the displayed average rating rather than being purely based on their perceived product quality (e.g., Moe & Schweidel, 2012; Ma et al., 2013; Muchnik et al., 2013). For example, Ma et al. (2013) estimate that increasing the average rating of a business on Yelp from 4 stars to 5 stars would lead to 30.5% more reviewers subsequently posting 5-star ratings. In addition, a largescale longitudinal field experiment showed that by artificially giving one upvote to newly created news comments, the final mean ratings (i.e., the number of upvotes minus the number of downvotes) of these comments increased an average of 25% after five months, compared to a control group (Muchnik et al., 2013). Hence, exploring effective debiasing approaches for bandwagon bias is of high practical and theoretical significance (Muchnik et al., 2013; Aral, 2014; Lee et al., 2015). However, bandwagon bias is hard to detect and has complex biasing mechanisms (Muchnik et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015), which restricts the applicability of existing rating debiasing approaches. Accordingly, the mitigation of bandwagon bias is still underexplored, and an efficient debiasing solution is thus lacking.

In this paper, we propose a bias warning approach for mitigating bandwagon bias. Online platforms can use a bias warning approach to deliver warnings to help reviewers infer the presence, direction, and magnitude of the target bias and self-correct initial assessments. This rating debiasing approach is promising for three main reasons. First, it does not differentiate between different biasing effects and is thus particularly suitable for mitigating rating biases (e.g., bandwagon bias) that have complex or unclear biasing mechanisms. Second, warnings are fundamental to human-computer interaction due to low operational costs and high technical feasibility. Online platforms have employed similar warning practices. For example, Yelp provided “consumer alerts” in 2012 to combat review manipulations. Third, broadly speaking, the bias warning approach provides us with an ex ante “modifythe-decision-maker” method for curbing online rating biases, thus complementing the existing rating debiasing approaches (Fischhoff, 1982; Soll et al., 2015).

Despite its debiasing potential, the bias warning approach also faces important challenges and thus requires additional research efforts. For one thing, the effectiveness of warnings has received mixed support in previous broad investigations in other contexts (Cheng & Wu, 2010; Schall et al., 2017). Some studies have found support for the effectiveness of warnings, whereas others found little or no debiasing effect (e.g., Compen et al., 2022; Verwijmeren et al., 2013). Hence, it is still uncertain whether warnings are capable of mitigating bandwagon bias in the online rating context. Further, warnings may cause negative interference in individual decisions in unbiased cases (e.g., Xiao & Benbasat, 2015; Jenkins et al., 2016). For example, Xiao and Benbasat (2015) found that warnings may cause users to be more likely to misjudge unbiased product recommendations (compared to biased ones). Hence, it remains unclear whether such warnings would cause similar unintended rating distortions and, if so, how the warning design could be improved.

Accordingly, in this paper, we employ warnings as the debiasing artifact and explore the content-design strategy of warnings (i.e., warning strategy, characterized by content-design factors) for curbing bandwagon bias. Specifically, we design an effective warning strategy in two steps. First, we start our exploration with the warning strategy commonly employed by prior research and theoretically analyze its deficiencies in mitigating bandwagon bias. The extant literature has widely used a “risk-alert” warning strategy, which alerts people to be cautious about potential risks, as a potential means to mitigate judgmental biases (e.g., Cheng & Wu, 2010; Schall et al., 2017). However, as the flexible correction model (FCM) predicts (Petty & Wegener, 1993; Wegener & Petty, 1995), the risk-alert warning strategy is subject to the critical risk of eliciting additional rating distortions in cases where bandwagon bias does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments. Second, drawing on FCM and the theory of valuations in behavioral economics, we develop a content-design factor of warnings, called a ranking task, which should make up for the deficiencies of risk alerts. Thus, we construct a “risk-alert-with-ranking-task” warning strategy, comprising a risk alert and a ranking task. FCM theoretically predicts that this warning strategy will lead to enriched bias cognitions of reviewers and thus the desired bias correction process.

Hence, our empirical work focuses on examining the debiasing effects of the above two warning strategies on individual ratings. Specifically, this work aims to answer the following key research questions: What are the effects of the risk-alert warning strategy (vs. no warning) and the risk-alert-with-ranking-task warning strategy (vs. no warning or the risk-alert warning strategy) on individual ratings in cases where bandwagon bias either occurs or does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments?

We conducted four controlled experiments to test our research hypotheses. The results verified the insufficiency of the provision of a risk alert in mitigating bandwagon bias and demonstrated the critical role of the inclusion of a ranking task in warnings. Overall, this work takes a first step toward the ex ante mitigation of bandwagon bias in online ratings. Additionally, this work also introduces a new bias warning approach for rectifying online rating biases. Our research findings can help enhance the informativeness of online ratings and construct a better e-commerce ecology.

## 2 Related Literature Review

This section reviews the literature on bandwagon bias, online rating debiasing, and warnings as a debiasing artifact.

## 2.1 Bandwagon Bias in Online Ratings

Prior literature has revealed two general types of online rating biases—reporting and evaluation biases. They refer to the rating distortions in customers’ reporting decisions (i.e., whether to post a rating) and evaluation decisions (i.e., what rating to post), respectively (Moe & Schweidel, 2012; Lee et al., 2015). The former involves unreported individual ratings and the latter refers to distorted individual ratings, both of which can result in the biased distribution of online ratings. We summarize the common online rating biases in Appendix A. Bandwagon bias is a well-known type of evaluation bias in which reviewers post ratings that are shifted toward the displayed average rating rather than being purely based on their perceived product quality (Moe et al., 2011; Ma et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015; Karaman, 2021). Numerous empirical studies have revealed this type of rating distortion (e.g., Schlosser, 2005; Moe et al., 2011; Sridhar & Srinivasan, 2012; Moe & Schweidel, 2012; Ma et al., 2013; Muchnik et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015; Adomavicius et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017; Karaman, 2021).<sup>1</sup> For example, based on examining hotel ratings on an independent travel website, Sridhar and Srinivasan (2012) found that the average rating exerts positive impacts on subsequent ratings both directly through the main effect and indirectly by moderating the effects of product-experience features and product failures on individual ratings. Moreover, based on an examination of reviews on Yelp, Ma et al. (2013) demonstrated that the average rating has an overall positive impact on individual ratings, moderated by reviewer characteristics and review features.

Bandwagon bias has complex biasing mechanisms (Muchnik et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015). A major source of bandwagon bias is cognitive biases such as anchoring effects (Adomavicius et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). However, even reviewers who form correct cognitive representations of the product quality may still post ratings with bandwagon bias due to other biasing mechanisms such as peer pressure and postpurchase information updating based on previous reviews (Schlosser, 2005; Lee et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2017). We introduce the relevant cognitive effects and summarize their relationships to and differences from bandwagon bias in Appendix A. As shown, the biasing mechanisms of bandwagon bias include both nonconscious and conscious information processing, indicating that it can occur in multiple subliminal and conscious ways.<sup>2</sup> In addition, bandwagon bias is also hard to detect because reviewers’ baseline evaluations are implicit, resulting in objective features of biased ratings that are not always apparent. Hence, mitigating bandwagon bias in online ratings is of high practical and theoretical importance.

We categorize these efforts into three general rating debiasing approaches: design altering, bias modeling, and rating filtering. As indicated in Table 1, these rating debiasing approaches possess their own debiasing foci, advantages, and limitations, thus complementing each other in curbing online rating biases.

The debiasing of bandwagon bias: Regarding bandwagon bias, the existing rating debiasing approaches are largely inapplicable. First, it is hard to find objective features that can specifically detect bandwagon bias, and it is unlikely that all biased ratings can be filtered because of their prevalence. Second, it is also difficult to establish a statistical model that fully captures the complex biasing mechanisms of bandwagon bias, and such statistical corrections can provide decision support for practitioners but cannot actually alter the biased ratings online. Third, the mechanism complexity makes it hard to modify system design or policies beforehand to efficiently curb potential biasing effects. These reasons partially explain why there are few debiasing explorations of bandwagon bias. As an exception, Zhang et al. (2017) attempted to mitigate bandwagon bias using the bias modeling approach, but their model only captures the rating bias resulting from assimilation effects when customers possess minor expectation-quality discrepancies and cannot objectively change biased ratings online. Hence, the mitigation of bandwagon bias remains underexplored.

Accordingly, we propose a bias warning approach in this paper (see Table 1). Taking this approach, online platforms would leverage warnings to help reviewers infer the target rating bias (i.e., bias presence, direction, and magnitude) and self-correct initial assessments. The bias warning approach possesses many research values. First, the bias warning approach is particularly suitable for mitigating rating biases (e.g., bandwagon bias) that have complex or unclear biasing mechanisms. Specifically, no matter how a rating bias occurred, by improving individuals’ bias perceptions (e.g., through risk alerts and comparative evaluations), they should self-correct the bias to some extent. Second, current human-computer interactions often use warnings because of low operational costs and high technical feasibility (Anderson et al., 2016b). Third, in a broad sense, the bias warning approach provides an ex ante “modify-the-decisionmaker” debiasing approach of online ratings and thus complements existing general rating debiasing approaches (Soll et al., 2015; Adomavicius et al., 2019). However, despite the debiasing potential of this approach, we still lack guidance on designing warnings to facilitate the desirable bias correction. Therefore, we turn to the warning research for further design implications.

## 2.3 Warnings as Debiasing Artifacts

Warnings have long served as fundamental artifacts (Fischhoff, 1982) in curbing various judgmental biases, such as those induced by biased product recommendations (Xiao & Benbasat, 2015), phishing messages (Jaeger, & Eckhardt, 2021), subliminal advertisements (Verwijmeren et al., 2013), and people’s inherent overconfident cognitions (Schall et al., 2017). Currently, online businesses are widely using warnings in practice (Anderson et al., 2016a; 2016b). For example, Yelp provides warnings on the review section of business pages to alert consumers of possible review frauds (i.e., the “consumer alerts program”).

Nonetheless, the debiasing effects of warnings have led to mixed findings (Cheng & Wu, 2010; Schall et al., 2017). Some studies have found evidence for the effectiveness of warnings (e.g., Cheng & Wu, 2010; Verwijmeren et al., 2013), whereas others have found little or no debiasing effect (e.g., Bird & Harris, 2018; Compen et al., 2022). Moreover, some prior studies have even reported negative effects, whereby warnings cause unintended biases in otherwise unbiased conditions (e.g., Xiao & Benbasat, 2015; Pennycook et al., 2020). A possible reason for these mixed findings is that recipients ignore warnings in some cases (Schall et al., 2017). Hence, prior research has directed massive effort toward exploring why people disregard warning messages (e.g., resistance, habituation) and optimizing the appearance design of warnings to reduce such potential for disregard (Anderson et al., 2016a; Anderson et al., 2016b; Kwan et al., 2017; Vance et al., 2018).

Notably, even if recipients notice a warning, the debiasing effect is still dependent on whether the message content affects recipients as expected. Hence, the predictable effects of warnings are likely contingent on the content design. For example, Nguyen et al. (2021) found that users’ acceptance of and anxiety about warnings from a crowdsourced phishing warning system are contingent on warning content, which further influences users’ phishing detection performance. The extant literature has widely relied on the provision of risk alerts for curbing judgmental biases (e.g., Cheng & Wu, 2010; Verwijmeren et al., 2013; Schall et al., 2017). This intuitive warning strategy (i.e., the risk-alert strategy), however, has led to the mixed effects mentioned above.

In sum, when designing warnings to mitigate bandwagon bias, we face two critical challenges. First, warnings should be capable of reducing rating distortions. Second, warnings should be able to avoid unintended rating distortions when bandwagon bias does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments. Prior warning research has mainly focused on optimizing the appearance design of warnings, leaving the design of content rarely explored. The prevalent risk-alert warning strategy is of value but is insufficient for bias mitigation in many instances. Accordingly, we focus on the content design of warnings in this paper. We begin by exploring the risk-alert warning strategy and then focus on other content-design factors that could tackle its deficiencies.

## 3 Theoretical Foundations and Hypothesis Development

In this paper, we concentrate on the bias correction stage activated by warnings rather than on the precedent bias incurrence stage. Accordingly, we first introduce FCM as an overarching theory to understand when and how individuals engage in bias correction; we then use this to further discuss the effects of warning strategies and elaborate related research hypotheses.

## 3.1 The Flexible Correction Model

FCM originates from contextual priming studies (Petty & Wegener, 1993). Petty and Wegener (1993) introduced FCM as an overarching theory to understand when and how people engage in judgmental corrections. Subsequently, research has broadly used FCM to examine bias corrections in stereotyping (Wegener et al., 2006), persuasion (Petersen & Hamilton, 2014), embodied cognition (Zestcott et al., 2017), and others.

As one of its core tenets, FCM’s “theory-based correction” argues that people conduct their bias corrections based on their theories of bias (Chien et al., 2014). Specifically, FCM predicts that people will access or generate naive “theories of bias,” which refer to their beliefs about the target bias in their initial judgments, including the presence, direction, and magnitude of the perceived bias (Wegener, 1994; Wegener et al., 2013). According to FCM, when individuals perceive that a bias arises (no matter whether the bias actually arises), they will engage in correction processes by adjusting their assessments of targets in a direction opposite to the perceived bias and in a magnitude corresponding to the magnitude of the perceived bias. If individuals perceive that no bias has arisen, they will maintain their initial assessments. The model also posits that individuals conduct bias corrections only when they have the motivation (e.g., from internal or external stimuli) and capacity to identify and correct the bias; otherwise, they retain their unadjusted assessments. We adapted a schematic representation of FCM from Wegener (1994), depicted in Figure 1.

FCM offers two debiasing inspirations. First, it is important to consider cases in which bandwagon bias does and does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments because the unnecessary adjustment of assessments may lead to the common side effect of additional rating distortions. Second, if individuals can develop a more accurate theory of bias (i.e., more accurate perceptions of the bias presence, direction, or magnitude), they will be able to make better assessments, which would offer theoretical support for the bias warning approach. We expect that individuals, if forewarned, generally have the motivation and capacity to examine and adjust their potentially biased assessments to some extent (Pennycook et al., 2020; Jaeger & Eckhardt, 2021). Thus, the debiasing effect of warnings is largely contingent on an individual’s theory of bias, which warning content can further shape. In other words, warnings can be used to deliver information that helps improve individuals’ perceptions of the presence, direction, or magnitude of a bias in order to facilitate bias corrections. Subsequently, we draw on FCM to understand the effects of different warning strategies on individual ratings (i.e., final assessments) and guide design extensions.

## 3.2 Effects of the Risk-Alert Warning Strategy

We start our exploration with the basic risk-alert warning strategy. We term the instantiation of the riskalert warning strategy a direct warning. Direct warnings alert individuals to be cautious regarding the possibility that their product ratings may be influenced by the displayed average rating (i.e., bandwagon bias). Direct warnings can shape an individual’s theory of bias presence and direction. Direct warnings can serve as signals that sensitize people to abnormal events (Xiao & Benbasat, 2015; Twitchell & Fuller, 2019). Consistent with the signal detection research (Scott, 2006; Lynn & Barrett, 2014), regardless of whether bandwagon bias occurs in the initial assessment, direct warnings make individuals more likely to perceive that a bias is occurring, compared with those receiving no warning. Direct warnings can also inform individuals on the direction of bandwagon bias (i.e., biases toward the displayed average rating). However, direct warnings do not provide additional information on the magnitude of bandwagon bias. As predicted by FCM, individuals are inclined to adjust their ratings away from the displayed average rating in a heuristic magnitude.

Note that FCM predicts the same correction process for cases in which bandwagon bias does and does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments because it is individuals’ perceived presence of the bias, rather than the actual presence of the bias, that guides their theorybased corrections (McCaslin et al., 2010; Chien et al., 2014). When bandwagon bias occurs in the initial assessment, such corrections will lead to bias mitigation. However, in cases where bandwagon bias does not occur, this unnecessary correction will elicit undesirable distortions in individual ratings. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

H1: In cases where bandwagon bias occurs in individuals’ initial assessments, individuals who receive a direct warning will post ratings with reduced bandwagon bias, compared to those receiving no warning.

H2: In cases where bandwagon bias does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments, individuals who receive a direct warning will post distorted ratings that are shifted away from the displayed average rating, compared to those receiving no warning.

## 3.3 Construction of the Risk-Alert-with-Ranking-Task Warning Strategy

The insufficiency of the risk-alert warning strategy in mitigating bandwagon bias compels us to explore other supplementary content design factors of warnings. As suggested by FCM, the supplementary factor should provide information gains for an individual’s theory of bias. To improve an individual’s perceived bias, the individual should examine their undisturbed baseline evaluation system. We draw on the theory of valuations in behavioral economics to obtain a theory-based means to show how this can be done.

The theory of valuations in behavioral economics discusses how consumers make product valuations in the marketplace. A traditional view of microeconomic theories assumes that consumers can accurately judge the fundamental value of products (Ariely, 2009). However, psychological research indicates that individuals’ preferences are so malleable that their product valuations are somewhat arbitrary (e.g., Russo, 2010; Schmidt & Bijmolt, 2020). Accordingly, Ariely et al. (2003) propose a contemporary view that individuals’ product valuations possess “coherent arbitrariness.” Specifically, individuals’ absolute valuations of products are so arbitrary that they are susceptible to irrelevant stimulus, whereas individuals’ relative valuations of different products appear to be relatively coherent (Ariely & Simonson, 2003; Simonson & Drolet, 2004; Hanley et al., 2009) such that stable demand curves still can be widely observed in the marketplace.

One can infer that when individuals post ratings online, the principle of “coherent arbitrariness” also works because product ratings essentially reflect individuals’ valuations of utility derived from the product qualities (Lee et al., 2015). Thus, although individuals’ absolute product assessments might be biased due to external or internal stimuli, their relative product assessments remain largely stable (Hanley et al., 2009). Based on this theoretical inference, we propose another contentdesign factor of warnings, the ranking task. The ranking task instructs individuals to rank the target product and a few comparable products (along with product ratings) as accurately as possible in the order of their overall preferences (i.e., comparative evaluation). We deduce that such a ranking task can deliver extra information about an individual’s undisturbed baseline evaluation system (Chang et al., 2009; Melrose et al., 2013), thus contributing to an improved theory of target bias and better bias mitigation. In Table 2, we describe an ideal case for illustration purposes. Based on the listed ranking task, an individual can infer that their reliable personalized rating of Target Product B is about 7 (out of 10). If the individual holds an initial rating saliently deviated from 7, they can infer the presence and magnitude of the bias from the ranking task. In contrast, if the individual’s initial assessment of Product B is around 7, then they will be confident in the accuracy of the initial rating and avoid unnecessary adjustments after completing the ranking task.

Notably, it is not easy to find comparable products along with accurate ratings that can fully reflect an individual’s baseline evaluation system. Nevertheless, even if only an imperfect ranking task is available, individuals can still obtain some information about their baseline assessments of the target product (Chang et al., 2009). Hence, we construct a new warning strategy, the risk-alert-with-ranking-task strategy, which consists of not only a risk alert but also a ranking task. Next, we discuss its debiasing effects on individual ratings.

## 3.4 Effects of the Risk-Alert-with-Ranking-Task Warning Strategy

We term the instantiation of this warning strategy a warning with a ranking task. Warnings with a ranking task not only alert individuals to the possible assimilation of their product ratings to the displayed average rating but also instruct them to perform a ranking task to obtain debiasing assistance. Warnings with a ranking task can comprehensively shape individuals’ theory of bias, including the presence, direction, and magnitude of the perceived bias. As discussed above, whether a bandwagon bias occurs or not in initial assessments, the risk alert makes individuals more likely to perceive the bias (Scott, 2006; Xiao & Benbasat, 2015) and informs individuals about the direction of the bias. However, the supplementary ranking task exerts different impacts in these two cases: (1) When bandwagon bias occurs in initial assessments, the ranking task helps individuals narrow the range of plausible values for their baseline evaluations (i.e., improved perception of the bias magnitude). As predicted by FCM, individuals tend to adjust their ratings away from the displayed average rating in the magnitude inferred from the ranking task, resulting in reduced bandwagon bias. This should thus lead to better bias mitigation compared with individuals lacking magnitude inferences and making heuristic adjustments (Simonson & Drolet, 2004; Epley & Gilovich, 2006). (2) When bandwagon bias does not occur in initial assessments, although the risk alert sensitizes individuals, the ranking task enhances individuals confidence in the accuracy of their initial assessments (i.e., improved perception of the bias presence). As predicted by FCM, individuals will avoid unnecessary adjustments and maintain their initial assessments. Hence, we offer the following hypotheses:

H3: In cases where bandwagon bias occurs in individuals’ initial assessments, individuals who receive a warning with a ranking task will post ratings with reduced bandwagon bias compared to those receiving no warning (H3a) and those receiving a direct warning (H3b).

H4: In cases where bandwagon bias does not occur in individuals’ initial assessments, individuals who receive a warning with a ranking task will post undistorted ratings similar to those receiving no warning.

Next, we present several controlled online and laboratory experiments that were used to test the above research hypotheses.

## 4 Method

We first introduce a main experiment that tested all the research hypotheses. We adopted the controlledexperiment method for three reasons. First, given the various online rating biases (e.g., incentive bias, selection bias, and dimensional bias) (Muchnik et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2018; Lin et al., 2019; Tunc et al., 2019; Schneider et al., 2021), it is difficult if not impossible to completely isolate bandwagon bias from other rating biases in a field setting. Second, manipulating the occurrence of bandwagon bias or the provision of warnings in a realistic situation may result in ethical and economic concerns for business owners (Pittenger, 2003). Third, in this paper, we focus on examining the debiasing potential of the warning strategies (i.e., content design); future research can further explore how to apply our findings in practical settings. Additionally, previous debiasing research has also widely employed the controlled-experiment approach (e.g., Cheng & Wu, 2010; Verwijmeren et al., 2013; Xiao & Benbasat, 2015; Schall et al., 2017; Adomavicius et al., 2019).

Pilot experiment: Prior to conducting the main experiment, we conducted a pilot laboratory experiment (N = 71) to establish a feasible manipulation method for the occurrence and nonoccurrence of bandwagon bias in individuals’ initial assessments. The results validated that bandwagon bias occurred for most participants when the displayed average rating largely deviated from their overall opinion valence, whereas bandwagon bias did not occur for most participants when the displayed average rating approximated their actual overall opinion valence—likely because the underlying biasing mechanisms were not sufficiently strong to interfere with most individual ratings. Thus, we employed this manipulation method in the subsequent formal experiments.

Experimental design: We employed a betweensubject factorial design and conducted an online experiment. There were seven groups in total. We summarize the descriptions of the experimental groups in Table 3. For the control group, no average rating and no warning were displayed; thus, we were able to regard these ratings as undisturbed evaluations. By comparing the control group, the BNW (bias-and-nowarning) group, and the NBNW (no-bias-and-nowarning) group, we were able to check whether we had successfully manipulated the occurrence and nonoccurrence of bandwagon bias in subjects’ initial assessments. By comparing the BNW group, the BDW (bias-and-direct-warning) group, and the BRW (biasand-ranking-warning) group, we were able to examine the bias mitigation power of the two warning strategies. By comparing the NBNW group, the NBDW (no-biasand-direct-warning) group, and the NBRW (no-biasand-ranking-warning) group, we were able to evaluate whether the warning strategies elicited undesirable distortions in individual ratings.

Materials: To feasibly imitate the consumption and evaluation processes in the lab, we focused on micromovies. Research based on observations from Amazon suggests that movies appear to suffer the most from social influence (Zhang et al., 2017). In this paper, we selected experimental micromovies based on four criteria. First, we selected micromovies with average online ratings ranging from 5 to 8 (out of 10) for manipulation feasibility (e.g., upward or downward adjustments). Second, we selected micromovies that were less well-known to elicit more reliable responses. Third, we selected micromovies that were no longer than 10 minutes to control for the experimental duration. Fourth, to enhance the generalizability of research findings, we selected micromovies of various types. Accordingly, in the main experiment, we chose four micromovies as the experimental materials (see Table 4). We set the three micromovies, Alike, Glue, and Ike Interviews God, as comparison items that the following ranking task could use if required. We used the remaining micromovie, Singularity, for the main task. We collected the descriptive information from pretests and movie.douban.com, one of the largest online movie review websites in China, on March 28, 2018.

Participants: We recruited 267 college students from two universities in northern China to participate in the online experiment. The recruiting included two rounds and each round took one week. We summarize the detailed demographic information of the participants in Table 5. We removed invalid responses from participants who had previously seen the target micromovie or reported an online experimental abnormality (e.g., abnormal display due to a network anomaly). In the end, we obtained 240 acceptable responses and used them in our final analyses. There were 36, 32, 34, 36, 37, 29, and 36 acceptable responses for the control group, the BNW group, the NBNW group, the BDW group, the NBDW group, the BRW group, and the NBRW group, respectively.

Manipulations: Based on the pilot experiment, to manipulate the occurrence of bandwagon bias, we adjusted the pretested average rating (6.9 out of 10) of the target micromovie (i.e., Singularity) downward by three stars to 3.9; to manipulate the nonoccurrence of bandwagon bias, we displayed the unadjusted pretested average rating for this micromovie. For the provision of warnings, participants in the BDW and NBDW groups received a direct warning, whereas participants in the BRW and NBRW groups received a warning with a ranking task; the other groups received no warning. We provide the content design of the two warnings in Table 6. We also provide illustrative examples of the two warnings in Appendix B.

Procedure: The online experiments occurred on December 17-18, 2021, and January 12-13, 2022. The duration of each experiment was about 30 minutes. We organized several online meetings using Tencent Conference software and conducted the online experiment using the Qualtrics platform. Participants received the experiment invitation message in advance and entered the online meeting rooms at the appointed times. Next, an experimental assistant provided brief instructions and the experiment link. Participants randomly joined one of the seven groups using the platform’s even randomizer function. After beginning the online experiment (using their personal computers), participants were told that the research center was developing a novel recommender system for micromovies and that this investigation was being conducted to collect users’ preferences in order to train this system. Participants were told that they would be paid approximately US\$7.9 for participating.

All participants initially viewed and evaluated the same three micromovies that served as comparison items in the subsequent ranking task if needed. For each micromovie, participants first viewed an introduction page, where they were informed that they would be watching a micromovie. To avoid missing critical plot turns, they could not view the next page until they had watched the micromovie for a predefined duration; fast-forwarding of the video was not encouraged. Next, participants viewed the description page, which included the poster, name, vendor, actor, type, country, language, release time, duration, synopsis, and several images from the micromovie. To rule out confounding factors, historical reviews were not displayed. Then, participants viewed the viewing page where the entire consumption experience was simulated. After that, participants viewed the rating page, which featured the micromovie poster. All participants rated the micromovies based on the 10-star rating scale widely employed by current online movie review websites (e.g., imdb.com).

After this, participants began the main experimental task, where they viewed and evaluated the targeted micromovie (i.e., Singularity). Participants successively viewed the introduction page, description page, viewing page, warning page (for the BDW, NBDW, BRW, and NBRW groups only), and rating page. These pages were similar to those described above. However, for the groups with treatments on average ratings (i.e., all groups except the control group), we also displayed the manipulated average ratings on the description and rating pages.<sup>3</sup> In addition, to increase the success rate of the warning manipulations, we delivered the warning using a pop-up box with a red background and white font. Participants who received the direct warning could not close it for 10 seconds, with a 10-second timer shown on the close button; participants who received the warning with a ranking task could not close it until they had completed the embedded ranking task. Screenshots of these pages are available in Appendix B.

Subsequently, participants completed a short questionnaire that included a manipulation-check question, demographic information, experimental anomalies, and questions about their perceived processing complexity of the warning and any aversion they encountered toward the warning. Finally, participants were debriefed. All the experimental interfaces were in Chinese.

## 5 Results

Dependent variable: To examine bandwagon bias in individual ratings, we employed distance to the displayed average rating (DDAR) as the dependent variable. DDAR refers to the absolute value of the difference between an individual rating and the displayed average rating (i.e., DDAR = individual rating minus displayed average rating). When bandwagon bias occurs in individuals’ initial assessments, they post ratings with a smaller DDAR, compared to cases where bandwagon bias does not occur. We used this insight for manipulation checks of the occurrence and nonoccurrence of bandwagon bias. Additionally, when bandwagon bias occurs in initial assessments, a greater DDAR of individual ratings reflects greater debiasing power of the warnings. In contrast, when bandwagon bias does not occur in initial assessments, a DDAR of individual ratings that is higher than normal reflects the unwanted rating distortions caused by the warnings. We used these insights to test our research hypotheses.

Manipulation checks: We listed the manipulationcheck methods in Table 7. We conducted both nonparametric tests and t-tests using SPSS version 24.0 to detect between-group differences in DDAR, and they produced identical results. We report the results of the nonparametric tests as a representative because they are more suitable when the normality assumption is not strictly satisfied (which is the case for DDAR). Participants in the BNW group posted ratings with a significantly smaller DDAR compared with those in the control group (Mann-Whitney U test: mean rank = 24.02 vs. 43.82, U = 240.50, p < 0.001). In addition, participants in the NBNW group posted ratings with a similar DDAR, compared to those in the control group (Mann-Whitney U test: mean rank = 34.31 vs. 36.63, U = 571.50, p = 0.628). Thus, we can infer that the manipulations of the occurrence and nonoccurrence of bandwagon bias were successful.

As indicated by Model 2 in Table 8, the estimated coefficients of both DW (β = 0.913, W (97) = 11.305, p < 0.001) and RW (β = 0.602, W (97) = 4.322, p = 0.038) were significantly positive. Inconsistent with our prediction, the coefficient of RW was slightly smaller than that of DW. A further nonparametric test detected no significant difference in the distributions of the DDAR between the two groups (Mann-Whitney U test: mean rank = 34.54 versus 31.091, U = 466.500, p = 0.454). Therefore, the warning with a ranking task did not result in greater bias mitigation compared with the direct warning; thus, H3b was not supported.

As indicated by Model 4 in Table 8, the estimated coefficient of DW (β = 0.815, W (107) = 7.661, p = 0.006) was significantly positive, indicating unwanted rating distortions due to subjects’ oversensitivity. In addition, the estimated coefficient of RW (β = 0.339, W (107) = 1.171, p = 0.279) was insignificant, indicating subjects’ avoidance of unnecessary adjustments. Hence, H2 was supported and H4 was not rejected.

Supplementary analyses: When adjusting subjective judgments to a plausible value, people generally exhibit insufficient adjustment if they are not completely aware of the exact value (Epley & Gilovich, 2006). Hence, we did not anticipate the occurrence of an overcorrection. Consistently, the mean ranks of the DDAR of the BDW group (69.51) and the BRW group (62.88) were between those of the control group (78.62) and the BNW group (45.83), thus mitigating the overcorrection concern. We also observed whether there were significant differences in participants’ message processing costs using scales adapted from previous studies (Speier & Venkatesh, 2002; Polman & Kim, 2013) (see Appendix D). Participants who received a direct warning reported slightly higher perceived processing complexity (Cronbach’s α = 0.907) compared with those who received a warning with a ranking task (t-test: t = 2.359, p = 0.020; mean = 5.699 vs. 5.236). However, we detected no significant difference in aversion (Cronbach’s α = 0.979) between the two warnings (ttest: t = 0.254, p = 0.800; mean = 3.897 vs. 3.839). The results suggest that participants regarded the direct warning as slightly more costly in terms of effort than the warning with a ranking task.

## 6 Robustness Checks

Prior to conducting the main experiment, we also conducted several laboratory experiments, which serve as robustness checks for the major findings. We summarize the descriptions of these experiments in Table 9.

We also employed ordinal regression models for data analyses using SPSS version 24.0. Similarly, DDAR was the dependent variable, and SW and RW were the focused independent variables. We present the distributional plots of DDAR for each experiment in Appendix C. Although we used different materials, examined cases in which we up-adjusted (rather than down-adjusted) the displayed average rating, instantiated the warning strategies in different appearances (e.g., no warning icon), and constructed the ranking task using less than three micromovies or using movies recalled from participants’ long-term memory (rather than those newly encountered in prior tasks), the empirical evidence for the biasmitigation performance of the two warnings remains consistent. As indicated by S3 in Table 10, the results again support H2 and H4. When we used different materials and constructed the ranking task using only two micromovies as comparison items, the results also provide consistent evidence. In addition, a minor difference in our findings was that participants in the lab reported different perceptions of message processing costs compared to those online. Specifically, we detected no significant difference in participants’ perceived processing complexity of the warnings (t-test: t = -0.675, p = 0.502; mean = 4.87 versus 5.08) in S1. We attribute the minor difference to participants’ behavioral differences between the online and the laboratory experimental environments.

## 7 Discussion

This section discusses the above-mentioned research findings, presents the contributions of this work to research and practice, summarizes its limitations, and offers suggestions for future research.

## 7.1 Discussion of Research Findings

The main experiment provided empirical support for all hypotheses except H3b. The results demonstrate that the risk-alert strategy can reduce bandwagon bias in individual ratings. More importantly, the results indicate that the risk-alert strategy can also produce unwanted distortions in individual ratings when bandwagon bias does not occur in initial assessments. However, our results show that the risk-alert-with-ranking-task strategy can mitigate bandwagon bias, without producing such rating distortions when bandwagon bias does not occur in initial assessments. We obtained consistent empirical evidence in the three supplementary experiments (i.e., S1, S2, and S3), indicating the robustness of the above findings. These findings emphasize that providing a risk alert alone is insufficient and highlight the importance of including a ranking task for mitigating bandwagon bias using the bias warning approach.

Contrary to our predictions, in the main experiment, the warning with a ranking task did not reduce bandwagon bias to a greater extent than the direct warning (i.e., not supporting H3b). These two warnings showed insignificant differences in the power to mitigate bias. S1 and S2 also replicated this finding. One explanation is that although the ranking task may deliver extra information about people’s baseline evaluations, it could also increase cognitive processing costs, thus resulting in no improvement. However, our supplementary analyses on the cognitive processing costs countered this explanation. In S1, these two warnings were insignificantly different in terms of subjects’ perceived processing complexity (including effort) and their aversion to warnings. In the main experiment, participants even reported lower perceived processing complexity for the warning with a ranking task compared to the direct warning. Thus, a more convincing explanation is that the selection of comparison items did not deliver enough information about individuals’ baseline evaluations. For example, if a subject selected three movies with ratings of 10, 9, and 9, respectively, for comparison in the main experiment, it can be inferred that by ranking this set of items and the target micromovie (with a pretested average rating of 6.9), participants would have been able to obtain little information about their baseline evaluations. Without obtaining enough information to narrow the range of plausible values, participants would have made adjustments similar to those who only received a direct warning. Such an explanation suggests that optimizing the selection of comparison items in the ranking task could result in better bias mitigation. However, we leave further examination of this issue to future research due to space limitations.

In sum, the results of our experimental studies provide an effective warning strategy—the risk-alert-withranking-task strategy—to the managerial problem that initially motivated our research. The fundamental riskalert strategy, however, works as a double-edged sword and should thus be employed with caution.

## 7.2 Research Implications

We summarize the research implications of this work in Table 11. Our research contributes to the literature on mitigating bandwagon bias in online ratings. Online customers increasingly rely on ratings to share product information, and business owners heavily rely on ratings to understand their customers and run business intelligence systems (Moe et al., 2011; Garg & Johari, 2021). Hence, online rating biases (e.g., bandwagon bias) cause saliently derogated informative values for customers and business values for business owners (Chen et al., 2016; Garg & Johari, 2021). Despite previous studies revealing the prevalence and severity of bandwagon bias (Moe et al., 2011; Ma et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2015), research on the ex ante mitigation of this bias is still lacking. Our research partially fills this gap by showing that warnings can be used to curb bandwagon bias and by developing an effective warning strategy for bias mitigation—the risk-alertwith-ranking-task strategy.

Accordingly, online platforms (e.g., Yelp and Amazon) seeking to mitigate bandwagon bias can provide carefully constructed warnings. We recommend two implementation approaches. First, platforms can add a browser extension on their sites to generate a system alert when a user clicks on the rating scale. Second, platforms can also directly embed a warning message into the review page, similar to the treatment in Garg and Johari (2021). It is noteworthy that the commonly used risk-alert warning may introduce negative effects; in contrast, we demonstrate that a warning with a ranking task is a safe and recommended alternative strategy. Platforms must be cautious in their use of risk-alert warnings and should use them in tandem with an efficient prediction algorithm of the bias. Additionally, although our analysis indicates that the subjects’ average aversions to warnings were low, we nevertheless suggest that online platforms run an AB test prior to the formal launch of such warnings.

This work also contributes to the broader literature on online rating debiasing. Because of the serious business consequences of distorted word-of-mouth, curbing online rating biases has attracted massive practical and research attention, especially in recent years (e.g., Chen et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2018; Adomavicius et al., 2019; Karaman, 2021; Garg & Johari, 2021). In this paper, we introduce a new bias warning approach to existing rating debiasing approaches. This approach is especially useful for mitigating online rating biases with complex or unclear biasing mechanisms. Moreover, from a broader view, it offers an ex ante “modify-the-decision-maker” approach for curbing online rating biases, thus leading to an enriched debiasing framework that could be used in future research on this topic.

When deciding on the primary debiasing approach for rating biases, we suggest that online platforms consider two key questions. First, should rating biases be curbed ex ante or ex post? Compared with ex post debiasing, ex ante debiasing curbs rating biases before they occur; it is thus likely more effective but may cause unintended interferences in terms of reviewer decisions. Second, should biases be attributed to the person or situation? Here, person-attributed (versus situation-attributed) bias means that the rating bias is predominantly attributed to improper personal decision processes (versus problematic objective decision situations), which is a loose but useful bias distinction (Fischhoff, 1982; Soll et al., 2015). Accordingly, the “modify-the-decisionmaker” (versus “modify-the-environment”) approach is more efficient in curbing person-attributed (versus situation-attributed) biases (Soll et al., 2015). Notably, the rating bias attribution is elastic and context related. For example, when the rating environment is difficult to modify, one tends to attribute the rating bias to the person, and vice versa (Soll et al., 2015). In Table 11, we show the recommended debiasing approaches for online platforms in different conditions. Platforms can also employ a mix of debiasing approaches to increase rating informativeness, with the recommended approach being primary and the others being secondary.

## 7.3 Limitations and Future Research

We recognize that this work has several limitations. First, we conducted experiments in the specific online micromovie-rating context; hence, researchers should use caution in extending the research findings to other contexts. Note that we selected the micromovie context because it was feasible to simulate the entire consumption process in the lab (Adomavicius et al. 2013) and because movies appear to suffer most from social influence (Zhang et al. 2017). Future research should further explore the generalizability of our findings in other contexts.

Second, in our research, warnings with a ranking task did not reduce bandwagon bias to a greater extent than direct warnings. We conjecture that an optimized selection of comparison items for the ranking task could produce better bias mitigation, which provides many research opportunities for follow-up studies. For example, future research could explore the following questions: How does the selection of comparison items, such as the number of items and their comparability, affect debiasing power? Does the rating accuracy of comparison items and their rating differences influence debiasing performance? Additionally, future technical research could investigate an algorithm design that comprehensively considers the above factors to support personalized item selection.

Third, despite the controlled-experimental approach, which has the advantage of high internal validity, the external validity of our research findings requires additional field investigation. Future research could investigate how to appropriately employ the warning strategy in practice. For example, researchers could examine how to minimize the annoyance and interruption caused by system alerts for individuals and determine what warning delivery style leads to better debiasing performance. Future research could also investigate whether there are other overlooked side effects of debiasing warnings.

## 8 Conclusion

Bandwagon bias seriously undermines the informativeness of online ratings, which can lead to a trust crisis in customers and failures of business intelligence. In this paper, we propose the bias warning approach for mitigating bandwagon bias and examine the effects of different warning strategies. The results of four controlled experiments demonstrate the insufficiency of the prevalent risk-alert warning strategy and support the effectiveness of the newly developed risk-alert-with-ranking-task warning strategy for curbing bandwagon bias. With the enriched understanding of online rating debiasing, our research lays a foundation for future theoretical and practical work in this area.
