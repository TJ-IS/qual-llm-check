# Social Networks, Funding, and Regional Advantages in Technology Entrepreneurship: An Empirical Analysis

Abstract. With the increased penetration of digital technologies into entrepreneurship, the traditional need for proximity to specific locations or large amounts of funding for infrastructure development has diminished. Instead, digital entrepreneurs now pursue locations that provide more opportunities for funding (rounds) and greater social network support. In this paper, we empirically estimate the role of social networks and funding opportunities in a location on entrepreneurs’ decisions to create a start-up in their existing or a new location. We use economic indicators made publicly available by the U.S. government; investment information from CrunchBase and PricewaterhouseCoopers; and demographics, professional histories, and network data from LinkedIn. Analysis of 1,418 entrepreneurs who successfully secured funding suggests that funding rounds per year play a significant and positive role in influencing start-up creation in that location, and local social network density creates stickiness to that location, thus negatively influencing entrepreneurs’ willingness to relocate. If entrepreneurs do relocate, we assume that the will create a start-up immediately or delay it only by a short time. In the latter case, our results show that larger social network density (in a new location) affects start-up creation decision. Additionally, we find empirical evidence that midcareer individuals (like millennials) are more likely than early-career or late-career-stage individuals to create a successful technology start-up in the same or a new location. Also, entrepreneurs are less likely to relocate for an Internet-based start-up but are likely to move if they have larger social network density in the new location. This research enhances our understanding of the factors that influence decisions to launch a start-up in a particular location and contributes to the limited literature on technology entrepreneurship in the field of information systems.

## Introduction

Digital technologies have disrupted many industries by impacting organizations’ value chains, business models, and strategies (Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Rai and Tang 2014). These technologies have especially impacted technology entrepreneurship by reducing start-up costs (Suster 2012), thereby giving entrepreneurs additional freedom when choosing the location for new ventures. In the United States, technology entrepreneurship is predominantly linked to a few technology clusters such as Silicon Valley, San Diego, Boston, and Austin. Although Silicon Valley and the greater San Francisco Bay area are still the dominant region, the Kauffman Foundation’s Start-up Activity Index (Morelix et al. 2015) suggests that cities like Atlanta, Austin, Los Angeles, and Miami are showing robust growth in start-ups and technology employment (Kotkin and Schill 2015). This growth of new technology entrepreneurship hubs might be attributed, in part, to the increasing costs associated with traditionally attractive locations (Cowen 2017). This has been true for Silicon Valley, which gained a prominent position for digital entrepreneurship (Kotkin and Schill 2015), but is now losing its charm because of increasing costs of real estate and talent (Rampton 2016). Given the changing nature and requirements of start-ups in the current digital economy, our research seeks to understand the factors that influence the movement of digital entrepreneurs. Thus, in this paper we address the following questions: (1) Do social ties help in the creation of a technology start-up in a given location or a new location? (2) Does funding (rounds or amount) attract technology start-ups to a new location? and (3) What regional, start-up, and entrepreneur attributes influence creation of a funded start-up?

Digital technologies have simplified start-up creation and management. Companies such as Google, Apple, and HP literally started in garages (Giang 2014) because their early technological innovations didn’t need massive infrastructure. Jarvis (2016) estimates that creation of a functional software start-up (ofCourseBooks) that generated over \$12,600 in revenue cost only \$1,125 owing to digital technologies and a remote/virtual workforce. According to data collected by Fundable (Entis 2013), 38% of start-ups receive funding from friends and family, whereas only 0.91% receive funds from angel investors and 0.05% receive funds from venture capitalists (VCs). Thus informal sources of funding make it likely that entrepreneurs who launch technology start-ups choose to locate in communities where they have personal ties. Strong ties (especially family bonds) have also been shown to provide necessary support during early stages and continued operations of a start-up (Jack et al. 2004). Using online social networks (OSNs), entrepreneurs are likely to identify and increase communication with their (stronger) ties that can provide support (Gruzd and Haythornthwaite 2011). The role of these social ties may be different for Internet-based start-ups (e.g., Pinterest, MailChimp) that need even fewer and nonspecialized resources.

Despite widespread interest in technology entrepreneurship and the importance of entrepreneurs to the local and regional economy (Shane and Ulrich 2004), we have a relatively limited understanding of factors that influence entrepreneurs’ decision to create a start-up in a new location, which contributes to the heterogeneous economic and technological growth in metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs). This study adds to the literature that examines the role of both aggregated metropolitan economic indicators and individual-level factors that influence individual (re)location decisions (Figueiredo et al. 2002, Dahl and Sorenson 2009). We analyze an original and novel data set of entrepreneur career paths with direct observation of personal connections to empirically model individuals’ entrepreneurial choices. The following subsection discusses the related background literature used to develop a formalized theory for the location choice of entrepreneurs.

## Technology Clusters

A large body of scholarship explores the foundations of regional advantage and the transformation of regions through technology entrepreneurship and explains why some regions have prospered and others have not (Saxenian 1994, 1999, Venkataraman 2004). Much of the literature is concerned with ecosystems of agglomeration, including spinoffs and regional clustering of firms (Marshall 1920, Schumpeter and Opie 1934, Porter 1990, Moore 1993, Saxenian 1994,

Fetters et al. 2010). As noted by Engel (2015), the literature on clustering explains how areas specializing in an industry gain competitive advantage through economies of scale, reduction of transaction costs, and capture of spillover demand (Krugman and Obstfeld 1997). However, it does not explain how highly innovative clusters support the continuous emergence of technology start-ups. Focusing on entrepreneurs’ location choice can help develop a richer understanding of what retains and attracts entrepreneurs.

Saxenian (1994) conceptualizes innovation-centered business clusters as geographic concentrations of related companies focused on a scientific or technological knowledge base. Firms in the cluster share common physical and human capital needs and often conduct business with each other. Many studies detail how Silicon Valley emerged as the model for an innovationcentered business cluster (Feldman 2000, Hellmann 2000, Rosenberg 2002, Wonglimpiyarat 2006). Literature on Austin (Smilor et al. 1989, Fetters et al. 2010) and Route 128 in Boston (Dorfman 1983) has also contributed to understanding innovation-centered business clusters, specifically offering the “technopolis wheel”<sup>1</sup> as a framework for operationalizing key economic indicators influencing start-up development and thus impacting entrepreneurs’ decisions (Smilor et al. 1989).

Although these structural properties and potential economic returns from a location are important, pioneering economist Alfred Marshall famously suggested that there is something “in the air” that promotes the concentration of innovative activity and the continuous renewal of technology clusters. Implicit in this statement is the idea that the networks and interpersonal activity between individuals that comprise the entrepreneurial ecosystem (entrepreneurs, investors, lawyers, accountants, etc.) fuel the technology cluster and, in turn, the local diffusion of ideas, energy, and tacit knowledge between individuals and firms. Thus, the macro and micro factors are tied together through social network theory. Although this has been discussed, research to date has not really sought to understand the influence of an individual’s social network within a location on technology startup creation decisions within an existing or new location.

## Social Networks and Embeddedness

In addition to the advantages of location-specific factors, individuals’ social networks also influence their career location choices (Dahl and Sorenson 2010) and migration patterns (Massey 1990). Research has shown that the size and strength of the local social network negatively impacts individuals’ propensity to move to a new location (Sjaastad 1962, Dahl and

Sorenson 2010), even after controlling for wage and cost of living differentials (Michaelides 2011). Prior research indicates entrepreneurs are likely to start a company in the same locale where they previously worked because this choice enables them to use their existing local networks (Rogers 1995, Sorenson 2003, Romanelli and Feldman 2004).

The literature also shows that entrepreneurs tend to start their businesses where they have strong social ties or deep roots—typically places where they have family and friends—reaping rich location-embedded social capital (Katona and Morgan 1952, Mueller and Morgan 1962, Michelacci and Silva 2007, Dahl and Sorenson 2009, 2012). Moreover, increasing penetration of OSNs allows entrepreneurs to more easily identify and connect with individuals who could potentially provide support (business, social, or financial) to grow their start-up. With the emergence of these OSNs and increased diffusion of information (Garg et al. 2011, Aral and Walker 2012, Singh and Phelps 2013, Tambe and Hitt 2013), it is reasonable to expect that these multiple online social connections will have an increased importance on decision making and knowledge creation (Huang et al. 2014).

This study contributes to the literature on technology entrepreneurship by investigating the location- and individual-level social network factors that influence entrepreneurs’ decisions about where to start a company. Understanding why certain locations are better able to retain and attract digital entrepreneurs is a key component in explaining regional variation in entrepreneurial activity. Next, we formalize a theory for entrepreneurs’ location choice and present testable hypotheses.

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

The decision of where to create a start-up is influenced by a location’s ability to reduce costs, generate growth signals, and acquire key resources, among other factors. The growth and ubiquity of digital technologies has reduced start-up costs (Symm 2016). Since technology start-ups usually have much lower infrastructure needs and remotely deployed resources (e.g., cloud-based computing) (Ross and Blumenstein 2015), technology entrepreneurs conceivably have more freedom in their location choice for new ventures (Heger et al. 2011) than those depending on tangible resources.

Growth signals for start-ups could include capital investment by external entities (e.g., VC firms and angel investors), patents, media coverage (Greenwood and Gopal 2017), growth of customer base, and interpersonal or electronic word-of-mouth diffusion by networks of friends, employees, stakeholders, or consumers (Aggarwal et al. 2012, Aggarwal and Singh 2013, Greenwood and Gopal 2015, Susarla et al. 2016).

An entrepreneur’s social network and local embeddedness are key resources in a start-up’s development. Social connections can help reduce search costs as entrepreneurs filter information to access and secure vital resources (e.g., tacit technology knowledge, talent pools, supply and distribution chains, customers) (Ruef et al. 2003, Sorenson and Waguespack 2006), and local embeddedness increases with increasing number of social connections, thus imposing high transition costs to relocation.

Movement across regions is not in itself costless, and characteristics of various metropolitan regions will affect each entrepreneur’s start-up creation decision differently. Thus, we define three paths that entrepreneurs can take when deciding where to locate a start-up: (1) entrepreneurs who choose to create a start-up in their existing location, (2) entrepreneurs who choose to move and immediately create a startup in a new location, and (3) entrepreneurs who choose to move to a new location as an employee (for 6 to 12 months) to be embedded in the new location before creating a start-up in that location. ## Social Network (Tie Density)

The presence of entrepreneurs’ social networks in a location is an important factor in the development of their new venture. Launching a start-up requires close relationships with mutual trust (Porter 1998, Feldman 2000, Hite and Hesterly 2001) between the entrepreneur and investors, board of directors, financial and legal service providers, industry leaders (Krabel and Mueller 2009), and other entrepreneurs starting new ventures (Hsu 2006). These relationships are difficult to create quickly and are facilitated by consistent face-to-face interaction over time—thus, relocating is costly in terms of developing new relationships in an unfamiliar context (Figueiredo et al. 2002). Working professionals with entrepreneurial ambitions can become locally embedded as they acquire and maintain social and professional ties. This creates a stickiness to their current location because they would reasonably face friction in launching a start-up in a new location where they are not locally embedded. Beyond the social and emotional utility they generate, these local ties and knowledge are critical to reducing search costs for key resources, such as talented labor (Ruef et al. 2003, Sorenson and Waguespack 2006) and preseed capital (Bygrave et al. 2003). Thus, we expect entrepreneurs to start firms in locations with a higher proportion of their network ties, or tie density.

Hypothesis 1. (a) Higher social network density in a current location increases the likelihood of the entrepreneur creating a start-up in the current location. (b) Higher social network density in a current location decreases the likelihood of the entrepreneur creating a start-up in a new location. (c) Higher social network density in a new location increases the likelihood of the entrepreneur creating a start-up in the new location.

## Internet-Based Ventures

Several characteristics enable Internet-based startups to be more location independent than other technology ventures. Internet-based ventures<sup>2</sup> have relatively low capital and infrastructure requirements and thus face lower initial start-up costs. Additionally, technologies like cloud computing and storage and open-source code frameworks have lowered costs for information technology (IT services and solutions over the past decade. For example, one venture development firm estimates that building web applications such as Twitter and Instagram would cost approximately \$100,000 to \$300,000 (Myers 2013), which is significantly lower than the start-up costs for other technology ventures in biotech, energy/clean tech, or robotics. However, whereas Internet-based ventures have broader options for where to locate, returns could be higher when ventures are colocated with key resources, partners, or suppliers (Sorenson and Audia 2000). That is, one can expect that Internet entrepreneurs are less likely to move to a new location unless they have a social network available in the new location to help generate early awareness and adoption. Digital marketing through social networking platforms such as Facebook and Twitter are effective, and wordof-mouth is critical for establishing early and sustained interest from users (Trusov et al. 2009) and can have cascading effects through online networks that enhance awareness and popularity around content or services (Susarla et al. 2016). Therefore, we expect entrepreneurs to be more likely to start an Internet-based venture in a new location when their network density in that location is higher.

Hypothesis 2. Entrepreneurs launching start-ups are more likely to start an Internet-based venture in a new location when their network density in the new location is higher.

## Work Experience/Age

The literature on the relationship between technology entrepreneurship and an individual’s age or duration of work experience is mixed. Theoretically, being young might be advantageous for tech entrepreneurs, given the association between youth and transformative or disruptive thinking, fewer familyoriented responsibilities, and more time/energy available to dedicate to the overwhelming demands of launching a venture (Planck 1949, Weinberg and Galenson 2019, Dietrich and Srinivasan 2007). Indeed, several of the most successful technology start-ups of the past few decades were pioneered by individuals in their 20s or early 30s (e.g., Mark Zuckerberg founded Facebook at age 20). The median age of high profile winners of entrepreneurship competitions (e.g., TechCrunch) and recipients of venture capital from prominent firms, is approximately 30 and 33 years, respectively (Azoulay et al. 2019). Frick (2014) reported that the median age of a sample of VCbacked highly successful “unicorns” was 31 years, and Ng and Stuart (2016) found that most technology entrepreneurs founded their start-ups five years after receiving their bachelor’s degree (i.e., late 20s and early 30s).

Other literature finds that individuals with longer work experience likely have accumulated larger stocks of human and financial capital, which are critically important to new technology ventures (Lazear 2004, Chatterji 2009, Jones 2009). Recent work reports that the median age of founders who received funding for technology start-ups is 42 years, with the youngest cohort being the computing sector, with a median age of roughly 39 (Azoulay et al. 2019). Integrating these viewpoints, we expect that cumulative work experience will be positively associated with founding a start-up (in any location) up to a threshold and expect to find diminishing returns to work experience toward the older side of the distribution.

Hypothesis 3. The effect of work experience on the likelihood of an individual creating a funded start-up is nonlinear. Although increase in experience (when in lower range of values of experience) is associated with the increase in likelihood of start-up creation, increase in experience (when in higher range of values of experience) is associated with the decrease in likelihood of start-up creation.

## Work Experience/Age and Social Network (Tie Density)

Evidence from prior work indicates that as individuals age, their social networks become less active, which leads to a decline in their size and composition. In a recent meta-analysis, Wrzus et al. (2013) report that the overall sizes of individual social networks increase during adolescence and young adulthood and steadily decline thereafter (English and Carstensen 2014). This empirical evidence supports the socioemotional selectivity theory, which posits that this association pattern is the result of changing goals and resource conservations (Carstensen 1992, Lang and Carstensen 1994). As network activity and connections decline over time, a stable core network persists that is more heavily weighted toward close relationships and family (Neyer and Lang 2003). Based on this prior work, we expect that network density in a new location will be a less important consideration in the location choices made by older entrepreneurs.

Hypothesis 4. Higher social network density in a new location increases the likelihood that individuals with less work experience (age) will move to that location and launch start-ups.

## Investment Opportunity (Funding Rounds)

For a new venture, profit in a given time period is likely to be dependent on the profits/investments received in the previous comparable time period because these greater profits/investments serve as positive market signals (Conti et al. 2010). For technology start-ups, capital investment from a prominent VC or angel group is a valuable signal of a start-up’s qualitystart-up (Aggarwal et al. 2015) This signaling function of investment can increase the likelihood of a start-up’s success because new ventures are risky and many service providers (e.g., lawyers, recruiters, consultants) refuse to collaborate with a start-up without it (Ferrary and Granovetter 2009). Moreover, prior research suggests that increased supply of venture capital in a location positively influences the number of start-ups launched there (Samila and Sorenson 2011). Thus, we expect that the number of funding opportunities in a location is likely to influence an entrepreneur’s decision to create a start-up there.

Hypothesis 5. (a) More funding rounds in a current location increases the likelihood of the entrepreneur creating a start-up in the current location. (b) More funding rounds in a new location increases the likelihood of the entrepreneur creating a start-up in the new location.

Although not focal in our analysis, we include unemployment rate, labor force, and innovation (number of patents) as control variables in our model.

## Social Tie Density

The estimates for tie density (in Table 4) are positive and significant for entrepreneurs who launched a venture in their current location (paths 1(a) and (b)). This indicates that entrepreneurs are significantly more likely to start a company in their current location if their tie density in that location is high, suggesting that social network density plays an important role in supporting entrepreneurs. This result supports Hypothesis 1(a). In addition, the tie density (current location) estimates are negative (paths 2(a) and 3(a)) and significant (path 3(a)) for entrepreneurs starting companies in new locations. Thus, if an entrepreneur’s tie density in the current MSA is high, the entrepreneur is significantly less likely start a company in a new location. This finding supports Hypothesis 1(b). Furthermore, we observe that the marginal effect of an entrepreneurs’ tie density in a new MSA is positive, suggesting that they are more likely to start a company in the new location (paths 3(b) and (c)). We observe this positive, significant estimate only in regard to path 3 (moving to a new location and working as employee for a year or less before starting a company) but not for path 2 (moving to a new location and starting a company immediately). This suggests that the presence of a social network in a new location does not influence the location choice of entrepreneurs who plan to launch a start-up in the new location immediately, but does influence an entrepreneur’s decision to move to a new location with the intent to launch a new venture after a short time. Thus, we find only partial support for Hypothesis 1(c).

## Work Experience

We find support for Hypotheses 3 for a positive and diminishing association between cumulative work experience and an individual’s likelihood of creating a technology start-up (in either the current or new location). For all three paths, this result is captured in the positive and significant estimate for work experience and the negative and significant coefficient for the square of work experience value. As shown in Figure 5, more work experience increases the predicted probability of entrepreneurs launching a technology start-up in a new location, but this probability begins to diminish beyond approximately 20 years of work experience. We find similar curvilinear associations in the predicted probabilities for paths 1 and 3.

We find partial support for Hypothesis 4 that the positive association between social tie density and the likelihood of moving to a new location to launch a start-up varies based on the years of work experience. For path 2(c), we find a negative and significant interaction effect between tie density and work experience. One explanation could be that social networks become less active over time or experience substitutes for the need for a social network. ## Investment/Funding

We also find a positive and significant association between the number of funding opportunities for start-ups in MSAs and the movement patterns of entrepreneurs. The results (paths 1(b) and 2(c)) indicate that number of funding rounds per year has a positive and significant effect on entrepreneurs’ decisions to start a company, thus supporting Hypothesis 5(a) and partially supporting Hypothesis 5(b). Interpreting results in Table 4 (path 1(b)), a 10% increase in the number of funding rounds per year increases the log-odds that entrepreneurs will stay in the same location to start their company by a factor of 0.037. Interpreting the coefficient for path 2(c) in Table 4, a 10% increase in the number of funding rounds per year increases the logodds ratio of the entrepreneur changing locations and starting a company immediately (path 2) by 0.021. Interestingly, the coefficient for number of funding rounds is not statistically significant for path 3, but the coefficient for amount of funding is significant. This suggests that entrepreneurs who move to new location as employees but plan to start a company within a year are likely to be attracted to the larger amount of funds (on average) available in that new location.

The estimates for average funding amount per year were not statistically significant for paths 1 or 2. This was somewhat surprising, because one would expect entrepreneurs to be driven by amount of funding avai lable.<sup>11</sup> Although we passed the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) tests, we still tried removing funding amount and retaining average funding rounds in the specification and found no change in the significance. Based on our discussions with entrepreneurs and angel investors, we found that digital entrepreneurs usually seek smal investments from angels and target larger amounts at a much later date. When deciding to start a company, they are likely to be attracted to a location that provides more opportunities for funding compared with the amount of funding. Entrepreneurs believe that the larger number of funding rounds in a location is an indicator of more opportunity for securing funding, whereas targeting larger amounts would make it more difficult to obtain funds (possibly why the path 3 has significant coefficient for average funding amount). Additionally, because start-ups are typically funded in stages (Gompers and Lerner 2010), entrepreneurs are likely to associate more funding rounds with a higher likelihood that their start-up will continue to receive additional funding rounds beyond initial investment capital.

## Robustness and Limitations

Although we have tried to mitigate most concerns around identification, our results still may face some limitations around generalizability, endogeneity, cross-sectional social network data, serial entrepreneurial behavior, or cofounders influencing the startup decision. In this section, we delve deeper into these limitations and possible remedies. As explained earlier, we find a positive and significant influence of entrepreneurs’ tie density on their propensity to start a company in the same location. However, a variety of unobserved factors may simultaneously affect both an individual’s tie density at a location and the propensity to start a venture there. This may bias the coefficient of tie density in our specification. We classify the possible unobserved factors into two categories and explain how our results are robust to these possibilities by discussing a few examples in each category.

## Individual-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Factors

Unobserved individual-specific factors may incentivize individuals to start a venture at a location where they have built a large social network. An individual’s tenure at a location may be correlated to the evolution of tie density and the individual’s propensity to start a venture there. Although we do not have time series data on the evolution of social networks, we address this concern by testing our results by simulating tie density in a MSA based on entrepreneurs’ tenures in current and past locations. A well-documented finding in the literature is that relationships are more likely to develop between colocated persons. In many social contexts, the likelihood of tie formation decreases rapidly as the physical distance separating two parties increases (Bossard 1932, Baker 1984, Kono et al. 1998, Sorenson and Stuart 2001). At the dyadic level, colocation increases the likelihood of interaction between two parties, and there is a tendency toward mutuality in relations over time because of the social pressure to reciprocate interactions (Gouldner 1960). Because tie density is simply the connections in a location divided by the total connections in all locations, a dynamic conception of entrepreneurs’ tie density is a function of their tenure in current and past locations. Formally, using a simple approach, this new measure of tie density is

$$
Y _ {i j \tau} = \frac {n _ {i j} \times (\sum_ {t \in [ 0 , \tau ]} t _ {i j}) / (\sum_ {t \in [ 0 , T ]} t _ {i j})}{\sum_ {j} (n _ {i j} \times (\sum_ {t \in [ 0 , \tau ]} t _ {i j}) / (\sum_ {t \in [ 0 , T ]} t _ {i j}))}.\tag{2}
$$

Here, Y is an individual’s time-varying tie density, subscript τ represents the time period in consideration in location j by individual i, n is the observed number of ties of individual i in location j, and t represents cumulative time spent in location j by individual i. Note that the denominator of this tie density also changes when an individual has a smaller number of connections in a location. Using this approach, we converted the static view of tie density per location into a time-varying dynamic tie-density panel.

Empirically estimating Equation (3), results as shown in Table 5, the direction and significance of the results hold, but the extent of statistical significance for path 3 is reduced, while remaining significant. Our assumption with this test is that there is a linear correlation of tie-density evolution with tenure in location. Although we did consider a few nonlinear models (like sigmoid function) for the relationship between social connections and tenure, the identification of a location-specific network evolution model is outside the scope of this paper.

$$
\begin{array}{r} D _ {i j t} \big (X _ {i j t}, Z _ {j t - 1} | j \big) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} X _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} Y _ {i j t} + \beta_ {3} X _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {4} S _ {i j t} + \beta_ {5} Z _ {j t - 1} + \delta_ {i} + \gamma_ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Additionally, we considered the actual tie count in a location versus the tie density and found similar results<sup>13</sup>: the number of connections in the current location positively influenced an entrepreneurial decision to remain in that location (path 1), the number of connections in a new location did not have a statistically significant impact on entrepreneurs moving to that location to start a company (path 2), and number of connections in a new location positively influenced the entrepreneurs’ decisions to move there and wait before starting a company (path 3).

Another factor that may affect an entrepreneur’s location choice is if a cofounder has a good network in that location. Most of the entrepreneurs in our database are the solo or primary founder of a start-up. Roughly 70% of these entrepreneurs reported their title as founder, whereas 30% identified as cofounder. As an additional robustness check, we specified models with a dummy variable for an entrepreneur being a cofounder. As shown in Table 6, there are no significant changes in the results. It is worth noting that the coefficient for cofounder is weakly significant for path 3 (move, be employed for a year or less, and then start a company), suggesting that cofounders do play a role in decisions to launch entrepreneurial ventures in a new location. Still, these cofounders don’t influence entrepreneurial decisions to start a company in the current location (path 1) or to move to a new location to start a company immediately (path 2). In addition to having a database comprising mostly solo entrepreneurs, another explanation for the weakly significant coefficient of cofounder is that there is likely to be a large degree of overlap in cofounders’ networks due to triad closure in social relations. Network closure would suggest that cofounders’ network density is likely to be similar because connections with one cofounder will likely become connections with the other cofounder over time.

## Location-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Factors

We also account for the unobserved location-related factors that may affect individuals’ tie density and propensity to start a venture in a particular location. We first use location fixed effects to account for timeinvariant location characteristics and various controls (e.g., unemployment rate, number of patents, size of employed workforce, and funding availability). To account for some other omitted time-variant location characteristics (e.g., changes to minimum wage, broadband penetration), location-year fixed effect is an ideal choice, but we don’t have enough observations at a location-year level to estimate such a model. Therefore, as a next best option, we re-estimated the model with the Milken Institute Best-Performing Cities Index, which includes a variety of measures of MSA performance, including job, wage, and gross domestic product (GDP) growth and high-tech industry growth, and found no significant change in our focal results.<sup>14</sup>

In addition to the factors discussed earlier, there may be other time-variant location-specific factors that may attract individuals to that location, espe cially for major entrepreneurial hubs such as San Francisco Bay area and New York. To mitigate some concerns, we estimated models for entrepreneurs decisions to start companies in these locations and found no significant difference in results. We found that for both movement paths (paths 2 and 3), the San Francisco Bay area had a larger positive and significant coefficient for tie density than New York. Still, we acknowledge that the omitted variable bias caused by time-variant location-specific factors is extremely hard to address with the cross-sectional data in our present study. This is a limitation of this study.

Overall, although this study may have some limitations around endogeneity, we believe that it still provides valuable insights into factors that influence entrepreneurial decisions made by technology entrepreneurs. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that uses the archival data on entrepreneurs’ career transition decisions as a unit of measurement.

## Discussion

This paper makes several important contributions to the literature on technology entrepreneurship and regional advantage. Our analysis is among the few that measures and examines the role of both aggregated metropolitan economic indicators and individual-level factors that influence individual (re)location decisions (Dahl and Sorenson 2009). As detailed previously, prior work has focused on the influence of regional economic factors and agglomeration economies that yield high rates of spinoffs and subsequent clustering of innovative firms. Recognizing that the creation of a new venture is principally an individual decision made by a founding entrepreneur, our analysis uses an original data set of entrepreneur career paths with direct observation of personal connections, in addition to key economic and investment factors, to model the location and entrepreneurial choices of individuals.

Our analysis differs from and extends prior work in several important ways. We use rich data from a popular social networking platform to directly measure entrepreneurs’ networks across their work history and in turn evaluate the influence of social connections on their decision on where to launch a start-up. Without access to data sources enabled by digital networking platforms, prior work relied on indirect proxy measurements for network density such as generic high school classmates (Dahl and Sorenson 2010), or the physical distance between entrepreneurs’ residential address and the addresses of their hometown, parents, and siblings (Dahl and Sorenson 2009).

In contrast, our analysis leverages a direct and clear measure of entrepreneurs’ professional and social network density in each location, and our data focuses on the location decisions made by founders of technology ventures. Related prior work has investigated founders of mostly nontech companies in traditional industries such as hospitality, food, business services, and construction. Moreover, this earlier work primarily conceptualized social networks as family and friend connections (Dahl and Sorenson 2009), which are perhaps more appropriate for successfully launching new nontech businesses primarily serving local clientele. A broader measure of professional social networks is more appropriate for the investigation of technology ventures, which often require richer social capital with connections to technological expertise, tacit knowledge (Sorenson and Audia 2000), and more specialized resources (Ferrary and Granovetter 2009), and are thus less likely to be found in narrower family-friends networks.

The results presented here have significant managerial and policy implications. First, conceptual frameworks like the technopolis or entrepreneurship ecosystem have heavily influenced public officials and business leaders, yet research that measures the role of individual-level factors and key economic indicators on entrepreneurs’ location decisions is still nascent. Our empirical analysis identifies factors that are critical for cultivating existing entrepreneurial talent and attracting outside individuals who intend to launch start-ups.

Specifically, our findings indicate that an entrepreneur’s decision to start a company in a specific location is partially driven by the number of funding rounds in that location. One surprising finding was that the amount of funding available in a location did not have a statistically significant impact on entrepreneurs’ location decisions unless they decided to relocate as employees in order to be more embedded and connected in the new location prior to launching a start-up. Given that the number of funding rounds has a significant positive impact on entrepreneurs location decisions, state and regional policymakers may want to consider policies that encourage the organization of angel network groups, which provide a larger number of seed investment rounds relative to the larger-dollar-amount capital infusions by VC firms. These policies might include financial and other regulatory incentives. This seems particularly relevant to declining industrial MSAs with stagnating local economies, which are striving to encourage local entrepreneurship and retain local talent that is flowing out to MSAs like the San Francisco Bay area, Austin, Boston, and Seattle. The entrepreneurial community widely perceives limited access to investment to be the dominant impediment to start-up success. Future studies should further test this association, perhaps exploring potential moderating factors that could establish indirect relationships.

Additionally, we see that social network density plays a strong role in keeping an individual in a location (stickiness) and plays a significant role in providing support to potential entrepreneurs deciding to remain in their current location or to move to a newer location before considering the entrepreneurial decision. Our findings suggest that entrepreneurial talent that is locally embedded is less likely to start a company in a new location. From a policy point of view, this suggests that activities and policies that promote social tie formation and local embeddedness might encourage entrepreneurs to remain in that location to launch their company, which will likely yield positive benefits for the local economy. For example, local government or local business managers/owners might consider sponsoring attractive social events (e.g., meetups, networking events, and concerts/arts/cultural experiences<sup>15</sup>) that facilitate interpersonal interaction and in turn promote social tie formation and potentially enhance entrepreneurial activity. Moreover, these findings suggest that public and business information systems that facilitate communication and interaction among groups/organization with a local community are likely to promote positive spillover effects for the local economy.

Furthermore, our result showing the influence of social networks for entrepreneurs who follow path 3 (moving to a new location and working for a short time before launching a start-up) suggests entrepreneurial talent might launch ventures in locations where expanding one’s network is easier and where there are lower barriers to accessing key resources through networked connections. For example, the entrepreneurial ecosystems in the San Francisco Bay area and New York City are notorious for gatekeeping the networking process, which inhibits quick access to resources like new venture capital. In contrast, locations such as Austin or Seattle are reported by entrepreneurs to have more decentralized resource networks, which facilitates access to financial and other key resources. We also observe that distance does not adversely affect selection of a location for start-up creation (we considered distance between the MSAs for paths 2 and 3 and found no statistical significance). Thus, the decision to create a start-up in a new location is influenced by the financial attractiveness of the location or the person’s social network.

Over the past five decades, we have seen certain regions (e.g., the San Francisco Bay area, New York City, and Boston) attract increasing numbers of technology entrepreneurs. Most of the extant research attributes this pattern to favorable economic conditions offered by these locations. Our research dives deeper and considers entrepreneurs’ characteristics and their social networks to investigate, at the micro level, their location decisions. As more data becomes available, scholars should put quantitative measurement to the many qualitative narratives that have developed concerning technology entrepreneurship. Still, our analysis has some limitations. First, although we used geocentric endorsement ties as proxy to measure network density, we lack relational data for the interconnectedness of the entire social network, which might provide deeper insights. Second, we focus only on technology start-ups, which may not be representative of the entire entrepreneurship ecosystem because broadband and mobile Internet penetration make physical locations less relevant. Technology (especially Internet-based) start-ups usually don’t need a physical infrastructure or localized resources. Thus, our research results are limited to technology entrepreneurship and may not be generalizable to other type of entrepreneurial ventures. Third, the significance of the postrecession time-period dummy variable suggests that increased entrepreneurial activity could be a result of job losses or government initiatives, but our analysis is unable to distinguish between these causal agents. Nevertheless, this research takes the first step in identifying the role that social networks, investment, and economic indicators have on technology entrepreneurship. Future research can further refine this analysis to better understand the selection of a specific location over multiple others that were not selected. This could enable a better understanding of the rise of selected entrepreneurial hubs in the country.
