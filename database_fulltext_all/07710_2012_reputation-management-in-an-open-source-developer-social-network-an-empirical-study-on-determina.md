# Reputation management in an open source developer social network: An empirical study on determinants of positive evaluations

Daning Hu <sup>a,</sup>⁎, J. Leon Zhao <sup>b</sup>, Jiesi Cheng <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Informatics, University of Zurich, Zurich, Switzerland

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Open source software Positive evaluation Reputation management Social network analysis

## a b s t r a c t

Successful development of open source software (OSS) projects requires a steady supply of self motivated software developers. Thus a large body of OSS studies focuses on studying the developers' participation motivations. One important motivation is an OSS developer's desire to gain good community reputation which is largely based on positive evaluations from his peers. Therefore, to better motivate developers' project participations through their reputation needs, our empirical analysis adopted a social network perspective to study what factors may affect a developer's decision to positively evaluate one other in a large online open source community called Ohloh. The results surprisingly show that a developer's positive evaluation decision does not depend on his evaluatee's level of OSS-related experience, but rather based on 1) his past reputation (i.e. existing number of positive evaluations), 2) their shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations such as mutual acquaintances, as well as 3) their homophily in location (city), nationality, programming language preference, and community status. We then discuss these <sup>fi</sup>ndings and their implications for inducing more positive evaluations and better reputation management among open source project members. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the <sup>fi</sup>rst research that investigates issues of reputation building and relationship management in an open source development context

## 1. Introduction

In recent years a novel model of production has emerged in which self-organizing individuals voluntarily collaborated online with one another to produce goods and service ranging from software to encyclopedias [46]. A phenomenal success of this production model is open source software (OSS) development whose <sup>fl</sup>agships include Linux, Firefox, MySQL, and many others. The OSS development is characterized by its voluntary developers distributed globally who mainly collaborated in online communities such as Sourceforge. This software development approach, facilitated by today's digitally network environment, has provided new ways for business to interact and collaborate with customers in software development and maintenance [12]. Thus the OSS model has been supported by more and more major software vendors such as IBM. Previous studies found that OSS project success largely depends on its volunteer participants who contribute considerable time and efforts in project development activities [3,35]. Therefore it is crucial to understand developers' participation motivations and how to promote such motivations for achieving better project success.

Among the widely quoted motivations for participating in OSS development projects such as altruism, learning, and career advance opportunities, a very important one is a developer's desire to gain good reputation [15,16,34,39] which is mainly based on the positive evaluations received from his peer developers in OSS communities. In the OSS community of our study – Ohloh, positive evaluation is represented by a Kudo link sent from one developer to another. An Ohloh developer A may choose to send a Kudo link to another developer B for admiring his OSS development work (e.g., source code, code comments) in general or for appreciating B's contributions if they both work in the same project(s). It may also be due to that A receives one-to-one help from B in OSS development (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>xing a software bug, solving a programming problem). To summarize, a Kudo link represents the sender's explicit positive evaluation on the receiver's individual characteristics or actions, such as outstanding performance, great programming skills or valuable contributions. For an individual OSS developer, receiving a large number of Kudo links and consequently gaining a good reputation can be an effective approach to attract attentions and potential collaborations. If an OSS developer is well known for his generosity, intelligence, excellent programming skills and leadership, it becomes much easier to convince others that collaborating with this developer will greatly bene-<sup>fi</sup>t them [34]. From an OSS project manager's perspective, improving the project members' opportunities to receive positive evaluations may enhance their reputation-based motivations for contributing to project development. OSS projects with many reputable developers may be able to attract more high quality developers through the preferential attachment process. Therefore, it is important to study what social and technical factors affect positive evaluation relationships. Although the importance of developers' reputationbased motivation has been acknowledged in OSS literature, the foundation of good reputation – positive evaluation – has received little theoretical attention.

A developer's positive evaluations received from her peers are often in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by many social and technical factors embedded in the OSS environment. In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned these factors as the determinants of positive evaluations. These determinants shape the evaluative perceptions of OSS community members on each other and eventually build up their reputations. We use social network analysis (SNA) and statistical methods to empirically identify and examine the determinants of positive evaluation relationships in an OSS developer network. We aim to provide insights and suggestions for both individual developers and project managers to promote participations, better manage positive evaluation relationships, and thereby achieve better OSS project success.

Although a growing body of research has explored the collaboration relationships among OSS developers, there is little effort in understanding developers' positively evaluation relationships mainly due to two problems. Firstly, there is a lack of large-scale real-world data sources on positive evaluations among OSS developers. Secondly, since each positive evaluation connects two parties – evaluator and the developer being evaluated, relational/network perspective is greatly needed to study positive evaluation relationships. Existing OSS studies that adopt relational/network perspectives mainly focused on developers' collaboration relationships which are derived from their joint project participations. Such derived collaboration link is a relatively weak proxy of personal relationships since two developers in a large project may not know each other at all. On the other hand, a developer positively evaluating another is an intentional and explicit relationship building action, indicating the evaluator has certain knowledge of the evaluatee. To address these two problems, we conduct an empirical investigation of an OSS developers' social network in which their positive evaluation relationships as links. We hypothesize that three types of network-related factors signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect these developers' positive evaluation choices: 1) preferential attachment and accumulative advantage factors, 2) homophily in various individual attributes, and 3) shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations.

Our study innovates by adopting a set of social network related factors and analytical methods to study OSS developer networks [17,22,33]. While OSS phenomena are not new, research interest in its social network perspective has only recently emerged. Social network analysis (SNA) methods have been widely used to study the collaboration relationships derived from joint participation in OSS projects [11,14,18,19,25,44]. However, those studies mainly focused on the static topologies and largely ignored the dynamic relationship formation processes behind the network evolution. We modeled that a developer choosing to positively evaluate another as a link formation process which is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by various social and technical determinants. We then empirically examined the hypothesized factors which are based on previous OSS and network studies [5,9,22,28,33,37].

In addition, we suggest this study has both theoretical and practical contributions. Theoretically, this study contributes to the theoretical understanding about how social network related factors affect individual behaviors by examining determinants of OSS developers' positive evaluation choices in an online collaborative environment. On the other hand, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this study can offer insights to OSS group leaders for increasing positive evaluations and thereby promote trust among group members. To this end, we have made a set of suggestions based on the discovered determinants of positive evaluations to facilitate more positive evaluations in an OSS community. The long term goal is to improve OSS group performance by cultivating a positive development environment.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review the related studies and develop the research hypotheses. Section 3 describes the data we collected from a large online OSS community for our empirical analysis. In Section 4, we present the research design in detail. Section 5 shows the results and Section 6 discusses their implications. At last, we conclude the paper and discuss future research directions.

## 2. Related studies and research hypotheses

## 2.1. Reputation-based participation motivation and positive evaluation

Since open source software development largely relies on the voluntary efforts of software developers, a large body of OSS research has focused on developers' participation motivations [16,23,35,41]. In general, the motivations can be categorized into two types – intrinsic and extrinsic motivations [23]. The intrinsic motivations for participations are the factors related to OSS developers' needs for satisfying themselves such as altruism and enjoyment, while extrinsic motivations are usually derived from external rewards such as desire for good reputation and career advancement opportunities. A motivation discovered in previous OSS studies and central for our research is the developers' desire to gain reputation from his peers. The lack of traditional monetary reward in an OSS development environment has made the reputation-based motivation as a major drive for developers' participations and contributions [40]. One of the most cited studies on OSS participation motivations done by Roberts et al., [35] has examined the relationships among developers' participation, performance, and motivation using empirical data from the Apache project. It found that Apache developers' desire to gain high community reputation can lead to above average participation levels. Moreover, developers with higher status (i.e. better reputation) within the Apache project community are found to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher wages. This suggests that an OSS developer's community reputation could be a signaling mechanism to his potential employers [40]. Another major study on Linux project participation [16] found that the intentions to improve one's own career opportunities are important motives for participation. These empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide additional supports and explanations for OSS developers' reputation-based motivation other than self-satisfaction.

However, a developer's desire to gain reputation can only in<sup>fl</sup>uence his participation choices but not directly build his reputation. A major source of an individual's community reputation is the peer evaluations he received. Zaacharia [48] categorized reputation mechanisms in electronic markets into two types – non-computational and computational ones. Non-computational reputation mechanism often relies on a central repository which keeps information about user activities (e.g., the scoring systems of credit rating agencies). On the other hand, computational mechanisms, based on the evaluation information collected from the evaluators (e.g., buyers at eBay), calculate an overall reputation score for the individual being evaluated (e.g., sellers at eBay). Such a reputation score re<sup>fl</sup>ects an aggregated view on how this individual's peers perceive him. The computational reputation mechanisms have been adopted in many collaborative online communities and ecommerce Web sites such as YouTube and eBay. With such mechanisms, we suggest that positive evaluations can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the composition and performance of an OSS project group through its members' reputation-based participation motivation. This conjecture is consistent with our previous empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the Ohloh community, which partly motivated this study. We found that the average user rating of each software project, which is an indicator of project success, is positively related to the total number of positive evaluations received by project members.

Although this empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding has implied the importance of developers' positive evaluation choices on OSS project participation and success, little research has been done in studying what factors may affect such evaluation choices and how to facilitate more positive evaluations among OSS developers in order to enhance their reputation-based participation motivations. Moreover, the datasets used in many empirical OSS studies often only include one or two major projects like Linux. Their empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings may not be generalized to other OSS projects, mainly due to the lack of large-scale OSS-related relational data, especially data about positive evaluations. At last, there is a lack of appropriate analytical methods to analyze such relational data. To address these problems, we adopted a social network perspective and formulated three SNA related hypotheses about the factors that may affect OSS developers' positive evaluation choices. The relevant studies and hypotheses are presented in the following sub sections.

## 2.2. A social network perspective of positive evaluation relationships

OSS developers form various collaboration and communication networks [15]. Thus social network analysis becomes an important approach for analyzing the relationships in those networks [11,14,20,25]. There are mainly two types of SNA methods: one focuses on analyzing the static network topologies; the other focuses on modeling various dynamic network processes.

SNA research focusing on analyzing network topologies mainly aims to explain how topologies affect network functions and behaviors [1]. Three models were developed to characterize the topologies of complex networks: random graph model, smallworld model, and scale-free model [4]. Among them, the scalefree model is particular relevant to our study since many empirically observed OSS collaboration networks are found to be scale-free with a power-law degree distribution, which means a very small fraction of the nodes in the network occupies most of the links while the majority of the nodes only have a few links. Madey [25] conducted one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical investigations on OSS collaboration network topologies based on data from SourceForge.net – the world's largest open source software development platform. He modeled the collaboration link as two developers' joint participation in a same project and found such collaboration networks display features of scale-free topology. Moreover, another topological analysis done by Jin et al. [20] on collaboration networks in SourceForge.net also discovered similar scale-free features. Based on these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we conjecture the Ohloh positive evaluation network has scale-free topology and is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the preferential attachment link formation process.

Other types of SNA methods focus on analyzing dynamic network processes [8], especially link formation processes [17,22,33]. A social network grows over time with the addition of new nodes and the formation of new links among nodes. While topological analysis only can capture static snapshots of social structures in OSS developer communities, studying the mechanisms behind the formation process of various links (e.g., participation, evaluation) can provide insights about developers' (link formation) choice decisions. Hahn et al. [15] found that a developer's decision to participate in an OSS project is positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by his or her past collaborations with the existing members in this project. Such factors that signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences the formation of (participation) links are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as determinants of link formation processes in social networks [22]. Previous dynamic SNA studies suggest three types of factors – preferential attachment, homophily, and shared af<sup>fi</sup>liation – may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' choices in forming relationships (i.e. links) with others [1,22,28]. Thus we hypothesize that these three types of factors are the determinants of positive evaluation link formation in OSS social networks.

## 2.2.1. Preferential attachment

Preferential attachment is one of the most well studied link formation processes that account for the scale-free network topology. This topology is observed in many real-world social networks, including many OSS-related networks [20,25]. The assumption for preferential attachment is that the nodes with more existing links are more likely to attract new links. This process, also referred as “the rich get richer,” will eventually generate a network in which a small fraction of the nodes occupy a large number of links while the majority of the nodes only have a few links (i.e., power-law degree distribution). In this study, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned a node's degree (i.e. the number of links a node has) as the preferential attachment factor. Barabasi and Albert [4] <sup>fi</sup>rst proposed a mathematical model for the preferential attachment process, assuming the probability ∏(k ) that a new node will link to a node i depends on i's degree k . Based on this model, several OSS studies [18] examined the degree distributions of OSS collaboration networks using linear regression technique and found that these distributions follow the power law, indicating scale-free topologies. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that a developer's degree factor may have great impacts on her project participation choices. The small fraction of the developers in an OSS community that occupy the majority of collaboration links can be explained by the conjecture that new developers tend to participate in projects consisting of many high pro<sup>fi</sup>le developers with good reputations. This is particularly true in the context of OSS development due to the lack of face-to-face interactions among the developers. They may face more challenges in accurately evaluating each other and thus may heavily rely on developers' online pro<sup>fi</sup>le information such as community reputation score. This conjecture suggests that a few OSS developers who had good reputations (i.e. receiving many positive evaluations) may be viewed more positively and will have better collaboration opportunities than others with less good reputations, and thereby are more likely to receive more positive evaluations from these collaboration opportunities. This preferential attachment process can continuously shape the OSS evaluation networks to the scale-free topology. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1a (H1a). The likelihood that a developer receives a positive evaluation in an open source developer social network is positively related to the number of her existing positive evaluations.

## 2.2.2. Accumulated advantage

Preferential attachment is largely based on the “Mathew effect” in sociology which is also often termed as “accumulated advantage effect”. The accumulated advantage effect is that individuals who already have certain economic or social resources can leverage them to gain more resources. This phenomenon was <sup>fi</sup>rst coined by Robert K. Merton to describe how famous scientists often get more credit than a relatively junior researcher with similar work [29]. In social networks, this effect enables nodes with accumulated advantages in certain attributes to attract more new links. In our study, such node attributes are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as accumulative advantage factors. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 1b (H1b). The likelihood that a developer will receive a positive evaluation in an open source developer social network is positively related to the levels of advantages this developer has in the accumulative advantage factors.

## 2.2.3. Homophily

Prior research on homophily theory suggests that people are more likely to interact and form relationships with individuals that they share similar attributes [7,28]. When evaluating an individual, a developer may heavily rely on information about the similarities in individual attributes between him and that person being evaluated. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned such attributes as homophily factors in this study. According to the self-categorization theory proposed by Turner [43], people self categorize themselves and others in terms of age, gender, education and various other individual attributes. Based on these categories, they further differentiate others into similar and dissimilar ones. Various studies have empirically examined the homophily theory. In the sociology literature, Leenders [24] used a continuous-time Markov model to study a children's friendship network and found that homophily in gender (i.e., being the same gender) signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects the friendship link formation. Marsden [26] studied core discussion networks and found that Americans tend to discuss important issues with people who have similar education levels. In addition, McPherson et al. [27] discovered homophily in socioeconomic status in the friendship networks of voluntary organizations. Finally, McPherson et al. [28] summarized that many social relationships, including marriage, work, and advice, are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the homophily principle.

Our research employed the homophily theory to explain OSS developers' positive evaluation choices. However, this research differs from prior OSS studies and homophily research in several ways. First, past homophily research mainly focused on individual attributes such as age or gender [24], while our study also explores homophily in contextual factors in OSS developer communities, such as programming language and community status. This is mainly because OSS developers often have limited access to others' personal information due to online anonymity. When evaluating others, they heavily rely on contextual information such as developers' community reputation scores. Second, past homophily research mainly focused on face-to-face interaction in co-located groups [47]. Our research is one of the few that studies how the homophily factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence the network link formation process in globally distributed groups. In addition, OSS developers are very diverse in terms of nationality, language, culture, and various other social and personal characteristics. Previous research suggested that the more diverse the group is, the greater the homophily effect in social networks [27], which also supports the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2 (H2). The likelihood that one developer will positively evaluate another is positively related to the similarity between these two developers in various homophily factors.

## 2.2.4. Shared affiliations

Moreover, prior SNA research suggests that individuals tend to form relationships with people who they have prior af<sup>fi</sup>liations with [36]. When evaluating a prior af<sup>fi</sup>liated individual, an Ohloh developer will often use his or her past experiences with this individual to determine whether to send him a positive evaluation (kudo) link. The shared af<sup>fi</sup>liation factors include shared activities, group memberships, and mutual acquaintances through various relationships. Kossinets et al. [22] analyzed an email communication network in a large University for one academic year. It was found that the number of shared classes and mutual acquaintances between two students signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the formation of their email links. Newman [31] also found that two previously unacquainted scientists are more likely to co-author a paper if they share one or more common coauthors before.

In OSS domain, Hahn et al. [15] discovered the positive impacts of prior collaboration af<sup>fi</sup>liations on OSS developers' future participation choices. They suggest an OSS project consisting of developers with prior collaborative af<sup>fi</sup>liations may be more effective in coordinating its members' distributed expertise, since these af<sup>fi</sup>liated members have knowledge of “who knows what”. Such advantages will enhance developers' past collaborative links and induce cohesive collaborative cliques in OSS social networks [13]. Developers may prefer to positively evaluate individuals who are in their cohesive cliques.

We also suggest that shared af<sup>fi</sup>liation through past positive evaluations is a stronger determinant than through past collaborations mainly for two reasons. First, the collaboration relationship is derived from two developers' joint participation of the same OSS project(s).

Thus the strengths of such relationships vary greatly among different pairs of developers in a project, especially in a large one like Linux which often consists of thousands of developers. Most of them may not know about each other. Therefore, these derived collaboration relationship can not accurately re<sup>fl</sup>ect the interpersonal relationships among OSS developers, and be used to predict future positive evaluation relationships. Second, collaboration links contains little information about the sentiments of developers towards each other. Many OSS projects fail due to various con<sup>fl</sup>icts among project members. Experiences in past failed collaborations can discourage developers from positively evaluating the collaborators in the future. Thus our study focuses on the evaluation relationship which contains developers' sentiment information. We suggest that shared af<sup>fi</sup>liation through positive evaluation is a better choice than past collaborations for determining OSS developers' positive evaluation choices. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). An OSS developer will be more likely to positively evaluate another if they have one or more shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations.

## 3. Dataset

The dataset used in this study was collected from a large online OSS community – Ohloh – through its application programming interface (API) [2]. Ohloh is an online community and a free public directory for OSS development. It collects various types of information about OSS projects and developers from public software version control systems such as concurrent versions system (CVS) and Subversion. Furthermore, it collects developers' activity information from these version control systems and keeps track of every change in the source code of each listed OSS project. With such information, Ohloh calculates software metrics for each OSS project such as the total number of changes (i.e. commits). Ohloh encourages developers to register, edit and discuss OSS projects in its online social-networking community. The dataset used in our study was collected in March, 2008, which contains about 94,330 developers and 11,530 OSS projects, ranging from well-known open source software such as MySQL to lesser-known projects such as CakePHP.

Ohloh is owned and operated by Geeknet, Inc., which also operates several other top OSS web sites such as Sourceforge.net – the world's largest OSS development platform. Ohloh does not host open source projects like Sourceforge.net, but focuses on retrieving and analyzing publicly available information about OSS projects and developers. Therefore, it provides a comprehensive and unique set of OSS development-related information which is not available at other major OSS web sites including Sourceforge.net. Such information includes OSS developers' project participation and positive evaluation choices, location, nationality, programming language preferences, development activities, and project statistics.

For our study, Ohloh provides a unique type of relational information through its evaluation mechanism – the “Kudo” links. Each Ohloh community member can send another member a link called a “Kudo” which shows a gesture of appreciation, praise, or endorsement from the sender to the receiver. By consulting with several senior OSS developers in the Ohloh community, we found that a Kudo link is given often in one of the following scenarios.

■ An Ohloh developer may appreciate another developer's OSSrelated characteristics (e.g., source code, code comments) and sends a Kudo link to him or her as recognition.

■ An Ohloh developer may appreciate the contributions of his or her co-developer in an OSS project and sends a Kudo link to this co-developer.

■ An Ohloh developer may receive one-to-one help from another developer in OSS development work and thus sends a Kudo link to express his or her thanks.

Therefore, a Kudo link represents a positive evaluation from the sender to the receiver.

## 4. Research design

The research design presented in Fig. 1 consists of two major steps. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step involves two components: network construction and determinants extraction. We constructed a positive evaluation network from the Ohloh dataset based on the Kudo links. The potential determinants of positive evaluation links are selected based on empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings and theoretical conjectures from previous OSS and SNA studies. The second step, network analysis, contains both social network analysis and conditional logistic regression.

## 4.1. Network construction

To construct the positive evaluation network from the Ohloh dataset, we <sup>fi</sup>rst identify the network nodes (i.e., Ohloh developers) and links (i.e., Kudo links). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the Ohloh developers that meet the following standards as positive evaluation network nodes: 1) the developers must have registered in Ohloh community with their detailed information such as location and nationality; 2) the developers have either sent or received at least one Kudo link at the time of data collection. The developers' information is needed for analyzing the effects of various social and technological factors on positive evaluation choices, while the Kudo links are needed to represent the positive evaluations. The <sup>fi</sup>nal positive evaluation network contains 3451 registered developers as nodes and 9827 positive evaluations as links.

## 4.2. Determinants extraction

The determinants of positive evaluation were extracted from the Ohloh dataset based on empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings or theoretical conjectures from prior OSS and SNA studies [22,28,33]. As mentioned in the literature review, the determinants we selected to examine are: 1) preferential attachment factors: OSS experience, coding experience [3]; 2) homophily [28] in country, location, programming language, and community reputation [32]; and 3) shared affiliation factors: past joint participations of OSS project(s) and mutual acquaintance(s) [22].

■ Developer network degree: the number of positive evaluation links she has received.

■ Coding experience: the total number of commits made by a developer in the Ohloh dataset (one commit refers to making changes to the source code for one time).

■ OSS experience: the total number of months in which a developer made at least one commit to her participated OSS projects.

These preferential attachment and accumulative advantage factors are selected assuming that a developer's network degree and experience level are related with the number of her future Kudo links. These factors are measured up until the time she receives a kudo link.

■ Homophily in country: a developer's nationality in his or her registration <sup>fi</sup>le.

■ Homophily in location: a developer's location (city level) in his or her registration <sup>fi</sup>le.

■ Homophily in programming language: a developer's primary programming language

■ Homophily in community status: a developer's score called KudoRank ranging from 1 to 10 calculated based on the number and quality of the Kudo links she receives.

In this study, the impacts of homophily factors on OSS developers' positive evaluation relationship are assessed with the above individual attributes. We include the homophily on developers' country and location attributes since similarity in individuals' geographic locations is found to be a determinant for forming various relationships in prior SNA research [18]. Homophily in primary programming language is examined since mastering the project programming language is a prerequisite for two project members to collaborate together.

■ Past OSS projects: the number of same OSS projects that two developers have been in.

■ Mutual acquaintance from past positive evaluation/collaboration relationships: the number of individuals two developers have both positively evaluated/collaborated before.

We include past OSS collaboration relationships since they may induce subsequent positive evaluations between two developers. Mutual acquaintance is included since it serves as a bridge for two previously unacquainted individuals to get to know each other [22].

## 4.3. Network analysis

Our choice of the statistical model for analyzing determinants of positive evaluation is based on both theoretical and empirical considerations. Theoretically, our study aims to model human (evaluation) choice decisions in social networks. The theoretical research question is how to discover the determinants that account for the differential (as opposed to random) patterns of link formations in social networks. Empirically, we need to model OSS developers' evaluation choice behaviors in an online community. Thus we choose to use the conditional logistic model that takes each choice as a unit of analysis, which in our case is the formation of a positive evaluation link between two OSS developers. The conditional logistic model has been widely used to model various human choice behaviors and examine the factors that affect these choices. In this model, for the Ohloh evaluation network, the probability that an OSS developer i will choose to send a Kudo positive evaluation link to another developer j from the alternative set $J _ { i }$ is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as: $\operatorname* { P r } ( y _ { i } = j ) = { \frac { \exp \left( X _ { j } { \boldsymbol { \beta } } \right) } { \sum _ { i } ^ { J } \exp \left( X _ { j } { \boldsymbol { \beta } } \right) } } ,$ where $y _ { i }$ is the observed choice for developer i and $X _ { j }$ is a vector of the characteristics of the chosen developer $j .$ The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients $\beta$ are estimated by maximum likelihood methods. The dependent variable is a binary indicator if a Kudo link is sent. The independent variables are the factors explained in Section 4.2.

## 5. Results and discussions

Table 1 presents the results from conditional logistic regression analysis with the odds ratios and the regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients. The odds ratios are obtained from the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients by using exponential function. That is, odds ratio $X _ { i } { = } \exp ( \beta _ { i } )$ where $\beta _ { i }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient. An odds ratio is the change of the probability of link formation caused by a unit increase in an independent variable. The probability of sending a Kudo will increase by a factor of the odds ratio when the corresponding independent variable increases by one unit. The odds ratio equals to one means that the independent variable has no effect on the dependent variable.

The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for the developer degree $( \beta = 0 . 0 9 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ indicates a developer's ability to attract new positive evaluations is positively with the number of her past ones; whereas neither a developer's coding experiences nor OSS experience signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect others' decision to send her Kudo links. In other words, Ohloh developers are more likely to positively evaluate others who already gained good reputation through past peer positive evaluations. This <sup>fi</sup>nding may be partly attributed to the availability of the information about an Ohloh developer's existing Kudo links at her pro<sup>fi</sup>le web page, while information about her OSS and coding experience is not easily accessible. Moreover, since the odds ratio of developer degree is only slightly larger than 1, indicating a developer's past reputation only has a moderate positive effect on the future ones. Thus H1a is supported while H1b is not.

The results also show that two developers' homophily in location $( \beta = 2 . 0 3 1 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ and country $( \beta = 1 . 5 0 5 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ have positive effects on the likelihood that they would positively evaluate each other. Moreover, homophily in primary programming language and community status was also found to be determinants of positive evaluations. Therefore H2 is supported.

In addition, the result for past OSS project(s) af<sup>fi</sup>liation $( \beta =$ $7 . 3 2 7 , p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ suggests that a developer is much more likely to positive evaluate another if they have been worked in the same OSS project(s) before. The odds ratio for this factor is much larger $( \chi = 1 5 2 0 . 9 3 5 )$ than other factors indicating that past collaboration experience plays a major role in developers' positive evaluation choices. Furthermore, mutual acquaintance through collaboration $( \beta = 3 . 4 5 8 ,$ $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ and evaluation relationships $( \beta = 3 . 1 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ are also found to be determinants of positive evaluations the latter has stronger impacts than the former.

We then offer a set of conjectures mainly based on previous OSS and social network research, aiming to provide possible explanations for the above empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. As H1a indicates, when a developer has already received many positive evaluations, he may have occupied an advantageous position in terms of connectivity in the Ohloh evaluation network and in the community. As such, this highly recognized developer has greater visibility and in<sup>fl</sup>uence on others. Newly joined project members may be largely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the reputation and social connections of such highly recognized developers and hence form high initial trust towards them. Such high initial trust may further facilitate more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient collaboration relationships, and thereby inducing positively evaluation among developers subsequently. As for the homophily factors, geographic propinquity (e.g., living in the same area) between two Ohloh developers may provides them more faceto-face interaction opportunities and hence is more likely to enable many in-depth interpersonal relationships such as close friendship, which may further facilitate successful collaborations and thereby induce positive evaluations. Moreover, homophily in location can be indicators of similarity in demographic characteristics such as language, culture, social status, ideology, religion, especially for people in countries with a dominant race or culture. As the homophily theory suggested, two individuals with similar demographic characteristics are attracted to each other since such similarity is a source of familiarity, predictability, and validation [21,45]. We plan to study homophily in these characteristics when empirical data becomes available.

An OSS development project also heavily relies on one or two major programming languages. One of the major participation motivations discovered before is the desire to gain career opportunities by improving programming skills. Thus, OSS developers may prefer to work with individuals who use the same programming language for more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient collaborations. Such preferences may induce positive evaluations among developers using same programming languages. In addition, two developers with the same Ohloh reputation score tend to have similar community status and experience in OSS related activities. These similarities may also facilitate positive feelings and thereby induce more positive evaluations.

It is not surprising that shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations in past OSS project(s) is also a determinant of positive evaluation choices, considering 93.1% of pairs of Ohloh developers with positive evaluation relationships have worked in the same project(s) before. Previous research [6] found that group members in a startup <sup>fi</sup>rm with prior shared company af<sup>fi</sup>liations often have a shared language which suggests a common perspective [42] and a frame of reference for observing the environment [30]. In addition, these group members often have a common narrative rooted from shared experiences, stories and examples of appropriate and inappropriate behaviors. In OSS projects, such shared languages and narratives, along with other organizational factors such as cultures and norms, form a common value framework for these previous af<sup>fi</sup>liated project members. This framework continuously shapes these developers' working behaviors, and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uences their evaluations on each other.

It was also found that two previously unconnected developers are more likely to positively evaluate each other if they share one or more mutual acquaintances through positive evaluation relationship or had participated in the same OSS project(s). Similar effects of mutual acquaintance factor have also been found in other social network studies [22,28] on friendship link formation processes. The mutual acquaintance often serves as a broker who facilitates the information and resource exchange between the two previously unconnected individuals, and provides them more potential interaction opportunities. In the Ohloh community, if two previously unconnected developers both have positively evaluate the same individual, their account names and home page URL will be listed on that individual's pro<sup>fi</sup>le web page. They are more likely to get acquainted through this hyperlink bridge. We termed this mechanism as the bridging mechanism. Moreover, a developer may introduce the developers he is collaborating with to each other for various reasons such as expertise referral. Both bridging and referral mechanisms can induce Ohloh developers to form circles of trust that include close acquaintances. In OSS projects, such close acquaintances are likely to form tight operational and social cliques, which in turn enhance their collaborative relationships and increase their capacities to act in development activities. This is also in line with the social closure theory [10] which suggests that the greatest value is obtained from networks that are densely connected with a high level of trust among network actors.

Another interesting <sup>fi</sup>nding is that two accumulative advantage factors – OSS and coding experiences – are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for Kudo links. It is congruent with a proposition in Stewart's study on OSS developer's community social status [38], that is, a developer's ability to attract positive evaluation becomes increasingly institutionalized or stable as his experience grows over time. We also offered another possible explanation for this <sup>fi</sup>nding in terms of information availability in Ohloh web site. The developers' OSS experience and coding experience information are stored in Ohloh database and not directly available to others. Thus, the two corresponding accumulative advantage factors are insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant may simply because they are not available when Ohloh developers make positive evaluation choices. To address this data issue, we plan to study these two factors using data from other OSS communities in the future. As Table 3 shows, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the determinants of positive evaluations vary from 0.048 to 8.118. It was found that the more personal the underlying relationships a determinant involves, the larger its coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is, indicating a larger impact on OSS developers' positive evaluation choices. For example, two developers are more likely to know with each other personally if they participate in the same OSS project than live in the same country. Accordingly, past OSS collaboration experience has a larger coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (impact) for forming positive evaluation relationships than homophily in nationality. This may be due to the personal nature of the evaluation links in an OSS community. Unlike the well studied collaboration links derived from two developers' joint participations of the same OSS project(s), a positive evaluation link is initiated directly by a developer towards another. This dyadic interaction implies the evaluator has certain level of knowledge for the evaluatee, indicating that they already or are likely to form various personal relationships.

We then discuss the implications of the above <sup>fi</sup>ndings for individual OSS developers and group leaders (i.e., project managers) and offer a set of suggestions for managing positive evaluation relationship, building good reputations, and thereby improving group performance. For an individual developer, the major motivation for attracting more positive evaluations is to build good reputation. To this end, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that the key is to increase the visibility of her development capability, programming skill, and previous success to other community members. For an OSS group leader, her major goal is to increase group performance through building a positive development environment. Such an environment can 1) attract more participation through developers' desires to gain good reputations and thus increase the projects' development capabilities, and 2) facilitate ef<sup>fi</sup>cient collaborations. To achieve the above goals, we offer suggestions based on the following factors:

Preferential attachment factors: The developers with good reputations are often experienced core members of OSS project groups. In order to sustain and better motivate their continuous contributions, we suggest the leaders to provide better exposures of their achievements to community members outside their groups, thereby attracting more participations and facilitating more positive evaluations. This virtuous cycle can continuously enhance core members' reputations and attract participations. Such promotion efforts can also attract developers whose main participation motivation is to gain community status. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we suggest the following managerial actions to promote core members' visibilities: 1) advertising core members' pro<sup>fi</sup>le web pages in project websites, 2) organizing online seminars hosted by these members for attracting more participations and collaborations, and 3) encouraging core members to use all available media, such as social networking websites to share their ideas and knowledge with other community members. Homophily: We suggest group leaders to provide more interaction opportunities for project members with similar characteristics, aiming to build a positive development environment. Such opportunities include: 1) organizing group activities in the cities where most project members lived in, 2) organizing seminars for the primary project programming languages, and 3) assigning group tasks to developers with similar homophily characteristics. Shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations: We suggest OSS group leaders to infer their group members' past af<sup>fi</sup>liations from these members' project participation information, and assign tasks to af<sup>fi</sup>liated developers who may develop closer personal relationships.

## 6. Conclusions

Although there have been a fair amount of OSS studies analyzing developers' collaboration relationships derived from their joint project participations, the more direct and explicit evaluation relationship is largely ignored. In this research, we empirically examined the impacts of three types of social network related factors on how OSS developers choose to positively evaluate each other: 1) the developer degree, 2) homophily in country, location, programming language, and community status, 3) share af<sup>fi</sup>liations through past projects and mutual acquaintance. The results show that they are all determinants of positive evaluation links. The three research hypotheses H1a, H2, and H3 were supported.

One surprising <sup>fi</sup>nding is that two accumulative advantage factors – the OSS and coding experiences of the evaluatees do not directly affect developers' positive evaluation choices. We suggest that they do not directly contribute the buildup of an individual's reputation in the Ohloh community due to the information availability issue: the information of these two factors is not available in Ohloh web pages. Newly joined community members largely depend on explicit information when making their positive evaluation decisions on others. Therefore, it is important to promote the visibilities of experienced developers through various managerial actions and information technologies.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst research studying the OSS developers' positive evaluation links and reputation management. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings lead to a richer understanding of how social and technical factors embedded in a self-organizing, collaborative network affect OSS developers' positive evaluation choices. We also offer a set of suggestions to individual OSS developers in increasing their likelihood to receive positive evaluations and subsequently building better reputations, and to OSS project managers in inducing more positive evaluations among their project members through 1) improving core members' community visibility, 2) providing more interaction opportunities to developers with similar homophily characteristics, and 3) assigning group tasks to developers with shared af<sup>fi</sup>liations. We suggest that the increasing positive evaluations can lead to trustful interpersonal relationships and cultivate a friendly and collaborative environment for OSS project group members, thereby improving group performance and project success.

For future research, we plan to 1) empirically validate the effectiveness of our suggestions for inducing positive evaluations and building better community reputations, 2) investigate positive evaluation relationships from other OSS data sources to examine more possible determinants, and 3) developing computational mechanisms or collaborative information systems to support reputation management in OSS communities.
