# Brand extension of online technology products: Evidence from search engine to virtual communities and online news

Peijian Song <sup>a</sup>, Cheng Zhang <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Yunjie(Calvin) Xu <sup>b,c</sup>, Lihua Huang <sup>b</sup>

## a b s t r a c t

Brand extension has been a subject of increasing interest and scholarly investigation for researchers in over a decade; however, little research has been directed toward understanding brand extension in the online context. The current study examines brand extension of online products and tries to <sup>fi</sup>nd the key factors that determine the popularity of extended products in the online channels. Drawing upon the categorization theory, we propose a research model and validate it in the context of a search engine's brand extension to a virtual community service and an online news service. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that perceived quality of a parent brand can be transferred to its extensions. Moreover, the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t between the parent brand and a new product positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived quality of the extension. This study also <sup>fi</sup>nds that, in the online environment, the perceived tie between the existing product and the extension positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences perceived quality of the extension. This research advances our understanding of the usage behavior between technology products sharing the same brand and provides insights into the marketing promotion strategies for IT products in electronic markets.

## 1. Introduction

After achieving success in one market, online <sup>fi</sup>rms often launch new products to capture new markets. To avoid the high risk of failure associated with the new product introduction, <sup>fi</sup>rms often exploit consumers' recognition of previously introduced brands to facilitate the entrance of the new products. For example, following its great success in the search engine market, Google launched the email and instant messenger service; while after its success in its web portal service, Yahoo launched the instant messenger service. Table 1 illustrates additional examples of such online brand extensions. This phenomenon poses an interesting question for online information technology (IT) product providers: How do consumers evaluate the brand extension of online technology products?

A brand is a name and/or symbol that uniquely identi<sup>fi</sup>es the products or services of the seller and differentiates them from those of its competitors [1]. The widespread acceptance of the Internet and electronic commerce has given rise to popular IT brand names such as eBay, Google, and Yahoo. As pure Internet <sup>fi</sup>rms, they have risen to become the top 100 global brands according to the Interbrand report [10]. Brand is a precious asset to such <sup>fi</sup>rms because the consumers' experiences with the established product, which provide consumers with the relevant information about the new product, could help reduce the uncertainties and perceived risks associated with the newly launched products. Therefore, when IT is not just a technology but also a product and when technology users are also consumers, the brand image of a technology product could be a salient determinant for technology acceptance.

Brand extension is the use of established brand names to enter new product categories or classes [42]. Indeed, brand extension is of particular relevance to online IT service products. The key asset and indicator of the success of an online IT product is its traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Firms are investing more money into online advertising to attract traf<sup>fi</sup>c. For example, according to the Interactive Advertising Bureau, expenses on online advertisements in 2008 had risen to \$23.4 billion in the US [71]. Another study, conducted by the European Interactive Advertising Association, found 70% of advertisers claim their allocated online ad spend is set to rise in 2009 [48]. Therefore, taking advantage of the existing traf<sup>fi</sup>c to introduce a new product represents a critical opportunity for <sup>fi</sup>rms. The understanding of how consumers' attitudes towards one IT product in<sup>fl</sup>uence their usage of the extension helps managers to better achieve the synergy of their online products.

Brand extension has been a subject of increasing interest and scholarly investigation for marketing researchers in over a decade; however, little research has been directed toward understanding brand extension in the online context. Kling and Smith [44] have warned about the limitations of current research on consumer attitudes regarding brand extension, stating that “in this area, concerns about external validity have taken a back seat to those about internal validity” [44]. Most previous studies have failed to take into account important background factors that could signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the generalizability of their <sup>fi</sup>ndings. As a marketing channel, the Internet has unique characteristics that are different from traditional marketing channels. For example, because of its extremely rapid transmission speed, the Internet is capable of delivering information products in minutes or even seconds. Such an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient delivery scheme is almost impossible with traditional channels [54]. The hyperlink is another distinguishing feature for online channels. The worldwide web enables vendors to easily associate one online service with another by creating hyperlinks. However, in traditional channels, the connectivity between products is relatively low. As is well documented, the online channel's unique characteristics have implications for developing and managing online brands [20,36]. Experience with this interactive medium also has shown that simply replicating traditional marketing efforts online is at least inadequate [49]. Thus, the current study is a response to the call of Kling and Smith [44] for more studies on the background factors and to make observations on online brand extension, as well as tries to <sup>fi</sup>nd the key factors that determine the popularity of extended products in the online channels.

Besides the introduction of the brand extension effect, this study also tries to bridge the gap in the study of the usage behavior between different systems, which has not yet been explored in the information systems (IS) literature. The notion of system usage has played a central role in IS research since the 1970s [9,14,47]. Many researchers have studied the antecedents of usage [16,25,26,67]. Over time, the <sup>fi</sup>eld has gradually progressed toward a general model of these antecedents [69]. Unfortunately, most research has examined usage behavior of different products in isolation. Rogers [57] pointed out that people should not view innovation in isolation. Instead, innovation can be considered as a bundle of interrelated technologies. The acceptance of one technology product may depend on the acceptance or usage of other related technology products. Although researchers have examined the relationships of system usage between different time periods [43,68] and different tasks [3], empirical research on the relationship of usage between different systems is scarce. Thus, an empirical study in this area complements the current understanding.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. The next section introduces the theoretical background and the research hypotheses, drawing on the categorization theory as the primary theoretical underpinning to explain the IT brand extension effect. The third section describes the research methodology used to test the research model. The fourth section presents the results of data analyses. The paper closes with a discussion of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings and implications, their limitations, and directions for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and research hypothesis

## 2.1. Categorization theory

Categorization is the process by which objects are recognized, differentiated and understood. The term implies that objects are grouped into categories, usually for some speci<sup>fi</sup>c purpose. The use of categories allows people to structure and simplify their world so that they can function more effectively in a complicated environment [51]. The categorization theory has enjoyed a rich tradition of empirical research in the social psychology [30,58], and marketing disciplines [12,62,63]. Some researchers have employed the categorization theory to examine consumers' attitudes toward brand extensions [2,12]. The validity of the categorization theory has been empirically con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in different marketing contexts. There are three reasons why the categorization theory is relevant to understanding the brand extension of online IT products. First, categorization is fundamental in human inference, decision-making and in all kinds of environmental interactions. Second, when one <sup>fi</sup>rm uses the existing brand to enter another product category, the extended products are viewed as new instances which are more or less similar to the existing products of the parent brand. If they are similar, consumers would naturally group the extended products and the existing products together. This, in fact, is the basic premise of the categorization theory. Furthermore, in the online context, because of the widespread utilization of hyperlinks, online <sup>fi</sup>rms can employ the existing products as a platform to promote the newly extended products [65]. Observing the links between products, consumers are more likely to associate extended products with existing products.

The categorization theory suggests that evaluations toward brand extensions or extended products can be formed in at least two ways [2,12]. In one way, if the extension is viewed as dissimilar to the existing products, an evaluation is computed from speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the extension, and the extension evaluation is a function of inferred brand beliefs and their evaluative importance. This process has been termed “analytical,” “piecemeal,” or “computational.” A second way relies on a categorization process to form an evaluation. If a new instance (e.g., brand extension) is viewed as belonging to a previously-de<sup>fi</sup>ned category (e.g., a brand), the evaluation associated with that category can be transferred to the new instance [12]. Consequently, the evaluation of the extension is a function of the overall evaluation toward the parent brand.

Fisker and Pavelchak [30] proposed a two-step process for the evaluation of categories. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step involves an attempt to match the new object with a current category. If the categorization is successful (i.e., if there is a match), a categorization process is evoked and the traits associated with the category are applied to the new object, which completes the evaluation process. If there is a poor match, a piecemeal process is evoked and the attitude is computed through a weighted combination of attributes. Smith et al. [58] also proposed a two-step model for judging whether a concept or object is a member of a category. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, an attempt is made to match the features of the category with the features of the object. When either a clear match or a clear mismatch emerges, a categorization process is evoked. When some features match and some do not, a piecemeal process is necessary. During the second stage, a more careful comparison of the de<sup>fi</sup>ning features of the category is made to determine whether or not the object is a member of the category. Therefore, the piecemeal process and categorization process should not be assumed to be mutually exclusive. Indeed, they are often combined in one cognitive process [30,58].

## 2.2. Research hypotheses

Perceived quality is the global assessment of a consumer's judgment about the superiority or excellence of a product [37,73]. Before encountering a brand extension, consumers may already possess an established evaluation toward the parent brand. As the extended product shares the same brand namesake with existing products, consumers will evaluate it on the basis of their evaluation toward the parent brand, and this evaluation toward the parent brand will be transferred to the extended product. In a market with imperfect and asymmetrical information, when consumers are uncertain about a product's attributes, its brand helps signal product attributes and image credibly. The reduced uncertainty lowers the information costs and risk perception of consumers, thus increasing their expected utility and evaluation toward the product [29]. Therefore, the impact of perceived quality of a parent brand on the perceived quality of an extension is positive. If the brand is associated with high quality, the extension bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from that relationship. If it is associated with low quality, the extension is harmed. The impact of the perceived quality of a parent brand on the perceived quality of extended product has been extensively tested in a variety of contexts [2,11,19,70]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1. A higher perceived quality of parent brand is associated with a higher perceived quality of the extension.

Brands and product categories are conceptualized as cognitive categories in consumer memory [12]. A brand extension in a new product category can be more or less similar to the brand and its existing products. Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the extension's perceived similarity to the parent brand primarily on dimensions such as product category and attributes [52]. If consumers perceive a <sup>fi</sup>t between the original and the extended product, the new extension will be more easily matched with the original category and the corresponding categorization process will ensue. Therefore, with category-based processing, the relevant perceptions of the parent brand would be transferred to the new brand extensions. When the <sup>fi</sup>t is poor, piecemeal processing will be evoked, and the attitude will be computed from the speci<sup>fi</sup>c attributes of the extension. Consumers will review the information available from tryout experiences, wordof-mouth and other sources, evaluate each piece of information separately, and through an attribute integration process, arrive at a <sup>fi</sup>nal judgment [62]. When the <sup>fi</sup>t is poor, consumers may even question the <sup>fi</sup>rm's ability to launch the extension product [23,52]. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2. The perceived fit between the parent brand and the extension has a positive association with the perceived quality of the extension.

Perceived tie is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the strength of perceivable interactions between different products [61]. The convenience of hyperlinks makes the existing online product an important platform for promoting a new product [65]. In traditional channels, vendors may not be able to use an existing product to promote a new extended product easily. Online products have unique advantages. For example, if consumers are using the Google search engine, they could easily access the Gmail service with one click. Connectivity provides a cue to the interaction between products since the high connectivity in an online channel will greatly facilitate the perception of a tie. If consumers perceive a close tie between the original and the extended product, they will perceive the two products as a coherent group in which the products are bonded together [60,61]. Consequently, the new extended product and the existing product will be more easily grouped within the same category, evoking a categorization process that leads to transferring brand perception to the new brand extension. On the other hand, if the perceived tie is low, consumers may question the rationale behind the <sup>fi</sup>rm's launching of the extension. This situation leads to the following hypothesis:

H3. The perceived tie between the existing product and the extension has a direct positive association with the perceived quality of the extension.

An overall brand evaluation may be stored and retrieved in memory separately from speci<sup>fi</sup>c attribute information. The perceived quality of the parent brand can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and the perceived tie through the retrieval of memory [13,33]. If the perceived quality of the parent brand is positive, consumers may expect a <sup>fi</sup>rm to have more skills and capability to launch a new service. Consequently, the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and the perceived tie will be higher. If the perceived quality of the parent brand is negative, consumers may question the ability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to launch the extended services; therefore, the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived tie will be lower. Thus:

H4. The perceived quality of a parent brand has a positive association with the perceived fit.

H5. The perceived quality of a parent brand has a positive association with the perceived tie.

For products, product quality is an important determinant of attitude [2,38]. Attitude is a psychological tendency that is expressed by evaluating a particular entity with some degree of favor or disfavor [22,28,59]. Attitude has been regarded as an important predictor of IS usage behavior [25,41,66,74]. According to the theory of reasoned action (TRA), behavior is a function of attitude which re<sup>fl</sup>ects a combination of evaluative judgments and feelings towards performing a particular behavior [4]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

H6. The perceived quality of the extension is positively associated with the usage behavior toward the extension.

Fig. 1 illustrates the research model and basic hypotheses. In order to highlight the value added by our proposed model, it is instructive to compare it with the technology acceptance model (TAM). Our model is comparable to TAM on two accounts: (1) it employs individual cognitive factors for predicting IT usage and (2) it re<sup>fl</sup>ects the beliefevaluation-intention causality characteristic of most IT usage theories.

However, the proposed model is different from TAM on at least two major counts. First, our model proposes brand as an important determinant for technology acceptance, a relationship that has not yet been explored. As we state in the manuscript, when IT is not just a technology but also a product, the brand image of a technology product could be a salient determinant for technology acceptance. Brand perspective enriches IT research by providing a new mechanism for user adoption. The second difference from TAM lies in the proposed model's primary focus on two technology products, while TAM focuses on a single technology product. Based on a common set of variables, TAM cannot provide a reasonable explanation for the relationship between the usages of different systems, e.g., users' continued use of one technology product while increasing the use of another. This model can explain this relationship when the extended technology product shares the same brand with an existing product.

## 3. Research design

Regarding methodology, experimental studies have largely dominated scienti<sup>fi</sup>c inquiry on brand extensions [23]. Eagly [27] warns that “if investigators look to only the most obviously relevant research, not only do they miss many potentially useful theoretical ideas, but also they allow their theories to be seriously limited by the constraints of their research paradigms, which often allow only certain processes to be manifest. Theory encapsulated within an experimental paradigm is thus limited in scope.” Therefore, we selected real brand extensions introduced in recent years.

A survey of the users of a Chinese search engine, Baidu.com, was conducted to test our model. Baidu was formally launched in June, 2000, and has dominated the search engine market in China since 2003, despite the competition from alternatives like Google and Yahoo [18]. After establishing its brand name as a search engine, Baidu launched new products. In 2002, Baidu introduced a news portal. In the following year, in December 2003, Baidu launched a virtual community service. There are several reasons why we consider Baidu's virtual community service and online news portal as the targets of our investigation. First, according to the Data Center of China Internet (DCCI), market categorization, search engines, news, and virtual communities belong to different categories. Therefore, news and virtual communities can be treated as new product categories for the search engine [24]. Second, Baidu launched the virtual community and online news services three and <sup>fi</sup>ve years, respectively, after the introduction of its search engine service. This ensures the temporal order of brand extensions. Third, virtual community and online news services are two crucial and commonly used applications in the Internet age. This ensures the availability of respondents and the relevance of our study to both practitioners and end users. Finally, the use of virtual community and online news services are usually volitional. This ensures that there is no in<sup>fl</sup>uence due to organizational mandate to confound user perceptions and behavior.

## 3.1. Instrument development

Theoretical constructs were operationalized using validated items from prior research. Minor changes in the wording were made so as to <sup>fi</sup>t them into the current investigation context of virtual community and online news. In some classical brand extension literatures, researchers have treated “attitude toward extension” and “perceived quality of the extension” as interchangeable terms [2]. In this study, we chose the items from Aaker and Keller [2] and Volckner and Sattler [70] as the measurement for perceived quality of parent brand and perceived quality of extension. Perceived <sup>fi</sup>t was assessed using items from Bottomley and Doyle [11] and Volckner and Satter [70]. For measuring perceived business tie, we used the items from Steward [60,61]. Behavior toward extension is conceptualized in terms of consumers' usage behavior toward the extension. Usage behavior was assessed using items from Kim and Malhotra [43]. All survey items are reported in Appendix A.

## 3.2. Data collection

An online survey, targeted at general search engine users, was conducted. The respondents could <sup>fi</sup>ll in a questionnaire (online or in a Microsoft Word document) regarding their opinions on Baidu's two extended products: one being a virtual community and the other a news portal. In order to attract more respondents to participate in this survey, a lucky draw was offered. All respondents were aware of the 10% chance of winning the lucky draw. The data was collected over two weeks. After discarding responses with incomplete data and inconsistent demographic information, we collected 162 valid responses, which gave us 324 (162×2) observations because each questionnaire evaluated two brand extensions.

## 4. Data analysis and results

Table 2 re<sup>fl</sup>ects the demographics of the respondents. To better judge the sampling bias, we also listed the demographics of Chinese Internet users for comparison. The majority of our respondents were males. The gender distribution of the respondents was similar to the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the China Network Information Center. The percentage of respondents with higher education levels was larger in our study, suggesting that the search engine was more popular among people with higher education.

To test non-response bias, the procedure recommended by Armstrong and Overton [6] was adopted. This procedure assumes that late respondents in a sample are similar to theoretical non-respondents. Using t-tests to compare early and late respondents based on their demographic characteristics, we found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the two groups. A Mann-Whitney test also revealed no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in their demographic characteristics.

Following the recommendation of Anderson and Gerbing [5], we tested the measurement model <sup>fi</sup>rst before undertaking hypothesis testing. The measurement model was tested for all multi-item constructs to ensure instrument quality. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using LISREL 8.80 to test the convergent and discriminant validity of items [39].

Convergent validity indicates the extent to which the items of a construct are related in actual data, and measures the correlation among items of a given construct. To assess this validity, Anderson and Gerbing [5] suggested three criteria. First, the standardized factor loadings must be statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Second, the composite factor reliability (CFR) and Cronbach's Alpha should be greater than 0.7. Third, the average variance extracted (AVE) of each factor should be greater than 0.5. The results of applying these three criteria to our study are listed in Table 3. It shows that all the standardized factor loadings are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant; the CFRs and Cronbach's Alphas are greater than 0.7; and that AVEs are all greater than 0.5. Thus the convergent validity was supported.

## 4.2. Structural model

Following the establishment of the measurement model, the structural model was assessed. The proposed model was tested through the structural equations modeling the (SEM) technique with LISREL 8.80. ## 5. Discussion and conclusion

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

The results generally support the hypotheses, indicating that perceived quality of the parent brand can be transferred to a extended product, and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences the perceived quality of the extension. Although some studies utilizing the categorization theory have found that perceived quality of the parent brand and perceived <sup>fi</sup>t are two important drivers for brand extension success [11,70], the current research empirically extends these results to the online environment.

The Internet not only facilitates interactivity and two-way communication between vendors and customers [36], but also facilitates the interactivity between technology products. The Internet enables online service providers to associate an extended product with existing products more easily by creating hyperlinks, thereby leading consumers to perceive a stronger tie between the extended product and existing products in the online environment. This kind of between-product interactivity represents a unique feature of online marketing. Although some studies have examined brand extension in of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels, the current research <sup>fi</sup>nds that simply replicating of<sup>fl</sup>ine marketing efforts online could be inadequate.

## 5.2. Implications

The theoretical implications of this study are multifold. First, this study extends the research of IT acceptance and usage to the transference of usage. The acceptance of one technology product may depend on the acceptance and usage of other related technology products. Although ignored in previous technology acceptance studies, this study shows that the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and tie among technology products play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in subsequent technology adoptions. These variables are particularly useful in the online setting as convenient traf<sup>fi</sup>c-directing mechanisms such as hyperlinks are available. Second, this study introduces a new theoretical perspective of brand extension to IT research. The brand nature of IT products is often ignored in past literature. Therefore, past theories such as TAM might offer only a partial explanation of the acceptance and usage of IT products. The brand nature of IT products suggests that the rich marketing practice of brand equity building can and should be applied in IT product marketing and product line extension. Particularly, in the online context, hyperlinks manifest perceived tie as an important antecedent of the perceived quality of the extension. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of very few that has attempted to investigate the impact of perceived tie on perceived quality of brand extension.

Finally, this study offers a new explanation for the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the bundling of IT service products. The key intuition informing the power of bundling is that the values consumers place on a collection of products typically has a probability distribution with a lower variance per product compared to the values they place on the products individually [7,8]. Brand extension can successfully explain the reduced heterogeneity of evaluation. From the brand extension perspective, if the bundled products share the same brand, consumers' evaluations of various bundled products will be correlated, and the heterogeneity of evaluation for the products in the bundle will be reduced.

Our study also has important implications for business practice. First, this research con<sup>fi</sup>rms the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fi</sup>t on the perceived quality of the brand extension in the online environment. Therefore, to succeed in new online markets, managers must select an extended product appropriately. The appropriate selection of the extended product will directly affect the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and, consequently, consumers' attitudes toward the brand extension. Second, this study demonstrates the important role of perceived tie in the perceived quality of extension. To attract more consumers through brand extension, IT practitioners should focus on the integration between existing and extended products. Nambisan [50] has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three main types of product integration: value-added internal integration, add-on module integration, and data-interface integration. Since value-added internal integration provides the maximum level of functional integration across products, online <sup>fi</sup>rms need to merge the data and functions of the two products seamlessly, and offer additional product features whose functions are based on the coupling between the two products [50]. This can be an effective way to increase the perceived tie, and to induce the adoption of the extended product. Finally, this study proposes a new mechanism to support online business growth: the idea that a multi-product <sup>fi</sup>rm can use the brand name of an established product to promote others. Moreover, increasing the perceived <sup>fi</sup>t and perceived tie can facilitate users adoption of newly-launched products when the same brand names are used.

## 5.3. Limitations and future research

This study is not without limitations, however, and it could be expanded in several ways. First, the survey is based on search engine extension users. Caution is required in generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other online products. Replication of this study in other contexts is necessary before the results can be generalized to other types of products. Second, search engines, online news and virtual communities are complementary products. This study may overestimate the role of brand in the evaluations of the extended products because of the cross network externalities [53,56]. Third, this research chose only the categorization theory as one theoretical perspective to explain the online brand extension. We believe that using other theoretical backgrounds, such as the halo effect [46,72], to explain the overall impact of the parent brand on the extension would be an important topic in future research. Fourth, when entering a new market, companies also can establish a completely new brand to differentiate its new product from an existing product. For instance, Toyota adopted Lexus for the entering high-end market and Google named its photography organization and editing tool as Picasa. It would be valuable to explore users' evaluation of extended products that don't share the same brand name as existing products. Finally, measures of all constructs in this study were collected at one time. However, the investigated constructs were not static. Consumers' reactions to the extended product may have included feedback effects on the original product and its brand [15,32]. Thus, this cross-sectional study may not fully capture the complexity of the phenomenon, limiting the extent to which causality can be inferred. Future research should use longitudinal studies to investigate the dynamics of branding extension, especially in the online environment.

In conclusion, this paper has sought to address an area that has been ignored in research despite the increasing interest in IT adoption and acceptance — namely, the brand nature of technology products. By examining the usage transfer behavior from the brand extension perspective, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the key determinants of brand extension adoption in the online context utilizing a large-scale survey of search engines and its extension users. Our study offers a deeper understanding of usage transfer behavior between technology products of the same brand. Hopefully, the results and discussion herein will promote additional theoretical and empirical investigation aimed at better understanding the drivers of usage behavior in the online environment.
