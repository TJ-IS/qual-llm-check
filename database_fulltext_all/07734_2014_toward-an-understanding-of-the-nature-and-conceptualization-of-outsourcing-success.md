# Toward an understanding of the nature and conceptualization of outsourcing success

Colleen Schwarz \*

## A B S T R A C T

Despite the pervasiveness of outsourcing, many outsourcing ventures have been unable to achieve success. I posit that one explanation for the elusiveness of the achievement of success relates to a lack of consensus regarding what constitutes ‘‘success.’’ This study provides a more complete, multidimensional definition of outsourcing success, which will enable a better understanding of success with a more effective and consistent measure of outsourcing success. The results indicate that practitioners and academics agree on the top criteria used to define success. A quantitative study confirms the conceptualization of outsourcing success as a first-order construct with eight dimensions.

## 1. Introduction

Since the genesis of IT outsourcing in the early 1960s and the growth of business-centric outsourcing in the mid-1980s [27,69], outsourcing has become a ubiquitous phenomenon [11] and the subject of considerable research [8,29]. Gartner estimates that the worldwide IT outsourcing market will reach \$288 billion in 2013, with some markets growing more than 13 percent in 2013 and 2014 [11].

While the outsourcing market is burgeoning, the focus of this work is on Information Technology (IT) outsourcing. IT outsourcing refers to the practice of shifting one or more organizational activities related to IT to an outside firm [8,1,15] and may involve a variety of options, including domestic, offshore, rural, local, and other emerging online providers in the cloud. Despite the pervasiveness of outsourcing, many outsourcing ventures have been unable to achieve the elusive status of success. A PricewaterhouseCoopers report found that 55% of clients reported that they were not realizing the benefits they had expected from outsourcing, yet an overwhelming 81% stated that they would outsource again [2]. This finding indicates that a majority of clients are not experiencing the benefits that they expected to achieve from the outsourcing process. When this occurs, we typically expect that organizations will consider the practice a failure, discontinue it, and search for alternative arrangements. However, in the case of outsourcing, most corporations indicate that they will continue with this practice. While this decision may appear paradoxical, it actually indicates a value proposition. Although clients have experienced the cost of not achieving the desired benefits, when the cost is compared to the benefits they hope to receive in the future, they see value in the outsourcing proposition. Therefore, while clients admit to having failed to experience anticipated benefits from outsourcing, they realize the necessity of the practice and hope to eventually achieve potential benefits as they continue in their quest for a successful outsourcing arrangement. Organizations are willing to pay the cost of working on an outsourcing relationship until they determine how to make it beneficial because they perceive value in it. However, less is known about what changes (if any) are necessary to achieve these benefits from outsourcing.

Without possessing the knowledge regarding how to develop a successful outsourcing arrangement, corporations engaged in outsourcing will experience negative consequences that emerge when a successful outsourcing arrangement cannot be achieved. Many examples exist of organizations that were unable to achieve IT outsourcing success including negative resulting effects. For example, the Indiana Family and Social Services Administration’s 10-year, \$1.6 billion privatization contract with IBM ended when both parties decided to sue each other in May 2010, which left the client with a large bill and no new services [39]. Some organizations have even made the decision to backsource [71,66] after their outsourcing experiences were deemed to be unsuccessful.

I posit that one explanation for the elusiveness of the achievement of success relates to a lack of consensus regarding what constitutes ‘‘success.’’ A survey by the consulting firm KPMG indicates that 72% of their customers either do not have or do not share criteria for measuring IT outsourcing success [25]. Researchers have utilized many factors to predict IT outsourcing success, and a significant amount of research has been undertaken to develop various models to examine the specific factors that predict outsourcing success. Nonetheless, even with a plethora of studies regarding factors that lead to success [3,49,24,34,35,67,32,26], the achievement of IT outsourcing success in organizations has been elusive due to a lack of focus on articulating the dimensions of the outcome, namely success.

This study will discuss a gap in the literature relating to the complex concept of outsourcing success. Specifically, it will provide a contribution to the literature by offering a more comprehensive understanding of the dimensions that comprise outsourcing success. I will begin with a discussion of the gap in the outsourcing literature. My review of the literature reveals that researchers have accepted a fragmented set of the dimensions of success. Because scant research has been untaken to increase our understanding of the multidimensional nature of the IT outsourcing success factor [23], we possess a limited understanding of both success and the mechanisms that drive success. By utilizing a subset of the dimensions of success instead of a more comprehensive view, researchers may omit distinct drivers of certain aspects of success that have been previously overlooked.

While researchers admit that is it extremely difficult to define and measure IT outsourcing success [23,13], it is a primary component necessary for the development of successful outsourcing arrangements. Without adequate knowledge of the dependent variable (outsourcing success), the quest to determine the independent variables leading to success will be inconsistent. If one model employs a particular set of success dimensions while another model employs a different set of success dimensions, it becomes difficult to build a cumulative research stream [44,22,63]. To effectively ground, build, and organize research on outsourcing success, we need a common conceptual platform [19,38] – specifically, a common definition of outsourcing success. This study closes the gap in the literature to provide a more complete, multidimensional definition of outsourcing success that is balanced between the views of clients and vendors and is developed through a triangulation of methods to include clients, vendors, and academics. This nexus of thought enables a better understanding of success and will provide a more effective and consistent measure of outsourcing success. To achieve this objective, I will report on a four-phase approach that includes a triangulation of methods and conceptual development. I will next present an overview of my approach.

## 2. Overview of approach

To achieve the research objective, I utilized a four-phase research approach outlined in Table 1. In Phase 1, I reviewed the extant research that examines outsourcing success, resulting in a framework of the dimensions of success. In Phase 2, I conducted a five-step Delphi study of practitioners and academic experts, resulting in the development of eight dimensions of success. In Phase 3, the results from Phases 1 and 2 were juxtaposed, highlighting how past research has had limited success in incorporating the broad aspects of outsourcing success. I conclude with an empirical investigation of the dimensions, where I investigate the factor structure of the dimensions of success.

Instead of simply utilizing extant literature to inform my development of the dimensions of outsourcing success, I pursue an alternative route by incorporating data from multiple sources – vendors, clients, and academic researchers – to define the dimensions of outsourcing success. Furthermore, I integrate extant knowledge from my review of the literature, thus allowing me to juxtapose existing work with the current thought regarding outsourcing. Utilizing this triangulation of data sources contributes to the literature by providing an alternative lens with which to view success.

This study also provides an updated view of outsourcing success. While previous frameworks have articulated various definitions of success [15,32,14], in the time that has evolved since these frameworks of success were published, outsourcing research has expanded to include additional aspects of outsourcing, including the need for relationship management, thus signaling a need to shift our conceptualization of IT outsourcing success. Moreover, the historical frameworks focused solely upon the perceptions of the client with little input from the vendor’s perspective. Given the emergence of thought about the importance of mutual benefits [45], I postulate that the dimensions of success should evolve to include the perceptions of the vendor and to assess mutual (rather than one-sided) benefits.

With the knowledge that this gap exists in the literature and the need for a more complete, multidimensional definition of outsourcing success, I examined the extant literature. Through a review of the research, I discovered a wide range of dimensions utilized to define outsourcing success. Because researchers have traditionally employed a wide variety of definitions of outsourcing success, there has been a fragmentation of the dimensions of success. This fragmentation has come at a cost, which is, namely, that we have failed to build a cumulative understanding of what constitutes success, and, therefore, axiomatically, what contributes to success.

A parallel concept (namely that of Information Systems success) has traveled a similar conceptual journey to that of outsourcing success. Both concepts went through a period of time in which the included dimensions of success were inconsistent across studies, resulting in a disjointed research stream. In 1992, DeLone and McLean noted that the dependent variable of success employed in many information systems (IS) studies was elusive to define because different researchers had addressed different aspects of success, which made comparisons difficult. Moreover, they proposed that if the research stream continued in this disjointed manner, the prospect of building a cumulative tradition for IS research would be elusive. Thus, they developed an IS success model that provided a basis for the development of a cumulative research tradition in terms of IS success. The DeLone and McLean model of information systems success has since been updated [70], utilized to measure e-commerce success [6], and employed in hundreds of studies [59]. Therefore, I postulate that unifying the literature will enable future research to follow a trajectory similar to what we have observed in the domain of IS success.

Other researchers have echoed this sentiment about the fragmented nature of the dimensions of outsourcing success. For example, Kim and Chung [23] declare that there is no consensus regarding the appropriate measures of the success of IS outsourcing because there has been a lack of studies investigating success in IS outsourcing. This study seeks to close the gap in the literature by determining what constitutes the dimensions of outsourcing success to provide researchers and practitioners with a better understanding of this concept. This focus can be utilized to begin the development of a shared intellectual paradigm [37] in the outsourcing research stream.

## 3. Phase 1: dimension review

The first phase focused upon summarizing extant work on IT outsourcing success to understand how prior research has conceptualized and measured success. Thus, I conducted a systematic search to accumulate a relatively complete census of the relevant outsourcing success literature [68]. The structured review approach used is as follows:

1. Journal search across multiple business disciplines. I began with a search of the term ‘‘outsourcing success’’ utilizing the Business Source Complete database, which includes 1100 scholarly business journals including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and other top journals. The search yielded 382 results. The abstract for each article was reviewed to determine whether the study was about IT outsourcing, with non-relevant studies eliminated from future examination.

2. Backward search. Articles that cited previous work in the articles that I located were also identified using the systematic approach. When I ceased to find new papers to add to the article set, I moved to the next step [68].

3. Analyzing the article set. Each article that investigated IT outsourcing was then examined. To identify the content for examination, I performed a full-text search on each document, electronically searching through each article in the set and then manually highlighting each relevant sentence for subsequent examination. All sentences that included a definition of the term ‘‘success’’ were isolated, eliminating sentence fragments that focused on antecedents (or critical success factors) and instead including those that were focused on dimensions or metrics of outsourcing success. I then transferred the definitions directly to a table, attempting to make sense of the dimension and capturing the richness of the concept [69].<sup>1</sup>

A review of the research revealed a fragmented approach to measuring outsourcing success with no consistency regarding the dimensions of success. Historically, three main frameworks have defined the different dimensions of outsourcing success, namely, the work of Grover et al. [15], Lee and Kim [32], and Goo et al. [55]. In 1996, Grover et al. defined three categories of outsourcing success: technological benefits, strategic benefits, and economic benefits, which were derived from a review of the literature. In 1999, Lee and Kim extended Grover et al’s model of outsourcing success by depicting technological benefits, strategic benefits, and economic benefits as elements of the business perspective of success and by adding a user perspective. However, because IT outsourcing has become more sophisticated and mature, it has been argued that economic benefits no longer constitute an explicit desired outcome of outsourcing success [14,55] and that instead, cost savings are implicitly expected to underlie all benefit categories. Thus, in 2008, Goo et al. defined the three categories of outsourcing success as technological benefits, strategic benefits, and functional benefits – replacing economic benefits from Grover et al’s model with functional benefits.

Thus, I would argue that because IT outsourcing has continued to mature and become essentially a ubiquitous phenomenon [69], the definition of success needs to evolve. During the growth of business-centric outsourcing, corporations focused mainly on benefits to the organization [13]. In IT outsourcing specifically, the organization sought benefits of their IT function in addition to technological benefits gained through outsourcing. However, as organizations began to develop long-term outsourcing arrangements, the focus shifted toward the development of a partnership and an outsourcing relationship. Thus, relationship quality became an important aspect of success [24]. Therefore, through a structured review of the literature, I discovered that the existing success factors can be grouped into four different aspects of success (see Table 2) – technological benefits, benefits to the IT function, benefits to the organization (also referred to as strategic benefits), and relationship quality (also referred to as the user perspective). I posit that these four aspects of outsourcing success represent the studied dimensions of success. I therefore created a framework of the known dimensions of outsourcing success present in the extant literature (using the prescriptions of what constitutes a good framework as presented by Schwarz et al. [57]).

The review of the literature demonstrates the following dimensions of outsourcing success:

\- I define the first dimension, benefits to the organization, as the level of benefit to a company from utilizing outsourcing to execute its strategies and achieve its business goals [8,10]. Achievement of this benefit requires a vendor to be skilled at understanding the link between technology and the desired business results, including the ability to simultaneously implement new systems while enacting change within the organization [10]. These benefits may derive from the outsourcing of routine activities, enabling the organization to focus on its core business [15,67,32,14]. To accomplish this objective, outsourcing may involve the deployment of IT to improve business performance [14,10], the achievement of increased profits, or a decrease in the overall costs of IT [5].

\- I define the second dimension, benefits to the IT function, as the level of benefit to a company from utilizing the expertise of an outsourcing vendor to improve IT as a corporate function [14,10] by making the IT function more efficient and effective [15]. When there is a there is a knowledge gap in the client firm [65], this benefit involves the acquisition of new technical skills and management competencies to improve the performance of an organization’s core IT resources [10]. This dimension is closely aligned with IT improvement [10].

In my review, no previous work has crossed all four domains to measure (or conceptualize) what constitutes the categories of success. Additionally, these dimensions are solely focused on the client and do not take the vendor into consideration. Therefore, I concluded that additional work needs to be conducted to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the constitution of the dimensions of success.

## 4. Phase 2: dimension definition

The results of Phase 1 of this research highlight a lack of consistency in the conceptualization of IT outsourcing success. To facilitate the development of a cumulative research tradition in the outsourcing success research stream, it is necessary to utilize a consistent measure for the dependent variable. Thus, I sought a method to develop a robust conceptualization of this concept with input from multiple experts. To examine the external validity of extant work and incorporate updated views of success, a Delphi study was conducted among experts in IT outsourcing. This study increases our understanding of success with the development of a broader multidimensional conceptual view of success. I will now review the Delphi methodology and the results from Phase 2 of the work of defining the dimensions of success.

## 4.1. Overview of the Delphi methodology

The Delphi method was developed by the RAND Corporation in the 1950s [21] and has become a popular tool utilized in information systems research [43,56] to obtain consensus from a group of experts by using repeated responses of questionnaires in addition to controlled feedback [21,42]. Specifically, I utilized the Delphi method to identify and prioritize the top criteria indicated by IT outsourcing experts and top academic researchers who study IT outsourcing utilize to define IT outsourcing success. Therefore, I selected experts for two distinct panels – an academic panel of IT outsourcing researchers and a practitioner panel of expert practitioners of IT outsourcing. The additional viewpoints of IT outsourcing success from the two panels allow for a balance between the practitioner’s experience with the knowledge of academic experts.

## 4.2. Practitioner expert panel

The first step was the recruitment of a practitioner panel of IT outsourcing experts. The selection of qualified experts constitutes one of the most critical steps in a Delphi study [43,56]. Thus, I did not limit the panel to a specified geographic area or industry, but I sought to broaden my search to locate a set of practitioners who were engaged in the practice of outsourcing and who could be externally validated as experts in outsourcing. Relying on a business-oriented social networking website, I identified and ‘‘connected’’ with individuals whose job experiences display expert-level experience with outsourcing. Based upon the qualifications of each candidate participant, I ‘‘connected’’ with qualified experts and invited the qualified contacts to participate in the study. To clarify that the respondent’s stated external qualifications were appropriate in order for the person to qualify as an expert, I asked a series of follow-up qualifying questions about his/her experience.

If a person’s answers demonstrated that he/she possessed extensive experience with IT outsourcing, then he/she was invited to participate in the survey. To qualify as an expert, the respondent had to hold a high enough position in the organization to be directly involved in managing an outsourcing relationship. This extensive qualification approach ensures that all of the panelists involved in the Delphi study are qualified to be categorized as experts. Additionally, to fill the gap in the literature by including the vendors’ views toward outsourcing success, I included both clients and vendors in my qualification criteria, and both groups were included in the final practitioner expert set.

Although there is wide variation in the recommendations for panel size [47], it is suggested that there be at least 10 people in each panel in a Delphi study [43,52,4]. Because I was certain that participant fatigue and attrition would be an issue with this group of highly experienced participants [20,53,18], I started with 21 practitioner experts in the first survey. As anticipated, participant fatigue and attrition were present with the practitioner expert panel, and 9 practitioner experts completed all of the surveys in the Delphi study.

Of the practitioner experts who completed all of the surveys, many of them were employed at Fortune 500 companies. The respondents were all members of upper management, including a CEO, CIOs, and a Vice President. The respondents worked in a variety of industries including aerospace and defense technology, insurance, upscale retail, financial services, and business consulting and internal auditing. Half of the experts were located in the USA, with the other half located in Europe or India (Table 3).

## 4.3. Academic expert panel

Similar to my approach to assembling the practitioner panel of IT outsourcing experts, my goal with the academic panel of IT outsourcing researchers was to create a panel of experts in the area, namely, the top academic researchers who study IT outsourcing. Therefore, I employed the publish/perish database to determine the top IT outsourcing academic researchers. I then e-mailed them with invitations to participate in the study. I sent out 45 invitations, with 16 academic experts agreeing to participate.

Although I was fairly certain that there would also be participant fatigue and attrition in the academic panel, I did not believe that these factors would be as significant as they would be with the practitioner expert panel. Thus, I began the study with 16 academic experts on the academic panel. Ten academic experts from across the globe completed all the surveys in the Delphi study, meeting the recommended number of expert panel participants [43,52,4].

## 4.4. Delphi study method

I utilized the procedures outlined by Schmidt [56] and Okoli and Pawlowski [43] in the design of the Delphi study. The study involved three general steps: (1) brainstorming a list of the top criteria used to define IT outsourcing success; (2) narrowing down the original list to the most important criteria used to define success; and (3) ranking the list of the top criteria used to define IT outsourcing success.

The Delphi study consisted of five rounds of surveys. For each round, a web-based survey was created and e-mailed to the respondents, and the respondents were given two weeks to complete and submit their thoughts. To participate in a subsequent round, the respondent was required to complete the assessment for the prior round.

In the first phase (brainstorming), I treated experts as individual respondents and did not distinguish between panels, as recommended by Okoli and Pawlowski [43].

## 4.4.1. Questionnaire one

In the first round, my objective was for the experts to list relevant criteria they use to define IT outsourcing success. I utilized an open-ended question, namely, ‘‘What are the top 6 criteria you use to define IT outsourcing success?’’ Following recommendations from Okoli and Pawlowski [43], I requested six criteria to focus the respondent on the most significant criteria, and I left the question completely open-ended to ensure that the task was not cognitively overwhelming. The experts provided 162 criteria, with some responses appearing as duplicates. Therefore, I consolidated the lists (regardless of the panels), removed exact duplicates, and unified the terminology [43]. I grouped the responses and developed 18 consolidated criteria that were used to define IT outsourcing success.<sup>2</sup>

## 4.4.2. Questionnaire two

The purpose of this survey was for the experts to validate my consolidation and rewording of their list of the relevant criteria they use to define outsourcing success. Specifically, I stated, ‘‘If you agree with my assessment and believe that the category descriptions are accurate. . .then you can continue to the next page and the next category. However, if you have any comments about the category description or the item(s) contained in the category, then you can enter your comments in the textbox.’’ Although the majority of the responses were positive, I made minor changes in response to the experts’ feedback. After refining the final version of the consolidated lists, I ended with 19 criteria used to define IT outsourcing success. In the second phase, I treated the experts as two distinct panels: a practitioner expert panel and an academic expert panel.

## 4.4.3. Questionnaire three

Now that an extensive list of criteria to define IT outsourcing success had been developed, the objective of the third survey was to begin narrowing down the criteria to determine the most important criteria utilized to define outsourcing success. I sent the list of criteria to each expert and asked them to select the ten most important criteria. Specifically, the third-round question was, ‘‘Select the top ten most important criteria you use to define IT outsourcing success.’’ For each panel, I retained the factors that were selected by more than half of the experts on that panel. Thus, I narrowed the list down to 11 criteria for the practitioner expert panel and to 9 criteria for the academic expert panel.

## 4.4.4. Questionnaire four

My intent with this questionnaire was to ask the experts in each panel to continually rank the criteria until a consensus was reached regarding the most important criteria used to define outsourcing success. In the fourth round, I asked the experts in each panel to rank the criteria on their pared-down lists. Specifically, I asked the experts in each panel to ‘‘click and drag the statements to rank the most important criteria you use to define IT outsourcing success.’’ I then used Kendall’s W to assess the consensus for each list within each panel. ## 5. Phase 3: conceptual sense-making

Phase 1 provided a review of the extant work on the conceptualization of outsourcing success, and Phase 2 highlighted the practitioners’ and academics’ views of the dimensions of success. Before moving on to an empirical investigation of the eight dimensions that I identified through Phase 2, I next juxtaposed the previous literature with the eight dimensions I defined previously. The intent of this analysis was to examine the overlap of the previous research with the current study. In re-reading the articles from Phase 1, I sought to compare the eight success factors identified in this study to previous conceptualizations of success identified in Phase 1. The results of this juxtaposition were as follows:

1. Twelve articles included three of the eight dimensions in their conceptualization of success, specifically, that the client acquires additional capabilities, the client receives financial benefits, and improved quality [15,34,67,32,23,13,33,51, 30,17,48,62].

2. Two articles included the success dimensions that the client acquires additional capabilities and that the client receives financial benefits [55,5].

3. One article included the success dimensions that the client acquires additional capabilities and improved quality [14].

4. One article included the success dimensions that the client receives financial benefits and improved quality [10].

5. One article included the success dimension that the client receives financial benefits [28].

6. One article included the success dimension that the client acquires additional capabilities [65].

7. One article included the success dimension of mutual satisfaction [39].

8. Four articles did not include any of the eight dimensions in their conceptualizations of success [3,49,24,31].

The analysis of the intersections reveals that while some extant work has examined aspects of the eight dimensions from Phase 2, no work to date has examined all eight of the dimensions that practitioners and academics have agreed constitute the dimensions of outsourcing success. Previous work focused on the client’s acquisition of additional capabilities and improved quality (both of which are technological benefits) in addition to the client receiving financial benefits (a benefit to the organization). While these factors constitute a limited set of the dimensions of IT outsourcing success, this study provides evidence of additional dimensions.

I believe that these findings can be interpreted by the shifts that we see occurring within the field of outsourcing. While clients still seek financial benefits, they also seek a mutually beneficial relationship. This focus on mutual benefits exemplifies the transformation toward the development of a partnership. The new outsourcing arrangement more closely typifies a relationship than a contractual transaction. Because this change has occurred, it becomes necessary to incorporate into our success factors the dimensions that emblematize this shift. For example, flexibility is necessary in a relationship, and outsourcing partners have realized that to be successful, the arrangement should allow the flexibility to accommodate changing circumstances and needs.

Moreover, although clients may seek to develop a partnership with their vendors, they balance contractual requirements within the context of the relationship. Time constitutes an important factor in determining success in addition to SLAs. Thus, because clients still desire to acquire additional capabilities and improved quality, if the product or service is delivered late or service levels are not met, then success will be elusive.

With the eight dimensions completed, we next turn to an empirical study of the conceptualization of the dimensions.

## 6. Phase 4: Empirical study of the conceptualization of the dimensions

In the final phase of the work, I conducted an empirical study to examine the impact of the proposed conceptualization. Specifically, I was interested in understanding if these eight dimensions constitute a first-order construct that can collectively be termed IT outsourcing success. I collected data from IT decision-makers to ascertain their views toward their current outsourcing providers. The data were then analyzed with AMOS (version 20) using the multiple indicators and multiple causes (MIMIC) approach. I will first detail the sample and empirical approach of Phase 4.

## 6.1. Sample

To analyze the conceptualization of outsourcing success, a national survey was conducted to collect data for this study. The population of interest was the principal IT outsourcing decisionmakers in firms that have engaged in outsourcing. I followed a systematic approach in constructing the mailing list for the survey.

First, firms that could serve in the sample were identified. To locate firms, a database of top IT executives, The Directory of Top Computer Executives, was employed as the basis for the sample. The Directory has been utilized in prior publications (e.g., [58,50]) and hence constitutes a reliable source for the sample.

Because I sought a particular target population, specifically, the principal IT outsourcing decision-maker, I created a survey respondent pool of qualified subjects from the database. Because I realized that some of the IT executives in the database would ‘‘not possess the attribute(s) necessary’’ [9] (p. 42) to complete the survey (e.g., they do not engage in outsourcing or would not be considered the principal decision-maker), the use of a survey respondent pool enabled me to pre-qualify the Computer Executives in the database. Therefore, I sent an opt-in message to each of the e-mail addresses listed in the database. By narrowing the e-mail database with this method, I enabled the key IT outsourcing decision-makers to opt in, and I could mitigate any unwanted messages [61,73,40] to recipients not in the target population.

I employed a multi-step process to collect data. First, all the IT executives in the database were sent a personalized prenotification e-mail, offering the recipient an opportunity to ‘‘opt in’’ to the survey respondent pool if they qualify as part of the target population. The respondents indicated their interest in participating by opting in or responding to the e-mail. They were then sent an e-mail with an embedded link that directed them to the web-based survey. There were 157 respondents who qualified for the study and ‘‘opted in’’ to the survey respondent pool.

## 7. Discussion

This study presents a set of the dimensions of IT outsourcing success that clients, vendors, and academic researchers conclude constitute the top criteria used to define IT outsourcing success.

The results from the Delphi study of outsourcing practitioner experts and outsourcing academic experts indicate that the practitioners and academics agree on the top criteria used to define success, namely that the client acquires additional capabilities, the achievement of objectives on time, improved quality, that the client receives financial benefits, that the arrangement allows for flexibility to accommodate changing circumstances/needs, that partners develop a mutually beneficial relationship, mutual satisfaction, and that SLAs (service-level agreements) are met or exceeded. A quantitative survey confirmed these findings.

This study demonstrates that as IT outsourcing evolves, researchers need to shift their focus to a broader set of desired outsourcing outcomes. Cost reduction is typically cited as one of the initial factors that lead a corporation to consider outsourcing. However, benefits no longer constitute the sole desired outcome of outsourcing success [14]; instead, benefit comprises only one of eight dimensions of success. While corporations still desire to achieve financial benefits, they do not want to sacrifice quality or service. With the continuing expansion of outsourcing into new markets, the supply increases, which enables clients to demand a wider range of benefits; therefore, I postulate that clients will continue to expand their desired benefits, expectations, and dimensions of success as long as new vendor markets are developed.

Moreover, partners are not merely interested in the final outcome of the outsourcing arrangement (i.e., financial benefits or achievement of objectives on time), but they desire satisfaction with the process (i.e., allowing for flexibility with changing circumstances or that partners develop a mutually beneficial relationship). Because organizations are increasing the breadth and depth of outsourcing, they do not want to be asked to endure an unsatisfactory outsourcing process even if they may ultimately achieve financial benefits or the on-time delivery of a project. Instead, their evaluation of success includes the process as well as the outcomes.

Another finding of the study is the desire for flexibility to accommodate changing circumstances/needs. In oftentimes complex outsourcing arrangements, unforeseeable events may occur, or the external environment may shift. In organizations with merely transactional contractual arrangements, the parties may be unprepared to address contingencies. However, the findings indicate that clients and vendors have either experienced this need for flexibility or they perceive the necessity of it. This dimension represents a benefit that may never be enacted or tested but could be detrimental if not present. Thus, it represents a dimension of success.

The study indicates that the dimensions of success include the development of a mutually beneficial relationship and mutual satisfaction. While some of the dimensions are focused only on one party (i.e., that the client receives financial benefits), these dimensions demonstrate the inherent level of dependence within an outsourcing arrangement and the importance of developing a partnership to mitigate that risk. Because dependence within an outsourcing relationship is fundamentally high, if only one party is satisfied or only one party achieves benefits from the relationship, then the other party may seek to balance out the relationship by creating the same level of satisfaction or benefits for the other party. Thus, it is beneficial for both parties to seek a successful outsourcing relationship by developing a mutually beneficial relationship in addition to mutual satisfaction.

Thus, each of these dimensions represents important criteria for IT outsourcing success in today’s environment. By utilizing these dimensions of success when studying outsourcing success, we can develop a cumulative research tradition rather than a disjointed collection of studies that purport to study the same concept of success but evaluate the concept by utilizing different measures. Moreover, by utilizing these dimensions of success when studying outsourcing success, researchers can ensure that they are measuring a more complete set of the dimensions of outsourcing success.

## 7.1. Implications for research

The findings of this study provide researchers with a more comprehensive list of the dimensions of outsourcing success. By providing a more complete understanding of this concept, we can ensure that researchers do not adopt an oversimplified view of this complex phenomenon. Because extant studies often employ only a few dimensions of outsourcing success, they do not adequately measure this multi-dimensional concept. However, the findings of this study provide researchers with a more thorough understanding of success. Without a complete view of the dimensions of this multi-dimensional concept, researchers risk finding misleading results by employing an incomplete view of outsourcing success.

Furthermore, because practitioners utilize the findings of these studies to inform their outsourcing decisions, it could lead to a decrease in success when certain dimensions are unknown or excluded. I posit that the dependent variable of outsourcing success is the most important concept in the outsourcing research stream, but the development of a robust and comprehensive view of the dimensions of this concept has been neglected, and each researcher has individually defined this important concept, which has led to a disjointed understanding of this concept. The findings of this study enable researchers to ensure that they adequately account for all of the dimensions of outsourcing success when contributing to this research stream.

Furthermore, this study also enables researchers in this stream to build a cumulative research tradition. With an accurate and complete view of the dimensions of outsourcing success, each researcher will not need to employ only a few unique success dimensions, but researchers can utilize this study to ensure the use of a consistent, comprehensive, and accurate dependent variable that leads to a cumulative research tradition and provides our colleagues-in-practice with insight into how to achieve outsourcing success.

Moreover, this study provides direct evidence of the inconsistencies between practitioners’ beliefs about the dimensions of outsourcing success and academics’ views about the dimensions of outsourcing success. For example, while only practitioners included effective communication between partners, contractual clarity, and that the partners desire to continue the relationship as top criteria used to define IT outsourcing success, only academics stated that the provider achieves financial benefits is a top criterion utilized to define IT outsourcing success. These findings indicate that although academics and practitioners agree upon a majority of the criteria utilized to define IT outsourcing success, some dimensions of success are not yet agreed upon. I suggest that these areas in particular should be investigated first to determine the root of the discrepancy and seek to address it.

Finally, I have articulated a set of dimensions of success derived from a triangulation of sources. However, there is no framework to guide researchers to understand success from a conceptual perspective. As such, I urge other researchers to approach the conceptualization of success by leveraging alternative theoretical perspectives to allow us to better understand the dimensions of success. 3

## 7.2. Implications for practice

These findings offer insights to practitioners by providing them with a set of criteria utilized to define outsourcing success. With practitioners struggling to achieve outsourcing success, knowledge of the concept is crucial. How can one achieve outsourcing success if there is incomplete knowledgeable about the concept? This study provides a more comprehensive view of the dimensions of outsourcing success, which enables practitioners who are engaging in outsourcing to develop more complete metrics to monitor the outsourcing arrangement. If the dimensions of success that were previously unknown are now incorporated into a practitioner’s audit of an outsourcing arrangement either before commencement or at some point during the project, then they can more easily be addressed and rectified, which would lead to higher instances of outsourcing success.

Furthermore, by utilizing a more comprehensive set of the dimensions of outsourcing success to measure the success of an outsourcing arrangement, both clients and vendors can better understand and communicate previously unincorporated aspects of success into their ex-post evaluation, including factors such as the arrangement allows for flexibility to accommodate changing circumstances/needs or mutual satisfaction, which can be difficult to codify in a traditional contract.

With these findings, our colleagues-in-practice can better measure the various dimensions of this important outcome to achieve the elusive goal of outsourcing success.

## 8. Limitations

Although this research uncovers information that will be useful in better understanding the dimensions of IT outsourcing success, certain limitations exist. First, in Phase 4, I focused only on the clients in the IT outsourcing relationship. Input from vendors would provide a more thorough understanding of this concept. While I acknowledge that this is a limitation in Phase 4, the definition of success was derived from the perspectives of both clients and vendors, and I urge other researchers to further investigate the perspectives of vendors as well.

Furthermore, the sample size from the survey was low (n = 106). Although the sample contained enough responses to analyze the data, a larger sample size would enable the testing of groups and extend our knowledge about the dimensions of IT outsourcing success. Nonetheless, the significance of the findings does provide initial empirical support for the dimensionality of the construct.

Because there was not a high enough number of participants in the practitioner panel who completed all 5 rounds of surveys, I was unable to separate the panels into a client panel and a vendor panel. This separate analysis may provide even richer data with which to better understand this multifaceted phenomenon. I urge other researchers to further examine the potential for an alternative panel structure.

Next, participant fatigue and attrition were present in the practitioner panel, as the number of experts who participated in the final rounds of the Delphi study was slightly lower than suggested – 9 experts completed all 5 rounds of surveys instead of the recommended 10 experts. Because a key factor in gathering quality data is ensuring that the respondents are highly qualified, the qualifications of the respondents were of paramount importance [43]. With a group of highly qualified practitioners and academics, participant fatigue and attrition is not unexpected. Moreover, according to Murphy et al. [41], ‘‘There is very little actual empirical evidence on the effect of the number of participants on the reliability or validity of consensus processes’’ (p. 37). Furthermore, although not desirable, the participant fatigue and attrition were present only in the ranking rounds with the panel number dropping to 9 only in the fourth round, which I posit should not significantly impact the results. The full practitioner panel contributed to the brainstorming and clarification rounds of the study.

## 9. Conclusions

IT outsourcing has brought potential benefits in addition to many examples of the great organizational losses associated with this practice. Even with awareness of the potential for failure, the IT outsourcing industry continues to grow, as organizations communicate their desire to engage in IT outsourcing and their determination to decipher a method that enables successful IT outsourcing relationships. Surely, increasing our understanding of IT outsourcing success through determining the dimensions of outsourcing success constitutes an intellectual contribution to the existing research and provides practitioners with knowledge on how to evaluate, measure, and achieve a successful IT outsourcing relationship.

With an understanding of the dimensions of outsourcing success, clients and vendors can better communicate about these dimensions prior to entering into a contract and throughout the relationship. Moreover, by increasing the awareness of the multidimensional nature of this complex concept, clients can more effectively measure the success of an outsourcing arrangement.

With a more complete view of outsourcing success, organizations must now determine how to best evaluate success. This multi-dimensional view of outsourcing success requires a multipronged approach for evaluation with input from multiple stakeholders. For example, management could evaluate whether the outsourcing arrangement enables them to acquire additional capabilities, and the IT department could evaluate whether the outsourcing arrangement provides an improvement in quality [64,28]. This confluence of evaluations provides a more comprehensive depiction of success.

This study presents an alternate view of the dimensions of IT outsourcing success by incorporating input from three groups – clients, vendors, and academic researchers. Furthermore, I have also explored the nature of the relationship between the dimensions and analyzed the conceptualization of outsourcing success.

Without a deeper understanding of the scope of the conceptual boundary of what constitutes ‘‘outsourcing success,’’ we hinder our ability to better understand the multi-stakeholder challenge of managing relationships between vendors and clients. Although we have traditionally viewed ‘‘success’’ from the view of only one party (typically the client), it has often been considered a win/lose phenomenon. However, by broadening our conceptual understanding to account for a stakeholder other than the client, we can begin to understand new outcomes (e.g., a win/win scenario). While prior work focused on how to drive the economic and strategic benefits that derive from outsourcing, these studies do not inform the community about how to engage in non-client outcomes and the impact of these non-client impacts on the vendor.

I propose that in the future, other researchers should embrace a more comprehensive and broader scope of the dimensions of success and employ two-actor models to investigate the drivers of success in addition to the overlap between client and vendor views of success (as well as the trade-offs that derive from both). Thus, driving positive relationships will correlate with economic and strategic achievement.

Furthermore, I encourage other researchers to investigate the dimensions as a multi-faceted set, viewing each dimension as a first-order factor. For conceptual simplicity, I have modeled each as a single item; however, I propose that more work should be performed to utilize the framework to build models that embrace the nuances of each dimension.

By providing insight into the dimensions of IT outsourcing success, this study seeks to positively impact the rate of achieving the elusive goal of IT outsourcing success.
