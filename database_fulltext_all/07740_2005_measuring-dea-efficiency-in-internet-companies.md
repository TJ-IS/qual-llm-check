# Measuring DEA efficiency in Internet companies

Carlos Serrano-Cinca<sup>a,</sup>\*, Yolanda Fuertes-Calle´n<sup>a</sup>, Cecilio Mar-Molinero<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Departamento de Contabilidad y Finanzas, Fac. CC Econo´micas y Empresariales, Universidad de Zaragoza, Gran Vı´a 2, Zaragoza (50.005), Spain

<sup>b</sup> Instituto de Organizacio´n y Control, Universidad Polite´cnica de Catalun˜a, Barcelona, Spain

Received 31 July 2002; accepted 31 August 2003 Available online 27 November 2003

## Abstract

This paper uses Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), a non-parametric approach to the estimation of production functions, in order to assess efficiency in dot com firms. These firms have two objectives: to make an impact in the Internet and to obtain revenues from their activities. For this reason, the outputs have been two: unique visitors—a web metric—and revenues. DEA efficiencies have been obtained under various input/output combinations. A ranking of dot com firms in terms of relative efficiency has been obtained. A method based on multivariate analysis has been proven to be successful at showing the strengths and weaknesses of individual dot com firms. It is shown that there is a relationship between the type of e-business (etailers, search/portal, content/communities), and the way in which efficiency is obtained. The paper suggests a new approach to the problem of deciding which inputs and outputs the model should contain.

Keywords: Efficiency; E-business; Dot com; Principal components analysis; Data Envelopment Analysis; Inputs/outputs selection; Web metrics

## 1. Introduction

The short story of the dot com business sector is full of highs and lows. In the early years of the Internet, their main objective was to be well known; i.e., gaining market share. During those early years, there was little financial information available for specific companies, something that resulted in an extensive use of non-financial information.

Using non-financial information when financial information is not available is not new. Studies on this subject analyze how tangible and intangible assets are associated with share prices and non-market-based value estimates [5]; concentrate on R&D and Capital Markets [26]; focus on assets and stocks prices [23]; focus on value-relevance of non-financial information in the wireless communication industry [4]; and analyze the value-relevance of intangible assets in software capitalization [1].

In the specific case of the dot com business, nonfinancial information normally takes the form of web metrics. These are obtained from Internet traffic measurement reports. The impact of a dot com firm can be assessed, for example, by looking at the number of ‘‘unique visitors’’ who access the web pages, or by the number of ‘‘page hits’’. Some empirical studies have concentrated on the relevance of web metrics on share prices of U.S. companies and web traffic [20]; have asked the question of whether the valuation of dot com firms is in any way different from the valuation of firms in any other emerging industry [11]; have constructed a non-parametric production function that contains page views as an output and web pages, scripts, and certain other web constructs as inputs [3]; and have studied the income of chief executives in relation to performance indicators, some of which are based on web traffic [12].

Achieving a high market share is of paramount importance in any emerging industry. Such considerations as cost control, profitability, income, productivity, or cut-off points take second place. Internet firms have been no exception. In fact, in the search for web impact, some firms paid little attention to expenditure; but, even in emerging industries, there must be a limit to how much expenditure a firm can stand in order to establish itself in the market place. Unable to convert this setting up expenditure into actual income, and not being a viable business proposition, many such firms failed starting in the year 2000. The Internet bubble and the way it bust has been extensively studied [6,13,14,21,34,35]. Web metrics have been used in managerial practice as a tool for valuing dot com firms [3]. The rational is that one of the objectives of dot com firms is to have an impact in the web. ‘‘Unique visitors’’ is one of the ways in which market share (reach) can be measured. However, the number of times that a web page is visited is not sufficient as an indicator. This has to be seen in the context of the effort made to obtain such visits. This is what the paper tries to do.

This paper concentrates on the study of efficiency in dot com firms. An efficient firm is defined here as one that, which with few resources—personnel, expenditure, infrastructures—obtains high levels of output—to be well known, to obtain high income, and so on. Financial information and web metrics are used as inputs and outputs in the assessment of efficiency. Efficiency can be measured in many ways. For example, taking an engineering point of view, it would be necessary to know the best performance that can theoretically be achieved with the given inputs. In business, there is no theoretical maximum that can be used as a benchmark: efficiency is a comparative concept. The paper estimates efficiency using Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA), a nonparametric approach to the empirical estimation of production functions. For an introduction to DEA, see, for example, Refs. [28,25,33].

An important decision in DEA modeling is the selection of inputs and outputs that are included in the specification, as different inputs/outputs combinations will produce different efficiency rankings of firms. A particular DMU may or may not be efficient depending on the selection of inputs and outputs. Decision makers may be reluctant to use a technique that is so sensitive to decisions taken at the modeling stage. The paper suggests a new approach to the problem of deciding which inputs and outputs the model should contain. A series of DEA specifications are contemplated, and the resulting efficiency scores are analyzed using multivariate statistical techniques. There is a further problem with DEA: efficiency is a mere score between 0 and 1 (or between 0% and 100%). Two different DMUs may achieve the same DEA score while, at the same time, being very different: they just take a different route to the achievement of efficiency. The approach proposed here makes it possible to go behind the score and explain what is special about the DMU, revealing the strengths and weaknesses associated with each DMU. A further methodological innovation relates to the ranking of DMUs; we propose a new approach based on principal components scores.

As far as model selection is concerned, we just list a series of inputs and outputs that could contribute to efficiency. Clearly, one has to be careful not to ignore anything relevant in the modeling process, but we do not want redundant information either. The methodology proposed here consists of estimating a wide range of possible models, and visualizing the results by means of Principal Components Analysis (PCA). PCA has the advantage of displaying what is similar and what is different about the various models; it is a data reduction technique, which reveals the dimensionality of the data; and it helps to relate models and DMUs in a very powerful way since we can identify similarities, differences and discordant behavior. This methodology is easy to implement in the form of a DSS, as the approach reveals that model selection is a management decision and not a technical aspect of the analysis.

Not all Internet firms are similar [7]. Some make a living out of advertising; others sell products; and others put the customer in contact with services for a share in revenues. The variety of Internet firms is reflected in the different models of e-business that coexist, such as search and portals, communities, and e-tailers. For any given dot com firm, the various DEA specifications produce different efficiency scores. The assessment of the specifications that show a given firm in the best possible light, and how this is related to the type of e-business, is another objective of this paper. The hypothesis that the type of ebusiness matters is tested in the paper using discriminant analysis.

Two outputs of dot com firms are revenues and unique visitors. When taken separately as outputs, how are efficiencies related? Can it be assumed that a firm that is efficient at obtaining visitors is also efficient at obtaining revenues? How are efficiency in obtaining revenues and efficiency in obtaining visitors related to profitability? The paper tests the hypothesis that they are related.

The paper unfolds as follows. Section 2 concentrates on the analysis of efficiency of dot com firms under various definitions of efficiency. This is followed by an analysis of efficiency scores using multivariate statistical techniques. The relationship between efficiency and profitability, before and after the year 2000 crash, is explored. A concluding section summarizes the findings.

## 2. Efficiency in internet firms

The objective of this section is to analyze the efficiency of dot com firms. In Economics, as in Engineering, the efficiency concept is associated with the ability of producing outputs with given quantities of inputs. For a discussion on this topic, see Ref. [17]. This is the subject of the analysis of Production Functions. Production function estimation can be performed using either parametric or non-parametric methods [18,16]. The non-parametric alternative to the estimation of production functions is DEA. DEA performs multiple comparisons using a linear programming based algorithm. It does not require the form of the function to be specified in advance. Inputs and outputs can be measured in their own units.

For these reasons, DEA has been chosen as an appropriate technical approach to efficiency measurement in the dot com business.

This section starts by describing the data set. It continues with a discussion of model specification, which covers the selection of inputs and outputs that enter the model. It ends by producing DEA efficiency estimates for the various model alternatives.

## 2.1. Data

DEA imposes conditions on the homogeneity of the data [19]. E-business is heterogeneous. It covers many different activities, from consultants to ISPs/ access providers. Some firms, the pure players, conduct all their activities on the Internet; other firms, brick and click, operate only partially in the Internet. Web metrics are not appropriate to assess the performance of all types of business. It has been argued that web metrics plays an important economic role in etail, content/communities, financial news/services, and search/portals [14]. To preserve homogeneity, only three areas of Internet activities have been included: e-tail, content/communities, and search/portals. The remaining area of activity in the list of Ref. [14], financial news/services, was excluded on the grounds that firms that met the conditions to be included in the sample are not pure players. For example, American Express, one of the firms in financial services, operates only very partially in the Internet.

A distinction could be made between firms that sell physical products and those that sell digital products [8]. We made a different distinction following Ref. [14]. The classification used here is based on the dominant type of business in which the firm engages: firms whose business activity is based on advertising—portals or searchers such as Yahoo; and firms that rely on the sale of products or services. Thus, under this definition, an e-tailer obtains revenues from sales, be it physical products as is the case of Amazon, or from digital products as Travelocity. The area of activity in which a firm operates has been taken from Internetstocklist.com. Internetstocklist provides the following definitions:

SEARCH/PORTALS. These are companies that run Web sites designed to be gateways to the

Internet. Most feature news and information organized by category, and all offer Internet search capabilities. Most search/portals rely primarily on advertising revenue, though all are seeking to increase e-commerce revenue.

CONTENT/COMMUNITIES. These are Web sites and networks organized around specific content (sports, politics, stocks, etc.) and personal or professional interests. A few charge membership fees or for premium content- and, like Search/ Portals, all are trying to increase e-commerce revenue- but most live and die by advertising revenue.

E-TAILERS. These companies sell stuff online, to consumers, business, or both. E-tailers include sellers of products like toys, books or computers, as well as services such as downloadable music, plane tickets, hotel rooms, etc. Consumer auction sites also are considered e-tailers.

Web metrics were obtained from Pcdataonline. com, which is today known as ComScore, an Internet Traffic Measurement Service. ComScore claims that its web metrics are based on the Internet navigation habits of a panel of 1.5 million people. The data correspond to the 31st March 2000. This date was preferred to end of year information, which might have been distorted by Christmas holidays. This date has the further advantage that it more or less coincides with the moment when financial information becomes available. Web metrics data collection precedes the collapse of the Internet sector later in the same year 2000. To be coherent, we used financial information relating to that very period, the most relevant set of accounts being that of December 1999. The selection of firms listed in the stock market in April 2002 was an attempt to study only firms that were alive at the date when the paper was written.

To be included in the data set, firms had to meet three conditions: first, to belong to an Internet type of business for which web metrics are available and are relevant for the purposes of this study; second, that financial information is available from the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC); and, third, that they are quoted in the stock exchange in April 2002. A total of 40 firms met all the conditions, of these 5 were search/portal, 19 were e-tailers, and 16 were content/communities. This is a sufficient number in view of the conditions on the minimum amount of data to be included in a DEA analysis [30]. Table 1 shows the ticker of the 40 firms, their Internet address, and their area of activity.

## 2.2. DEA model specification

A question of crucial importance in efficiency models is the selection of inputs and outputs that enter the specification. The issues relating to input and output selection have been discussed in Ref. [29] and in Ref. [22]. The discussion can be summarized as follows. DEA’s non-parametric nature means that there is no clear specification search strategy but the results of the analysis depend on the inputs/outputs included. There are some common sense rules to be followed such as, for example, that the model should not contain redundant information, although it is very difficult to assess when information is redundant. The procedure described below addresses this very problem.

In this study, we define a dot com company as a firm that has staff, assets, and makes expenditure in order to achieve its objectives. These are to obtain market share in the Internet and to obtain revenue. Three inputs have been considered: the number of employees, total assets in dollars, and total operating expenses (also in dollars). The number of employees is a generally accepted input in this type of study. Numbers for the other inputs were obtained from financial statements. We were guided by the relevance, availability and accuracy of the data. Financial statements for dot com firms often offer incomplete information. We first thought of using marketing expenses and R&D as possible inputs, but many firms do not report their values. Thus, total operating expenses was chosen as an input from the profit and loss account.

Total assets was chosen as an input because it includes in its definition the sum of intangible assets, current assets, and fixed assets. A possible alternative would have been to differentiate between these three forms of assets and use them as three possible inputs. However, in this we were limited by the data, as most intangible assets are only shown in the balance sheet as a result of a merger or a takeover. Total assets are also a typical input in DEA studies in other areas such as banking [9].

The outputs considered have been revenues and a web metric. We obtained financial information, as on December 1999, from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, http://www.sec.gov. We next discuss the choice of web metric.

Several web metrics put themselves forward as candidates for inclusion in the model; examples are:

‘‘unique visitors’’ ‘‘page hits’’, ‘‘time spent’’, and ‘‘loyalty’’. Exact definitions for these web metrics can be found in ComScore. ‘‘Page hits’’ is important when the firm obtains revenues from advertising. In our particular case, the web pages of 7 firms out of the 40 did not contain any publicity, and this web metric was discarded from the list of outputs. ‘‘Time spent’’ is relevant for dot com firms whose revenue depends on the time that the user spends in the web site, and this is an unusual way of generating income. ‘‘Loyalty’’ takes into account repeated sales to a particular customer. It is not available for many of the firms in the sample. ‘‘Unique visitors’’, according to Com-Score, ‘‘provides an unduplicated count of all individually identified machines that made a visit to a selected domain during a given analysis period’’. Clearly, in the dot com world, only visitors can become customers, and in order to obtain many customers, it is first necessary to obtain many ‘‘unique visitors’’. It can be argued that this is a general and relevant measure of potential customers, or market penetration. For this reason, it was chosen as an output in the DEA model.

An obvious question to ask is whether the number of unique visitors has an impact on revenue. We calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficient between revenues and unique visitors, and found a value of 0.42. This is positive and significantly different from zero, although is not very high. This means that about 18% (the square of 0.42) of the variance of revenues may be explained by the variance of web metrics, leaving 82% unexplained. This shows that there is a weak relationship between unique visitors and revenues, even if it is not a strong one. A rationalization of this finding would be that the higher the number of visitors, the higher are publicity revenues; and the higher the number of potential customers who buy in the virtual shop. The fact that the correlation coefficient is not very high may just mean that many firms obtain visitors who do not result in revenues. However, we must remember that we are dealing with the original data, and that firms may not be operating efficiently.

We are trying to measure the efficiency in achieving two different objectives: to have an impact in the web, and to obtain revenue. Thus, to achieve a high number of ‘‘unique visitors’’ is taken as an objective of the same magnitude as achieving high revenues. The real question is whether firms that are efficient at generating visits are also efficient at generating revenues. The concept of efficiency will be discussed next, and we will study the way in which efficiency and profitability are related, if any relationship exists. These subjects will be explored in the empirical study.

In summary, the inputs and outputs considered are:

Input A: Number of employees Input B: Total operating expenses (\$000)

Input C: Total assets (\$000) Output 1: Unique visitors (\$000) Output 2: Revenues (\$000).

A requirement of the DEA model is homogeneity. For this reason, all financial data relates to end of year accounts. ‘‘Unique visitors’’ was taken as to the end of March in order to avoid any distortion that might be introduced by the Christmas period.

The model implemented in this study is the inputoriented, constant returns to scale, envelopment form of the DEA [10], which can be written as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Min } \Phi \\ \sum_ {j} \lambda_ {j} x _ {j m} \leq \phi x _ {j _ {0} m}; \quad m = 1, 2, \dots , M \\ \sum_ {j} \lambda_ {j} y _ {j n} \geq y _ {j _ {0} n}; \quad n = 1, 2, \dots , N \\ \lambda_ {j} \geq 0; \quad j = 1, 2, \dots , J \end{array}
$$

where: $\phi$ is the inverse of the DEA efficiency; $x _ { j m }$ is the value of input m for decision making unit $j ; j _ { 0 }$ is the company whose efficiency is being assessed; $y _ { j n }$ is the value of output n for decision making unit j; $\lambda _ { j }$ is a decision variable to be calculated from the data.

The structure of the DEA model (in the envelopment form) is such that there is an equation for every input and output. Calculations for each different input and output are kept apart. This means that stocks and flows can be used at the same time, and that every input and output can be measured in its own units, be it monetary or physical.

A standard way of proceeding is to estimate a model that includes all three inputs and the two outputs in the specification. Since such model contains inputs A, B, and C and outputs 1 and 2, it will be described as model ABC12. It is often interesting to study other combinations of inputs and outputs. We could consider, for example, estimating the model ABC1—which contains only unique visitors as an output; the model ABC2—which contains only revenues as an output; or other specifications. There are two reasons to do this. First, since efficiency results depend on the choice of inputs and outputs, we may wish to assess how the efficiency score for a given company depends on such choice. This may reveal the weaknesses and the strengths of a firm. Second, we may wish to assess which combinations of inputs and outputs are equivalent.

A possible way of proceeding is to estimate many different versions of the model, but some of them may be equivalent in the sense that they will produce equivalent results. In other words, some models will be redundant. The problem of discarding redundant information is known in Statistics as ‘‘data reduction’’. We could have chosen a number of preferred models and then proceed to perform the analysis on a reduced set of results. However, this means an a priori view on what is redundant. Rather than considering simplified specifications in an ad-hoc manner, all possible combinations of inputs and outputs were estimated and the results visualized. In this case, with three inputs and two outputs, there are 21 possible DEA specifications. We preferred to use formal statistical tools, such as PCA, since we wanted to know why models were similar or different. In the case that two models were found to be different, we would be interested in assessing the way in which they differed. This use of PCA, which is an innovation in the practice of DEA, has the advantage of showing strengths and weaknesses for each firm, and clearly displays which ones have discordant behavior (mavericks).

## 2.3. DEA results

The efficiencies of all dot com firms were calculated under each of the 21 specifications. The full list of specifications can be seen in the heading of the columns in Table 2. Table 2 shows, for each of the 40 firms and every specification, the efficiency score achieved. This score has been written in percentage values. It varies between 0% and 100%.

It can be observed that, given a particular firm, efficiency depends on the specification estimated. Visual inspection of Table 2 reveals some interesting features. ASKJ, BOUT, BUYX, LFMN, MQST, ONHN, SWBD, TVLY, UBID, and UPRO are 100% efficient under the complete model, ABC12. But, are there any distinguishing features that are specific to each one of the firms? The answer to this question must be positive, since these firms achieve varied efficiency scores under other models. For example, ASKJ—the Ask Jeeves search engine—is 100% efficient under the full model ABC12, and under the model ABC1, but only 7% efficient under model ABC2. It is clear that this firm is fully efficient when all its aspects are considered, has a strength in obtaining visitors (output 1), but is weak at obtaining revenues (output 2). The converse can be said of, for example, the virtual retailer BUYX. BUYX is 100% efficient under 12 models, all of them containing output 2 (revenues) as an output, but its efficiency drops in models that include only output 1 (visitors).

There are other firms—such as ADBL, MCNS, and TMCS—that achieve very low efficiency scores, with values approaching 0%, under the majority of the models, but that achieve higher efficiency under specific models. This suggests that such firms have specific strengths that are only revealed under given specifications. Such specific features would not have been revealed if only the full model, ABC12, had been estimated.

To reveal the full features of the data in Table 2, visualization techniques based on multivariate statistical analysis will be used. This is the purpose of the next section.

## 3. Multivariate analysis of DEA efficiency scores

The contents of Table 2 are visualized using multivariate statistical analysis techniques in order to reveal the full features of the data. Similarities and differences that exist between firms in terms of the 21 DEA models of efficiency will be revealed using Principal Components Analysis (PCA). The reasons for such similarities and differences will be explored using regression analysis. The section will be completed with some hypothesis tests to assess the relationship between the type of e-business and DEA efficiency. The relationship between efficiency and profitability, before and after the year 2000 crash, will be the subject of Section 3.4.

## 3.1. PCA results

In Table 2, models can be considered as variables and dot com firms as observations. This is, therefore, a multivariate data set with 40 observations on 21 variables. As in any multivariate data set, there might be redundancy in the sense that a smaller data set may account for all the relationships that exist between the observations. It is customary, for this reason, to use standard data reduction techniques in order to try to achieve a more parsimonious data set.

A standard data reduction technique is Principal Components Analysis (PCA). PCA removes redundant information, highlighting hidden features of the data set, and visualizes the main relationships that exist between observations [15]. PCA relies on numerical values; i.e., on the actual DEA efficiency ratings. However, it is well known that actual DEA efficiency ratings are very sensitive to actual values in the data used to calculate them, as given in Table 1. Changes in the data contained in Table 1 will have an impact on DEA efficiencies and, in turn, on the results of PCA analysis. It could be conjectured that, unless they are very large, changes in the values of inputs or outputs may affect the calculated DEA efficiencies of any two companies, but they are unlikely to change the relative ordering of the companies in terms of DEA efficiency. Working with orderings is certainly a way around this problem. This made us think of an alternative approach to data analysis which relies on orderings: Ordinal Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) [24].

MDS is more general than PCA and returns the same results if certain restrictive conditions apply. In the end, we performed the analysis in three different ways: using PCA, using PCA on the ranking, and using MDS. The results returned by PCA and MDS are almost identical, in which case PCA was preferred since it is a better known technique and the results remained unchanged. When PCA was performed on rankings, the results were substantially unchanged. To check this, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated between the ranking of the firms in the first principal component of both analyses, and the result returned was 0.972. The equivalent result under the second component was 0.839. This means that the results of PCA under the original data and under the orderings are almost interchangeable. In what follows, we will just refer to the results of PCA on the efficiency scores given in Table 2.

Three principal components are associated with eigenvalues greater than 1, the usual cut-off point in PCA. The first principal component accounts for 57.29% of the total variance. The second principal component accounts for a further 31.68% of the variance, and the third principal component contributes 6.19%. The first two principal components account, therefore, for just over 88.98% of the variance.

The traditional way of interpreting principal components is to study component loadings. The principal component loading matrix can be seen in Table 3.

In Table 3, we can see the models that weigh highest in each component. All models load with positive sign on the first component. The highest weight is associated with the complete model, ABC12, followed by models with two outputs: BC12, AB12, AC12, A12, B12, and C12. In situations such as this one, the first principal component is normally interpreted as an overall measure of size. In this case, it is easy to attach meaning to this component: it can be associated with an ‘‘overall measure of efficiency’’.

Models ABC1, AC1, BC1, C1, AB1, A1, and B1 are associated with negative loadings. All of them have output 1 (unique visitors) in their definition. Models that contain both outputs in their definition are associated with negligible loadings in the second component. Thus, the second component discriminates between models of efficiency that concentrate on revenues and models of efficiency that concentrate on unique visitors. This component can be labeled as ‘‘revenue-oriented versus market impact oriented’’.

The third principal component is associated with specialization in efficient use of inputs. Positive weights are associated with input B (total operating expenses): models B12, AB12, B2, AB2, B1, BC12, ABC12, and AB1 load positively on this component. Models that do not contain input B load with negative sign: C1, C2, C12, AC1, AC2, AC12, A1, and A12. This component can be labeled ‘‘cost efficiency’’.

The above discussion has been based on the mathematical results. In what follows, such results will be visualized in order to highlight the most salient efficiency characteristics of individual dot com firm.

## 3.2. Efficiency in individual dot com firms

The study of component scores reveals how efficient each dot com company is under each model. Fig. 1 shows the plot of the first and the second component scores. This produces a very good representation of DEA efficiency in the dot com world, since the first two principal components account for 89% of the total variance in the data. When PCA was performed on rankings, the results were substantially unchanged, although the plot was less cluttered. It is certainly nice to see a figure where the points are evenly spread, but working with the original data has the further advantage of clearly showing clusters and mavericks.

Firms are identified in Fig. 1 by means of their ticker. On the extreme right hand side is TVLY, a firm that is 100% efficient under 12 out of the 21 models. On the extreme left hand side is ADBL, a firm that achieves very low efficiency scores under all models. Thus, efficiency increases from left to right in this figure, confirming the interpretation of the first principal component as an overall measure of efficiency.

The second principal component was identified as a contrast between efficiency in the achievement of revenues and efficiency in creating an impact in the Internet. This is exactly what can be seen in Fig. 1. Towards the top right hand side, we find BUYX, UBID, and EGGS, all of them 100% efficient under most models that include output 2 (revenues). Towards the bottom of Fig. 1, on the right hand side, we find ONHN, UPRO, SWBD, LFMN, ASKJ, BOUT, and MQST. They are 100% efficient under most models that include output 1 (unique visitors) in their definition.

![](/api/attachments/AT39YW5C/fulltext/images/aeafd6c7d8e498ab82f2f80676f6e01aa04b4961af38f6b7b23fa5858cf56e68.jpg)  
Fig. 1. Plot of component loadings. PC1 vs. PC2. Firms have been identified by means of their tickers. Oriented vectors for DEA models.

Looking further into the area of e-business into which firms specialize, we see that the top of Fig. 1 appears to be dominated by e-tailers, while contents/ communities and search/portals appear to be located towards the bottom of this figure. The position of the firm in Fig. 1 appears to be related to the type of ebusiness. This conjecture will be tested below.

Fig. 1 also shows a set of directional vectors. These are to be interpreted in the same way that North/South, East/West directions are interpreted in geographical maps. Each vector is associated with a particular model. For example, the vector associated with ABC12, the full model, points towards the right hand side, almost coinciding with the first principal component. This vector points towards the right, indicating that dot com firms that are located towards the extreme right hand side of the first principal component achieve very high DEA efficiency scores under this model. This is certainly the case. If one orders firms in the direction of this vector, their efficiencies under model ABC12 are also roughly in order. At the positive end of this vector, one finds (efficiencies are given in brackets): TVLY (100%), ONHN (100%), and BUYX (100%). At the negative end, one finds TMCS (16%), MCNS (11%), and ADBL (10%). The methodology employed to plot the vector, known as Property Fitting, is regression based; for a discussion of the mathematical model and an application, see Refs. [31,32]. Being regression based, the quality of the fit can be measured by means of the coefficient of determination, $R ^ { 2 }$

Other vectors are to be interpreted in the same way. Very high values of $R ^ { 2 }$ were obtained in each case. These ranged between 0.82 and 0.99. This means that the vectors are a good representation of the way in which efficiency in the model is related to the position in figure. The set of vectors form a fan that opens towards the right hand side of the first principal component. This means that efficiency always increases as the first principal component increases. In other words, an overall ranking of firms in terms of efficiency can be obtained by looking at the first principal component score for each dot com firm. The ranking of firms under DEA is a matter that has created much debate, and many methods have been proposed to do this [2].

The directional vectors associated with the 21 models divide neatly into three clusters. Almost coinciding with the first principal component is a fan that includes: C12, AC12, BC12, B12, ABC12, A12, and AB12. All these models contain both outputs in their definition and clearly measure efficiency in the achievement of both outputs. Towards the top of Fig. 1 is the set of models B2, AB2, A2, BC2, ABC2, AC2, and C2. These models contain only output 2 (revenues). Towards the bottom of the figure, one finds C1, AC1, A1, ABC1, BC1, AB1, and B1. This third set contains the models that include only output 1 (unique visitors) in their definition.

Two observations are in order at this stage. If the angle between two vectors is small, the efficiencies obtained by dot com firms under each model are highly correlated, and the models are largely interchangeable. Also, if the two vectors are orthogonal to each other, the efficiencies of individual firms under each model are independent; i.e. efficiency as obtained from a model has nothing to do with efficiency as obtained from the other model. It can be seen that the fan of vectors that is associated with output 1 is orthogonal with the fan of vectors that is associated with output 2.

It is now possible to answer the question: are those firms that are efficient in terms of generating unique visitors also efficient in terms of generating revenues? The orthogonal position of the directional vectors suggests that this is not the case. This has been confirmed by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficient between efficiencies under models ABC1 and ABC2, both of them being central to their group. The correlation obtained, - 0.014, was not significantly different from zero. It has already been observed that there was a non-zero correlation between revenues and unique visitors. However, this should not be interpreted to mean that firms that are efficient at achieving visits are also efficient at achieving revenues. Actual results and the efficiency in achieving them are two different concepts.

Fig. 1 can be used to assess what are the strengths and weaknesses of individual firms, an issue that was raised earlier in Section 2.3 of this paper. It was then observed that BUYX, ASKJ, ONHN, and UBID achieved 100% efficiency under the full model ABC12, and we asked the question if any differences existed between them in the path that led them to achieve full efficiency. It can be seen in Fig. 1 that BUYX and UBID are located towards the top of the figure, where revenue efficiency is salient (output 2). ASKJ and ONHM, on the other hand, are located towards the bottom of the figure, where unique visitors are prized. All firms are efficient, but they are not equal in terms of the way in which efficiency is achieved.

## 3.3. Area of e-business and efficiency

The 40 dot com firms that have been analyzed can be allocated to three types of e-business: e-tailers, content/communities, and portals/search. Table 1 shows the type of e-business for each dot com firm, according to Internetstocklist. Here we concentrate on the study between the various models of efficiency and the type of e-business.

Firm tickers in Fig. 1 have been replaced by type of e-business and the result has been plotted in Fig. 2. Visual inspection suggests that there are, indeed, differences. E-tailers tend to be towards the top of the figure, while content/communities and portals/ search tend to be towards the bottom of the figure. There appears not to be any clear differences between these two latest categories. This suggests that e-tailers base their efficiency on the achievement of revenues, while the other two groups are more oriented towards obtaining unique visitors. This conjecture was tested using Linear Discriminant Analysis.

These DEA models take a central position in their respective vectorial fans.

The first step was to fit one variable discriminant functions. Each one of the models was taken in turn, and its ability to discriminate was assessed by means of univariate ANOVA analysis. ## 3.4. Efficiency and profitability before and after the year 2000 crash

The bottom line is, of course, profit. In which way is profit related to efficiency? Should firms concentrate in achieving efficiency in obtaining unique visitors or in generating revenues? How are efficiency in obtaining revenues and efficiency in obtaining visitors related to profitability?

Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the number of unique visitors and earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) was found to be - 0.095. This is not significantly different from zero, and would appear to imply that there is no observable relationship between profits and unique visitors. However, even more puzzling is the correlation between revenues and EBIT, - 0.836, which is highly significant, indicating that, in this area of business and in the period studied, the higher the revenues the higher the losses. The sensitivity of these values to influential observations and outliers was tested, and the conclusion remained unchanged. A parallel study with Spearman’s correlation coefficient, which relies on orderings and is less sensitive to discordant behavior, came to the same conclusions. Before the year 2000, the world of dot com companies was a mad one. To see this in perspective, Pearson’s correlation coefficient between revenues and EBIT was calculated for 405 firms belonging to the automotive industry for the same year and was found to be 0.878; this is what one would expect.

What is the relationship between efficiency and profitability? Should firms aim for efficiency in order to achieve profitability? To explore this, correlation coefficients were calculated between efficiencies and profitability (EBIT/Total Assets). Pearson’s correlations were negative, - 0.371 (efficiency in obtaining unique visitors and profitability) and - 0.475 (efficiency in obtaining revenues and profitability), indicating that the higher the efficiency, the lower the profitability. Spearman’s correlation coefficients indicated no relationship between profitability and efficiency.

The more a firm sells, the worse the situation appears to be. However, there is a possible explanation for at least some of these strange findings: creative accounting practices. Manly and Leonard [27] argue that ‘‘some e-commerce firms used questionable accounting practices’’. This may include inflating the figure for revenues since, in the absence of profits, analysts concentrated on revenues and firms were keen to show that their revenues were high. Manly and eLonard [27] list ways in which accounting choice has been exercised by dot coms in order to show increased revenues while leaving profits unchanged. Barter transactions provide an example: two firms that agree to barter advertising space may both record this transaction as an income and as an expense, thus inflating revenues. Besides, when examining the data for individual firms, it was found that margins (sales minus cost of sales) were at times negative: some firms were selling below the cost of purchase. This is a clearly untenable situation in the long-term, and begs the question of how it evolved in later years.

The study was repeated using data from 2 years later, leaving enough time for the effects of the year 2000 crash to vanish from the data. In this case the number of unique visitors relates to May 2002, and financial information to the 2001 accounting year. For this later period, financial information was obtained from the Worldscope data base, and web metrics information from ComScore. The post 2000 data set contained 49 firms of which 30 were portals and 19 were e-tailers. The two samples contained 16 firms in common. These 49 firms were more mature, in the sense that they all had been in existence for at least four years. The findings with the new data set were as follows.

The post-2000 correlation between unique visitors and revenues continued to be positive but stayed low: 0.237 (Pearson). Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the number of unique visitors and profits was found to be - 0.032. The correlation between revenues and profits was no longer significantly different from zero: - 0.100 (Pearson).

As far as efficiencies are concerned, the correlation between profitability and efficiency had changed dramatically. In 2002, the correlation between efficiency in generating revenues and profitability had become positive and significantly different from zero at 0.606 (Pearson). What is now found is that the higher the efficiency at generating revenues, the higher the profitability. The relationship between efficiency in generating unique visitors and profitability remained negative, although no longer significantly different from zero: - 0.156 (Pearson).

## 4. Conclusions

E-commerce has been accused by financial analysts of being a mad world. This is due, at least in part, to its emerging industry nature. Many firms have accepted initial losses in order to position themselves in the Internet market. In fact, before the year 2000 crisis, profitability and revenues were found negatively correlated. This means that non-financial information, as well as financial information, has to be examined in order to judge their performance. In this paper, we have studied one aspect of performance: comparative efficiency. A dot com firm is comparatively efficient if, for given inputs, it cannot be demonstrated that better outputs could have been obtained. Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) has been used to perform the comparisons.

The paper makes a methodological contribution: proposes a new method for model selection in DEA based on multivariate statistical analysis. When faced with a variety of inputs and outputs that could be included in the model, the methodology requires evaluating efficiencies under all possible input/output combinations. It is clear that such methodology produces much redundancy, but also generates valuable information. The matrix of efficiencies by models is then analyzed by means of data reduction techniques, such as Principal Components Analysis (PCA).

This methodology permits the joint graphical representation of models and DMUs, and thus, it makes it possible to explain up to what point two models are equivalent, and if they are not equivalent, why they are not equivalent. The relationship between models and DMUs becomes clarified. By supplementing the representations with the results of Property Fitting—a regression based technique, it is possible to assess why a particular DMU achieves high efficiency scores under some models and low efficiency scores under other models. The choice of model specification becomes a model supported management decision under the framework proposed here.

Data for a sample of 40 firms have been analyzed. Inputs have taken the form of human resources (number of employees), assets, and expenditure. Two very different outputs have been included in the model: a web metric (unique visitors), and revenues obtained. The choice of unique visitors as an output has been based on the grounds that it indicates market penetration, and gives an indication of potential customers.

In the last instance, the aim of a dot com firm is to make a profit. But few do it during the first years of their existence. Most make losses. In our sample, only 5 firms out of the 40 were reporting profits. Some firms may be sacrificing today’s butter to tomorrow’s jam. This would explain the fact that Amazon or other companies were making losses at the time of the study. We concluded that, on the basis of the data used, dot com firms could not be assessed on the grounds of profitability, but we could examine their relative efficiency in the DEA sense with two objectives in mind: to achieve an impact in the market and to obtain high revenues.

Efficiency scores have been calculated for a global model that contains all inputs and outputs. However, we have gone beyond this model, and considered simplifications. In total, 21 combinations of inputs and outputs have been considered as efficiency models and their DEA scores have been obtained. The results have been studied with the tools of multivariate statistical analysis, favoring visual representations.

A picture has been produced that shows both an overall ranking of dot com firms in terms of global DEA efficiency, and the reasons underlying the score obtained. It has been shown that models classify neatly into three groups: models of overall efficiency, models of efficiency that rely on revenues, and models of efficiency that rely on unique visitors. Unique visitors and revenue models have been shown to produce unrelated scores, suggesting that impact in the Internet and revenues is an independent concept.

Three types of e-business have been examined. It has been shown that there are significant differences between e-tailers, and non-e-tailers (portals/search and content/communities), and that such differences are related with the outputs they emphasise in their search for efficiency. E-tailers are more efficient in achieving revenue, while portals/search and content/communities are better at obtaining unique visitors.

The study was repeated using post-year 2000 data. Much had changed as a result of the crisis period. In particular, it was found that after the crisis, efficiency in obtaining revenues was associated with profitability, as one would expect in any mature business.

Efficiency in obtaining unique visitors was found not to be positively associated with profitability in either period. If web metrics serve any useful purpose, it appears that assessing profitability is not one of them. Receiving many unique visitors, and operating efficiently in order to so, is important. However, even more important is to transform visitors into customers, as any shopkeeper knows. This opens new avenues for research using new web metric indicators such as indexes of visitor’s buying power.
