# Sharing Knowledge in Social Q&A Sites: The Unintended Consequences of Extrinsic Motivation

LI ZHAO, BRIAN DETLOR, AND CATHERINE E. CONNELLY

LI ZHAO (Li.Zhao@oulu.fi; corresponding author) is a faculty member in the Department of Information Processing Science at the Faculty of Information Technology and Electrical Engineering at the University of Oulu. He holds a Ph. D. in information systems from McMaster University in Canada. His research interests include knowledge management, security, online games, social networks, social media, and e-marketing. His research has been published in leading journals and conferences in the information systems field.

BRIAN DETLOR (detlorb@mcmaster.ca) is chair and associate professor of information systems at the DeGroote School of Business at McMaster University in Canada. He received his doctoral education at the University of Toronto’s iSchool. His research interests lie in the interaction of users, information, and technology. His scholarly work bridges the fields of information systems (IS) and library and information science (LIS), and he has published extensively in leading journals, conferences, monographs, and textbooks in these fields. His research projects investigate topics such as digital storytelling, information literacy, and the adoption and use of information systems.

CATHERINE E. CONNELLY (connell@mcmaster.ca) is a tier II Canada Research Chair and associate professor of organizational behavior at the DeGroote School of Business at McMaster University. Her research focuses on the attitudes and behaviors of nonstandard workers, employees’ use of communication technologies, and knowledge sharing and hiding in organizations.

ABSTRACT: In order to motivate individuals to share their knowledge in online communities, the use of extrinsic rewards and goals is a typical approach. However, extrinsic motivation may have unintended consequences. Although past studies have examined the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation, no research to date has investigated how extrinsic motivation moderates the impact of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing, or how the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether a member is active or not. Drawing on attribution theory and theory of planned behavior, the study was conducted with data collected from a large social Q&A site consisting of multiple online communities with millions of registered users; the data were analyzed with moderated regression and structural equation modeling. Results show that the effect of enjoyment in helping others on attitude toward knowledge sharing is undermined by virtual organizational rewards, while the effect of knowledge self-efficacy on attitude toward knowledge sharing is undermined by reciprocity. The results also show that the effect of virtual organizational rewards on enjoyment in helping others is contingent upon whether members are active or not. Specifically, for active members, virtual organizational rewards undermine enjoyment in helping others; for inactive members, however, virtual organizational rewards increase enjoyment in helping others. These findings enrich the research on unintended consequences of extrinsic motivation specifically, and the theory of motivation in general. Additionally, these findings provide practical insights on how and when to use extrinsic rewards/goals to motivate individuals to share knowledge in social Q&A sites.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: active involvement, crowding out, extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation, knowledge sharing, online communities, online community participation.

With advances in social computing and online communities, websites exist today that help people share knowledge with others from across the globe [4, 28, 74, 78]. A prominent example is the emergence of social Q&A sites where people ask, answer, and discuss questions regarding common practices or interests [70]. A social Q&A site typically consists of multiple online forums categorized by topics. Participants in each online forum discuss questions and solve problems relating to a particular topic, thereby forming an online community. In the community, participants deepen their knowledge on a topic by interacting with others on an ongoing basis. These online communities are the core parts of a social Q&A site. When these social Q&A sites were first created, they were mainly sustained by participants intrinsic motivation, such as their altruism or enjoyment in helping others [50, 78, 83] or their innate need to feel competence and pride in their ability to answer questions [50, 83]. More recently, operators of social Q&A sites have tried to extrinsically motivate participants to share their knowledge by using virtual points [36] or fostering reciprocity [40]. However, a question that operators have to consider is: does extrinsic motivation have hidden costs or unintended effects? This question is important for information systems (IS) practitioners and academics. In particular, Zwass [84] calls for investigations into the potential unintended effects of extrinsic motivations in online community contexts.

The hidden costs of extrinsic motivation can be divided into three groups: (1) the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation (e.g., extrinsic motivation driven by financial incentives may undermine intrinsic motivations [77, 84]); (2) the direct undermining effect of extrinsic motivation on behavior and/or a person’s attitude or intention to conduct the behavior [35]; and (3) how extrinsic motivation affects, or more specifically moderates, the effect of intrinsic motivation on the behavior and/or attitude toward the behavior.

Most studies on the unintended effect of extrinsic motivation fall within the first group. For instance, Roberts, Hann, and Slaughter [66], Jordan [41], and Wang and Hou [77] examined the direct (undermining) effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation, based on prior studies in social psychology (e.g. [21, 22, 23, 24, 48]). The findings of prior social psychology studies are illustrated in a metaanalysis by Deci, Koestner, and Ryan [24] that combined the findings from 128 laboratory studies of intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation/rewards conducted over the past three decades, showing that most types of extrinsic motivation/rewards undermine intrinsic motivation.

Within the second group, a recent study (Garnefeld, Iseke, and Krebs [35]) found that the motivation of explicit monetary incentives has a negative direct effect on active members’ long-term posting intentions in an online community. All these studies were derived from the work of deCharms [20], who first hypothesized the interaction between extrinsic motivation and intrinsic motivation in affecting behaviors [12]. The author’s hypothesis [20] also suggests that extrinsic motivation may influence the effect of intrinsic motivation on behavior and/or the attitude toward the behavior. This moderation or interaction effect falls into the third group mentioned above.

Further, our literature review revealed inconsistent effects of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing in online communities. These inconsistent effects suggest that there is a need to investigate whether extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing in online communities, although other influences may also contribute to this inconsistency. Based on our literature review, it appears that many studies have investigated knowledge sharing in online communities such as electronic networks of practice [74, 75, 78], open source software communities [28, 30], social Q&A sites [50, 60], Wikipedia [4, 58, 81], and online citizen science [59]. Although it is typically believed that intrinsic motivation, such as enjoyment in helping others, can motivate people to share knowledge with others in online communities, inconsistent results have been found. For example, some studies (e.g., Lou et al. [50]) found a positive impact of “enjoyment in helping others” on knowledge sharing in online communities, whereas Wasko and Faraj [78] found only insignificant results, and Yang and Lai [81] found a negative effect. Yang and Lai [81] adopted the same items (i.e., questions) for the construct as Wasko and Faraj [78], although they used a different construct name; and Lou et al. [50] also used the same items. Although there might be other factors (such as context) leading to these inconsistent results, given the suspected hidden cost of extrinsic motivation mentioned above, there is a need to detect whether extrinsic motivation moderates the impact of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing in online communities.

Our literature review also suggests that many studies have investigated the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation. However, prior studies do not distinguish between active versus inactive users, perhaps because employees in realworld organizations are all assumed to be active, and thus the active/inactive distinction is not regarded as salient in the organizational or experimental context. In social Q&A sites, however, participation is voluntary; participants do not have to be active [63]. The findings of prior studies on the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation may therefore apply exclusively to active participants in social Q&A sites. That is, although extrinsic motivation may undermine intrinsic motivation for active participants, it can actually increase intrinsic motivation for inactive participants in the first place because their intrinsic motivation is low [8]. When extrinsic rewards entice more engagement in an activity, through this process, inactive participants may realize they actually enjoy doing so, thereby increasing their internal interest and intrinsic motivation.

This study investigates two categories of moderation effects: (1) how extrinsic motivation moderates the impact of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites, and (2) how the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether members are active or not. This study is the first to explore how extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites. This study is also the first to examine whether the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation to share knowledge is contingent upon whether members are active or not. By investigating these two categories of moderation effects, the current study attempts to fill gaps in the literature and provide insights for IS practitioners on how to better manage individuals’ intrinsic and extrinsic motivation when promoting knowledge sharing in online communities.

## Conceptual and Theoretical Background

There are two main classes of motivation: extrinsic and intrinsic [68]. Extrinsic motivation relates to the drive to perform a behavior to achieve specific external rewards/goals [26, 76], and intrinsic motivation involves people partaking in an activity for some sort of internal satisfaction such as enjoyment, pride, or challenge [62, 68]. Deci and Ryan [25] further distinguish between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation by emphasizing one’s locus of control: extrinsic motivation is controloriented whereas intrinsic motivation is autonomy-oriented. But they believe there is a continuum between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation. As noted earlier, many studies have found that intrinsic and extrinsic motivation can affect knowledge sharing in different contexts. The intrinsic and extrinsic motivations for knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites are detailed below.

## Intrinsic Motivation for Knowledge Sharing

In social Q&A sites, individuals may help others to solve problems by answering their questions due to the enjoyment obtained from helping others [78], or altruism, in which a person seeks to improve others’ welfare [61]. A psychic reward of helping others [44], enjoyment is regarded as an important intrinsic motivation for answering questions in online communities [51, 78, 82]. When enjoying helping others, an individual has a positive attitude toward sharing his/her knowledge with others [10].

People may also share knowledge with others because helping others solve challenging problems is a way to demonstrate mastery. People who share knowledge with others are said to be intrinsically motivated because they share their knowledge for “the challenge entailed rather than because of external prods, pressures, or rewards” [68, p. 56], and this satisfies their innate psychological needs to feel competent and proud of their knowledge [24]. Succeeding in a challenging task “provides the strongest information” for increasing self-efficacy [71, p. 69], which is concerned not with the actual skills people have, but with perceptions of what people can do with the skills they possess [7]. The more challenging the task is, the more self-efficacy is enhanced. People who answer others’ questions (especially when these questions are challenging) gain confidence in their own abilities and acquire the benefit of increased knowledge self-efficacy [17, 43]. This belief can serve as an intrinsic motivation for knowledge providers to share knowledge with others in online communities [16, 82]. As Gist and Mitchell [38, p. 186] point out, “selfefficacy is an important motivational construct.” Kankanhalli, Tan, and Wei [43, pp. 116, 122] further suggest that “enjoyment in helping others” and “knowledge selfefficacy” are two intrinsic benefits acting as motivators of knowledge sharing, echoed by Kollock [45].

## Extrinsic Motivation for Knowledge Sharing

In an organizational context, employees are extrinsically motivated by rewards such as increased salary, bonuses, and job security to engage in activities desired by organizations [9], such as knowledge sharing [43, 49]. In online Q&A communities, however, these rewards do not exist, so a newer form of reward—virtual points—is used to extrinsically motivate community members to perform desirable behaviors such as knowledge sharing. For example, when a knowledge seeker posts a question, he or she also indicates how many virtual points are promised as a reward to whoever can answer it. When answers are provided, the knowledge seeker awards points, based on the quality of the knowledge that was shared by the provider. These virtual points are useful; they can be redeemed to obtain free services, higher status recognition, or gifts. Different online Q&A communities may have different policies regarding the benefits awarded to members who have earned high scores from sharing knowledge with others [42].

Similar to the physical world where people may pay for someone else’s help or alternatively reciprocate someone else’s help, potential knowledge providers in online Q&A communities may be extrinsically motivated by reciprocal assistance from others [45, 64, 80]. The lead author of this paper witnessed this when he immersed himself in information technology (IT) professional Q&A community sites and observed discussion forums on these sites. A simple indicator of this observation is that questions posted by members who help others tend to have much longer discussion threads. When a member who always answers other people’s questions, posts a question, others in the community actively answer it and participate in the discussion of the posted question [64]. Such an observation is echoed by other researchers who observe online communities (e.g. [45, 64, 80]). These researchers indicate that “anticipated reciprocity” may be an extrinsic motivation that encourages members to help others in their online communities. Kollock [45] acknowledges how various characteristics of online communities, such as a user ID, membership, discussion lists, and ongoing interactions make it possible for reciprocity to happen in these sites.

Thus, reciprocal benefits exist in online Q&A communities. However, reciprocal benefits differ from virtual organizational rewards, such as the use of virtual points described above, though both are extrinsic [34, 77]. Specifically, using Wang and Hou’s [77] description of soft and hard rewards, reciprocal benefits can be considered a “softer” extrinsic reward than virtual organizational rewards in two fundamental ways. First, with virtual organizational rewards, the amount of virtual points is promised and indicated in advance, which acts as a “contract” between the knowledge seeker and potential knowledge provider; thus this reward is a hard reward. However, with respect to reciprocity, there is no firm contract to specify reciprocal benefits; they are based on mutual understanding. Second, the trade based on virtual points is accomplished in one transaction and the price (i.e., virtual points) is paid immediately. This differs with reciprocal benefits, which are not delivered immediately, but in the future.

## Unintended Effects of Extrinsic Motivation

Although both extrinsic motivation and intrinsic motivation can motivate behavior, deCharms [20] notes that they may not combine additively in affecting behavior. Extrinsic motivation may have unintended effects. For example, Frey and Oberholzer-Gee [33] report that in a community in Switzerland, 50.8 percent of the survey respondents initially agreed to have a nuclear waste repository built in their community. Surprisingly, the level of acceptance decreased to 24.6 percent, when monetary compensation was offered to extrinsically motivate the community members. This phenomenon can be explained using attribution theory [20, 39, 46, 79], which is concerned with how individuals interpret events and how these interpretations affect their attitudes and behaviors [39, 79]. Attribution theory addresses the “explanation of effects via the identification of their causes” [46, p. 387] and stipulates that people try to determine why they do what they do. A person may make internal or external attributions based on intrinsic and extrinsic motivations respectively [39]. The effect of extrinsic motivation can be ascribed to two interrelated psychological processes: diminished self-determination and impaired self-esteem.

When individuals are extrinsically motivated by a reward or goal, they perceive it as an external intervention that reduces their self-determination [27, 32]. These individuals would perceive that the locus of control shifts from inside to outside of themselves [67], leading them to become a “pawn” to the external influence [21, p. 105]. When rewards are used to extrinsically motivate people, they may perceive that their own interests are not acknowledged, feeling that their involvement and competence are not appreciated [32]. Eventually, they feel deprived of the chance to display their own interest and involvement [32].

In these two intertwined psychological processes, an individual may still be intrinsically motivated, but may not believe that this intrinsic motivation caused his or her behavior (or attitude toward the behavior) [32]. In extreme cases, even intrinsic motivation may be undermined. Eventually, contrary to expectations based on common sense, hidden costs, or unintended effects of extrinsic motivation occur.

When applying attribution theory to investigate the unintended effect of extrinsic motivation in this study, the context of social Q&A sites should be kept in mind. Specifically, participants face time constraints when answering questions in social Q&A sites because they have their own daily work in the physical world. If they are busy with work or family obligations, it is difficult for a person to share knowledge on these sites, even if they are intrinsically or extrinsically motivated to do so. According to the theory of planned behavior (TPB), time constraints make participants lose certain control over their knowledge sharing behavior [2, 73]. Given this, a participant’s “attitude toward sharing knowledge” more purely and directly reflects the effects of his/her intrinsic and extrinsic motivations for knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites than behavior does. As TPB suggests, an individual’s attitude toward knowledge sharing, together with other factors, consequently influence a person’s knowledge sharing behavior in social Q&A sites. According to TPB/TRA (theory of reasoned action), any other factors that affect behavior do so only indirectly by influencing an individual’s attitude (or norm) [19].

A further consideration stems from the fact that participation in social Q&A online communities is open and voluntary, so people freely decide whether to participate actively or not [63]. As such, participants in social Q&A sites may be active or inactive. However, the literature is lacking in studies that examine the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation on active and inactive members. As such, more research in this area is needed.

## Hypotheses Development

As noted above, the present study investigates two categories of unintended effects of extrinsic motivation in social Q&A sites: (1) how extrinsic motivation moderates the impact of intrinsic motivation on a member’s attitude toward knowledge sharing in an online community, and (2) how the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether members are active or not. Given that these two categories of interaction effects represent two perspectives, it is impossible to put these two categories of interaction effects in a single research model. Thus, we discuss these two categories one by one.

## Extrinsic Motivation Interacts with Intrinsic Motivation to Affect Attitude

## Enjoyment in Helping Others and Virtual Organizational Rewards

As noted above, when individuals are extrinsically motivated by virtual organizational rewards, such as virtual points, to share knowledge in social Q&A sites, these individuals perceive that the locus of control shifts from inside to outside themselves [67]. This shift leads individuals to be more easily influenced by the availability of virtual points [21]. Further, others in social Q&A sites may perceive that those who answer questions only do so as a means to get promised virtual points, and potential knowledge sharers may be aware of these negative perceptions. Influenced by these perceptions, an individual is likely to attribute the knowledge-sharing behavior to the motivation of obtaining virtual points, instead of believing that he or she helps others because he or she enjoys doing so. In other words, a person would not believe his/her knowledge sharing behavior or positive attitude toward the behavior is caused by enjoyment in helping others, thereby undermining the causal relationship between them. Thus, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 1: The motivation to obtain virtual organizational rewards negatively moderates the effect of enjoyment in helping others on an individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge.

## Knowledge Self-Efficacy and Virtual Organizational Rewards

Prior research suggests that individuals are motivated intrinsically to share knowledge with others because “engaging in intellectual pursuits and solving problems is challenging” [78, p. 40]. The action of answering others’ questions to help solve challenging problems satisfies a knowledge provider’s inherent need to feel competent and accomplished. In fact, the more challenging a posted question is, the more self-efficacy is enhanced [6, 71]. In this regard, when an individual is intrinsically motivated to gain knowledge self-efficacy, he or she would prefer to answer more challenging questions. In contrast, when an individual is extrinsically motivated by virtual organizational rewards, such as virtual points, he or she may be inclined to share only very basic information with others simply to obtain the virtual points quickly, despite the fact that doing so is not likely to increase one’s sense of self-efficacy [5, p. 201]. In other words, he or she would answer highly scored, but easy-to-solve questions, in order to maximize the amount of virtual points earned with limited effort. Consequently, the extrinsic motivation to obtain virtual organizational rewards undermines the relationship between knowledge self-efficacy and an individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge. Hence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Hypothesis 2: The motivation to obtain virtual organizational rewards negatively moderates the impact of knowledge self-efficacy on an individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge.

## Enjoyment in Helping Others and Reciprocity

By definition, altruistic acts are committed without any expectation of reciprocal benefits. However, others may question the underlying motivation of such actions, especially if there is a perception that reciprocal assistance is provided for behaving altruistically. As such, negative perceptions of others may influence those who enjoy helping others. The knowledge provider may not believe that his/her behavior or positive attitude toward the behavior is derived from his/her altruism (or enjoyment in helping others). Given the suspicion, the motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits undermines the relationship between enjoyment in helping others and one’s attitude toward sharing knowledge in the social Q&A site. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3: The motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits negatively moderates the effect of enjoyment in helping others on an individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge.

Knowledge Self-Efficacy and Reciprocity

Answering others’ questions in a social Q&A site provides an opportunity for knowledge providers to feel proud of their helpfulness and their abilities [17, 43]. However, if a knowledge provider is extrinsically motivated by reciprocity, knowledge sharing may be constrained by the knowledge provider’s opinion about whether his or her actions will be reciprocated by others. Specifically, an individual who is motivated by reciprocity would consider whether the people he or she helps have the ability to reciprocate that help by answering his/her questions in the future. If that person perceives others as not knowledgeable or less knowledgeable than he or she is, then that person may not even help others since that person perceives those others as not capable of returning the favor down the road. Thus, when an individual is extrinsically motivated by reciprocal assistance from others, even if this individual is capable of answering a challenging question (through which he or she can strengthen knowledge self-efficacy), this individual may not share knowledge if he or she believes that others are not knowledgeable enough to help him/her in return. In an extreme case, the knowledgeable member may leave this social Q&A site entirely, if he or she feels other members are not knowledgeable enough. Hence, extrinsic motivation to obtain reciprocal assistance undermines the causal relationship between knowledge self-efficacy and one’s attitude toward sharing knowledge with others in social Q&A sites. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4: The motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits negatively moderates the effect of an individual’s knowledge self-efficacy on his/her attitude toward sharing knowledge.

The Effect of “Attitude Toward Knowledge Sharing” on “Knowledge Sharing Behavior”

As noted earlier, according to TPB/TRA, people tend to engage in behaviors toward which people have a positive attitude [10, 19, 31]. These theories suggest that in a knowledge sharing context, if people have a positive attitude toward knowledge sharing, then people will carry out knowledge sharing activities. In regard to social Q&A sites, if a community member has a positive attitude toward sharing knowledge with others, then that member is likely to answer others’ questions posted in the online community. This leads to the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 5: An individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge with others is positively related to his/her knowledge sharing behavior in a social Q&A site.

The above-mentioned four interaction effects are shown together in Figure 1 because they have the common criterion variable “attitude toward knowledge sharing” (which consequently affects actual knowledge sharing behavior). In Figure 1, certain direct paths (e.g., the relationships between extrinsic motivations and attitude toward sharing knowledge, and between intrinsic motivations and attitude toward sharing knowledge) are shown by a dotted line. Although these direct effects are discussed in the theory development section earlier, they are not specifically hypothesized because, statistically, the direct paths are already implied in the interaction effects.

## Extrinsic Motivation Interacts with Active Involvement to Affect Intrinsic Motivation

As noted earlier, the use of extrinsic rewards to motivate members in a social Q&A site to share knowledge with others should take into account whether members are active or not.

## Virtual Organizational Rewards Interact with Active Involvement to Affect Enjoyment in Helping Others

When using virtual organizational rewards to motivate active members to share knowledge, they would feel that they are deprived of the chance of displaying their own interest and altruism. In contrast, inactive members’ interest is originally low; therefore, when using virtual point rewards to extrinsically motivate inactive members to share knowledge, they would not feel so strongly that they are deprived of the chance of displaying their own interest. Instead, when an inactive member is extrinsically motivated by virtual organizational rewards, this person may share more knowledge with others than before; this process may allow inactive members to discover that they actually enjoy answering others’ questions and helping others. As Bandura and Schunk [8] point out, most of the activities that an individual enjoys doing might have been of little or no interest to him/her originally. For instance, young children may not innately be interested in playing the piano or solving mathematical equations. However, after a period of involvement, they may discover that they like doing these things; that is, their interest in the activity develops through favorable continued involvement [8]. Thus, virtual organizational rewards may eventually lead inactive members to find the enjoyment in answering questions and helping others. In contrast, inactive members previously had few chances to realize whether they enjoy helping others in the social Q&A site due to inactiveness. Hence, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Reciprocity Interacts with Active Involvement to Affect Knowledge Self-Efficacy

Individuals who actively participate in discussing questions posted on social Q&A sites may do so simply to show others that they are knowledgeable, thus satisfying their innate needs to feel competency and pride in their own abilities and expertise [43]. However, the motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits means that they also expect others to answer their questions. When they expect others to answer their questions, they are implicitly acknowledging that others are more knowledgeable, thereby undermining their own knowledge self-efficacy. In contrast, inactive members’ interest in showing that they are knowledgeable is low (otherwise, they would actively participate in discussing the questions posted in social Q&A sites to get the chance to show their competency and gain pride). Therefore, when inactive members are motivated to obtain reciprocal benefits, they would not feel so strongly that they will lose the chance to show their competency. Instead, when inactive members are motivated by reciprocal benefits to share knowledge, this process may allow them to actually realize that they gain confidence in their ability by answering others’ questions, and thereby experience a sense of internal satisfaction. This internal satisfaction can then help them build intrinsic interest [8]. Thus, the motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits may eventually lead inactive members to engage in knowledge sharing through which they can gain knowledge self-efficacy. This leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 7a: For active members, reciprocity has a negative effect on knowledge self-efficacy.

Hypothesis 7b: For inactive members, reciprocity has a positive effect on knowledge self-efficacy.

Compared with the first category of interaction effects, the second category of interaction effects represents a different perspective, as noted earlier. Thus, it is impossible to put these two categories of interaction effects in the same research model (i.e., in Figure 1). Otherwise, it may distort results and compromise readability. However, the second category of interaction effects does not share a criterion variable; thus, it is not warranted to put these two interaction effects together. Thus, no structural model for the second category of interaction effects is graphed, but each interaction effect is graphed separately in the following data analysis section.

## Research Method

The current research aims to complement prior experimental studies by increasing the relevance of findings by virtue of its field setting. Specifically, the research site was a large social Q&A site in China targeting IT professionals that has millions of registered members. This social Q&A site provides more than ten Internet forums (categorized and named by topics such as “.Net,” Java, WEB programming, PHP, and Database development) where IT professionals can post, answer, and discuss questions relating to these topics. Although members can participate in any of these forums,<sup>1</sup> each forum has a relatively permanent set of participants because each individual’s expertise is typically concentrated in one main area. Over more than ten years, IT professionals in each forum have formed their own Q&A online communities correspondingly. A survey instrument was administered to the registered members of the Q&A online communities that constitute the social Q&A site.

The measures in this study include five self-reported measures and two objective measures. All the self-reported measures were adapted from well-established constructs [11].<sup>2</sup> For example, enjoyment in helping others was adapted from Wasko and Faraj [78] and Kankanhalli, Tan, and Wei [43], and the other intrinsic motivation construct, knowledge self-efficacy, was adapted from Kankanhalli, Tan, and Wei [43]. Virtual organizational reward and reciprocity were adapted from Kankanhalli, Tan, and Wei [43]. Attitude toward knowledge sharing was adopted from Bock et al. [10]. Appendix A provides a thorough description of the five self-reported measures and their sources, and Appendix B illustrates the two objective measures in detail. Appendix C documents how virtual organizational reward was adapted from the “organization reward” construct.

In order to plot the interaction effects, each of the moderation effects was analyzed using moderated regression [69] following the procedures outlined by Aiken and West [1]. The first category of four interaction effects (that share a common criterion variable, see the research model in Figure 1) were then tested simultaneously using partial least squares (PLS), a structural equation modeling (SEM) approach that is capable of estimating interaction effects and multiple criterion variables simultaneously [15]. The results were double-checked using Mplus [56].

## Data Collection and Analysis

## Respondents

Given the online research setting of this study, a Web survey was administered. To recruit participants, a link to the survey was posted on the home page of the social Q&A website under investigation. In total, 968 responses were received. Generally, respondents were well-educated with 61.1 percent indicating that they had a university degree, and 11.9 percent having a “master’s” or “Ph.D/” This is reasonable to expect given that the Q&A online community members are IT professionals. Most respondents had been members of the social Q&A site for a substantial period of time, with tenure in the Q&A online community varying from less than one year to ten years. The years of work experience varied from less than one year to sixteen years. Because the IT profession is male-dominated [47, 55], it is not surprising that most participants were male (93.9 percent).<sup>3</sup> Moreover, statistical analysis was performed to test for nonresponse bias. Time-stamp data collected with the questionnaire were used to divide the responses into three quartiles based on the submission time. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was applied to test the omnibus difference between early and late responses. Wilks’s lambda of 0.931 (with P-value = 0.09) for submission time revealed no omnibus differences between these groups. This result indicates that on average, the earlier responses did not differ from the later ones, and thus nonresponse bias was unlikely to be a serious problem.

Following procedures prescribed by Aiken and West [1], two simple regression lines were plotted (see Figure 2); the intrinsic motivation variable, that is, enjoyment in helping others, was plotted at the values corresponding to –1, 0, and +1 standard deviations from the mean. Interaction effects are very difficult to detect in field studies [54]. As Evans [29] concluded, interaction effects explaining as little as 1 percent of the variance in the criterion variable should be considered important. In Champoux and Peters’s [13] and Chaplin’s [14] reviews, field study interactions typically account for only l–3 percent of the variance in the criterion. ## Discussion

## Discussion of Findings

In what follows, we discuss the findings of this study. First, we discuss the interaction between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation in affecting knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites. We then discuss how the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether members are active or not in these online communities.

## Extrinsic Motivation Interacts with Intrinsic Motivation

Although the results of this study suggest that intrinsic motivation has a positive effect on one’s attitude toward knowledge sharing, this effect is undermined by extrinsic motivation. In the single interaction test, all four moderation effects of extrinsic motivation on the relationship between intrinsic motivation and attitude toward knowledge sharing are significant. In the multiple-interaction test, two moderation effects are still significant, whereas the remaining two moderation effects (which were originally weaker in the single interaction test) are no longer significant, because the shared contribution to the criterion variable has been partialed out. The following are plausible reasons why these two moderation effects are weaker and partialed out.

As a reward, reciprocal benefits are “softer” than virtual organizational rewards, as noted earlier. For virtual organizational rewards, the amount of virtual points is promised and indicated in advance (which acts as a “contract” between the knowledge seeker and potential knowledge provider), whereas reciprocity is based on mutual understanding rather than an explicit contract. Hence, with respect to their undermining effect on the impact of enjoyment in helping others on attitude toward knowledge sharing, it is reasonable to find that reciprocity’s undermining effect is weaker than that of virtual organizational rewards because reciprocal benefits are a “softer” reward than virtual organizational rewards.

However, for the undermining effect on the impact of knowledge self-efficacy on attitude toward knowledge sharing, the undermining effect of reciprocity is stronger than that of virtual organizational rewards, because the expectation to obtain reciprocal benefits is completely opposite to one’s purpose of showing his/her own knowledge self-efficacy, whereas virtual organizational rewards do not have such a connotation relating to self-efficacy. Specifically, the motivation to obtain reciprocal benefits means that an individual also expects others to answer his/her questions. When expecting others to answer his/her questions, this individual is acknowledging that others are more knowledgeable, thereby strongly undermining his/her own knowledge self-efficacy.

## Extrinsic Motivation Interacts with Active Involvement

As noted earlier, participants in social Q&A sites vary considerably in terms of how active they are. This study investigated how the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether members are active or not. As the results from this study show, the interaction effect between virtual organizational reward and active involvement on enjoyment in helping others is significant, whereas the interaction effect between reciprocity and active involvement on knowledge self-efficacy is not. The significant interaction indicates that the effect of virtual organizational rewards on enjoyment in helping others is contingent upon whether members are active or not (i.e., for active members, virtual organizational rewards would undermine enjoyment in helping others; for inactive members, virtual organizational rewards would increase enjoyment in helping others).

One likely explanation for the insignificant moderation effect of active involvement on the effect of reciprocity on knowledge self-efficacy lies in the strong undermining effect of reciprocity. As noted earlier, if a person wishes to receive reciprocal assistance, it implies that others are more knowledgeable than him/her. This request then defeats the person’s aim to show that he/she is more knowledgeable than others (through which he/he gains knowledge self-efficacy). Given that the undermining effect of reciprocity on knowledge self-efficacy is very strong, even for inactive members, reciprocity may not lead them to realize their interest in showing knowledge self-efficacy.

A post hoc analysis: Our data analysis revealed that the relationship between virtual organizational rewards and attitude toward knowledge sharing was not significant, so we conducted a post hoc analysis to test whether this relationship is contingent upon members being active or inactive in the social Q&A site. The result of this test is shown in Appendix I. As depicted in Figure I1, for active members, the motivation to obtain virtual organizational rewards has a negative effect on attitude toward knowledge sharing; for inactive members, the motivation to obtain virtual organizational rewards has a positive effect on attitude toward knowledge sharing. We also tested whether the relationship between reciprocity and attitude toward knowledge sharing is contingent upon whether members are active or inactive in the social Q&A site. As shown in Figure I2, for active members, reciprocity has a negative effect on attitude toward knowledge sharing; for inactive members, reciprocity has a positive effect on attitude toward knowledge sharing. A detailed discussion and results are presented in Appendix I.

## Contributions to the Literature

As mentioned at the beginning of this paper, the hidden costs of extrinsic motivation can be divided into three groups: (1) the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation, (2) the direct undermining effect of extrinsic motivation on the behavior (attitude or intention to conduct the behavior), and (3) how extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on the behavior (or attitude toward the behavior). Most prior studies on the unintended effect of extrinsic motivation (e.g., Roberts, Hann, and Slaughter [66] in IS, Wang and Hou [77], and Jordan [41] in management, and [21, 22, 23, 24, 48] in social psychology) fall within the first group, while one IS study (Garnefeld, Iseke, and Krebs [35]) falls within the second group. The present study has addressed the gap in the literature by investigating the third group of hidden costs of extrinsic motivation. This is the first study to investigate how extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on an individual’s attitude toward knowledge sharing on social Q&A sites. This study has found that the effect of enjoyment in helping others on attitude toward knowledge sharing is undermined by virtual organizational rewards, while the effect of knowledge self-efficacy on attitude toward knowledge sharing is undermined by reciprocity.

Based on attribution theory [39, 46, 79], when an individual is extrinsically motivated by a reward/goal, he or she would perceive such a reward/goal as an external intervention that reduces his or her self-determination [27, 32]. This individual would perceive that the locus of control shifts from inside to outside of himself or herself [67], and may feel that his/her own interest is not acknowledged and his/her involvement and competence are not appreciated [32]. Although the individual may still be an intrinsically motivated person, he/she does not believe that his/her intrinsic motivation caused the behavior or attitude toward the behavior [32]. This attribution undermines the causal relationship between the intrinsic motivation and the behavior or attitude toward the behavior. In extreme cases, even the intrinsic motivation may be undermined.

Although prior studies have investigated the direct effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation (i.e., the first group of hidden costs of extrinsic motivation mentioned above), they do not distinguish between active and inactive users, perhaps because employees in real-world organizations are all assumed to be active, and thus the active/inactive distinction is not regarded as salient in the organizational or experimental context. Therefore, the findings of prior studies regarding this effect may apply exclusively to active users. However, participation in social Q&A sites is voluntary and open, and thus people freely decide whether to actively participate or not. The effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation may differ for active participants versus inactive participants. Research on such a contingency effect is lacking. By addressing this gap, the present study has found that the effect of virtual organizational rewards on enjoyment in helping others is contingent upon whether members are active or not. Specifically, for active members, virtual organizational rewards undermine enjoyment in helping others; for inactive members, however, virtual organizational rewards increase enjoyment in helping others. This is consistent with Bandura and Schunk’s [8] argument that inactive persons could develop intrinsic interest through favorable involvement. Such involvement is initiated by extrinsic rewards instead of intrinsic interest. In so doing, the current study has dug deeper into the effects of extrinsic motivation, and provided a nuanced understanding of the complex interactions between intrinsic and extrinsic motivations for knowledge sharing.

## Implications for Practice

These findings have important implications for people who operate social Q&A sites. When the operators of social Q&A sites extrinsically motivate participants to share knowledge, they assume that extrinsic and intrinsic motivation are additive in motivating participants to share knowledge with others. However, extrinsic motivation may have hidden costs. The findings of this study suggest that virtual organizational rewards undermine the effect of enjoyment in helping others on participants attitude toward knowledge sharing, and reciprocity undermines the effect of knowledge self-efficacy on attitude toward knowledge sharing. Hence, practitioners should be aware of these hidden costs. Extrinsic rewards motivation may satisfy “lowerorder” needs, but it may neglect “higher-order” needs for self-esteem and selfactualization [52, 53]. Operators of social Q&A sites should keep in mind participants’ intrinsic motivation, which are closely related to participants’ higher-order needs for self-esteem and self-actualization [52, 53].

In addition, whether members are active or inactive should be taken into account when operators are considering how to effectively motivate members to share knowledge in social Q&A sites. The findings of this study suggest that the effect of virtual organizational rewards on enjoyment in helping others is contingent upon whether members are active or not: for active members, virtual organizational rewards can undermine enjoyment in helping others, whereas for inactive members, virtual organizational rewards can increase enjoyment in helping others. Therefore, how to use virtual organizational rewards to motivate members depends on the situation of the particular social Q&A site. In reality, some social Q&A sites cannot sustain participation because too many members are not very active. In these kinds of social Q&A sites, operators can use the virtual organizational reward to extrinsically motivate members to share knowledge; virtual organizational rewards can lead members to realize that they actually enjoy answering others’ question and enjoy helping others. In this way, inactive members can consequently become active and such social Q&A sites can survive. Furthermore, some social Q&A sites are newly established and members are inactive because these social Q&A sites are still in an early stage of development and use. In these kinds of social Q&A sites, operators can use virtual organizational rewards to extrinsically motivate members to share their knowledge, thereby helping inactive members realize their interest in helping others. In contrast, some social Q&A sites are mature and successful where a majority of members are generally active. In such social Q&A sites, operators should be aware of the undermining effect of virtual organizational rewards. For example, in cases where most members are active, virtual organizational rewards should be downplayed or eliminated entirely as these virtual point rewards can undermine some active members’ enjoyment in helping others.

## Strengths and Limitations

This work has several strengths that enhance the validity of its findings. This study is based on field data with a large sample size, with hypotheses tests independent of control variables and without serious nonresponse bias. In addition, this study has used two observed measures to mitigate common method bias. Statistical analysis (see Appendix J for details) confirms that common method variance in this study was very limited and the related bias was not a likely contaminant of the results. Despite these strengths, some limitations should be acknowledged.

One limitation is that this field study used cross-sectional data. Like most IS field studies, the current study established causal relationships based on established theory, which was further confirmed with results that were consistent with the theoretical predictions. Although the field data increase its empirical relevance, this study shares the same limitation with other field studies that use cross-sectional data; that is, it could not manipulate predictor variables (i.e., as in laboratory experiments). Given that a field experiment would combine the strengths of field studies and laboratory experiments, future research could use that method to examine how extrinsic motivation interacts with intrinsic motivation in affecting knowledge sharing. Alternatively, a longitudinal study design could also be used in future research. Although longitudinal data alone cannot establish causation either [37], it would help understand how intrinsic/extrinsic motivation develops over time in social Q&A sites. Future research could use longitudinal data to test a mediated model.

It should also be acknowledged that this study was conducted in a particular culture and a specific social Q&A site (although it consists of more than ten Q&A online communities with millions of registered users). Testing across different cultures and different social Q&A sites would enhance the external validity of the results. This could be addressed in future research.

## Future Research

Using the current study as a foundation, several studies could be conducted in the future, such as interaction effects on behavior in experimental settings, and threeway interactions (between extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation, and active involvement).

## Interaction Effects on Behavior in Experimental Settings

For the purposes of comparison, we also tested whether extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing behavior. The result shows that this moderation effect was insignificant. There are three plausible reasons to explain this insignificance. First, based on TPB/TRA, motivations (acting as salient beliefs relevant to knowledge sharing) affect attitude first, and attitude consequently affects behavior [2, 10, 31]. Second, the special characteristics of social Q&A sites makes the influences on knowledge sharing behavior more complicated than the influences on attitude. In particular, participants face time constraints when answering questions in social Q&A communities because they have their own daily work in the physical world. According to TPB, time constraints make the participants lose control over their knowledge sharing behavior [2, 73]. Given this, attitude more purely and directly reflects the effects of motivations than behavior does. Third, the objective measure for knowledge sharing behavior used in this study contains only a single item, and thus has its limitations. This single-item measure might not fully capture all the nuances of knowledge sharing behavior.

Future research that uses comprehensive measures of knowledge sharing behavior can examine the moderation effect of extrinsic motivation on the influence of intrinsic motivation on knowledge sharing behavior. In addition, future research can use an experimental setting to manipulate the “time constraints” factor, and investigate its influence in depth. Comparatively, the online environment is more complicated than the organizational context, while the organizational context is more complicated than lab experiments. This may also help explain why prior studies observed the direct undermining effects of extrinsic motives on intrinsic motivation in lab experiments more often than in real-world organizations.

## Three-Way Interactions

In addition, it would be interesting to examine whether the interaction effects between extrinsic and intrinsic motivations on attitude toward knowledge sharing (i.e., the interaction effects posited in H1–H4) are contingent upon whether members are active or nonactive. This produces a three-way interaction: extrinsic motivation × intrinsic motivation × active involvement. In a post hoc analysis, we tested this possibility, however the results were nonsignificant. Typically, higher order interactions (such as three-way interactions tested here) are extremely difficult to detect in field studies [54]. A much larger sample size is needed to detect three-way interactions in field studies than in experiments. For a nonexperimental field study to have efficiency for assessing the three-way interaction comparable to an experiment with 100 observations requires 6,400 observations (i.e., 64 times as many observations) [54]. Although the sample size in the present field study (n = 968) is large, it is still much below the above requirement (i.e., 6,400). Therefore, future research is necessary to investigate such three-way interactions, in an experimental setting where a much smaller sample size is needed.

## Conclusion

This paper has investigated how extrinsic motivation moderates the effect of intrinsic motivation on an individual’s attitude toward sharing knowledge with others in social Q&A sites, and also investigated whether the effect of extrinsic motivation on intrinsic motivation is contingent upon whether a member is active or not. With data collected from a large social Q&A site consisting of multiple Q&A online communities with millions of registered users, this study shows that the effect of enjoyment in helping others on attitude toward knowledge sharing is undermined mainly by virtual organizational rewards, while the effect of knowledge self-efficacy is undermined mainly by reciprocity. Further, this study reveals that the effect of virtual organizational rewards on enjoyment in helping others is contingent upon whether members are active or inactive. For active members, virtual organizational rewards undermine enjoyment in helping others, whereas for inactive members, virtual organizational rewards increase enjoyment in helping others. The nuanced analysis of this study has painted a more complete picture of how to motivate individuals intrinsically and extrinsically to share knowledge with others in online communities, thereby providing a more accurate guideline to practitioners who are promoting knowledge sharing in social Q&A sites. Given the extensive use of extrinsic rewards in motivating individuals to conduct desired behaviors, such research is certainly warranted.
