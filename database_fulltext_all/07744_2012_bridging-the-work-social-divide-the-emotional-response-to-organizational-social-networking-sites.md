# Bridging the work/social divide: the emotional response to organizational social networking sites

Hope Koch, Ester Gonzalez and Dorothy Leidner

Department of Information Systems, Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Hope Koch, Department of Information Systems, Baylor University, 1 Bear Place #98005, Waco, TX 76798-8005, U.S.A. Tel: 254 710 4071; Fax: 254 710 1091; E-mail: Hope\_Koch@baylor.edu

## Abstract

Organizations seem to be split on their policies governing social networking sites (SNSs) in the workplace. Recent surveys indicate that while many organizations severely restrict or ban SNSs (i.e., Facebook and Twitter) at work, a large majority are actively using, or evaluating the use of SNSs. The purpose of this study is to investigate the implementation of an internal SNS designed to help a large financial institution’s IT new hire program. On the basis of a case study informed by boundary theory and the theory of positive emotions, the research describes the SNS, its uses and how it impacted both the employees and the organization. We found that SNSs blur the boundary between work life and social life and that this boundary blurring creates positive emotions for the employees that use the system. These emotions create personal resources, which then have organizational impacts. While some of the non-users of the system, the IT middle managers, experienced isolation, frustration and resentment, the executives overseeing this SNS attribute improved morale, better employee engagement and even reduced employee turnover to the internal SNS.

## Introduction

Social networking sites (SNSs) are web-based services that allow individuals to construct a digital identity by developing a profile, articulating a list of users with whom they share a connection (i.e., friending people), viewing their list of connections, and viewing connections made by others within the system (boyd & Ellison, 2007). A Nielsen (2009) report indicates that two-thirds of the world’s Internet population visit social networking or blogging sites and that time spent on social networking and blogging sites is growing more than three times the rate of overall Internet growth. Organizational SNS adoption is fueled by hopes of leveraging the benefits these technologies elicit for individuals. Originally characterized as personal recreation applications, SNSs are now becoming an integral part of organizational life (Armano, 2009; Emerald Publishing, 2009). A November 2010 report indicated that 73% of companies are using social media in marketing alone (Williamson, 2010). Internally organizations implement SNSs to connect employees, facilitate mass collaboration and improve communication (Dimicco et al, 2008; Majchrzak et al, 2009; Li, 2010). Employees use internal SNSs, like IBM’s Beehive (Dimicco et al,

2008) and Kaiser-Permanente’s Ideabook (Lofgren, 2009) to campaign for projects, advance their careers and connect with co-workers on a personal level (Dimicco et al, 2008; Majchrzak et al, 2009).

The private nature of internal SNSs elicits liberal information sharing and minimal use of privacy settings, which enables co-workers to reach out to people that they do not know and engage in people sensemaking (Dimicco et al, 2009). These personal workplace connections can build rapport, create stronger working relationships, provide social support, generate capital and foster corporate citizenship behaviors (Dimicco et al, 2008; DiMicco et al, 2009; Skeels & Grudin, 2009), all of which may humanize work by providing employees intrinsic motivations like job satisfaction, freedom and fun (Tan et al, 2009).

While organizational SNSs offer many benefits, the blurring of work and social boundaries implicit in these systems is generating concerns at both the organizational and individual levels (Dimicco et al, 2008; Skeels & Grudin, 2009; Burrus, 2010). Some concerns associated with internal SNSs include time wasting (Skeels & Grudin, 2009; Friedman, 2011; Harris, 2011), personal views affecting career progression (Hewitt & Forte, 2006; Skeels & Grudin, 2009), juggling multiple personas (Dimicco et al, 2009) and crossing organizational hierarchy.

Two workplace studies offer examples of internal SNS concerns. A study of software developers (O’Driscoll & Cummings, 2009) found that while younger employees used new media like Facebook and Twitter to develop software, more experienced software developers viewed this new media as a distraction. Another workplace study (Skeels & Grudin, 2009) found that subordinates were uncomfortable with superiors viewing their profile and/ or interacting with them personally on SNSs.

Among the emerging internal uses of SNSs in organizations are in employee socialization programs. Over the years, human resource professionals have tried a variety of initiatives to acclimate new employees into the workplace (King & Sethi, 1997; Allen et al, 2008; Trauth et al, 2009). These initiatives include buddy systems, mentoring programs, work-life balance programs and social events (Allen et al, 2008; Trauth et al, 2009). In the current economic environment, organizations are asking many of their employees to do the work that was once covered by two or more employees. This affords managers and professionals little time to adequately mentor and build friendships with new employees. This environment becomes especially problematic when an organization hires primarily recent college graduates to assume highly technical jobs. Often these college graduates relocate from far geographical distances, have no social networks in the area and are assuming their first professional position of which they have very little knowledge.

The purpose of this paper is to show how one organization was able to implement an internal SNS to acclimate IT new hires into the organization. This system shifted some of the burden of new hire acclimation off of human resource professionals and middle managers and to the SNS, which was maintained by more senior new hires. Our study will be one of the first to conduct a theoretically guided investigation of SNS use for employee acclimation. Grounded in the accounts of the relevant stakeholders, this interpretive case study follows a contextualist approach (Madill et al, 2000) to investigate the interplay of organizational context and the meaning employees assign to the system. Following theory presentation in the next section, we then present the method, a case description and the analysis. The paper concludes by discussing findings and drawing practical and theoretical implications.

## Theoretical foundation

To evaluate the benefits of implementing an internal SNS into employee acclimation programs we rely on boundary theory (Ashforth et al, 2000; Sundaramurthy & Kreiner, 2008) and the broaden and build theory of positive emotions (Fredrickson, 1998). These two theories present frameworks that are well suited for interpreting the integration of work roles and social roles as well as the emotions this integration may create.

## Boundary theory

Boundary theory explains that people assume various roles (e.g., friend, boss and co-worker) and these roles have different identities characterized by goals, values, beliefs, norms, and interaction styles (Ashforth et al, 2000). Roles are dynamically determined by the task that the individual performs and the social system – family, friends, supervisors, subordinates, peers – in which they interact (Katz & Kahn, 1978; Perrone et al, 2003). Three important concepts in boundary are role conflict, role segmentation and role integration.

Role conflict Individuals assume multiple roles: they may have a professional role in an organization, a volunteer role in a community center, in addition to multiple family and friendship roles. One role can interrupt another role and impede an individual’s ability to fulfill the role. Also, members of an individual’s role set (e.g., co-workers and superiors) can make conflicting demands on the individual (Ashforth et al, 2000; Sundaramurthy & Kreiner, 2008). As a result, individuals face role conflict (Huarng, 1998; King & Sethi, 1998; Igbaria & Guimaraes, 1999; Moore, 2000; Solli-Saether, 2011). They do not have clear and consistent expectations regarding what actions are required in a given time and place.

Role conflict occurs (1) when the expectations and demands of a role conflict, (2) when the demands of one role are incompatible with those of another role, (3) between an individual’s internal standards and desired job behavior, and (4) between time, resource or capabilities of an individual and required behavior (Rizzo et al, 1970; Igbaria & Guimaraes, 1999; Tarafdar et al, 2007).

Boundary theory is frequently used to explain role conflict (Ashforth & Kreiner, 1999; Ashforth et al, 2007;

Koch & Schultze, 2011). Appendix A shows many information systems (IS) studies dealing with role conflict, such as the conflicting demands placed on IS professionals (Bento, 1996; Huarng, 1998; Moore, 2000), managing technologies that blur boundaries between client jobs (Breu & Hemingway, 2004) and dealing with end-user development (Galletta & Heckman, 1991). These studies link role conflict to role stress, emotional exhaustion and burnout. People facing role conflict often become dissatisfied with their jobs, negatively affecting their commitment to the organization and their intention to stay with the organization (Igbaria & Guimaraes, 1999; Moore, 2000). There are two means often used to cope with role conflict: role segmentation and role integration.

Role segmentation To avoid role conflict and stress, individuals may choose to segment their roles along physical, temporal and psychological boundaries (Clark, 2000). Physical boundaries such as the walls of a home or workplace define where domain relevant behavior takes place. Temporal boundaries (i.e., hours) divide when different activities take place (e.g., personal activities vs work activities). Psychological boundaries define the appropriate thinking patterns, behavior patterns and emotions for a given domain. Because boundaries exist within everyday life such as home, work and church (Nippert-Eng, 1996), individuals attempt to simplify and bring order to their environment by creating a sense of role identity in these set boundaries. Thick boundaries exist between extremely segmented roles, making it easy for the role inhabitant to understand the expectations of a given role. For example, a senior executive segments his/her managerial role from his/her family member role, community role and/or friend role. This role segmentation may decrease the stress and role conflict associated with assuming multiple, diverse roles (Sundaramurthy & Kreiner, 2008).

Increasingly, the introduction of modern communication and information technologies in organizations is interfering with role segmentation: laptops, mobile phones and widespread Internet accessibility make it difficult for individuals to fully distance themselves from their work role even if they are in a social or family setting (Tarafdar et al, 2007; Lindgren et al, 2008). This has resulted in role integration: the development of permeable roles in which individuals micro-transition from one role to another (Ashforth et al, 2000).

Benefits of role integration While role segmentation research suggests that segmenting roles helps reduce role conflict, emerging work on role integration is pointing to the potential of individuals flourishing in the midst of permeable roles. Role integration may bring together knowledge from various domains by linking people separated by hierarchy, location or function (Levina & Vaast, 2005). Role integration has also been shown to be helpful in developing shared mental models (Koch &

Schultze, 2011), managing outsourcing relationships (Gopal & Gosain, 2010) and creating intellectual property (Breu & Hemingway, 2004). In addition, sharing aspects of a person’s social life in the workplace allows employees to get to know one another better (Sundaramurthy & Kreiner, 2008). When supervisors and co-workers understand one another’s various roles, they are less likely to make unrealistic demands, thereby reducing a major source of role conflict and helping to both reduce stress and increase organizational commitment (King & Sethi, 1998; Clark, 2000).

Organizational SNS implementations are designed to incorporate aspects of an employee’s social life into the work setting. By their very nature, SNSs blur what were formerly distinct boundaries between work roles and social roles. Even though theory largely predicts that such blurring of roles has negative emotional consequences, the use of SNSs in practice is suggesting positive emotional results. Indeed, if the introduction of SNSs into organizations only served to obfuscate the role identities of employees and create undue role stress, then organizations would not be implementing systems with such alacrity – a survey conducted in 2010 suggests that 67% of organizations currently have at least one social media tool on their intranet (Ward, 2010). In order to delve further into the study of SNSs in organizations and the emotional response of employees, we will next present literature on emotions in IS research.

## Emotions in IS research

Emotions are an integral part of everyday life. Psychology research suggests that emotions like stress and joy influence attitudes, behaviors, decisions, and thinking processes (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Loewenstein et al, 2001). Various factors may prompt emotional responses in individuals. Emotions are likely to arise when individuals perceive an important situation (Lazarus, 1991; Bagozzi et al, 1999) and when an individual experiences an interruption or routine change (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005).

Organizational IS implementations can trigger emotional reactions. Individuals may experience positive emotions (e.g., joy or happiness) if they perceive that the IS is an enjoyable and useful tool (Davis & Bagozzi, 1992; Davis et al, 1992; Venkatesh, 1999), whereas they may experience negative emotions (e.g., frustration, anxiety or fear) if they perceive that the IS is difficult to use (Venkatesh, 2000; Cenfetelli, 2004). Negative emotions such as fear, anger, anxiety, irritation and frustration may negatively impact an individuals’ intention to use a system (Venkatesh et al, 2003; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) and perceived ease of system use (Cenfetelli, 2004). In contrast, positive emotions such as joy, happiness and contentment have been positively related to individuals intention to continue using a system and their perceived ease of system use (Davis & Bagozzi, 1992; Davis et al, 1992: Venkatesh. 1999: Koufaris. 2002: Cenfetelli. 2004).

While positive and negative emotions may be at the core of an individual’s experience with IS (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010), most IS research disregards the role emotions play in individuals’ decisions to use a system (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Loewenstein et al, 2001). Instead IS research focuses on cognitive models and predicts IS use based on perceptions and beliefs about the IS (Rogers, 1983; Davis, 1989; Venkatesh et al, 2003; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010) without investigating the emotions (e.g., anxiety, irritation, apprehension, joy, happiness or contentment) that may impact user beliefs. Of the IS studies that have considered emotions, a limited number of emotions have been studied and there is a need to better understand the role of positive emotions in IS usage (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010). Furthermore, IS research on emotions lacks a framework that will predict and explain the emotions that create certain consequences for IS use (Venkatesh, 2000). To close these gaps in IS research, our study highlights how a system can create positive emotions and how these positive reactions create resources for the individuals using the system.

The broaden and build theory of positive emotions (Fredrickson, 1998) serves as a framework to study how the experiences of positive emotions contribute to individual well-being. According to the theory, positive emotions create urges to act, which then serve to maximize an individual’s well-being over long periods of time (Fredrickson, 2004). The broaden and build theory of positive emotions states that experiences of positive emotions (i.e., joy, contentment, pride and love) broaden an individual’s thought-action behavior and thinking, which leads to building personal resources (Fredrickson, 2001). The theory identifies four personal resources. Table 1 defines these resources and provides examples of how emotions contribute to these resources.

These various thought-action inclinations – to play, to explore, to integrate or to envision future achievement – each represent ways that positive emotions broaden habitual modes of thinking or acting (Fredrickson, 1998, 2000, 2001; Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005). Individuals who find themselves experiencing positive emotions and embarking on actions such as exploration or playfulness will increase their tendency to repeat the positive behavior and continue to build personal resources. Furthermore, these resources serve as a reserve that individuals can draw upon when they face negative situations (Fredrickson, 2004).

## Summary

In summary, boundary theory suggests that individuals create roles and responsibilities within various boundaries such that the mixing of roles across boundaries can create stress and negative emotional response. Yet organizations are implementing SNSs – systems that by their very nature bridge the boundaries of professional and personal lives. To understand how organizations are experiencing positive results from the use of SNSs, we undertake a case study of a large U.S. financial institution that attributes higher morale and reduced turnover among IT employees to the implementation of its internal SNS.

## Method

We chose an interpretive case research methodology for this study (Walsham, 1995, 2006). This method is particularly appropriate because knowledge on internal SNSs is in its nascent stage (Myers, 2009) and our research question delves into how organizations use internal SNSs and how employees respond emotionally to these systems.

## Data collection

Relying on planned opportunism (Pettigrew, 1990), we chose SWBank<sup>1</sup> for our case study when the first author heard a March 2008 presentation on its internal SNS. The authors pursued this research because it represents a novel situation: few organizations had implemented internal SNSs and SWBank’s system seemed to have had a positive impact on its IT new hire program.

While we began studying the SNS in 2008, data collection on SWBank’s new hire acclimation program began in March 2007 and continued through May 2010. Ninety-five percent of data collection occurred face-toface. Collection techniques included attending meetings (8), attending presentations (3), attending job fairs (8), conducting focus groups (2), spending time off with employees (i.e., lunches), observing events (1), and conducting interviews. Interviews averaged 30 min. Interviews conducted during the Summer of 2009 were structured around the recent SNS implementation and how this had affected SWBank. See interview guides in Appendix B and Appendix C.

Appendix D details the interviews conducted by date and organizational level. All respondents worked in SWBank’s IT department. With o3 years experience, most new hires and interns were male millennials with ages between 21 and 27 years. The IT managers were male members of Generation X, who started their careers with SWBank upon college graduation. In 2009, they were approaching 40 years of age and had about 16 years of experience with SWBank. The executives consisted of two females and one male. The females were in their late 40s and had spent 15 and 25 years with SWBank. The male was 40 and had been with SWBank slightly over a year.

During interviews the authors took notes capturing as many of the interviewees’ verbatim expressions as possible. Supporting human resource material was also collected. Upon collecting data, the authors shared the emerging interpretations with peers and SWBank’s employees.

## Data analysis

Our data analysis followed a grounded theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). We did not seek to impose an a priori theory on our data or to test a theoretical framework (Walsham, 1995). Rather, we reviewed the literature on SNSs, role boundaries and organizational climate, to identify an initial set of pertinent themes that formed the basis of our theoretical lens. This served as a sensitizing device (Walsham, 1995) to guide our Summer 2009 data collection. We initially prepared an account of SWBank, its new hire program, the SNS’s features and its impact on SWBank. We then analyzed the data using open and axial coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). The open coding involved understanding how the SNS affected SWBank’s new hires, employees and the organization. In this process, how the system encouraged employees to mix their work and social lives, the emotions this created, and how this led to personal resources and positive organizational outcomes became apparent.

As such, we decided that boundary theory and the broaden and build theory of positive emotions were the best lenses through which to further analyze the data. Boundary theory introduced work-related uses, socialrelated uses, integrated uses and the negative and positive emotions this boundary blurring created. The broaden and build theory of positive emotions allowed us to study the positive emotions and personal resources this boundary blurring created. Upon choosing these theories, we coded chunks of data related to how the system was used, the emotions use evoked and the resources use created. One sample matrix placed organizational level (executive, manager and new hire) as rows with the emotions the SNS evoked as columns. ## Case description

SWBank is one of the largest providers of insurance, banking, investments, and financial planning to U.S. citizens. In spite of the hardships in the U.S. economy, in 2008 SWBank entered a growth and expansion phase. As of 2011, SWBank had more than 22,000 employees, revenue in excess of U.S.\$18 billion and assets totaling more than \$80 billion.

Because SWBank’s data includes identity information on all of its’ members, SWBank is very secure. As an example, through June 2009 SWBank had a strict Internet policy that barred employees from Internet browsing, accessing personal email, sending communications outside of SWBank and social networking. This strict policy created some problems in the IT department, which has pioneered many technology innovations for SWBank’s customers. With the Internet and social networking becoming a major part of SWBank’s member services strategy, many of its more than 2500 IT employees found it unfair that they were not allowed to use these technologies at work.

This unfairness was especially disruptive to SWBank’s 250 IT new hires, who were accustomed to using these technologies in their daily lives. Unsurprisingly, the IT department had historically been plagued with a 60–70% turnover among the new hires during their second year of employment. An IT manager commented on the problem, ‘We are having to face the fact that it simply costs too much to bring these people in and train them, only to have them leave shortly thereafter. We have to do something to keep them’. IT management attributed the problem to a poor new hire program. As an IT manager explained, ‘The program was disjointed and unorganized. New hires lost connection with one another. New hires received poor training and were unwanted by management. The program was sloppy and disconnected’.

In 2007, SWBank charged an IT director with revamping the new hire program. The IT director persuaded management to relax the strict Internet policies and begin allowing employees to access outside websites at their own discretion. Then, in 2008, SWBank went a step further to leverage the power of social networking, creating its own SNS called On-Board.<sup>2</sup>

On-Board is SWBank’s internal SNS. Since most new hires have moved hundreds of miles to start their new jobs with SWBank, they initially use the social pages of On-Board as an introduction to the community. After a year or so with SWBank, the more senior new hires begin using On-Board to acclimate and mentor incoming new hires. On-Board embodies two areas: a technical system and a social system. The technical system, ‘On-Board’, is a Web 2.0 technology based on SharePoint. It consists of a variety of features that are both social and work related, see Table 3.

Under the supervision of executives, SWBank’s 250 IT new hires develop and manage the SNS’s content. Referred to as the On-Board group, these new hires have o3 years of experience and are led by an elected six-member core team. SWBank encourages all of its On-Board members to apply for an On-Board leadership role. The core team’s main responsibility is to run the social and technical aspects of the On-Board system such that the new IT employees hired into SWBank are smoothly socialized and assimilated into the IT department.

The core team is also tasked with guiding the events that the On-Board group hosts. SWBank’s executives both sponsor and provide budgets for these events, which must fall into one of four pillars: networking, career development, workforce initiatives or continuing education. Table 4 describes and explains the four pillars. Past events have included executive luncheons, volunteer projects (i.e., Girl Scouts, Science Fair), paintball competitions and an American Idol-like competition. Since any On-Board member can suggest, plan or lead an event, On-Board provides all of its members leadership opportunities and chances to increase their exposure within SWBank.

Since the inception of On-Board, two of SWBank’s IT executives and two middle managers concur that SWBank has experienced decreased turnover, higher morale and increased employee engagement. IT management believes that these organizational improvements are largely attributable to the On-Board initiative. For instance, the vice president of applications and infrastructure said, ‘Since the implementation of On-Board, there has been a greater retention rate visible within SWBank’. When asked why management supports On-Board, the vice president of applications and infrastructure stated, ‘retention and morale’. SWBank does not know with certainty whether the reduced turnover can be attributed to the economic decline or the On-Board system, but as an IT executive explained, ‘Although it is difficult to tell that On-Board is the reason for the decline in turnover, there has been a greater retention rate visible within the organization’. Furthermore, the director in charge of On-Board explained, ‘no one [new hires] has voluntarily left in the last 2 years’. Yet while management believes the On-Board system and group are to credit for these positive outcomes, it is not fully understood why the implementation of an internal SNS should have such a marked influence on employee behavior and emotional well-being. Our analysis will seek to uncover the reasons for the apparent success of the SNS. In particular, we focus on the emotional response evoked by the system.

## Emotional responses

New hires Various emotions were evoked in the new hires’ descriptions of their SNS use. Interviewees described themselves as ‘happy’, ‘content’, ‘enjoying work’, ‘fitting in’, ‘comfortable’ and ‘feeling proud’ as a result of their using the SNS. New hires described the colleagues whom they have met via the SNS as ‘friends’ or ‘family’. They described arranging and attending ‘meet up’ events with co-workers both during and after working hours and claimed to ‘enjoy hanging out with friends formed through On-Board’ during these meet up events. The new hires described On-Board as the ‘best tool for getting involved and forming relationships’. The resulting relationships inculcate a ‘sense of belonging’ and ‘a nice helpful comfortable feeling’. One new hire likened the internal relationships to family, stating that ‘even though it is large, the organization feels like a family’. The metaphors of family and friends suggest that the new hires are developing an emotional attachment to their work colleagues and to the organization. Rather than creating stress by blurring work and social boundaries, the new hires believe that the system helps to ‘balance work and social life’. However, it is important to note that this so-called ‘balance’ is achieved mostly by incorporating social activities into everyday work rather than by incorporating work activities into after hour social activities.

Indeed, it is telling that the new hires describe ‘having fun’ and being ‘playful’ at work. They express eagerness to ‘unwind’ and have ‘breaks from work’ during work itself. The core team even emphasized that some aspects of On-Board, such as pulse, are designed to be ‘something fun to do while working’. The new hires described ‘using On-Board to take a break’ when they are ‘tired and incapable of staying focused on work’. On-Board is viewed as a ‘refresher’ and an ‘energy boost’. This is not to suggest indolence on the part of the new hires, but rather relief from the tediousness of some of the work they experience as entry-level IT employees. The new hires describe much of their typical workday as ‘boring’, ‘routine’ and ‘technical’. They enjoy the respite provided by such activities as challenging people they have never met to a sporting event (e.g., Ping-Pong) during lunch or organizing a fun social event (a bar-b-que or a Casino Night). While some of these activities are purely social in nature and are occurring during work hours, the activities are helping the employees ‘form relationships’, ‘build a community’, ‘enjoy work’ and ultimately, in the minds of the employees, ‘get things done faster and better’.

As the new hires experience the positive emotions that accompany the use of On-Board, they continue to invest time in On-Board. One new hire reported ‘working for months’ on an American Idol-like event that was held during the workday. The event simulated the national television program in that new hires performed to a song and upper management served as the judges. At the end of the event, the event leadership presented awards based on judges’ comments and audience votes, which were taken via text messages. Approximately, 150 employees participated in the event either as performers or in the audience. We observed the event during one of our organization visits and watched as the new hire, who organized it, beamed with excitement as she shook people’s hands following the event and absorbed the many congratulations on her event’s success from attendees. She expressed excitement that executives were aware that she was the event’s organizer. Other new hires explain that organizing an event ‘increases their prestige and helps them make a name for themselves in front of senior management’. In short, the new hires have responded favorably to On-Board and report a range of positive emotions resulting from On-Board use.

Middle management and executives In contrast to the feel good perspective of the new hires, IT middle managers and senior executives expressed more negative emotions with respect to On-Board. Since the SNS helped new hires build relationships with one another and circumvent hierarchy to develop relationship with the senior executives that sponsored On-Board events, some middle managers expressed feelings of isolation and inequity. The workplace that the middle managers had risen the ranks in had changed from tenure to committee-based promotion; therefore, On-Board’s socializing would give the new hires more exposure to management, which may affect promotions and job assignments. For example, one feature of On-Board – the CEO luncheon – gives new hires an opportunity to meet the CEO. One IT middle manager protested that he had ‘never had lunch with the CEO’. Other middle managers reported feeling ‘left out of the fun’ and expressed resentment that the new hires were allowed to engage in social activities at work. An IT director described it as ‘a problem we are experiencing in which more tenured employees feel jealous and discontent because they are neither able to participate in these special events nor gain exposure to top management’.

The senior executives’ response, while not as negative as the IT middle managers’ response, is certainly more circumspect than that of the new hires. SWBank’s executives have not entirely embraced the socializing aspect of On-Board. Because many of the On-Board sponsored events take place during the workday, executives expressed concern that the ‘On-Board group needs more discretion on what they do during work hours’. One executive, while impressed with the turnout for the Idol event, bemoaned the fact that ‘the Idol-like event today is taking 3 h out of the workday’. He was left wondering ‘how they are spending their time and our money’. Executives would like to see new hires using On-Board to ‘discuss more work-related topics’ rather than for ‘setting up the next bar-b-que meet-up event’. They recognize that On-Board is an SNS, but nevertheless envisioned it as being used more for discussions on work projects and updates. An IT executive stated that ‘On-Board is currently used for purely social aspects; I wish it was a little more work related’. Because it has been difficult to see the benefits of On-Board in terms of knowledge sharing and project teamwork, executives remain ambiguous as to On-Board’s ultimate value. On one hand, they are optimistic about On-Board’s potential for ‘sharing technical knowledge and working on developments’ but on the other hand, they are worried that the new hires view On-Board primarily as ‘a tool to make friends’. Yet in spite of this apparently ambiguous emotional reaction, the executives continue to support On-Board. In part, they fear that they must ‘recognize the needs of this new generation’ for socializing at work and in part, they remain convinced that at least part of the increase in morale and decline in turnover observed in the IT department is attributable to the On-Board system.

## Users develop personal resources from positive emotions

The broaden and build theory of positive emotion posits that external stimuli such as the use of the On-Board system can elicit positive emotions, which then engender personal resources including physical, intellectual, social and psychological resources that are then available as part of an individual’s repertoire of responses to situational stimuli (Fredrickson, 1998). Our interviews suggest several resources emerging from the positive emotions that the On-Board users experienced. These include strong internal relationships (a social resource), physical well-being (a physical resource), opportunities for learning (an intellectual resource) and a sense of cultural fit (a psychological resource). Our analysis, depicted in

Figure 1, also suggests that these personal resources themselves reinforce On-Board use.

New hires who are excited and happy are more likely to be involved in activities that provide physical resources. On-Board can be used to schedule sports activities during lunch. On-Board users, who challenge others to sport matches during lunch reported feeling both energy (a physical resource) in the morning in anticipation of the sports break and ‘feeling refreshed in the afternoon with energy to concentrate more intensely on their work tasks’. Because On-Board mediates the score keeping, members reuse it to post or monitor current scores. These results, which are incorporated into user profiles, allow members to arrange matches with people that appear similar in skill. In this way, On-Board use encourages physical resource development as members engage in sports activities and social resource development as members meet new co-workers with similar sporting interests.

Similarly, individuals who attend ‘meet up’ events report the development of friendships (a social resource), which can be helpful in solving work-related problems. For instance, a new hire responsible for running tests described working very long hours on a project, which required the use of a server that was down. Because she had made a social contact through On-Board whom she knew had the expertise she needed, she was able to access him and acquire his help in troubleshooting the server. She stated, ‘I am thankful for the support group I’ve picked up through On-Board. Because I knew Brad, I was able to troubleshoot the server without going through all the red tape’. Thus, while the new hire did not use On-Board, the technical system, to solve her problem, its social features assisted in creating a social resource (a relationship) that became instrumental when the new hire needed access to someone that could help solve her problem. Furthermore, the On-Board friends who were not directly involved in problem solving provided a sense of support during the stressful time.

The intellectual resources are created by interest. Through the urge of exploration and the desire to learn new things, new hires tap into their intellect and cognitive thinking. The new hire group mentioned, ‘On-Board gives people exposure to new topics, helps them develop new skills and provides exposure to senior executives’. For example, members who attend the guest speaker lectures are exposed to a variety of topics, which can then be discussed further on discussion boards and in workshops. House call visits serve as another means of exploration because they allow an On-Board member to try out a new area, which helps them understand how the functional areas interact with one another, allows them to develop new skills and opens their mind to the possibility of bidding on a new position.

Members also develop resources when they plan events. In addition to developing project management skills (an intellectual resource), these events increase interactions between the new hires and top management (a social resource). For instance, an On-Board member discussed how playing black jack with the Chief Information Officer (CIO) made it easier for him to interact with the CIO in meetings and made him like his job more (a psychological resource). Furthermore, the success of Casino Night and the new hire’s exposure to top management, led top management to invite him and his peers to run SWBank’s United Way Campaign for the following year. As new hires gain interest and act upon these interests, they are building resources that enhance their minds and thought processes.

Lastly, psychological resources, while more difficult to ascertain, nevertheless appear present. Employees who are satisfied at work have been shown to be more productive, have better performance (Levy, 2003), demonstrate a decreased intention to leave, (Mowday et al, 1984) and tend to be more committed to the organization (Porter et al, 1974). Task variety, autonomy, workload, learning opportunities and job complexity have been shown to be important predictors of job satisfaction (Smith et al, 1969). Work tasks that are considered boring or routine can lead to job dissatisfaction. On-Board does not make work tasks themselves more varied, flexible or less routine, but it does increase variety and flexibility at work, thus making the new hires’ work life less rigid. As such, the employees display high satisfaction with their work lives at SWBank. This satisfaction is a psychological resource, which helps the new hires go to work not because they have to, but, as they say, ‘because it is fun’. Even while the work itself is not described as fun, the act of going to work and being at work is satisfying and gratifying for these new hires.

In summary, the use of On-Board and the positive emotions and resources generated from On-Board use, is having a marked impact on SWBank’s IT department. Referring again to Figure 1 (Box 1), On-Board use is associated with positive emotions, which form the foundation for the development of personal resources, which then encourages further On-Board use. While emotions themselves are ephemeral and attached to a specific event, the personal resources created through the emotions are considered to be durable and long lasting beyond the emotion that initially created it (Fredrickson, 2004). The resources that the new hires are developing reinforce the positive emotions felt by the new hires, generating high commitment to the department and the organization. These personal resources are also useful in overcoming negative situations: as new challenges arise, new hires should be better equipped to manage the issues rather than feeling overwhelmed and stressed.

## Implications

The preceding analysis demonstrates that the introduction of the On-Board system and the various initiatives executed by the On-Board group facilitated both workrelated and social-related activities, leading to emotional and personal resource outcomes. These findings offer implications to our understanding of workplace impacts of enterprise SNSs, the broaden and build theory of positive emotions and boundary theory.

## Workplace impacts of SNSs

Much of the work on the impact of IS assumes that organizational impacts accumulate from the aggregation of individual benefits, such that the greater the number of users experiencing positive outcomes from use, the greater the organizational impacts of the system (Delone & McLean, 1992; Seddon, 1997; Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007). While our case does not devalidate this assumption, it does suggest that the impacts (i.e., emotional response and productivity) experienced at the individual vs departmental level may be of a qualitatively different kind. The new hires themselves experience positive emotional outcomes from their use of the system. For the department, the major outcome is the higher morale and reduced turnover of the new hires. Furthermore, the study suggests that non-users of a system also experience emotional outcomes from a system used by others. One of the benefits of the On-Board system was that middle managers were freed from the burden of mentoring and would no longer feel distracted from their work tasks by having to train new hires. Ironically, middle managers, even though they had wanted to be freed from this burden, experienced a negative emotional response to the system when they realized that the new hires had managed to accrue social capital with senior managers and social experiences that they had not had access to in their many years of work. Because IS research is typically concerned with the impact of system use on users, our study suggest that future research should consider the differing emotional responses of users and non-users to a system as well as the responses that executives can take to mitigate any tension.

This study expands our understanding of playfulness in the workplace. In this case, the On-Board users (i.e., the new hires) experienced a sense of playfulness, which is an action rendered by a sense of joy (Fredrickson, 1998). According to Webster & Martocchio (1993), people are more likely to become intrigued and engaged if they view tasks as play vs work. However, the tasks themselves did not change in nature, nor did the perception of the tasks. But by engendering a sense of playfulness in non-task areas, SWBank was able to create a sense of playfulness at work. Playing at work enables creativity and exploration (Glynn, 1994) and playing can cultivate a workplace culture characterized by learning, positive attitudes and high morale (Webster & Martocchio, 1993). This is particularly evident with millennials who experience higher positive attitudes, greater learning and increased engagement when tasks are considered play (Webster et al, 1990). Our study extends these findings by showing how a SNS (i.e., an IT artifact), which is perceived as fun, can break the monotony of boring, rigorous or demanding job tasks. Using a SNS at work serves as a relief from work and facilitates opportunities for experiencing positive emotions. Therefore, despite the characteristics of the job, as long as individuals’ perceptions of playing while at work continue, they are more likely to engage themselves in activities that reinforce positive emotions and attitudes.

## Theories on emotions

The On-Board study contributes to the broaden and build theory of positive by providing insight to Fredrickson’s (1998) question: What good are positive emotions? Our study extends Fredrickson’s framework (1998) to organizational uses of technology by suggesting that technology use can create positive emotions, which promotes well-being and broadens individuals’ ability to cope with negative situations. This fills gaps in both psychology and workplace IS research. Psychology research has emphasized negative emotions in theory building and testing (Fredrickson, 1998). IS research calls for additional research on the role of positive emotions in IS use (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2010).

Furthermore, this study advances our understanding of social personal resources explicated in the theory of positive emotions. Using the On-Board system helped facilitate social capital development in three ways. First, it gave the new hires access to people who could provide useful information and new perspectives (Granovetter, 1973). On-Board allowed the new hires to meet more senior new hires and executives. These relationships set the new hires at ease during work meetings, helped them understand where to go for help and increased their commitment to SWBank’s mission. Second, On-Board helped the new hires maintain relationships with one another, thus facilitating a network of acquaintances that can do small favors and helping build emotionally close friendships (Ellison et $^ { a l , }$ 2007; Valenzuela et al, 2009).

Third, by allowing the new hires to access information on the SNS, meet other new hires and develop and maintain relationships with their peer group, SWBank was able to shift some of the burden of acclimating the new hires away from middle managers and human resources.

Through these three mechanisms, the internal SNS contributes to the development and maintenance of weak ties. Because weak ties allow individuals to access people beyond their immediate circle of friends, weak ties can foster knowledge building, new idea circulation and expansion of one’s sphere of influence (Granovetter, 1973). The effect of SNS-facilitated social capital on working relationships offers several promising avenues for future research. How influential and useful are a new employees’ weak ties developed through internal SNSs? Are these ties temporal or enduring? What is the capacity for weak ties? How many new weak ties can an employee absorb before the costs (i.e., time and effort) of maintaining the ties outweigh the ties’ potential benefits?

## Boundary theories

Finally, this paper offers implications to theories dealing with boundaries (see Appendix A for a list of IS studies dealing with boundaries). Whereas boundary theories highlight primarily negative emotional responses from mixing work life and social life (Ashforth et al, 2000; Sundaramurthy & Kreiner, 2008) and from technology that allows work life to interfere with social life (Stanton & Stam, 2003; Tarafdar et al, 2007), our research offers two insights. First, integration can generate positive emotions, which in turn generate personal resources and positive organizational outcomes. Second, rather than interfering with social life (e.g., parents with blackberries) or work life (e.g., using Facebook at work), internal SNSs such as On-Board have several features that enable new employees to become central participants in the organization (Lave & Wegner, 1991; Clark, 2000, p. 766). It is important to keep in mind that the boundary blurring in our case involved bringing more social life into the work environment rather than vice versa and that the interaction occurred entirely during work hours. Future research is needed to understand the implications of work tasks merging with social time, such as employees posting about their work on a personal SNS and the implications of non-work related social media use during work hours. As the use of social media on mobile devices continues to increase, organizations are likely to experience a variety of boundary blurring with both positive and negative consequences.

## Conclusion

This study’s findings need to be considered in light of its limitations. First, the results may be biased toward the positive effects of internal SNSs. While we did not specifically ask about positive emotions during our interviews, users may have wanted to present the organization in a positive light. Given that many of the current studies (Culnan et al, 2010; DiGangi et al, 2010;

Gallaugher & Ransbotham, 2010 and others) tout the benefits of SNS, future research should critically examine the use of SNS in organizations to determine how they may overcome or create inequalities. A second limitation involves the use of qualitative methods. Our data can neither speak to causality nor to significant associations. Future research using quantitative methods would be valuable to explore in detail the relationships between SNS use and various emotions and outcomes. A third limitation is that this research relied on a single case study, which frequently raises generalizability issues (Lee & Baskerville, 2003). This is particularly a concern when the case is chosen for theoretical sampling reasons (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1994). While we make no claims of across-case generalizability, our study does achieve within-case generalizability (Lee, 1989; Pan & Tan, 2011). Through iterative exploration of prior theory and our empirical data, we achieve generalization from empirical description to theoretical statements (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, p. 236). As a result of this inductive approach, our insights can be seen as ‘well-founded but as yet untested hypotheses’ (Lee & Baskerville, 2003, p. 224).

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study adds an organizational perspective to the SNS literature, which has been largely dominated by studies investigating personal SNS use and SNS marketing (Huysman, 2011).
