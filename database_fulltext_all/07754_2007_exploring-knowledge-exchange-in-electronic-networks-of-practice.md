## Abstract

The recently developed knowledge-based view of the firm argues that knowledge is the firm’s most valuable resource. Within this field of study, informal social networks are rapidly gaining attention as mechanisms that facilitate knowledge flows. Electronic networks of practice (NoPs) are a special case of informal network where the sharing of practicerelated knowledge occurs primarily through computer-based communication technologies. However, we know relatively little about the dynamics of knowledge exchange that occur in these electronic networks. This conceptual paper posits that there is a relationship between the structural properties of electronic NoPs and successful knowledge exchange. The theoretical positions of social network theory and the knowledge-based view of the firm are used to support this claim. A review of the extant literature reveals that a clear conceptual framework for NoPs is lacking. The objective of this research is to contribute to the development of such a framework by addressing a largely neglected area within the growing network of practice literature – namely, the relationship between electronic network communication structure and knowledge flows. ## Introduction

O <sup>ver</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>past</sup> <sup>two</sup> <sup>decades,</sup> <sup>as</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>result</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>managerial</sup>initiatives such as delayering, reengineering and initiatives such as delayering, reengineering and team-based designs, most organisations have become flatter and more flexible. This has significantly changed the way work gets done. Employees are no longer constrained by the role of formally prescribed relationships in organisations. More work is being done through informal networks, and ‘supporting collaboration and work in these informal networks is increasingly important for organisations competing on knowledge and an ability to innovate and adapt’ (Cross et al., 2002). Informal relationships among employees are often far more reflective of the way work happens in an organisation than the relationships established by position within the formal structure (Cross et al., 2002). Yet, these relationships rarely appear on formal organisational charts.

Further, pressure is being placed on organisations by the increased internationalisation of business, resulting in collaboration and cooperation becoming more distributed. Collaboration between organisations has been the focus of attention in recent years with the recognition that success in a global economy comes from innovation. This is the only way an organisation can keep pace with the rapid developments in technology, increasingly demanding customers and changes in the competitive environment through deregulation, social changes and the actions of competitors. Innovation depends on the exchange of ideas and insights through trusted relationships, which depend on knowing how to collaborate effectively (Nooteboom, 2004).

The diffusion of innovative knowledge has become one of the major research interests in management. Traditional organisational forms (markets and hierarchies) show serious deficits in organising the complex nature of knowledge (Kelly and Jones, 2001). Recently, in the field of knowledge management (KM), there has been an increased recognition of the importance of less structured forms of knowledge and the role played by social networks in stimulating innovation and creation of new knowledge. Many companies are discovering that the real value of KM is not in the sharing of documents, but in the sharing of insights and ideas. Communities of Practice (CoPs) are seen by many as providing a way in which this form of knowledge can be created, shared and sustained. A CoP consists of a relatively tight-knit group of members who know each other, work together face-to-face, and continually negotiate, communicate and coordinate with each other directly. CoPs are regarded as the essential building blocks of the knowledge economy, and are being promoted within organisations as sources of competitive advantage (Justesen, 2004; Teigland and Wasko, 2004; Brown and Hagel, 2006). Current research has focused on the role of CoPs for encouraging knowledge exchange and innovation within organisations; however, we know much less about the role that CoP members play in creating linkages to external knowledge sources. Previous research has found that organisational members may simultaneously be members of a CoP as well as members of broader occupational communities (Van Maanen and Barley, 1984). These individuals perform the dual roles of generating local knowledge within an organisational CoP while providing linkages to knowledge and innovations outside the organisation. These inter-organisational networks have been referred to as networks of practice (NoPs). NoPs are social structures linking similar individuals across organisations who are engaged in a shared practice, but who do not necessarily know one another (Brown and Duguid, 2000). An example of an NoP would be academics meeting at an international conference to discuss their chosen field of study.

While the participation of individuals in NoPs is not a new phenomenon, the ability to access these networks has increased due to recent advances in information and communication technologies. Electronic NoPs (ENoPs) are a special case of NoP where the sharing of practicerelated knowledge occurs primarily through computerbased communication technologies (Wasko and Faraj, 2005). In ENoPs, individuals may never get to know one another or meet face to face. They generally coordinate through technologies such as blogs, listservs or bulletin boards. Previous research has shown that external knowledge trading through ENoPs is beneficial for the firm (Bouty, 2000; Teigland and Wasko, 2003), thus making the study of ENoPs of prime interest for researchers and practitioners. Open-source software communities are one example of an ENoP, and these communities have been the subject of much scholarly attention in recent times. Opensource projects such as Linux, Apache and Gnome have achieved remarkable success and have, on occasion, displaced commercially produced software.

This conceptual paper examines ENoPs through the theoretical lens of social network theory. This body of literature shows that particular structural properties of networks create or constrain possibilities for action by individuals and networks. This paper includes three points of departure from extant network theory research. Firstly, over the past several decades, social network research has favoured either egocentric (e.g. employee) (Burt, 1992; Nebus, 2006) or bounded networks (e.g. organisation) (Ibarra and Andrews, 1993; Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006) as the primary unit of analysis. The focus of this paper is upon the network, which includes structural properties of both its individual members and the collection as a whole. Secondly, despite the significant role played by informal networks in facilitating KM (Birkinshaw and Sheehan, 2002; Wenger et al., 2002; Wasko and Faraj, 2005), there has been relatively little social network research on the structural properties of informal networks and their consequences for knowledge exchange. The few studies that have examined this relationship (Borgatti and Cross, 2003; Cummings and Cross, 2003) have tended to focus upon networks internal to one organisation. As Chen and Duan (2003) note, there is a great difference between how knowledge is transferred between organisations as opposed to within an organisation. Internally, organisations try to maximise the knowledge available to employees, but at the inter-organisation level there is a paradox. The firm has to open its boundaries to facilitate knowledge transfer, yet it also has to protect its own knowledge base and intellectual capital. Our understanding of how network communication structures facilitate knowledge exchange at the inter-organisational level is limited. Thirdly, very few network researchers have attempted to apply network theory concepts specifically to electronic networks. A review of the 11 papers included in the recent Academy of Management Review special issue on network theory development emphasises this research gap. While the growing importance of electronic networks is mentioned (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006; Parkhe et al., 2006), this collection of network studies examines either face-to-face networks (Dhanaraj and Parkhe, 2006; Joshi, 2006; Nebus, 2006) or strategic alliance issues, such as change (Kim et al., 2006; Koka et al., 2006) and embeddedness (Hagedoorn, 2006). This lack of research is surprising as ENoPs have been found to be influential in fields as diverse as software development (Roberts et al., 2006; Shah, 2006), astronomy (Ferris, 2002), law (Wasko and Faraj, 2005), information technology (IT) consultancy (Teigland and Wasko, 2004), public health (Vaast, 2004) and sports products (Franke and Shah, 2005).

IT resources or assets have been seen to enable better performance only when leveraged in tandem with complementary human abilities in an organisational context (Powell and Dent-Mitcallef, 1997). The information systems (IS) field thus requires conceptual models that can clarify the effects of IT in relation to the particular context of social or organisational behaviour in which its use is embedded (Lee, 1999). A review of the extant literature reveals that a clear conceptual framework for NoPs is lacking. The objective of this research is to contribute to the development of such a framework by addressing a largely neglected area within the growing NoP literature – namely, the relationship between electronic network communication structure and knowledge flows. This paper posits that there is a relationship between the structural properties of ENoPs and successful knowledge exchange. To support this claim, a review of the knowledge-based view of the firm is presented in the following section. Traditional approaches to KM have focused on the use of IS to capture, codify and store structured or explicit knowledge. As discussed in the third section, the limitations of this approach are now becoming apparent, and knowledge workers are turning to IT to create dynamic online discussion forums, or ENoPs, rather than utilising and contributing to static knowledge repositories. A conceptual framework that links the roles of CoPs to ENoPs within the knowledge-based view of the firm is introduced and illustrated, and its utility subsequently discussed. To further develop this model, it is suggested that there exists a relationship between three independent variables (i.e. core/periphery structure, connectedness and the density of ENoPs) and knowledge exchange. Further research will involve the empirical testing of these propositions – the methodological tools to be used are detailed in the final section.

## Knowledge-based view of the firm

This study is grounded in the knowledge-based view of the firm. This recently developed theory argues that knowledge is the firm’s most valuable resource. Producing unique products and services, or producing them at a lower cost than competitors is based on superior knowledge of the production process and superior design. In fact, prominent authors such as Drucker (1994), Grant (1996) and Spender (2003) suggest that knowledge is perhaps the only true source of competitive advantage.

Much tension exists as to the different views of knowledge. This debate is essentially centred on whether knowledge can be captured, stored and transferred. Many people feel that if something is to be managed, it must be able to be quantified, counted, organised and measured (Glazer, 1998), and it must be able to be built, owned, controlled and its value maximised (Davenport, 1997). This view of ‘management’ has influenced attempts to manage knowledge by quantifying, capturing and controlling it as an object. Consequently, the emerging field of KM has also attracted some criticism, much of which relates to the perceived over-emphasis on codifying and storing knowledge as data. There is a real danger that KM is just becoming another label for IS rather than an innovative attempt to increase knowledge in the organisation (Goldkuhl and Barf, 2002). More recent developments have recognised that this approach to the KM is too restrictive and that some aspects of knowledge cannot be captured. This debate has led to a confused picture of knowledge within the KM community as researchers and practitioners have sought to define knowledge, which can be captured and that which cannot be. For example, some commentators are of the opinion that tacit knowledge can be captured (Huang, 1997), or that it is merely ‘difficult’ to articulate (Teece, 1998). Others feel that it cannot be codified without being invalidated (Buckingham Shum, 1997), or that it simply cannot be captured or codified at all (Leonard-Barton and Sensiper, 1998).

There is a clear need for a different view of knowledge in order to overcome this challenge of managing knowledge, which cannot be captured, codified and stored. In this regard, Kimble and Hildreth (2004) argue that knowledge is not made up of mutually exclusive opposites of hard and soft (see Figure 1). Instead, they are mutually dependent where all knowledge is regarded as being both hard and soft. Only the proportions differ. Viewing knowledge in this way lends itself more easily to the notion that knowledge can be exchanged through digital media. It is recognised that higher levels of tacit knowledge can be exchanged through face-to-face contact. However, knowledge can be made more transportable by increasing the hard/explicit element in relation to the soft/tacit element.

Assuming that knowledge is a critical input to production processes, then competitive advantage stems from the ability to integrate the specialised knowledge of individuals (Nonaka, 1994; Spender, 2003; Grant and Baden-Fuller, 2004). Therefore, one of the key issues underlying the knowledge-based view of the firm is to understand how knowledge is integrated into the firm to create competitive advantage (Hansen and Kautz, 2004). Informal networks such as NoPs are rapidly gaining attention with this view of the firm. Knowledge may be the organisation’s most valuable resource; however, no firm can possess all the knowledge it requires. Therefore, it must look outside its formal boundaries (Wasko and Faraj, 2005). Nooteboom (2004) suggests that in dynamic fields, organisational innovations derive from knowledge exchange and learning from network connections that cross organisational boundaries. An organisation’s members benefit through informal interaction by acquiring knowledge they did not have.

## Limitations of KM systems

Information and communication technologies have been proposed as effective tools to support knowledge sharing. KM systems (KMS) refer to a class of IS designed specifically to facilitate the generation, integration, sharing and dissemination of organisational knowledge (Tsui, 2003). While not all KM initiatives involve the use of ICTs, and warnings against an emphasis on the use of ICTs for KM are not uncommon (Davenport, 2005), many KM initiatives rely on ICTs as enablers. In fact, Smith and McKeen (2003) report that managers found that there was a bias in their firms towards using technology to accomplish KM goals over more humanistic approaches. KM can be supported with an array of ICTs, including the Internet, intranet, groupware, data warehouses, computer-based yellow pages, simulation tools, intelligent agents, videoconferencing and so forth. These systems enable knowledge exchange to occur regardless of location, reducing the importance of geographic and time constraints. To date, organisational KMS have focused on two processes: creating knowledge repositories and databases of best practices that are accessible by all employees of the organisation, and creating knowledge maps to generate knowledge flows by connecting experts with knowledge seekers.

Despite the obvious advantages of KMS, a number of shortcomings have been identified in the literature. A fundamental problem is that current technologies for knowledge workers are not doing a good job of capturing their knowledge. As Davenport (2005) puts it, ‘The dreamythat knowledge itself – typically unstructured, textual knowledge – could be easily captured, shared, and applied to knowledge worky[has not] been fully realisedyProgress is being madey[but] it’s taken much longer than anyone expected.’ Coakes et al. (2002) also suggest that KMS may be useful to share and disseminate information, or explicit knowledge, but the extent to which they can achieve real sharing of tacit knowledge and experience, and fostering of a learning organisation, is debatable. In fact, McAfee (2006) suggests that we should refer to ‘knowledge management systems’ in the past tense as they do not show up on recent surveys of media used by knowledge workers. Most current platforms, such as KMS, informational portals, intranets and workflow applications, are highly structured from the start, and users have little opportunity to influence this structure (McAfee, 2006).

Kelly and Jones (2001) investigated the use of a groupware system to share knowledge in a large professional services organisation. The company in question invested in a Notes-based application called ‘TrackApp’ that stores details of all interactions with clients, project descriptions and documentation, and staff skill profiles. The investment enjoyed limited success partly due to the formal nature in which the system was used. Many employees expressed anxiety about the possible consequences of making information about work activities publicly available in TrackApp for fear that this might be interpreted and used by colleagues in other offices. As a result, the information posted was usually uncontroversial, sanitised accounts of their work. Others even avoided divulging their knowledge, in case they would be criticised by their colleagues. There are plenty of other examples that illustrate that simply investing in IT does not increase knowledge sharing. Stewart (2000) suggests that knowledge repositories preserve more knowledge than create it. Likewise, 50% to 70% of KM projects fail to meet objectives, and this has been attributed to over reliance on IT (Baxter, 2006). Kelly and Jones (2001) argue that management needs to cultivate communities that share a social context within which they can interpret the contributions of others; norms and behaviours that reduce feelings of vulnerability and promote trust; and a sense of mutual solidarity as a motivation for participation.

Some scholars (Sutton, 2000; Wasko and Faraj, 2005) suggest that these shortcomings of KMS are due to the failure to recognise the importance of knowledge workers in context. Thus, these systems do not reflect the actual knowledge exchange practices in organisations. Research on work practices has consistently shown that social relationships are an important factor in knowledge flows (or lack of them). However, the twist is that individuals generally form these relationships not according to what the formal organisation dictates, but based on personal biases and preferences for others, who may be either inside or outside the firm. These relationships are the basis for informal, naturally occurring networks or NoPs. Organisations such as Xerox and PricewaterhouseCoopers are discovering that even when KMS are available, people tend to rely on people for expertise. For this reason, employees are exploiting interactive communication technologies to develop networks of people with expertise and interest around a specific area of practice, rather than utilising and contributing to static knowledge repositories. The BBC is one organisation that has seen success from its NoP initiatives. Rather than investing in an IT-based KM system, it installed simple bulletin board technology in order to move knowledge around rapidly. The site currently gets 450,000 page views a month from 8,000 unique users – startling in an organisation of 25,000. The primary role of the forum is to ask questions and get answers, but according to the BBC’s Chief Knowledge Officer, ‘the board wouldn’t have taken off if it were restricted to the dry discussions of pure business. The board has entered the daily life of BBC employees because it’s fun and interesting as well as useful’ (Weinberger, 2005).

## A conceptual framework

When individuals have a common practice, knowledge readily flows across that practice, enabling individuals to create social networks to support knowledge exchange (Brown and Duguid, 2000). In fact, Brown and Duguid (2000) conclude that the key to competitive advantage is a firm’s ability to coordinate autonomous CoPs internally and leverage the knowledge that flows into these communities from network connections.

Recent empirical findings by Teigland and Wasko (2003) highlight the contribution NoPs can make to the innovative capacity of an organisation. They found that high reliance by individuals on internal CoPs as sources of help results in lower levels of creativity. This suggests that the knowledge of an individual’s local CoP may be largely redundant and that the use of this local knowledge, although efficient owing to a shared practice, does not appear to positively impact individual performance. On the other hand, participation in intra-organisational distributed NoPs enhances creativity as evidenced by the positive relationship between internal knowledge trading and both creativity and efficient performance. To alleviate the stagnant nature of internal CoPs, this paper proposes that ENoPs can be used to create ‘bridging links’ between strong tie communities to enhance the flow of new ideas and knowledge. Electronic networks have certain advantages over social networks, mainly in rapidly transferring explicit knowledge, rapidly developing weak ties and greatly reducing communication costs (Grandori and Soda, 1995).

A conceptual model that connects the above views together is presented in Figure 2. Internal to each organisation are a number of CoPs. Through conversation, mentoring, war stories, etc., CoP members help each other to make sense of ambiguous, problem-centred situations. As a result of the increased access of information and communication technologies, members of internal CoPs may also be members of external NoPs. Individuals participating in externally distributed NoPs are able to act as bridges between local CoPs, accessing non-redundant knowledge from other locations and integrating it with knowledge of their own (Teigland and Wasko, 2003). According to Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998), innovation occurs when knowledge from previously separated domains is exchanged and combined in new ways. This is what can happen when internal CoPs interact with inter-organisational ENoPs. As an example, a pharmaceutical engineer is faced with a complex problem. To gain the knowledge to solve this problem, he turns to his local CoP. His co-located colleagues may be able to offer suggestions and the problem is solved. Then again, the highly efficient structures that support knowledge integration in a CoP may evolve into core rigidities and competency traps – inappropriate knowledge sets that preserve the status quo and limit new insights (Leonard-Barton and Sensiper, 1998). In this example, the problem is not solved internally, so the engineer turns to his ENoP. He posts details of the problem on the ENoP bulletin board and seeks help. Other ENoP participants, who may even be from rival firms, post up responses and interact with our engineer. These interactions may lead to idea generation, innovation adoption, a new way of thinking or even a novel solution. The knowledge garnered from the ENoP can then be integrated back into the engineer’s internal CoP, raising the knowledge pool to a higher level.

A key question for researchers and practitioners is how to turn an empty electronic space into a vital, active forum devoted to knowledge exchange (Teigland and Wasko, 2004). Much of the emerging literature in the ENoP field aims to understand the reasons why individuals participate and contribute knowledge to these electronic networks when the optimal/rational action would be to ‘free ride’ off the efforts of others (von Hippel and Von Krogh, 2003; Teigland and Wasko, 2004; Wasko and Faraj, 2005; Roberts et al., 2006; Shah, 2006). This paper examines ENoPs from a different angle and asks – what is the relationship between network communication structure and knowledge exchange?

Structural property concepts are reviewed in the following section.

## Social network theory

In the field of social psychology, an important tradition of study on networks is that of social network theory. Social network theory views social relationships in terms of nodes and ties, where nodes are the individual actors within the networks and ties are the relationships between the actors. It indicates the ways in which they are connected through various social familiarities ranging from casual acquaintance to close familial bonds. In its most simple form, a social network is a map of all of the relevant ties between the nodes being studied (see Figure 3).

Figure 3 A social network map.

The power of social network theory stems from its difference to traditional sociological studies, which assume that it is the attributes of individual actors that matter. Social network theory produces an alternate view, where the attributes of individuals are less important than their relationships and ties with other actors within the network. It suggests that at least some properties and outcomes of a social network are a function of its complete structure and are not reducible to either an individual actor or a single link (Degenne and Forse, 1999).

The structural properties of the social network help determine the network’s usefulness to its individuals. When talking about the structural properties, what is meant is the impact of group communication structure on collective performance outcomes. Structural properties refer to concepts such as density, connectedness, centrality, core/ periphery structure, coreness, symmetry, closeness, etc. The structure of the social network helps determine a network’s usefulness to its individuals. Smaller, tighter networks can be less useful to their members than networks with lots of loose connections to individuals outside the main network. More ‘open’ networks, with many weak ties and social connections, are more likely to introduce new ideas and opportunities to their members than closed networks with many redundant ties. For the purposes of this paper, three structural properties (i.e. core/periphery structure, connectedness and density) have been selected, and a proposed relationship with successful knowledge exchange developed.

## Core/periphery structure

A network has a core/periphery structure if it can be partitioned into two sets: a core whose members are densely tied to each other, and a periphery whose members have more ties to core members than to each other (Schenkel et al., 2001). Research into core/periphery structure has a long tradition dating back to the 1940s and 1950s. This body of literature consistently shows that networks with a strong core/periphery structure are better for the diffusion of routine information, while networks with weak core/periphery structure are better for solving complex tasks (see Figures 4 and 5). For example, Bavelas (1948) showed that ‘communication nets’ with centralised structures (e.g. wheel) improved the diffusion of information in simple tasks, whereas decentralised structures (e.g. circle) delayed the diffusion of information. Groups with decentralised communication nets took less time to finish complex tasks than groups with a centralised structure (Shaw, 1964). More recently, Cummings and Cross (2003) found that strong core/periphery structure was negatively related to group performance for complex, non-routine work. ## Connectedness

Perhaps, the most fundamental aspect of an NoP is mutual engagement. Through engagement, individuals participate in narration, collaboration and social construction. The result of this interaction is a complex network of social relations and interdependency. In social network analysis, connectivity is the extent to which actors in the network are linked to one another by direct or indirect ties (Monge and Contractor, 2003). This is sometimes measured by the maximum, or average, path distance between any two actors in the network. Several empirical studies have shown that higher levels of communication and information gathering are associated with higher levels of performance in R&D project groups (Katz and Allen, 1982; Pelz and Andrews, 1966). Therefore, one can expect that higher levels of connectivity between individuals in an ENoP will result in more successful knowledge exchange.

Proposition 2: Higher levels of connectivity between ENoP participants will be positively associated with successful knowledge exchange for complex, non-routine work.

## Density

Density in social network terms is the ratio of the number of actual links to the number of possible links. The density of a network measures the degree of cohesion in the group (Wasserman and Faust, 1999). Schenkel et al. (2001) suggest that a dense network has far more power in terms of influence and transmitting tacit knowledge. A dense network consists of individuals who are, for the most part, directly connected to each other, rather than connected through intermediaries. Therefore, a dense network holds the ability to improve the speed and flexibility within which information and knowledge diffuse within a group. This researcher proposes that the more direct relationships an individual has with other ENoP participants, the more likely they are to acquire the desired knowledge.

Proposition 3: The density of ENoPs will be positively associated with successful knowledge exchange for complex, non-routine work.

## Future research

ENoP research is still in its infancy, and we know relatively little about the dynamics of knowledge exchange in these significant organisational forms. Previous research has been limited by the fact that researchers have tended to concentrate on only one ENoP, usually internal to one organisation (Teigland and Wasko, 2004; Wasko and Faraj, 2005). Empirical evidence that links ENoP structures to performance is needed as previous research in the social network field shows that informal methods of knowledge transfer are more effective than formal processes (Burt, 1992; Grant and Baden-Fuller, 2004). As a step in this direction, this research-in-progress will address the following question: what is the relationship between ENoP structural properties and knowledge exchange for complex, non-routine work? Previous research will be extended by analysing multiple categories of knowledge workers across multiple organisations.

The first stage of this research will be to identify six to eight inter-organisational ENoPs. For example, one might be an open-source software network; another might be a discussion forum for chemical engineers, etc. Once identified, the independent variables of core/periphery structure, connectedness and density of these ENoPs will be measured using a tool called social network analysis. This tool provides both a graphical display of the network as well as precise mathematical measures of its structure. According to advocates of SNA, patterns will become visible, and they can be used to better understand the relationships between individuals, assess the health of a group and identify which individuals are playing key roles within a network (Scott, 2000). The second stage will involve measuring the dependent variable – the success of the knowledge exchanged. Successful knowledge exchange in ENoPs is determined by both the quantity of knowledge exchanged as well as the quality. Knowledge quantity will be measured by a process called content analysis. The number of questions and responses posted on the various bulletin boards, online forums, email lists, etc., will be recorded over a 4-month period. To measure the quality of knowledge exchanged in an ENoP, an expert from each field will rate the usefulness of responses posted. A survey of ENoP participants will serve as a second measure of knowledge quality. For example, participants can be asked how useful they found the ENoP for solving complex problems. To test the propositions, the various measures of ENoP structural properties will be related to the measures for successful knowledge exchange.

## Conclusion

A review of the extant NoP literature shows that a clear conceptual framework is lacking for these increasingly important organisational forms. This research represents an initial attempt at the development of such a framework. The conceptual framework developed here is unique as it focuses on the relationship between the communication structure of ENoPs and knowledge exchange. A review of the literature has shown that scant attention has been paid to this relationship by previous NoP researchers. Understanding this relationship is essential in order to turn these empty electronic spaces into vital active forums dedicated to knowledge exchange. This paper posits that there is a relationship between the structural properties of ENoPs and successful knowledge exchange. The knowledge-based view of the firm and social network theory are used to justify this claim. The knowledge-based view of the firm argues that knowledge is the firm’s most important strategic asset, whereas the social network theory shows that network outcomes are determined by its structure. A conceptual framework that links a firm’s competitive advantage to the interaction of face-to-face CoPs and inter-organisational ENoPs has been presented. Propositions which relate ENoP structures to successful knowledge exchange have been developed. Future research will see the application and testing of these propositions on multiple categories of knowledge workers across multiple organisations.

## About the author

Eoin Whelan is a lecturer in IS at the National University of Ireland, Galway. He is also undertaking a Ph.D. with the Centre for Innovation & Structural Change in the same institution, studying innovation in electronic NoPs. Prior to joining NUI Galway in 2004, Eoin worked as a management accountant in Ireland, New Zealand and the United States. He earned his Master’s degree by research from the University of Limerick in 2002 and was awarded the Government of Ireland Scholarship in Humanities and Social Sciences for this research. His research interests include all aspects of KM systems, ICT-enabled forms of collaboration, and IS investment evaluation.
