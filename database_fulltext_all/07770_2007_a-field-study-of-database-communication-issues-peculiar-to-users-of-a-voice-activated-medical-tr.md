# A field study of database communication issues peculiar to users of a voice activated medical tracking application

James A. Rodger <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Parag C. Pendharkar <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Indiana University of Pennsylvania, MIS and Decision Sciences, Eberly College of Business and Information Technology, Indiana, PA 15705, USA

<sup>b</sup> Information Systems, School of Business Administration, Capital College, Pennsylvania State University, 777 W. Harrisburg Pike, Middletown, PA 17057, USA

Received 28 July 2005; received in revised form 11 May 2006; accepted 14 August 2006 Available online 22 September 2006

## Abstract

This paper investigates database communication issues peculiar to users of a voice activated medical tracking application (VAMTA). Specifically, this study investigates voice activated user–database interaction, in terms of user gender, speech speed in words per minute (WPM) and technical computer experience of the users. A VAMTA device is employed to help solve the business and clinical needs of medical personnel, and to help them facilitate communication and interaction with database systems and designers, who are interested in improving technical specifications. The VAMTA device was integrated into a Dragon Naturally Speaking commercial system. The ability of the VAMTA to interact effectively and efficiently with the database of medical patient signs and symptoms has enhanced the productive usage of database systems that are vital components of the medical organization information systems. In this paper, we address the communication issues in voice activated user–database interaction and the philosophical perspectives of database modeling, by identifying gender and experience factors as important barriers and issues tha must be further investigated to improve communication between user and database systems. Our research studies the impact of user's gender, speech speed in WPM and user's computer experience on the performance of a speech recognition system. We also investigated the interactive effects of gender and user, user and WPM, and gender and WPM. Our research investigates 33 users; a voice activated medical tracking application and a mobile healthcare fieldwork environment. The field study suggests that VAMTA can be applied to either an emergency medical services or preventative care setting. We illustrate that the user's gender and computer experience has a significant impact $( p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ on the use of voice interface as an input to a medical database of patient signs and symptoms. WPM and the interaction of gender and WPM show a weak impact $( p { < } 0 . 1 0 )$ on system performance. © 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Database; Voice activation; Human–computer interaction; Field study; Decision Support System

## 1. Introduction

This research builds on a previous study that investigated the development of a continuous voice recognition device [50]. The present research utilizes information and experiments from an original field study to investigate Voice activated user–database interaction is a special case of user–computer interaction. Voice activated user–database interaction focuses on the voice recognition communication issues between users and database systems, from both a design and development viewpoint. Spoken dialogue systems allow users to interact with computer-based applications such as databases and expert systems by using natural spoken languages. Although spoken dialogue systems can be traced back to the 1950s, it is only within the last decade that advances in speech technology have allowed for large scale working systems that can effectively interact with commercial databases [39]. Further, researchers have developed approaches such as the mixed initiative dialogue and the dialogue planning algorithm as an acoustic user interface to databases [24]. These advances in database communications with voice activated user interfaces are impacted by issues peculiar to end-users. These issues include user gender and technical computer experience of the users [50].

Automated speech recognition (SR) has been a subject of research for over five decades, and most of the research in SR has focused on technical and implementation issues of these systems [35,40,48]. Although the impact of gender in human–computer interaction has been noted recently, the differences between female and male voices have been recognized by only a few researchers [34], and very few studies have used gender as a variable to compare the performance of SR systems.

Most “gender” related studies in human–computer interaction (HCI) were concerned with “gender” manifested in the voice output as opposed to the voice input [34,42]. The manifestation of the gender as a voice input is important as most SR software digitize spoken words, identify individual sounds (phonemes), and use mathematical models to select phrases and discrete words [48]. Thus, it is likely that the “gender” of the voice input may have an impact on the performance of SR systems.

Several studies have shown that performance of SR system improves with the user experience. It has been suggested that one way to improve the performance of SR system is to train its users [48]. Four characteristics of a user in the field work environments have been identified [46]. These characteristics are dynamic user configuration, limited attention capacity, high-speed interaction, and context dependency. While it may be true that the relative importance of these factors vary, with the type of fieldwork, a technology savvy/trained user may be able benefit most from the mobile computing devices than a naïve user.

The objective of our research is to study the impact of gender, speech speed (WPM), user's technical experience and their interactions, on the performance of SR system in a mobile field environment. We use a healthcare setting as our mobile fieldwork environment, and a voice activated medical tracking application (VAMTA) as our SR system. The performance of VAMTA is measured in terms of correct word recognitions. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the healthcare fieldwork, the VAMTA prototype, and the database interaction. We also propose six hypotheses in this section. Section 3 provides the overview of the field study and presents the results of our hypotheses tests. Section 4 concludes the paper with the results and conclusions.

## 2. The Healthcare fieldwork environment, voice activated medical tracking application in health care and issues in speech recognition as a database communication interface

Several authors have investigated appropriate application areas for natural language interfaces (NLIs) to databases [12,55]. NLIs can be compared with competing approaches, including other user friendly interfaces and training of users with less user friendly interfaces such as complicated spreadsheets, statistical packages or programming languages. It is suggested that as users become more familiar with NLIs, they can attain flexibility that is unattainable by simpler interfaces [3]. While NLIs have become increasingly sophisticated, practical application of these technologies have been limited [22,41,58]. NLIs are more expensive to develop than a menu or form-based interface [1]. There are several costs associated with a NLI [12]. One cost is an up front cost of developing the system. Another cost is associated with the organizational and application specific costs of customizing the NLI for the area needed, such as a medical database [5,15]. Finally, if a system has few users, then it may be cheaper to train these users to work with a less user friendly interface. With many users to train, an NLI can reduce the training costs [1].

In order to understand the various aspects of speech technology and its usage in health care, it is necessary to describe recent management of information (MOI) trends within this industry. As the technological infrastructure of organizations becomes increasingly complex [25], information technology (IT) is increasingly being used to improve coordination of activities within and across organizations [10]. Computers and video networks provide long-distance healthcare through medical connectivity. Connectivity can lead to shared care that comprises the continued, coordinated, and integrated activities of different people from different institutions applying different methods in different time frames, all in a combined effort to aid patients medically, psychologically, and socially in the most beneficial ways [16].

IT in healthcare is used primarily for reporting, electronic information sharing and connectivity, and staff and equipment scheduling. Reporting is important in a healthcare setting because patient lives depend on accurate and timely information [49]. Functional departments within the healthcare facility must be able to access and report new information in order to respond properly to changes in the healthcare environment [36]. Four types of information are reported in a healthcare facility:

• Scientific and technical information

• Patient-care information

• Customer satisfaction information

• Administrative information [51].

Scientific and technical information provides the knowledge base for identifying, organizing, retrieving, analyzing, delivering, and reporting clinical and managerial journal literature, reference information, and research data for use in designing, managing, and improving patient-specific and departmental processes [29]. Patient-care information is specific data and information on patients that is essential for maintaining accurate medical records of the patients' medical histories and physical examinations. Maintaining accurate information about patient-care results and discharges is imperative to delivering quality healthcare [6]. Customer satisfaction information is gathered from surveys and takes into account socio-demographic characteristics, physical and psychological status, attitudes, and expectations concerning medical care, the outcome of treatment, and the healthcare setting [38]. Administrative information is necessary to determine the degree of risk involved in financing expansion of services [14].

Coupling computer recognition of the human voice with a natural language processing system makes speech recognition by computers possible. “Speech recognition is the process of recognizing and appropriately responding to the meaning embedded within human speech. Human speech consists of a series of individual sounds called phonemes, roughly corresponding to the sounds of each letter of the alphabet. A spoken word is a series of interconnected phonemes. Continuous speech is a series of phonemes interspersed with periods of silence. Recognizing individually voiced (spoken) phonemes is not a difficult computational problem. Sound can be captured in analog form by a microphone, converted to digital form by digital sampling, and the resulting digital pattern compared to a library of patterns corresponding to known phonemens” [8]. This SR process involves the conversion of analog human voice sound waves to digital sampling through phoneme recognition. By allowing data and commands to be entered into a computer without the need for typing, and computer understanding of naturally spoken languages frees human hands for other tasks. Speech recognition by computers can also increase the rate of data entry, improve spelling accuracy, permits remote access to databases utilizing wireless technology, and ease access to computer systems by those who lack typing skills.

The VAMTA has been developed to improve the database interface, by enhancing the electronic management of patient data. Typically, medical data is entered by hand onto paper forms and later transcribed into a word processor to create a finished report. The process of manual note-taking and entering data via keyboard into a computer database is time-consuming, inefficient, and prone to error. For example, transcription errors are the eighth leading cause of death, in the United States, and lead to increased iatrogenic induced morbidity and mortality. Examples of poor handwriting in a medical setting have lead to the prescription of inappropriate medications and synergistic drug interactions [51]. To remedy these problems, the VAMTA prototype was developed, allowing data to be entered into portable laptop computers to collect medical data. However, the need for mobility by medical personnel, and the inability to have both hands free to type during a medical encounter make the use of laptop computers during a walk-around survey difficult. Clearly, a hands-free, space-saving mode of data entry that would also enable examiners to access pertinent information during a medical encounter was desirable. VAMTA prototype was developed to fill this need.

The VAMTA prototype was developed to replace existing, inefficient, repetitive medical data entry procedures with a fully automated, voice interactive system for voice-activated data input. In pursuit of this goal, the VAMTA team developed a lightweight, wearable, voice-interactive prototype capable of capturing, storing, processing, and forwarding data to a server for easy retrieval by users. The voice interactive data input and output capability of VAMTA reduces obstacles to accurate and efficient data access and reduces the time required to complete a medical encounter. VAMTA's voice interactive technology allows a trainee to interact with a computerized system and still have hands and eyes free to manipulate materials and negotiate his or her environment. Once entered, medical encounter data can be used for local reporting requirements and decisionmaking intelligence. Improved data acquisition and transmission capabilities allow connectivity with other systems. Existing printed and computerized surveys are voice activated and reside on the miniaturized computing device. VAMTA has been designed to allow voice prompting by the medical program, as well as voiceactivated, free-text dictation. An enhanced microphone system permits improved signal detection in noisy environments. All of these capabilities will contribute to the improved efficiency and accuracy of the data collection and retrieval process by medical personnel.

In order to comprehend speech user interface applications in healthcare, and to provide a baseline for this field study, it is necessary to provide a short history, as well as the advantages and disadvantages of this technology. Since 1987, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has provided standards to evaluate new voice interactive technologies [44]. The task associated with applying these standards was to implement automatic speech recognition technology by determining the lowest word error rate [19,21,26,44]. Excellent performance was achieved at several sites, both domestic and abroad [47]. For example, IBM-developed systems achieved the lowest overall word error rate of 13.5%. The application of statistical significance tests indicated that the differences in performance between systems designed by IBM, the French National Laboratories' Laboratoire d'Informatique pour la Mechanique et les Sciences de l'Ingenieur, and Cambridge University's Hidden Markov Model Toolkit software were not significant [45]. Lai [33] also reported that no significant differences existed in the comprehension of synthetic speech among five different speech-totext engines used. Finally, speaker segmentation has been used to locate all boundaries between speakers in the audio signal. It enables speaker normalization and adaptation techniques to be used effectively to integrate speech recognition [7].

Speech technology has been applied to medical applications, particularly emergency medical care that depends on quick and accurate access of patient background information [32]. The U.S. Defense Advance Research Projects Agency organized the Trauma Care Information Management System (TCIMS) Consortium to develop a prototype system for improving the timeliness, accuracy, and completeness of medical documentation. One outcome of TCIMS was the adoption of a speech–audio user interface for the prototype [28]. Even though applications of speech recognition technology have been developed with increased frequency, the field is still in its infancy, and many limitations have yet to be resolved. For example, the success of speech recognition by desktop computers depends on the integration of speech technologies with the underlying processor and operating system and the complexity and availability of tools required to deploy a system. This limitation has had an impact on application development [37,59].

Use of speech recognition technology in high-noise environments remains a challenge. For speech recognition systems to function properly, clean speech signals are required, with high signal-to-noise ratio and wide frequency response [2,18,53,57]. The microphone system is critical in providing the required speech signal, and, therefore, has a direct effect on the accuracy of the speech recognition system [4,56]. However, providing a clean speech signal can be difficult in high-noise environments. Interference, changes in the user's voice, and additive noise–such as car engine noise, background chatter, and white noise–can reduce the accuracy of speech recognition systems. In military environments, additive noise and voice changes are common. For example, in military aviation, the stress resulting from low-level flying can cause a speaker's voice to change, reducing recognition accuracy [11].

The control of the speech recognition interface poses its own unique problems [23,54]. The inability of people to remember verbal commands is even more of a hindrance than their inability to remember keyboard commands [43]. The limited quality of machine speech output also affects the speech recognition interface. As human–machine interaction becomes increasingly commonplace, applications that require unlimited vocabulary speech output are demanding text-to-speech systems that produce more human-sounding speech [27]. The accuracy of modeling has also limited the effectiveness of speech recognition. Modeling accuracy can be improved, however, by combining feature streams with neural nets and Gaussian mixtures [17]. The application of knowledge-based speech analysis has also shown promise [30].

Pallett et al. [44] pointed out that potential problems associated with the search and retrieval of relevant information from databases have been addressed by the Spoken Document Retrieval community. Furthermore, standards for the probability of false alarms and miss probabilities are set forth and investigated by the Topic Detection and Tracking program [13]. Decision Error Trade-off plots are used to demonstrate the trade-off between the miss probabilities and false alarm probabilities for a topic [31]. Security issues and speech verification are major voids in speech recognition technology [20]. It is well recognized that speech is not presently valued as an archival information source because it is impossible to locate information in large audio archives [31].

From the proceeding literature review, it can be seen that continuous voice recognition software and its interaction with databases, is a developing research area that requires further investigation. Before this technique can be applied to routine practice, in both business and clinical medical scenarios, further research is necessary. In order to address some of these issues, we have endeavored to study a group of users in a mobile healthcare fieldwork environment. In the current research, we investigate the impact of the user's gender, user's speech speed (WPM) and user's experience with technology on system performance. We also studied the interaction effects between gender and experience, gender and speech speed, and experience and speech speed, on the performance of speech recognition system. Particularly, we pose the following hypotheses and test them using a field study.

H1. The user's gender will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

H2. The user's experience with technology will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

H3. The user's speech speed, in WPM, will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

H4. The interaction between the user's experience with technology and the user's gender will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

H5. The interaction between the user's experience with technology and user's speech speed in WMP will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

H6. The interaction between the user's speech speed in WPM and user's gender will have an impact on the correct word recognitions by VAMTA.

## 3. Field study, method, and results

A pilot field study was initiated to investigate and test end-user performance on using the VAMTA. A total of 33 end-users were used in this phase to test the VAMTA. Testing of the VAMTA started on 8 March 2002 and ended on 1 April 2002. The purpose of this pilot study was to evaluate end-users of VAMTA in a preventive healthcare setting, as well as to collect data for use in a preliminary assessment of VAMTA. The applicability of the system was accessed by system performance and the ability of VAMTA to enter and retrieve data from an Access database of patient signs and symptoms. Over 50 patient names and data were stored in the database. Although the database contained similar names, there was no reported misrecognition due to these similarities.

The study procedure and experimental apparatus are described in detail in the following methods section. A copy of the script also appears in Appendix A. A convenience sample of 33 individuals volunteered to test the VAMTA, including 11 females and 22 males with various levels of experience and education. Experience ranged from test subjects who had never seen the application before they tested it to developers who built the application. A number of test subjects had limited medical backgrounds. Education ranged from high school graduates to PhDs.

Fujitsu 3400S pen tablet computers with the Microsoft Windows 98 operating system were used for all testing. CAT 51 microphones were used. CAT 51 microphones allow for hands-free, voice-activated computer operation in high-noise environments.

Each test subject was shown a demonstration of the VAMTA application prior to testing. Test subjects were then required to build a new user account and speech profile. Subjects ran through the application once using a test script to become familiar with the application. Next, the test subjects went through the application again while being videotaped. No corrections were made to dictated text during the videotaping. This allowed the tracking of voice dictation accuracy for each user with a new speech profile. Test subjects completed the entire process in an average of 2 h.

The performance of VAMTA during testing was measured in terms of voice accuracy, voice accuracy total errors, duration with data entry by voice, and duration with data entry by keyboard and mouse. Voice accuracy and voice accuracy total errors were measured by comparing a test script and the subject's printout of responses. Duration of the data entry by voice and by manual method was made operational, by reviewing and measuring these activities after they were recorded on audio and video tapes, by a camcorder. Viewed together, these statistics provide a snapshot of the accuracy, speed, and overall effectiveness of the VAMTA system and its interaction with the database.

Each test subject's printout was compared with a test script printout for accuracy. When discrepancies occurred between the subject's printout and the test script, the printouts were compared with the audio and video recordings, to determine whether the test subjects said the words properly, stuttered or mumbled words, and/or followed the test script properly. Misrecognitions occurred when the test subject pronounced a word properly and the speech program recorded the wrong word. These misrecognitions were verified by reviewing the audio and video tapes.

The accuracy of voice recognition, confirmed by videotaped records of test sessions, averaged 97.6%, with 6 misrecognitions. The minimum average voice recognition was 85%, with 37 misrecognitions. The maximum average voice recognition was 99.6%, with only 1 word misrecognition. Median voice recognition was 98.4%, with 4 misrecognitions. Total errors include both misrecognitions and human errors. Human errors occurred when a test subject mispronounced a word, stuttered, or mumbled. The total accuracy rate of the VAMTA, for 33 end users, was 95.4%. The minimum accuracy was 85%, the maximum accuracy was 99.2% and the median accuracy was 96%. Human error accounted for 2.2% of the total errors within the application.

The duration of each test subject's voice dictation was recorded to determine the average length of time required to complete a medical encounter while using the VAMTA. The average time required to complete a medical encounter in which data entry was conducted by voice was 8 min and 31 s. The shortest time was 4 min and 54 s, the median time was 6 min and 59 s and the longest time was 32 min and 51 s. Descriptive statistics for gender and WPM were not collected.

All 33 of the test subjects entered medical encounter information into the VAMTA only by voice, in a secluded room, in order to minimize the detrimental effects of environmental noise on speech recognition. In addition, 22 test subjects (11 females and 11 males of varying technology experience) entered the same medical encounter information, into the database, using a keyboard and mouse. Voice experiments were conducted prior to the manual methods. Because the subjects could correct errors as they typed, there were no manual test errors. In addition, there may have been some learning curve effects, as the same participants had previously entered data, via voice. The average time required to complete a medical encounter in which data entry was conducted with keyboard and mouse was 15 min and 54 s. The shortest time was 7 min and 52 s, the median time was 15 min and 31 s and the longest time was 24 min and 42 s.

The average duration of sessions in which data entry was performed by voice dictation was compared to the average duration of sessions in which data entry was performed with a keyboard and mouse. On average, less time was required to complete the documentation of a medical encounter using VAMTA when data entry was performed by voice instead of with a keyboard and mouse. The average time saved using voice versus a keyboard and mouse was 7 min and 52 s per medical encounter. The duration of each medical encounter included the dictation and printing of the entire Chronological Record of Medical Care form, a Poly Prescription form, and a Radiologic Consultation Request/Report form.

In order to test our six hypotheses, we performed a univariate Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) to determine the relationship between correct classifications (dependent variable) and the independent variables gender (male/female), user experience with technology (medium/high) and speaker speed (continuous covariate) in WPM. We also tested interaction effects between user's gender and his/her experience with technology, user experience and speech speed in WPM and gender and speech speed. The three-way interaction (Sex × WPM × User) was not an original hypothesis of the research. The results indicate that four of the six hypotheses were accepted. The R-squared for the model was .563. This indicates that model-independent variables explain 56.3% of the variance in the dependent variable. The results of the analysis, as well as the individual contributions of each independent variable factor, are shown in Table 1.

The results from Table 1 indicate that user's gender, and user's experience in technology play an important role for determining performance of VAMTA. Speech speed in WPM and the interaction of gender and speech speed also contributed to the system performance. The proposed model shows a good fit, with a R-squared= .563 (adjusted R-squared= .440).

## 4. Conclusions, limitations and future research opportunities

This study investigates voice activated user–database interaction, in terms of user gender, speech speed in WPM and technical computer experience of the users. A VAMTA device is employed to help solve the business and clinical needs of medical personnel, and to help them facilitate communication and interaction with database systems and designers, who are interested in improving technical specifications. The ability of the VAMTA to interact effectively and efficiently with the database of medical patient signs and symptoms has enhanced the productive usage of database systems, which are vital components of the medical organization information systems. In this paper, we addressed the communication issues in voice activated user–database interaction and the philosophical perspectives of database modeling, by identifying gender, speech speed and experience factors as important barriers and issues that must be further investigated to improve communication between user and database systems.

While our field study used a field study of 33 end-users in a relatively quiet preventative care setting, our research suggests that VAMTA can be applied to either an emergency medical services or preventative care setting. A possible future research area to extend this work is to test VAMTA in high noise contexts such as emergency care services, and surgical suites. Our results show that, in a mobile healthcare fieldwork environment, the user's gender and computer experience has a significant impact $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ) on the use of voice interface as an input to a medical database of patient signs and symptoms. This is a field study, designed to test the prototype VAMTA device. Since participation in the pilot testing was voluntary in nature and documentation of the experimental results was time consuming, it was difficult to muster a large sample, with equal gender group sizes, that mirrored clinicians delivering medical care in the field, to test our hypotheses. With three independent variables, a larger sample would have helped to overcome issues such as gender imbalance. Future studies, with a larger sample, more equal group sizes, and real health care providers, are needed to address these issues, in order to validate the results of this study and improve its external validity.

Lee [34] has noted that the characteristics of the female voice poses certain technical challenges, as an output. In our field study, we noticed a similar technical challenge when the female voice was used as an input. It is fairly obvious that voice pitch could be a factor in explaining why female SR is lower. Higher frequencies could have more static and greater attenuation. While the gender of the user appears to impact the performance of VAMTA, there are several other factors that may have impacted our results. For example, Lee [34] and Carli [9] argue that task factors should be considered when considering the impact of voice output.

The average time of the 33 users to complete the task was 8 min and 31 s, utilizing voice recognition. The average time to complete the assigned task, using the mouse and keyboard, was 15 min and 54 s. Overall, speech recognition outperformed the traditional mouse and keyboard by 7 min and 23 s. The weakly significant speech speed, in words per minute, indicates that the more natural and continuous the end-users dialogue, the better the NLI will perform. Our study also found a weak correlation between performance difference in speech speed between men and women. Future research may extend our study by considering task factors.

Certain language features may have impacted our results as well. Although the participants read from a script that included numbers, a majority of the SR revolved around language. Several authors have investigated appropriate application areas for natural language interfaces (NLIs) to databases [12,55]. NLIs can be compared with competing approaches, including other user friendly interfaces and training of users with less user friendly interfaces such as complicated spreadsheets, statistical packages or programming languages. It is suggested that as users become more familiar with NLIs, they can attain flexibility that is unattainable by simpler interfaces [3]. While NLIs have become increasingly sophisticated, practical application of these technologies have been limited [22,41,58], and NLIs are more expensive to develop than a menu or form-based interface [1]. It has been postulated that if a system has few users, then it may be cheaper to train these users to work with a less user friendly interface. However, with many users to train, an NLI can reduce the training costs [1]. Due to the limited sample size of our experiment, future research is needed to test this conjecture.

Future research may also focus on a more thorough analysis of errors. For example, it may be interesting to determine how many of the errors were caused by homonyms (e.g., “write” vs “right”), and how many were completely off-base (e.g., “desk” vs “deck”). Superiority of the voice to keyboard entry might also be partly attributable to the fact that subjects “read” the script, as opposed to “say” what they want. Although these examples may be viewed as a limitation of voice recognition, end-users must be trained to follow certain rules and procedures for accurate speech recognition, much like the policies that impact accurate manual entry of data. However, despite current limitations in speech recognition technology, the VAMTA prototype was successful in reducing the time needed to complete medical data entry, in supporting local reporting requirements, and in enhancing decision-making intelligence. End-user performance and productivity tests of the device were generally favorable. End-user results supported the fact that the prototype would save time and improve the quality of medical reports that are entered and retrieved from a database. Our study indicates that quality will be improved because timeliness is a component of the quality construct. The average duration of sessions in which data entry was performed by voice dictation was compared to the average duration of sessions in which data entry was performed with a keyboard and mouse, and on average, less time was required to complete the documentation of a medical encounter using VAMTA, when data entry was performed by voice instead of with a keyboard and mouse.
