# Health information technology and its impact on the quality and cost of healthcare delivery

Indranil R. Bardhan ⁎, Mark F. Thouin

Naveen Jindal School of Management, The University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75083-0688, USA

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 6 October 2012

Keywords: Health information technology Electronic medical record Process quality

## a b s t r a c t

The impact of health information technologies (HIT) on the quality of healthcare delivery is a topic of significant importance and recent research has yielded mixed evidence. We use archival data on HIT usage in combination with data on quality of care processes to conduct a three-year longitudinal study of a large panel of U.S. hospitals. Our analysis extends earlier research on the association between HIT and healthcare quality among healthcare providers that have previously focused on outcomes associated with cost reduction. We study the impact of HIT applications, not only on hospital operating expenses, but also on the process quality associated with evidence-based measures for treatment of four major health conditions. Our results indicate a positive association between usage of clinical information systems and patient scheduling applications and conformance with best practices for treatment of heart attacks, heart failures, and pneumonia. Our results also suggest that usage of <sup>fi</sup>nancial management systems is associated with lower hospital operating expenses. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and urban hospitals are more likely to exhibit greater conformance with process quality metrics, while for-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts exhibit lower operational expenses. Our results have important policy implications for investments in health IT and studying their cost and quality implications

## 1. Introduction

An important, and sometimes overlooked, dimension in the debate over healthcare reform is the quality angle. As part of the economic stimulus package enacted in February 2009, the US government has allocated approximately \$19 billion a year in funding for programs to help healthcare providers implement electronic health records (EHRs). These programs are designed to encourage adoption of IT-enabled systems and processes that will support computerization of physician orders, automate dispensing of drugs, and enable digital storage of patient records [38]. The intent is to improve the quality of care delivered by ensuring timely access to patient records, thus eliminating errors or duplicative tests and yielding signi<sup>fi</sup>cant savings. These incentives come with strings attached. The federal government will cut Medicare reimbursements to healthcare providers that do not attain the target goals speci<sup>fi</sup>ed under the “meaningful use” guidelines related to EHR adoption by 2015 [11].

The meaningful use guidelines, developed by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), entail speci<sup>fi</sup>c objectives and process-based measures to evaluate the extent to which healthcare providers attain these objectives. They re<sup>fl</sup>ect evidence-based best practices that are associated with the quality of care delivery by healthcare providers. Information technologies used in healthcare have the potential to improve both the quality and effectiveness of healthcare providers [26,32]. The purpose of this study is to examine the impact of usage of different types of hospital IT on the quality and cost associated with healthcare processes. While earlier research pri marily focused on the impact of healthcare IT on hospital costs, scant attention has been paid toward analyzing their effect on the outcomes associated with healthcare process quality [30].

In this study, we evaluate the impact of HIT usage on evidencebased, process-speci<sup>fi</sup>c measures of healthcare quality using a large panel of US hospitals from a recent three-year time period. While much attention has been focused on the overall hospital performance, such as patient mortality and re-admission rates, we believe that it is more important to focus on process-based measures that allow us to link the effect of IT usage on the quality of decisions made during the delivery of healthcare. Considering the government's meaningful use mandates for attaining speci<sup>fi</sup>ed goals on process-centric quality metrics, we need to trace the impact of health IT on operational decisions that have an impact on healthcare process quality. In addition to studying the effect of IT usage on the quality of care, we also examine their effect on hospital operational expenses, thereby providing additional insights into the relationship between investments in IT and costs associated with healthcare processes.

## 2. Background

We now summarize the current state of research on health IT and outcomes, as a prelude to our development of the theoretical foundation.

## 2.1. Summary of prior research

A recent, government-sponsored survey of more than 2600 doctors in the US, on their use of computerized patient records, indicates that 82% of doctors who do use EHRs feel that such records have improved the quality of clinical decisions, 86% report that they helped avoid medication errors, and 85% felt that they improved the delivery of preventative care [28]. A recent study on health IT adoption patterns found that states with more patient safety initiatives also exhibit higher IT adoption rates [16]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the study reports wide variations in the adoption of electronic medical records between for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t and not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t/government-run organizations; small, medium, and large hospitals; as well as between teaching and non-teaching institutions, and suggests that it is necessary to control for these variables in studying the relationship between technology adoption and hospital performance. Other studies report that only a small minority of US hospitals have implemented comprehensive electronic medical record (EMR) systems, and that capital requirements and high maintenance costs pose signi<sup>fi</sup>cant barriers to implementation [24].

Prior healthcare research in the literature has primarily focused on two areas: (1) understanding the factors related to the adoption of health IT [1,17,24], and (2) evaluating the productivity impact of health IT, while focusing on ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and cost savings associated with HIT usage. For instance, Devaraj and Kohli [14] found that HIT usage is associated with a reduction in patient mortality rates and greater hospital productivity measured as revenue generated per admission. Menon et al. [32] provide evidence of a positive impact of medical IT capital on hospital output as well as marginal hospital revenue, based on hospital data from the State of Washington. Recent studies by Menon et al. and Das et al. [10,33] <sup>fi</sup>nd that clinical information systems improve hospital output in the short run while administrative systems have a detrimental impact on hospital output in the long run.

More recently, there has been a growing body of research related to the impact of HIT on various aspects of healthcare services and outcomes. Based on a meta analysis of 257 published studies on the impact of HIT between 1999 and 2005, Chaudhry et al. [9] report that the major effect of HIT on the quality of care was its role in (a) increasing adherence to protocol-based care, through the use of decision-support tools that are embedded in EHRs or computerized provider order entry (CPOE) systems, and (b) reduction in adverse drug events and medication errors. The reported ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from these studies have been decreased utilization of care, while the effect of HIT on time utilization is mixed. Furthermore, most of the studies on multi-functional HIT systems are based on the data from four benchmark research institutions. Similarly, Goldzweig et al. [18] surveyed 179 studies between 2005 and 2007 and reported the prevalence of a large number of stand-alone HIT systems other than EHRs. While most of these studies have been published in clinical journals, their conclusions suggest that there is a growing momentum among health policy researchers to study the “return on investment” from HIT investments along multiple dimensions related to the cost, quality of care, ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of healthcare providers.

Linder et al. [27] studied the association between EHR use and the quality of ambulatory care in the United States. Based on their crosssectional study of patient visits in the 2003 and 2004 National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NAMCS), they found that there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference on 14 of the 17 quality indicators between visits that were associated with EHR usage and those without EHR usage. These quality measures measured whether patients received recommended care and included indicators such as medical management of common diseases, recommended antibiotic prescribing, preventive counseling, screening tests, and avoiding potentially inappropriate medication prescription among elderly patients. Only 2 of the 17 quality indicators reported signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better performance for visits with EHR usage versus those without EHR usage. In a similar vein, McCullough et al. [29] measured changes in the quality of care following the adoption of EHR systems using a national sample of U.S. hospitals from 2004 to 2007. Based on their study of six process quality measures associated with best-practice clinical care guidelines, they conclude that the usage of CPOE and EHR systems resulted in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements in only two process quality measures related to treatment of pneumonia, with larger effects reported among academic than non-academic hospitals.

Several studies have reported evidence of the positive impact of HIT on healthcare quality, including lower mortality rates [2], higher vaccination rates [15], and patient safety indicators [4]. In a widely cited study, Amarasingham et al. [2] report that greater automation of hospital information is associated with reduced rates of inpatient mortality, complications, costs and length of stay, based on a study of older patients across a cross section of 72 hospitals in North Texas. Their results suggest that an increase in automation of electronic records is associated with a decrease in fatal hospitalizations, and higher rates of electronic medical order entry were associated with lower rates of myocardial infarction and coronary artery bypass graft procedures, across all medical conditions. Appari et al. [3] also report that hospitals with higher EHR capability perform better on healthcare process quality.

However, recent studies have questioned the value of EHRs and clinical decision support systems and their impact on the quality of patient care. Based on a recent study of approximately 4000 U.S. hospitals, Himmelstein et al. [21] report that higher computerization scores are weakly associated with lower rates of acute myocardial infarction, but do not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant association with other health conditions such as heart failure or pneumonia. They also report that the overall computerization scores are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with hospital administrative costs, and hospitals on the “100 Most Wired List” compiled by Hospital and Health Networks magazine performed no better than others on quality or administrative costs. Two recent and widely cited studies, by DesRoches et al. and Romano and Stafford [12,37], also report limited improvements in the quality of patient care associated with implementation of EHRs and clinical decision support (CDS) systems. DesRoches et al. [12] found no significant relationship between EHR adoption and process quality measures for acute myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, and pneumonia, and modest improvement in the treatment of surgical complications. They also report no evidence of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in risk-adjusted length of stay, thirty-day readmission rates, and total hospital costs for hospitals that have implemented EHR systems and those without EHRs. Based on their analysis of physician survey data on more than 250,000 patient visits in non-federal of<sup>fi</sup>ces and hospitals for the 2005–2007 period, Romano and Stafford [37] report that, among EHR visits, only one of 20 quality indicators showed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better performance compared to visits without CDS. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between EHR and CDSs with better quality, and raise concerns about the ability of HIT to improve care quality.

## 2.2. Limitations

In spite of a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant volume of anecdotal evidence with respect to the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of EHRs, there is a shortage of research that empirically examine the usage of information technologies among healthcare providers and their impact on the quality of care delivery. Although recent studies have reported mixed evidence on the impact of HIT on the quality of patient care, they have been limited by data limitations and de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the econometric estimation methods used. For example, Linder et al. [27] use cross-sectional, pooled data analysis of patient visit data (with and without EHRs) but do not take into account the possibility of serial correlation among multiple visits by the same patient over time which may lead to biased estimates in statistical regression models. McCullogh et al. and DesRoches et al. [12,29] focus speci<sup>fi</sup>cally on two types of HIT applications – EHR and CPOE systems – and study whether hospitals that implement these systems exhibit greater levels of process quality compared to hospitals that have not implemented these systems. They ignore the role of ancillary HIT applications, such as radiology, laboratory and order communication systems, that support decisionmaking and information <sup>fl</sup>ow related to patient care. Similarly, the impact of non-clinical applications, such as patient scheduling systems, human resource systems, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems, on operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency has not been studied in the extant literature. We argue that it is important to consider the role of administrative applications in addition to ancillary clinical applications as one considers the impact of HIT on process “quality of care” measures as well as metrics associated with administrative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

A recent commentary on the digital transformation of healthcare identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three major areas that warrant further research in the HIT literature [1], page 796. They are: (1) HIT design, implementation, and meaningful use; (2) measurement and quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of HIT payoff and impact, and (3) extending the traditional realm of HIT. Our study squarely focuses on the second research area, with respect to the impact of HIT on the cost and quality of healthcare delivery. We study the impact of HIT on the quality outcomes associated with four types of healthcare processes: treatment of acute myocardial infarction, heart failure, pneumonia, and surgical infection prevention. These hospital care processes represent the common types of patient treatment processes for which there exist speci<sup>fi</sup>c guidelines that govern the best practices in the delivery of care, and they represent a key area of attention of the Federal government. Understanding the effect of health IT on process quality measures associated with these practices is of paramount importance, since they may explain the downstream differences in the overall patient outcomes, such as mortality rates and length of stay. Hence, we focus our attention on developing and empirically testing a model of the relationships between HIT usage, process quality, and the costs associated with healthcare delivery.

## 3. Theory foundation

Prior research dealing with the productivity impact of healthcare IT has mostly focused on the impact of IT investments on hospital-level performance [13,25,32,39]. These studies lumped all IT spending, including investments in hospital computer hardware, telecom infrastructure, and peripherals, under one category, typically measured as “IT capital.” While IT capital is a useful measure of organizational IT investments, it does not allow us to isolate the process-level impact of software applications on the delivery of patient care. Second, examining the direct relationship between IT spending and a hospital's overall performance, such as patient length of stay, mortality rates, and patient satisfaction, can lead to misleading conclusions, since the competitive advantages associated with IT-enabled processes may be appropriated before they are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the hospital's aggregate performance. Since IT investment decisions are often made at the application level, we must focus on their impact on healthcare processes to trace their impact on IT-enabled capabilities associated with evidence-based best practices in the delivery of patient care.

New regulations that require US healthcare providers to use EHRs and adhere to strict data coding standards can lead to signi<sup>fi</sup>cant behavioral changes needed to standardize provider practices. EHRs and CPOE systems require clinicians to follow a menu of de<sup>fi</sup>ned procedures when requesting services, tests or drugs for patients [26]. Such systems, when combined with clinical decision support tools that provide doctors with best practice guidelines for medical procedures, help to enforce greater compliance and support adoption of standard operating procedures and evidence-based best practices.

From a quality perspective, EHRs can provide the means to integrate patient data across disparate sources and direct medical staff toward protocols that have been clinically proven to be more effective in treating various types of health conditions [34]. They can sharply reduce the risk of prescription errors and adverse drug events by mapping patient medical history with information from drug formularies to <sup>fl</sup>ag potential negative interactions. A recent study by McKinsey estimates that lack of such early warning systems to <sup>fl</sup>ag drug–drug interactions can cost hospitals between \$8000 and \$15,000 per bed each year [26]. Similarly, HIT applications that provide capabilities for electronic exchange of patient health records among providers can reduce the need for duplicate tests, and provide clinicians with greater visibility into a patient's history, thus saving time and additional costs while not compromising on the quality of care. For example, a regional provider in Canada that successfully implemented EHRs across four hospitals was able to develop standard guidelines for medical procedures and decision support protocols, which was associated with reduced healing time of cardiac care patients [26].

From a productivity perspective, materials management and scheduling systems can help administrators to optimize the scheduling of critical resources (such as operating rooms, equipment, and surgeons) and the level of staf<sup>fi</sup>ng. Such systems not only can help eliminate administrative waste but also can improve asset utilization including bed management in inpatient settings. Studies have shown that these savings amount up to \$20,000 per bed in labor utilization alone [26]. Similarly, automated encoders within <sup>fi</sup>nancial management systems can improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of claims processes by promoting consistent coding, adherence to internationally accepted standards (such as International Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of Diseases-9), and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of treatments, which results in improved <sup>fi</sup>rst-time pass-through rates of claims and lower rates of unreimbursed claims. Hence, the focus of our research is on studying the relationship between HIT usage, and their impact on process quality and cost of healthcare delivery across a large cross-section of hospitals during a multi-year period.

## 4. Research hypotheses

Drawing upon the capabilities perspective associated with resourcebased theory, we study the impact of clinical and administrative systems usage on the process quality of patient care associated with four major health conditions: acute myocardial infarction (AMI), heart failure, pneumonia, and surgical infection prevention (SIP). We posit that healthcare IT improves decision-making capabilities associated with various care processes associated with delivery and management of patient care in a hospital setting. For instance, EMR systems can enable physicians to use CPOE at the point of contact with patients to prescribe medications. This not only speeds up the transmission of a patient's prescription to the pharmacy, thereby reducing delay time, but has two added bene<sup>fi</sup>ts: (a) reduces the need for nurses or physician assistants to transcribe the physician's prescription thereby lowering the potential for prescription errors, and (b) provide decision support capabilities necessary to <sup>fl</sup>ag possible drug interactions at the time that the physician enters the prescription in the system. Such IT-enabled capabilities within CPOE applications reduce the incidence of adverse drug events and are expected to yield signi<sup>fi</sup>cant savings in inpatient care as well as outpatient visits [2,26].

Clinical information systems also aid in short-term preventive care as well as chronic disease management. For example, heuristics within EMR systems can identify patients in need of speci<sup>fi</sup>c services (such as mammograms, prostate exams), remind physicians to offer needed tests during patients' visits, and provide consistent records of clinical test results leading to better clinical outcomes. Case management systems within EMRs help coordinate work<sup>fl</sup>ows, such as communication between multiple specialists and high-risk patients. EMR systems can integrate evidence-based recommendations for preventive services (e.g. screening exams), with patient data (age, sex, family history) to identify patients needing speci<sup>fi</sup>c services, and provide automated reminders to such patients to schedule preventive care visits. Such clinical decision support capabilities are extremely valuable in helping clinicians treat cases related to AMI, heart failures, and pneumonia, since they provide automated checklists of procedures that need to be undertaken on such patients. For instance, the system can remind a clinician to give an ACE inhibitor to a heart attack patient for Left Ventricular Systolic Dysfunction, provide adult smoking cessation advice/counseling, and prescribe Aspirin at arrival. Such systems can also provide alerts to remind clinicians that patients diagnosed with pneumonia may be given a pneumococcal vaccination, adult smoking cessation advice/counseling, and an initial antibiotic within 4 h after arrival.

Hospital administrative systems also play an important role in the delivery and coordination of patient care. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts management portals enable the cross-functional integration of data across multiple departments, while patient administration systems track the patients' movement of care in inpatient settings and allow clinicians and support staff to improve the quality and utilization of patient care by reducing waiting times at the point of admission, discharge or transfer. Other administrative applications such as personnel management systems support staff needs related to education and training. These systems can support the delivery of online training for medical staff and ensure staff compliance with medical guidelines and best practices to improve the quality of patient care.

Considering that the single largest cost to a hospital is incurred in the operating room, many hospitals use computerized scheduling systems to improve the utilization of existing operating rooms and maximize the productivity of their labor resources [15]. Hospital patient scheduling systems can provide dynamic decision support capabilities necessary for scheduling patients to the right types of doctors/nurses based on case volume, patient condition, and diagnosis. Such scheduling applications can improve utilization management by tracking the use and appropriateness of care delivery. Hospital <sup>fi</sup>nancial information systems, such as encoders, allow health management personnel to improve the accuracy of coding of healthcare procedures and diagnosis. Such systems can play a vital role in reducing medical errors, thereby improving the quality of care delivery by ensuring accurate coding of patient data.

We focus on the process quality metrics since they capture the rate at which hospitals provide the recommended treatments for speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of medical conditions, rather than the frequency of quality outcomes [29].<sup>1</sup> Hence, we argue that IT-enabled capabilities associated with clinical and administrative IT applications will have a positive impact on the quality of patient care processes by enabling greater conformance to evidence-based best practices in medicine.

Hypotheses 1. Implementation of HIT is associated with greater conformance to evidence-based best practices related to the quality of healthcare delivery.

H1a. Implementation of clinical information systems is associated with greater conformance to process quality related to the delivery of patient care.

H1b. Implementation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial information systems is associated with greater conformance to process quality of patient care.

H1c. Implementation of scheduling systems is associated with greater conformance to process quality of patient care.

H1d. Human resource systems implementation is associated with greater conformance to process quality of patient care.

We now turn to focus on the potential impact of IT applications on the operating expenses incurred in running a health provider organization. Electronic messaging systems, an integral component of many EMR applications, offer a low cost alternative to patient communication that reduce staff time and expenses involved in responding to patients. Ancillary clinical systems, such as laboratory and radiology information systems, support the collection, veri<sup>fi</sup>cation, storage, and reporting of laboratory test results and digital images, respectively, and reduce the need for expensive physical storage of such records. They also reduce the latency (wait time) associated with searching for patient record information and improve staff productivity by enabling information exchange across multiple departments. Prior research has estimated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant savings from such IT capabilities of clinical systems that can be attributed to outpatient savings due to lower transcription costs, fewer chart pulls, and data storage costs, and inpatient savings due to lower length of stay, nursing times, and storage costs of medical data.

In a similar vein, administrative systems, such as hospital <sup>fi</sup>nancial and scheduling systems, also play an important role in reducing operating costs. For example, encoders within <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems improve the accuracy of coding patient data which optimizes billing and the recovery of reimbursements from insurance companies. General ledger and budgeting systems provide management with relevant analytical capabilities necessary for <sup>fi</sup>nancial analysis, forecasting, and planning to reduce costs. Materials management systems manage procurement and inventory control functions, and recent advances in radio frequency identi<sup>fi</sup>cation devices (RFID) have allowed hospitals to track their assets (such as beds, medications, supplies) more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently [5]. Patient scheduling applications coordinate scheduling of all provider components, identify con<sup>fl</sup>icts, and reduce costs by matching staff work schedules based on patient volume [15]. Hence, we argue that hospital IT applications can reduce operating costs by providing relevant decision support and analytic capabilities that lead to better resource optimization, increase staff productivity, and minimize redundant costs.

Hypotheses 2. Hospitals with high levels of HIT implementation are likely to exhibit lower overall operating expenses.

H2a. Implementation of clinical information systems is associated with lower hospital operating expenses.

H2b. Implementation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems is associated with a reduction in hospital operating expenses.

H2c. Implementation of scheduling systems is associated with lower hospital operating expenses.

H2d. Implementation of human resource systems is associated with lower hospital operating expenses.

We control for several hospital-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the impact of health IT usage on hospital process quality measures and operating expenses. First, we control for hospital size as measured by the number of beds, since prior studies have reported that larger hospitals are more likely to adopt electronic medical records and other advanced health IT applications. Next, we control for hospital type by measuring whether a hospital is a for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t or not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t entity. For-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals may have different incentives than their not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t counterparts in terms of their adoption and usage of health IT applications and motivation to improve the cost and quality of related healthcare delivery processes. We control for the hospital case mix index (CMI) which accounts for the average severity of patients' disease case mix in a hospital. CMI may affect the overall quality and cost outcomes of hospitals due to differences in patient case severity across hospitals and is a proxy for the percentage of Medicare/Medicaid patients. Furthermore, we control for hospital location by classifying whether a hospital is located in an urban versus rural location based on its address zip code. Hospitals are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as urban if their zip code is included in one of the metropolitan statistical areas de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the US Of<sup>fi</sup>ce of Management and Budget. All other non-urban hospitals are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as rural. Since teaching hospitals have access to more advanced resources, in terms of technologies and staff training, we control for these differences by differentiating between teaching and non-teaching hospitals in our data.

Similarly, HF, PNEUMONIA, and SIP represent the average quality scores associated with the process quality measures for treatment of heart failure, pneumonia and surgical infection prevention cases, respectively. In a similar vein, CLINICAL, FINANCIAL, SCHEDULING, and HR, represent the IT usage of hospital clinical, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, patient scheduling, and human resource management systems, respectively. HOSP\_SIZE represents the number of beds in hospital i in year t, while HOSP\_TYPE represents a dummy variable where for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals are coded as one and not-for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals are coded as a zero. CMI represents the case mix index for hospital i in year t. LOCATION represents the size of the geographic market serviced by hospital i in year t with a value of one corresponding to hospitals in an urban location, and a value of zero assigned to hospitals in a rural location. TEACHING\_STATUS represents the teaching status of hospital i in year t with teaching hospitals assigned a value of one and non-teaching hospitals assigned a value of zero.

TOT\_QUALITY represents a total quality measure which measures conformance to best practices for treatments across all four types of cases: AMI, HF, Pneumonia, and SIP. In this respect, it captures the variations in quality measures across the four types of treatments and aggregates them into a single quality index.

Finally, we estimate the impact of HIT applications and hospital characteristics on hospital operating expenses as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Eq. (6).

where $\mathrm { O P E X _ { i , t } }$ represents the operating expenses incurred by hospital i in year t and all other variables are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as before.

We use an unbalanced <sup>fi</sup>rm-year panel data to test our hypotheses. In this setting, it is well known that OLS standard errors can be biased when the residuals are not independent, resulting in a biased estimation of the true variability of coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates. The possible dependence in the residuals may result from within-hospital, across-year autocorrelation in the errors (time-series dependence) or within-year across-hospital correlation in the errors (cross-sectional dependence). We deploy a random effects estimation model to estimate the system of equations described in Eqs. (1) through (6), which views hospitalspeci<sup>fi</sup>c constant terms as randomly distributed across cross-sectional units as in our panel data [19], pp. 469–470. Random effects models utilize not only the variation within each hospital across time but also the variation between hospitals, and provide more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient parameter estimates while accounting for unobserved time-invariant industry or <sup>fi</sup>rm-level factors if they are uncorrelated with explanatory variables in the model. We estimate the random effects models using the XTREGAR procedure in the STATA statistical software. We performed diagnostic checks to ensure the stability of our results and did not detect any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant problems [6]. We also checked for multi-collinearity in our models and ascertained that variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factors were within the acceptable threshold. Furthermore, we conducted a Hausman [20] test to assess the potential endogeneity of the IT usage factors following a procedure using “instrumental variables” [40]. Our test failed to reject the null hypothesis for exogeneity of IT usage in our models.

In addition to the random effects estimation method, we compute standard errors that are robust to clustering across time and <sup>fi</sup>rms, using a two-way, clustered regression methodology as described in Cameron et al. and Petersen [8,36]. ## 6. Research data

We now describe the data sources used in our study and our sampling strategy, along with a detailed description of the variables used in model development.

## 6.1. Study population

We obtained research data from three different sources which we tracked at the level of an individual hospital, our unit of analysis. First, we obtained data on hospital IT usage from the Dorenfest Institute for Health Information Technology Research database [22]. It represents a comprehensive, nationally representative, annual survey of hospital chief information of<sup>fi</sup>cers across a large cross-section of hospitals in the US. For the purpose of this study, we used hospital panel data over a three-year period from 2004 to 2006. Approximately 17.3% of these hospitals are for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals, while the remaining are non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. The average size of hospitals in our sample was 249 beds at an operational expense of \$630,353 per bed.

Second, we obtained data on hospital process quality measures from the U.S. Department of HHS Hospital Compare Program. We obtained process quality data for the same set of hospitals on which HIT usage data was available through the Dorenfest database. Third, data on hospital operating expenses was obtained using publicly available data from the U.S. Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). The three data sources were combined using the National Provider Identi<sup>fi</sup>ers or Medicare Identi<sup>fi</sup>ers available at each source.

## 6.2. Measurement of variables

Devaraj and Kohli [14] propose a measure of IT usage that is based on the processing of hospital records as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the number of times a particular report or data report is accessed and/or executed, and the utilization of central processing unit time. In our study, we focus on the usage of various types of hospital IT applications, since it allows us to trace the impact of health IT applications on speci<sup>fi</sup>c healthcare processes where they are used.

Based on the overall frequency and consistency of IT usage, as provided by respondents to the HIMSS survey, we identify a total of thirteen HIT applications as being commonly used by hospitals during the time period of our study. We used exploratory factor analysis to identify four IT factors among these hospital IT applications.

Healthcare quality processes are assessed using the data obtained from the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. The program identi<sup>fi</sup>es evidence-based, best practices for treating four types of health conditions: AMI, heart failure, pneumonia, and surgical infection prevention [21]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c process quality metrics are used to assess the hospitals' conformance with the recommended best practices. Hence, meeting the recommended best-practice is indicative of adherence to a pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned quality measure. We track each hospital's conformance to the recommended best practices for each condition based on the data obtained independently from the Hospital Compare program. Table 2 lists the four health conditions and their respective process quality measures.

Each hospital in our sample reports the degree of conformance to the process quality measures that correspond to the four healthcare conditions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when a patient is treated for a given condition (e.g. AMI), a hospital reports whether or not the recommended process of care treatment was followed. A score between zero and one is calculated for each process quality measure by recording the number of times that the recommended process of care guideline is followed and dividing it by the total number of patients treated for the given condition. A score of one for a process quality measure indicates that the best-practice guidelines for treatment of that condition were followed 100% of the time, such that every patient treated for that condition received the “recommended quality” treatment. A score of zero indicates that no patient received the recommended process of care treatment for the given condition. We calculate an overall quality score for each health condition by averaging the scores of the individual process quality measures. An overall score between zero and one for a given health condition indicates the proportion of process quality measures for which the hospital followed the recommended best-practice treatments. A “Total Quality” index was also calculated for each hospital in our study sample by averaging the quality scores across the four health conditions.

Finally, our results indicate that urban (coeff.=105,360, pb0.01) and teaching hospitals (coeff.=36,982, pb0.01) exhibit greater operating expenses per bed which may be attributed to possible differences in staff pay and asset maintenance costs in urban locations and the increased resource requirements associated with a teaching hospital, respectively.

## 8. Discussion

Our regression estimation results, based on a large panel sample of US hospitals tracked over a three-year period from 2004 to 2006, indicate signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the usage of HIT systems and their association with the quality of healthcare delivery processes and hospital operational expenses. On one hand, clinical information systems, which include EMR and clinical decision support systems, are associated with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant improvements in the overall hospital process quality, primarily with respect to greater conformance with evidencebased, best practices related to AMI treatment. On a similar note, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that usage of hospital scheduling and HR systems is associated with greater conformance to best practices for AMI, heart failure, pneumonia, and surgical infection prevention cases. These results suggest that IT investments in EHRs and systems for electronic patient scheduling and human resource management have a positive impact on the overall quality of healthcare delivery. On the other hand, <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems are associated with reduced conformance with process quality measures. Unlike prior studies which primarily focus on the overall hospital outcomes (such as mortality and readmission rates), ours is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to explore the impact of health IT on evidence-based measures of process quality which represent intermediate metrics that are antecedents of hospital-level quality outcomes.

Our results suggest that while healthcare IT offers a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant value in terms of their impact on the overall quality associated with healthcare processes, their effect on hospital operating expenses needs to be factored into consideration when making implementation decisions. Analyses of the operational expense data over a three-year horizon suggest that <sup>fi</sup>nancial and human resource management systems have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact in terms of lower hospital operating expenses, while implementation of clinical information systems and scheduling systems is associated with higher operating expenses. We posit that, since EMR implementation initiatives are typically multi-year, expensive projects, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from their use may only be realized in the long term.

Another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nding of our study pertains to the signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher process quality prevalent among non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals compared to their for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t counterparts. Non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals are more likely to report greater conformance with evidence-based, best practices related to the treatment of AMI and Pneumonia cases, while for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals exhibit greater conformance for treatment of heart failure cases. We also observe that for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals report signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly lower operational expenses than non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals. The results suggest that organization mission, as measured by the hospital charter, plays an important role in determining the focal point of a hospital's resources. While non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals are likely to realize greater process quality in terms of conformance with established healthcare process delivery metrics, for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t hospitals are more likely to focus on the bottom line by lowering operating expenses.

We also observe that urban hospitals exhibit higher total quality processes conformance across the four types of health conditions. This result may be attributed to the greater resources available at urban hospitals for treatment of acute health conditions such as AMI and heart failures, whereas rural hospitals are more likely to be pro<sup>fi</sup>cient with treatment of diseases that are more easily diagnosed and treated by hospital staff. Finally, we note that case mix index has a positive impact on all process quality measures such that hospitals that treat patients with more severe cases exhibit greater conformance to established process quality measures. This result may be attributed to greater hospital process adherence, the possibility that high quality medical staff is attracted to treating complex cases, and that hospitals treating more severe patients have greater awareness of new best practice guidelines due to demands associated with treating severe cases.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications as they demonstrate the cost–quality tradeoff associated with the use of IT in hospitals. Our results also have important implications for policy makers since they provide empirical evidence on the effectiveness of measuring IT-enabled healthcare quality. Our results imply that application of health IT, without developing a better understanding of treatments that work and those that do not, may be counter-productive and lead to cost in<sup>fl</sup>ation as proponents of comparative effectiveness research have observed [23]. They also suggest that it is important to differentiate between the different types of HIT applications when considering their impact on healthcare process quality instead of lumping all types of IT spending into one category. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to estimate the differential impact of HIT applications on process quality, and our results suggest that there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in the impact of clinical and administrative systems on healthcare quality and costs.

## 9. Conclusions

We extend earlier work on the productivity bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of health IT systems by focusing on the impact of IT at the application-cluster level instead of focusing on the dollar amount of IT spending alone. Our study focuses on developing a better understanding of the relationship between the different types of hospital information systems and the quality and cost associated with healthcare delivery processes. While prior IS research has primarily focused on the cost impact of healthcare IT, our research is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst studies to examine the impact of the different classes of health IT applications on process-level measures of health care quality. Such measures have become increasingly important in the current healthcare debate as demonstrated by the government's focus on improving the quality of healthcare delivery through incentives to spur the adoption of electronic health records [11].

Our results show that spending on health IT does matter. While prior studies have reported only weak evidence of the impact of health IT on costs and quality of patient care, our results indicate that it is important to measure quality outcomes at the process level, and not only at an aggregate institutional level. The positive impact of clinical, patient scheduling, and HR systems on the overall process quality implies that these systems make a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in providing relevant decision support capabilities to clinicians and enable care givers to conform to evidence-based, best practice guidelines. Similarly, our results suggest that the usage of <sup>fi</sup>nancial management systems results in lower hospital operating expenses through better planning, resource utilization, and elimination of redundant costs. Considering that the time period of our study represented an early stage of EMR adoption within US hospitals, we believe that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of such systems will be more pronounced as we consider more recent data as clinicians become more comfortable using HIT applications to store and process patient health data for clinical use.

Our study provides several insights into the challenges faced with measuring IT value in a healthcare setting. From a research perspective, our results suggest that it is important to examine IT-enabled capabilities and the impact that these capabilities have on process quality outcomes in order to explain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived from IT adoption and usage. Future studies on health IT may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from measuring the impact of decision support capabilities on the effectiveness of patient diagnoses and treatment. Furthermore, with the rollout of Stage One meaningful use criteria in 2011, a set of IT-enabled capabilities could be readily analyzed with respect to their impact on health productivity and quality. From a managerial perspective, our study suggests that an emphasis on “meaningful use” related to the use of health IT to collect and report patient care process data (such as problem lists, medication and allergy lists, electronic exchange of clinical data among providers) has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on quality.

Nevertheless, our study does have a few limitations. One limitation may be attributed to the lack of data on the overall patient outcomes such as length of hospital stay and mortality rates [35]. While such measures provide additional dimensions for extending the current study, they would entail data collection from other sources and digress from our focus on the process-level quality measures in our research. Another limitation is that our IT usage variables are based on zero–one binary indicators that represent the use or non-use of an IT application. These measures may not capture the actual degree of usage of these HIT applications by healthcare providers, and present an interesting area for future data collection and research. We also observe that it takes time for HIT applications to support recently developed best-practices, and most of the process quality measures in our research represent well-documented, evidence-based guidelines in the healthcare domain. Evaluating the relationship between newly developed best practices and HIT represents another area for future research. Another fertile area of research lies in the extension of our results using patient-level records to evaluate the impact on individual-level patient quality and costs of hospital stay [2]. We believe that such extensions will provide a more holistic picture of the relational patterns between IT spending, HIT usage, and process and organizational measures of quality and cost control.
