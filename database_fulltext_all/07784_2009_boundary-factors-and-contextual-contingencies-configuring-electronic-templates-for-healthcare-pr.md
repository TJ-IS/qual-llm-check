# Boundary factors and contextual contingencies: configuring electronic templates for healthcare professionals

Pernille Bjørn<sup>1,2</sup>, Sue Burgoyne<sup>3</sup>, Vicky Crompton<sup>3</sup>, Teri MacDonald<sup>3</sup>, Barbe Pickering<sup>3</sup> and Sue Munro 4

## Abstract

In this paper, we propose an approach to balance the legitimate and yet conflicting perspectives between standardization and reconfiguration embedded within hospital information systems (HIS) design activities. We report on an action research study of the customization process of an electronic triage and tracking system that was reconfigured to be used in eight Canadian emergency departments. We argue that during HIS design activities, it is essential for both practitioners and system designers to articulate and identify which aspects can be standardized without constraining important local flexibility and which aspects require local reconfiguration to function in a particular work context. To identify these differences, we suggest an analytical distinction between boundary factors and contextual contingencies, which can be used in a design and reconfiguration process. We argue that the process of designing shared electronic templates should be perceived as a common design process, where multiple stakeholders articulate, identify, and negotiate boundary factors and contextual contingencies. Boundary factors are then represented within the shared electronic system, whereas contextual contingencies form the basis for constructing localized versions of the shared application. All local versions include both boundary factors and the reconfigured contextual contingencies.

Keywords: healthcare technology; standardization; boundary factors; contextua contingencies; boundary objects; action research

## Introduction

Over the last decade, designing and implementing electronic information systems in hospitals has received tremendous attention as a way to improve health care through standardization (e.g. Raghupathi, 1997; Bates, 2002; Roukema et al., 2006). Standardizing health data enables comparison of cases between various departments or even hospitals, which is fruitful and necessary for medical research (Richesson & Krischer, 2007). However, forcing healthcare personnel to enter data in a pre-defined and structured format applying standardized electronic templates does not come without a cost for the primary work of healthcare professionals (Berg, 1997; Timmermans & Berg, 2003; Ash et al., 2004). Although the use of the electronic templates might enable standardized data collection, it may also constrain the work practices of the healthcare professionals (Orlikowski, 1995). In this way, conflict is inevitably embedded within the agendas of standardization. This conflict is also referred to as the conflict between standardization and flexibility (Hanseth et al., 1996). This flexibility does not refer to interpretative flexibility (Orlikowski, 1992, p. 407). Instead, flexibility refers to the degree to which a technology supports flexibility of use and openness to further customizations and reconfigurations. Hospital work is characterized by a high level of structural and environmental uncertainties, especially within emergency departments (EDs). Previous research found that in high-uncertainty situations, flexibility-to-change within information systems (IS) is essential and even cost efficient (Gebauer & Schober, 2006). Hospital information systems (HIS) design is known as a major challenge (Cho et $a l . ,$ 2008), and we argue that when designing and reconfiguring electronic templates to support existing hospital work practices, it is essential to preserve, within new templates, significant flexibilities in patterns of use related to previous templates (paper as well as electronic). In order to ensure that the essential flexibility of existing work practices continues to exist, it is crucial that standardized electronic templates are reconfigured to support the presumed situated work practices of the future users.

It is not our intention to advocate against standardization per se; however, if we take the significance of reconfiguration activities supporting future usage of electronic templates seriously, we find ourselves in a dilemma. We strongly believe that the design of electronic templates must take the situated work practices into account through reconfiguration and customization. Nevertheless, the agenda of reconfiguration is clashing with the agenda of standardization. Previous studies of HIS suggest that implementing specialized software technologies entails continuous technology-in-use mediation developing technology-in-use practices by reconfiguring both technology and work practices (Davidson & Chiasson, 2005; Boulus & Bjørn, 2008). Technology-inuse mediation begins before the system goes into operational use and continues during system use developing technology-in-use practices. Standardization in continuously changing contexts such as EDs is a critical area within IS research; nevertheless, studies of standardization concepts and processes are notably absent (Lyytinen & King, 2006). Following the approach of Pollock et al. (2007), we try to go beyond simply stressing how technology might bring unwanted organizational change or insisting on localization as the main driver of system design. Instead, we investigate the dilemma by asking: How can we develop concepts useful to articulate and identify the conflicting perspectives between standardization and reconfiguration embedded within design activities of HIS?

We investigate this research question in an action research study (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996) of the design and reconfiguration process of an electronic triage template to be used in multiple EDs. We focus on the conflicts between the adult EDs and one pediatric emergency department (ED). Interestingly, the electronic template was already in use in three adult EDs prior to the reconfiguration process and was planned to be used in eight EDs (including the pediatric ED) within a year. This meant that local customizations focusing on the local work practices of the pediatric ED also affected the other EDs participating in the project and vice versa. In this way, the design and reconfiguration project was caught between the need for standardization and the need for reconfiguration.

We found that essential negotiations related to the design of particular data fields and how these were displayed within the user interface were characterized by strong differences formed by the very foundation of the local contexts. In these cases it was impossible to negotiate a shared design solution flexible enough for local use in the involved EDs. We identify the distinction between the common and context-dependent design negotiations as the process of balancing between standardizing and configuring, and we suggest the concept of boundary factors and contextual contingencies as a tool for articulating these essential differences for practitioners and designers. Briefly, boundary factors are plastic, adaptable, and meaningful across borders and social worlds in the same way as boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989). In addition, boundary factors can be represented and displayed within a technology without constraining important flexibility for how actors from different social worlds perform their work. In contrast, contextual contingencies are not directly transferable across borders but are highly interlinked with particular contexts. Representing and displaying contextual contingencies within a technology will constrain significant flexibility in work practices of particular contexts while enabling others. We argue that when designing and reconfiguring standardized electronic templates, designers and practitioners must identify and articulate both the boundary factors and the contextual contingencies during the design processes. Through negotiations, they must ensure that contextual contingencies are reconfigured into localized versions of the common template, while the boundary factors stay common between the localized templates.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section we present the theoretical framework of standardization, stabilization, and integration from prior research as well as conceptualize the proposed two main concepts of boundary factors and contextual contingen cies. In the subsequent section we present the empirical case, the design project, and the technology in question. Then in the section after that our action research approach is described as well as our data sources. This is followed by the main analysis divided into four subsections. In the discussion section we combine our theoretical framework and the empirical observations, arguing for the use of the suggested conceptualization of boundary factors and contextual contingencies in design and reconfiguration processes. Finally, we offer a conclusion.

## Theoretical framework

## Standardization and stabilization

Configuring common electronic templates for multiple healthcare settings is basically a process of standardization. Standardization using a common system approach is not new and has been widely reported in literature on the implementation of large ERP systems (e.g. Cadili & Whitley, 2005). In 1981, Keen et al. (1981) argued that using the common system approach as a strategy for large-scale development poses complex problems of design and management. In their study of the implementation of a common banking system in a large global banking organization, one of their main findings was the technical problem of defining the ‘core’ elements of the technical application (Keen et al., 1981). One of the key questions was how much of the system is core and how much is adaptable to local configurations? What is striking here is that even back in 1981, when most database management software systems were ‘too expensive and inefficient for large transaction processing’ (Keen et al., 1981, p. 25), the implementation of standardized systems was viewed as an adaptation process whereby central groups defined a core system that was then modified to fit particular local needs. Nevertheless, today, where processing power and data storage space are not issues for the development of large worldwide applications, it is still a surprise to many organizations (e.g. hospitals) that local reconfigurations of generic systems are essential for successful implementation.

Reconfigurations of generic systems to local practices can be viewed as a process of stabilization and closure (Bjiker & Law, 1992). Closure results from stabilization of an artifact (Cadili & Whitley, 2005) – in our case, a computerized electronic template. The artifact reaches closure through a negotiation activity where stakeholders align meanings and interests and subsequently base the reconfiguration of the electronic system on these agreements. When the technical artifact has reached stabilization, stakeholders agree, at least for the time being, on the current design, thus the artifact has reached closure. Standardization attempts are, in this view, a process of alignment; and successful standardization entails the achievement of stabilization and closure in the definition as well as in the boundaries of the standard (Hanseth et al., 2006). Standards do not emerge from natural laws but form a set of agreed-upon rules for the production of an object that spans more than one community at a given time (Bowker & Star, 2002, pp. 13–14). This perspective on standards puts negotiation activities at the top of the agenda when investigating reconfigurations. Previous research found that failures in creating one common standard for electronic templates within hospital work were grounded in the attempts to translate and align heterogeneous elements into one closed and stabilized template in practice. This approach resulted in a process where categories and ordering effects from one world created disorder in others (Hanseth et al., 2006, p. 5). Hanseth et al. (2006) conceptualized this interesting observation – that ordering attempts seem to increase disorder – as reflexive standardization.

## Integration and unanticipated consequences

Standardization in healthcare settings typically includes political visions of integrating common HIS at a larger scale. However, the concept of ‘integration’ is ambiguous and open to interpretation. The meaning of integration is inherently transformed through the process of pursuing integration, which suggests that stakeholders need to prepare and to refocus their efforts, since the vision of shared systems will change (Ellingsen & Monteiro, 2008). Implementing new technology always transforms practice in a dual and paradoxical way, since it both is the engine as well as the barrier for change (Star & Ruhleder, 1996). It is impossible to anticipate all contingencies that might arise locally after the implementation of technology (Gerson & Star, 1986; Kiesler, 1986; DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). In many cases, these unforeseen conflicts between the structures of technology and the work practices are solved by practitioners developing and applying workarounds in their daily work (Gerson & Star, 1986). Workarounds seek to intentionally use the technology in a different way than it was designed or simply to avoid using the technology and instead rely on alternative means to accomplish the work (Gasser, 1986). In constructing conceptualizations useful for articulating conflicts between technology structures and work practices, we try to reduce the number of unanticipated conflicts by prompting negotiations early on, thus decreasing the need to develop workarounds after the implementation.

Work is a dual, interlinked phenomenon consisting of formal and informal actions (Schmidt & Bannon, 1992). This differentiation is referred to as the relation between the formal representation of how work is supposed to be handled (plans) and the informal tasks (situated actions) essential to get the work done (Suchman, 1983, 1987; Robinson, 1991; Star & Strauss, 1999). Plans are the formal representations of the work embedded within artifacts, documents, and structures. They are the resources used by participants to orient their actions with the purpose of obtaining the best possible position when enacting their embodied skills, on which success depends (Suchman, 1987). In EDs, plans are inscribed within the formal workflows of patients and papers, as well as in the use of the paper-form templates, chart racks, archives, folders, and whiteboards. However, when examining the actual work performed within the EDs, it is apparent that plans do not represent the work, but rather they leave out the situated practices and their circumstances from all of the concrete details (Bjørn & Rødje, 2008).

## Boundary factors and contextual contingencies

Design of IS requires and relies upon a representation of the work and its relevant objects (Suchman, 1983). HIS tend to embed models of work based on formal representations of the medical work rather than on how work is performed. This process has derived from an interest in developing and introducing code systems and classifications of medical work intended to make cases more comparable, allowing statistical analysis (Kuhn & Giuse, 2001). Boundary object is an analytical concept for those elements that inhabit several intersecting social worlds (such as different EDs) and satisfy the informational requirements of each, such as information repositories and standardized forms (Star & Griesemer, 1989; Bowker & Star, 2002). Thus, designing and reconfiguring standardized electronic templates can be understood as the process of constructing a boundary object. Boundary objects are plastic enough to be adapted (flexible for interpretation) to local needs and in this way are meaningful across borders and social worlds. Even though they inhabit different meanings, the structure of the boundary object is stable and in this way comprises a mean for translations (Luna-Reyes et al., 2005). Viewing the standardized electronic form as a boundary object thus implies that the same type of structure is flexible for interpretation and has different meanings across different contexts. Negotiating boundary objects resembles the construction of a conscription device where the object has not reached closure yet (Karsten et al., 2001). From this perspective, the design process of the standardized electronic triage template can be perceived as a negotiation process producing the boundary object.

Using particular categorization schemes (structure of boundary object) enables particular work practices while constrains others (Orlikowski, 1995). This means that even though the design of the boundary object (the electronic triage template) might enable the production of comparable data between EDs, it might also constrain the actual triaging of patients because the workflow embedded does not match the situated actions of the triage nurses. Context matters, and a growing number of researchers investigating HIS implementation use contextual approaches (Davidson & Chiasson, 2005; Cho et al., 2008). In a Swedish case of the implementation of HIS, complex contextual dynamics related to both the implementation content and context were found to have disruptive effects (Cho et al., 2008). This study showed how the implementation content and context were shaped interactively, suggesting that contextual inquiry can help implementation processes. Another study revealed that the process of contextualizing specialized software technologies such as electronic medical records involved identification and reconciliation between (mis)matches in the technical application and the organizational practice (Davidson & Chiasson, 2005). Interpretative flexibility is key when referring to contextual factors, and it includes investigations of how technical issues such as data definitions mesh with the non-technical conditions (Hanseth et al., 1996). Technical objects should be flexible for interpretations between contexts by taking the non-technical conditions into account. We built on, while extending, these contextual studies by suggesting two concepts that can be actively used by system designers and hospital practitioners during the process of contextual inquiry. The proposed concepts help to identify, articulate, and reconcile clashes between technical issues and organizational practices that might arise during the data definition process. The strength of our concepts is that they were developed with practitioners through active participation in the design, reconfiguration, and implementation process of an ED information system over 2 years. In this way, they were developed to articulate particular difficulties experienced during processes of contextualizing the ED information system. The concepts are contextual contingencies and boundary factors.

Contextual contingencies describe the parts of work that clash with the standardized structure of the boundary object and that are closely related to the particular context. Contextual contingencies are not directly transferable across borders and are immalleable in nature. Immalleable does not mean static and impossible to change, since it is possible to change the contextual contingencies. However, changing the contextual contingencies would also risk destroying the very foundations of the context. Examples of the contextual contingencies are patient population, the organization of work, and the physical layout of the department (Balka et al., 2008).

This notion of contextual contingencies (in relation to the design of electronic templates) as a concept describing the immalleable elements of the work requires yet another concept to describe the parts of the electronic template that are malleable and open for shared configurations between multiple stakeholders. Boundary factors describe the elements that are meaningful across borders and can be represented and displayed in the same way without losing important flexibility for interpretation in different contexts. Boundary factors represent the core part of the system. One of the key problems for large standard systems is to identify what constitutes the core elements. It is a highly complex process to identify which parts of the system are ‘core’ and which are reconfigurable (Keen et al., 1981). In this paper we propose the concepts of boundary factors and contextual contingencies as concepts that can help identify and articulate the core and the reconfigurable elements of standard systems. We argue that, during standardization processes of shared electronic templates for multiple ED settings, it is essential that designers together with practitioners attend both boundary factors as well as contextual contingencies in deciding what can be shared and what should be localized.

## The empirical case

## The pediatric ED

The pediatric ED sees approximately 38,000 children yearly and operates with two parallel patient areas: acute area for high urgency patients (17 beds) and fast track area resembling a walk-in clinic (six beds). The ED is part of a provincial tertiary pediatric hospital, which means that all severe pediatric cases from the whole province are transported by ambulance, jet, or helicopter to the ED. Children are pediatric patients until their 17th birthday. For each shift, 24/7, one to three ED pediatric physicians are on duty working in 8-h shifts. Sometimes MDs from other departments, or from one of the ambulatory clinics, bring patients to the pediatric ED to use the facilities. Finally, consultants (e.g. orthopedic, oncology, neurology) are also regularly called to the ED. There are approximately 75 registered nurses in the ED working 24/7, mostly in 12-h shifts. Each nursing shift consists of seven to nine nurses.

In Canada there are standardized guidelines for assessing and sorting patients entering Canadian EDs, referred to as the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS or PCTAS<sup>1</sup>). PCTAS forms guidelines and five categories for triaging pediatric patients’ level of urgency (PCTAS 1–5, where PCTAS 1 is practically dead and PCTAS 5 is not sick). At the end of 2004, the pediatric ED implemented an electronic triage system (ETRIAGE), which consisted of an electronic template for guiding the triage interview while documenting results. However, due to various reasons written about elsewhere (e.g. the electronic system slowed down the work in the ED, creating queues of patients waiting) the system was abandoned 18 months later (Balka & Whitehouse, 2007; Bjørn & Balka, 2007), and the ED gradually returned to their paper forms in 2006.

## The design and reconfiguring project

In Spring 2007, the pediatric ED applied for funds from the provincial health authority for implementing a new ED information system, and in late Spring 2007, the ED was granted significant capital funding. However, the money came with constraints: (1) the pediatric ED had to implement the Cerner FirstNet triage and tracking system; (2) they should engage in collaboration with a regional health authority <sup>2</sup> already using Cerner FirstNet (in practice, sharing resources such as servers and expertise); and (3) they had to have Cerner FirstNet fully implemented (used all the funding) by the end of the fiscal year – 31 March 2008. The pediatric ED had 10 months to negotiate and reconfigure the existing triage and tracking system so it also supported their local context.

The existing Cerner FirstNet triage and tracking system was used in three adult/child EDs at the beginning of the project and was planned to be fully functional in eight EDs by 31 March 2008: seven EDs managed by the regional health authority, and in the pediatric ED managed by the provincial health authority.

Initially, the idea behind the project was to reconfigure and customize the existing system to support the pediatric ED, in particular, the tracking part of the system, which included an electronic whiteboard. However, as the project evolved, it became clear that the revisions and changes made by the pediatric ED related to the electronic triage template were also supposed to be incorporated into the electronic templates used by the other EDs. In this way the project evolved into a balancing act between the need to standardize triage documentation among the EDs and the need to configure the electronic triage template to support the local context of the pediatric ED as well as the other EDs.

## The Cerner FirstNet technology

Cerner FirstNet is a hospital IS including electronic orders, results, bed management, patient charts, electronic triage, and patient tracking. Only the triage and patient tracking components of the Cerner packet were to be configured and implemented. The patient-tracking component contains functions to track patients’ whereabouts and status within the ED through electronic whiteboards and desktop monitoring. The triage component encompasses an electronic template for documenting the triage nurse assessment and PCTAS value of the particular patient.

Initially, the electronic triage template consisted of two main pages (Chief Complaint and acuity) and nine subpages (related to particular complaints) (see Figure 1). There were no mandatory fields and the system was designed so that the triage nurse would use the Chief Complaint page and the acuity page in all cases, but only pick the relevant sub-pages in cases where the triage nurse found it useful. So. in cases where the Chief Complaint was of respiratory concern, the triage nurse would use the respiratory assessment sub-page. The subpages within the system consisted of respiratory (airway breathing), cardiovascular (heart complaint), neurological (e.g. seizure complaint), Glasgow coma scale (alertness scale), gastro-intestinal and genito-urinary system GI/GU (e.g. abdominal pain), eye-ear-nose-throat EENT (e.g. ear pain), obstetrics and gynecology OB/GYN (e.g. miscarriage), musculoskeletal/integumentary (e.g. bone fractures and skin issues), and mental health (e.g. depression).

On the Chief Complaint page, the triage nurse documents all initial examinations of patients, such as visit reason, vital signs, temperature, and health history. On the acuity page, the triage nurse indicates the PCTAS score based on the examination of the patient.

## Action research of the configuring process

The use of action research within the field of IS has become more common within recent years, and although different IS action researchers describe different kinds of research processes, the fundamental processes and activities are similar (e.g. Susman & Evered. 1987: Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996; Checkland & Holwell, 1998; Avison et al., 1999; Mathiassen, 2002; Davison et al., 2004). Action research investigates and acts upon the world to understand complex human processes through actions instead of searching for universal prescriptive truth (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). The two basic stages in action research are problem diagnosis and analytic intervention (Baskerville & Myers, 2004). Problem diagnosis involves the analysis of the social situation by both the researcher and the practitioners, whereas the analytic intervention involves activities of change combined with analytic examination of the effect of the changes. Action research consists of change and reflection on a problematic real-life situation during iterative cycles of activities. Activities include problem diagnosis, intervention, observation, and reflection on outcome.

Action research seeks to contribute both to the practical concerns of the particular case investigated as well as to science by taking initiatives towards a joint venture between researchers and practitioners (Rapoport, 1970). The joint venture of this action research project is captured by the research question: How can we develop concepts useful to articulate and identify the conflicting perspectives between standardization and reconfiguration embedded within design activities of HIS? This question has direct practical relevance for situations where multiple hospitals or medical departments are engaging in the process of reconfiguring common electronic templates. At the same time, the joint venture is relevant for research, since we need to develop approaches within the information systems research community to solve the issue of balancing standardization and reconfiguration. We know that standardization and work practices mutually shape and constitute each other and therefore should be understood as a socially co-constructed negotiation process (Ellingsen et al., 2007). However, we still need to identify concepts and processes for handling such negotiation processes (Lyytinen & King, 2006).

The action cycles of the project constituted a repeating set of activities in the form of 22 design workshops between July 2007 and January 2008. Each design workshop focused on articulating existing work practices and reconfiguring the electronic triage and tracking system in both a general manner between EDs and in a contextual manner ensuring local flexibility. Each workshop lasted between 4 and 8 h and included 3–12 participants. The participants consisted of representatives of the different nursing functions (triage, charge, bedside), clinical nurse coordinator, the clinical nurse educator, the clerks, the emergency program manager, the patient safety liaison, the physicians, and the vendor. In general, there was a core group of participants attending the workshops, which included a clinical nurse educator, a clinical nurse coordinator, a charge/triage nurse, a unit clerk, the ED program manager, a Cerner functional application specialist, a minute taker, and the first author. In total, the first author spent approximately 132 h participating in design workshops. In particular, the workshops that focused on ED work practices were facilitated by the first author. After each design workshop the first author wrote reflective notes as well as observation notes on the activity.

In addition to these design workshops, observations of ED work practices took place during 12 observations between November 2006 and August 2007, and 11 observations in March, June, and November 2008, focusing on the implementation and use of the electronic triage and patient tracking system. Here, the first author was located in different places within the ED observing the work while conducting informal interviews. Ten of the observations were focused on the triage nurse and registration clerk. Here, one to five triage nurses (at busy times there can be up to three active triage nurses triaging at the same time; also, other nurses might take over during breaks, etc.) and one to two registration clerks were observed at each incident. Four observations included both front-end and back-end observations. The remaining nine observations were focused on the charge nurse, unit clerk, fast track nurse, and seven bedside/break nurses working each shift, including their use of whiteboards, clipboards, chart racks, and other artifacts. Observations took place in the acute area and in the fast track area. In total, the front-end was observed for 47.85 h and the back-end for 50.5 h. During all observations, informal interviews were conducted with 39 different ED staff members in total; 35 different nurses (triage, charge, break, float, and bedside nurses), two clerks (one registration and one unit clerk), and two physicians. Many staff members were observed more than once. Digital images of a wide range of artifacts and copies of various paper forms were collected during observations.

In addition, eight formal interviews were conducted: one group interview, one with the clinic information officer, three with the clinical nurse educator, and three with the emergency program manager. Five of the formal interviews were recorded and transcribed. Moreover, a large number of informal conversations took place with the clinical nurse educator and the emergency program manager, as well as with various nurses, clerks, and physicians between July 2007 and January 2008, when the first author was present at the hospital on average 2.5 full days each month. Other data materials include a field trip to two of the regional hospitals, with the core project group observing triage work using the Cerner Firstnet application in two adult EDs, and a phone interview survey with eight out of the 10 pediatric EDs in Canada (Bjørn, 2007).

During the action research activities, especially during design workshops, the researcher’s involvement was to notice and articulate aspects of the design that might conflict with existing work practices as they were experienced by the researcher during the ethnographic study. In this way the researcher acted as an outsider with a high degree of insider knowledge (Forsythe, 1999), bringing possible clashes to the design table. The observations made it possible to notice aspects invisible to the staff due to their tacit knowledge and bring these up for negotiations during the design workshops. In this way the researcher’s role was to act as an active participant during the design workshops, noticing issues and aspects that might be conflicting between the electronic system and the existing work practices. The research brought conflicting issues to the table early on, which prompted essential negotiations between design, work practices, standardization, and reconfiguration. However, decision-making was entirely up to the practitioners at the table (ED staff and health authorities). The researcher was careful never to judge mismatches, and thus kept herself ‘neutral’ during decision-making activities.

## Reconfiguring the electronic triage template

## Strategy of accumulative functionality

When the project began in July 2007, the extent to which the existing design of the electronic triage template would be reconfigured was unclear. At first, the design workshops were all about revising the electronic template to include support for the pediatric ED’s work practices. By comparing screen shots of the electronic triage template with the paper triage forms currently used, aspects of reconfigurations were identified. This activity resulted in ED staff’s requests for new data entries as well as more nuanced categories for particular nursing assessments, such as categories describing various types of coughing.

Initially, adding new data fields to the electronic template was unproblematic. The pediatric ED would negotiate a clear definition for new data entries that they required and would argue why existing data entries were not comprehensive. An example of a newly added functionality was ‘airway assessment’. ‘Airway assessment’ in pediatric triage entails assessment of whether the airways are compromised by, for example, a foreign object; this is distinct from the ‘respiratory assessment’, which is an assessment of a patient’s breathing in terms of, for example, asthma symptoms. A significant percentage of pediatric patients have respiratory complaints, thus the pediatric ED requested an additional field to document airway assessment as part of the vital signs. Assessing airways at the regional hospitals was done in relation to the respiratory assessment and therefore not differentiated to this level of detail, but they saw no problem in increasing the level of details with regard to airway assessment. A new data entry for airway assessment was added to the shared electronic triage template.

‘Weight’ and ‘vital signs’ are examples of these difficult negotiations. ‘Weight’ is essential information used within pediatric emergency medicine, since it directly influences the care for pediatric patients, such as medication dosage and calculations of fluids volume (input/output volume). The weight is, in most cases,<sup>3</sup> documented for all patients in the pediatric ED by the triage nurse in the triage template. Weight is pertinent information in pediatric treatment. Scrolling for weight would constrain the work of the pediatric ED, so a request was made to move the data entry, making it prominently displayed on the Chief Complaint page. Based on the negotiations, the data entry of weight was moved to a more pertinent location.

Documenting ‘vital signs’ is essential in all types of ED work; however, the order in which vital signs are documented differs depending on the particular type of patient population. In pediatric triage, the triage nurse must assess patients’ vital signs using the pediatric nursing triangle (ENA & Association, 2008), also referred to as nursing A (Airway assessment), B (Breathing (respiration)), C (Circulation (blood pressure)). In pediatrics, the assessment of airway and breathing should be done independently and in this order. If the airway is obstructed due to a foreign object, it is pertinent information affecting the PCTAS level even though the child might breathe normally. Pediatric anatomy is different from adult anatomy because children’s bodies are not fully developed in areas such as respiration. A large percentage of patients coming to the pediatric ED have respiratory complaints requiring immediate airway and breathing assessments. However, in the adult population, respiratory complaints are less common, and the largest percentage of patients entering the EDs have cardiovascular complaints,<sup>4</sup> which require immediate circulation assessment. This vital difference between pediatric and adult triage became highly transparent in the negotiations about the order of ‘vital signs’ on the electronic triage template. Whereas the regional EDs accepted additional fields such as ‘airway assessment’, they insisted on vital signs as ‘circulation’, ‘breathing’, ‘airway’ (C, B, A). At the same time, the pediatric ED insisted on vital signs as ‘airway’, ‘breathing’, ‘circulation’ (A, B, C).

Negotiations on aspects such as ‘vital signs’ were difficult to resolve due to the standardization strategy of the electronic template; however, they were essential issues that needed to be addressed. At first, a decision was made to leave the negotiations, which seemed irresolvable, and in the design workshops focus on aspects where it was possible for the multiple EDs to negotiate a shared solution.

## Extending categories

In October 2007, the strategy of ‘adding’ but never deleting content in the electronic triage template significantly increased the number of categories displayed as check-box or radio-bottom choices inside existing fields. One example was the data entry of ‘immunizations’, which, in the previous electronic triage template, included ‘up to date’, ‘outdated’, ‘none received’, and ‘unavailable’. All these immunization categories are relevant for adult patients. However, immunizations are a significant focus in pediatrics because children are in the process of getting their immunizations; therefore, detailed information is essential for triage assessment, requiring categories such as ‘within 6 weeks’ and ‘incomplete’. ‘Within 6 weeks’ indicates the patient’s complaint might be a reaction to an immunization, whereas ‘incomplete’ indicates that the child is in between immunizations, thus the presenting complaint might be related to the missing immunization. Also, in pediatric triage, the immunization category ‘outdated’ is not as useful. The EDs negotiated the solution so that in the standardized triage template the check-box possibilities related to immunizations were increased from four to six choices, where one would never be relevant for pediatric patients and two would only be relevant in a smaller percentage of the patients in the regional EDs, namely their pediatric patients.

Another example of redefining categories was nurseinitiated protocols. Nurses can initiate protocols without consulting a physician in particular cases. It could be to order X-rays directly at triage on ankle or wrist complaints. The data element ‘Protocols initiated at triage’ contains clearly defined medical protocols that nurses might initiate without consulting a physician. The EDs share some of these protocols, such as asthma and X-rays protocols. However, the lists of protocols are distinctly different between the regional and the pediatric hospitals (see Table 2).

The pediatric ED has a protocol for fever/neutropenia, which is a list of interventions used for patients under the age of 1 with fever. Because the anatomy of children under the age of 1 is not fully developed, their health is very fragile, which means that fever is a very serious condition and can cause irreparable damage. Therefore, when a triage nurse encounters fever in small children they start particular interventions right away as prescribed within the fever/neutropenia protocol. As with newborns, children with cancer also have very fragile health, and since the pediatric hospital includes a pediatric oncology ambulatory clinic, children with cancer frequently enter the pediatric ED. In these cases, the triage nurse has a special protocol, referred to as the sepsis protocol, stipulating interventions (similar to the fever/netropenia protocol, just for cancer patients).

Because the general population at the regional EDs is not newborns or children with cancer, the regional EDs do not have the fever/netropenia or sepsis protocol. Instead, they have a protocol for ‘chest pain’ and one for ‘hot stroke’, since these are typical conditions for the adult population.

The strategy with regard to immunizations was to extend the category, but extending the category of nursing protocols was problematic. Extending nursing protocols could risk creating medical documentation errors. A triage nurse using an extended category might, by mistake, indicate that a protocol had been initiated, which was not a part of the ED policy. It would be incorrect medical documentation if a pediatric nurse had documented that a regional protocol had been performed and vice versa. Even though nobody would expect a nurse from one hospital to actually conduct a protocol from another hospital on an actual patient, the risk that the triage nurse mistakenly checked the incorrect protocol would exist. This would jeopardize the data integrity for statistics and it might confuse the back-end personnel (physician and nurses) when reading the triage form and treating the patient. Extending the category of immunizations was possible; however, extending the category of nurse-initiated protocols was impossible and incorrect because it increased safety risk for patients. Further, there may be liability issues if a patient has a special condition (oncology child coming to the regional ED) for which a protocol is listed (e.g. sepsis) but not implemented in practice (sepsis was not implemented at the regional ED).

## First standardize – then localize

When the negotiations involving the data elements where a shared solution was possible, and had been solved, essential aspects such as ‘vital signs’ and ‘nursing protocols’ were still unsolved. At first, the strategy was that these negotiations ‘went up a level’; thus the management of the involved hospitals, including representatives from the individual hospitals’ statistical departments, became involved. However it was clear that it was impossible to reach a shared solution without constraining crucial work in either the pediatric or the regional hospitals.

Therefore a different strategy was adopted. Namely, that all involved EDs agreed on the shared electronic template thus far, and in this way the standardization had reach some sense of closure. This newly standardized electronic template then became the basis for further localization. Consequently, the pediatric ED entered in a new phase of design workshops where customization of the generic template was reconfigured with only the pediatric ED in mind. In this way the design process formed two phases. First, a phase where a shared electronic template was designed based on the negotiations between the involved participants, and then a phase where the standardized electronic template was localized into multiple reconfigured templates: one for the pediatric and one for the regional EDs.

## Approach of boundary factors and contextual contingencies

There are fundamental differences between work practices in hospital departments. The foundations for these differences are shaped by the existing artifacts (paper forms, equipments, physical layout, etc.) and the particular context of the department (patient populations, work organization, etc.). Investigating the reconfiguration process of the electronic triage template, we found factors in the design that were malleable, such as adding the field of ‘airway assessment’, moving the ‘weight’ field to a more visible location, and extending the category of ‘immunization’. Different social contexts (hospital departments) can agree on the structure and form of these factors, because these are plastic and flexible enough for local interpretation. The factors are transferable between the various contexts, and we refer to them as boundary factors. Boundary factors might vary in definition and interpretation between various contexts. Immunizations can be both ‘outdated’ and ‘within 6 weeks’. It is possible for healthcare professionals from the adult and pediatric contexts to agree on a common definition that captures the richness of the category and remains flexible enough for local interpretation. In this way, a strategy of accumulative functionality for redesigning the generic electronic template was possible in reference to the boundary factors. The strategy of accumulative functionality implies that designers built the organizational reality of the adopting organizations into the generic kernel of the application packet but continue redesigning and tailoring the template to fit particular needs, and in this way expand the design by continuously adding new functionality (Pollock et al., 2007).

During the design process we identified data elements of the electronic template that were closely related to the particularities of the work practices and organizational immalleability. These elements are strong candidates for localization, for example, ‘vital signs’. Here, the medical guidelines embedded within the work practices in the pediatric ED required a particular display of the vital signs, while the guidelines embedded within the work practices at the regional EDs required a different display of the vital signs. We also identified distinct differences related to nurses-initiated protocols. These protocols will always be different among hospitals. Simply extending the category of nurses’ protocols to include a shared list between the EDs would increase the risk of medical documentation mistakes and errors. We conceptualize these immalleable elements as contextual contingencies. Contextual contingencies form the particularities of the organizational context, which are not logically part of the ‘core’ kernel of the generic system. Designing and localizing electronic templates includes reconfiguration of contextual contingencies. This approach requires a different strategy than accumulative functionality, since such as strategy is bound to fail.

The contextual contingencies (e.g. vital signs and nurse protocols) increased the complexity in solving the design problem. The initial aim was to reconfigure one standardized electronic triage template, applying one generic template based upon a database of comparable data for research. Nevertheless, it is vital that the design of electronic templates takes the contextual contingencies of situated work practices into account, since the technology (the triage template) will be an additional artifact shaping the work of the ED (Timmermans & Berg, 2003).

Implementing new technology will always transform the work practices (Berg, 2001), and the design of technology should aim to support essential work practices within the context of use, ensuring important flexibility. Ellingsen et al. (2007) propose perceiving standardization of nursing through IS as incomplete until adapted into the work through co-construction of the standards by the users, leading to significant unintended consequences. They show in their study how nurses transform the standards of medical terminology embedded within information technology during their enactment of the terminology. Ellingsen et al. (2007) argue that for a standard to work properly, it is essential that it is transformed in the very process of use, and therefore standardization and work practices are highly interlinked. Our approach to standardization extends the work of Ellingsen et al. (2007) by proposing to support the design and configuration of technology both before the actual implementation and use, as well as processes of reconfiguration after implementation. Given the unanticipated use, we acknowledge that it is impossible to design a perfect system prior to actual use, which makes continuous reconfigurations of both technology and work practices after implementation based upon experienced use essential. This has been conceptualized as the importance of technology-in-use practices in previous literature (Boulus & Bjørn, 2008). In the case reported here, a new position, clinic information officer, was created, and the pediatric ED employed one of their nurses in this position. One of the essential activities for the clinical information officer was to continuously collect information about use of the system and suggestions for improvement as well as to communicate these to the system designers.

Even though we acknowledge that perfect design is impossible to predict prior to use, designers should still strive for a good design, and we argue that the distinction between boundary factors and contextual contingencies can help to construct a good initial design foundation – a foundation that can be used as a baseline for continuous reconfigurations after the implementation. We agree that standardization should be viewed as a negotiation process, co-constructing the standard in relation to use practices. Because technology as an artifact will enable some work practices while constrain others (Suchman, 1994; Orlikowski, 1995; Tjora, 2000), it is essential that a newly negotiated electronic triage template enable the important activities of triage work in the concrete contexts, ensuring essential flexibility.

The conflict between standardization and configuration is thus a negotiation process between the involved participants where they try to reach closure on a specific artifact (Cadili & Whitley, 2005). What we argue in this paper is that ‘success’ in reaching closure is not a process where all participants must agree on every detail, as in maximal flexibility. Instead, reaching closure should be perceived as a negotiation process where the core part of the system (boundary factors) is agreed upon while the local differences (contextual contingencies) are identified and reconfigured within the local entities of the system.

Here, we follow the approach to think in degrees of flexibility rather than maximal flexibility (Hanseth et al., 1996). However, while Hanseth et al. (1996) do not provide further guidelines on how we can think in degrees of flexibility, we extend the approach by providing concepts that can be applied within a design process involving multiple organizations. Boundary factors and contextual contingencies can be used directly by practitioners to articulate, identify, and distinguish between degrees of interpretative flexibility related to particular data entries.

We propose the conceptualization of boundary factors and contextual contingencies as helpful to articulate the essential distinctions between the immalleable and malleable aspects of local work practices directly relevant for the design. During the design workshops, we learned that some work practices in emergency work could be aligned between contexts, while other practices were distinct in nature and thus required localization of the generic template. This empirical observation is in line with prior research where localization has been found essential (Pollock et al., 2007). The possible aligning features thus form the boundary factors and local diversities are the contextual contingencies. Both types of elements are essential to bring to the design table for electronic triage templates, however, in different ways. While it is possible to design and create common data elements for the malleable boundary factors between contexts, it is impossible and also unwanted to design and create common fields for the immalleable contextual contingencies because this would either constrain essential work practices (e.g. related to the vital signs), or increase the risk of mistakes in documentation (e.g. nursing protocols). The contextual contingencies form the specific and unique clinical requirements of a particular department, which must to be taken into account when designing a new technical artifact. We agree with Keen et al. (1981). who states that implementing common systems as a standardization process is not a simple process of installing the same applications on computers at different sites, but instead implies that central and local groups negotiate and define the ‘core system, which then is modified to support local practices. Expanding on this perspective, we here offer conceptua lizations of the 'core' parts as boundary factors and the ‘modifications’ as contextual contingencies, while arguing that closure of the artifact (the electronic template) should not be viewed only as successful in the cases where various stakeholders managed to agree on one common standard. In this way, we challenge the suggested perspective on standardization of healthcare templates referred to as reflexive standardization (Hanseth et al., 2006) by arguing that successful standardization is not achieved when stakeholders agree on all aspects on the artifact, but is instead achieved when relevant stakeholders define and agree on boundary factors for the design of the core part of the technical artifact, and then local reconfigurations of the artifact are addressed by local groups based on the contextual contingencies emerging from investigations of local work practices. Instead of observing the increased disorder emerging from attempts to create one standard for all, we should realize that ‘one standard for all’ is an idealized and unreachable goal, and instead embrace the heterogeneous elements between multiple contexts and create designs supporting local contextual contingencies.

Even though many of the contextual contingencies in the empirical case presented in this paper are closely related to the particular patient population, we will argue that there will always be distinct differences between same profession departments related to specific and unique clinical requirements, organization, and context (Balka et al., 2008). The spatial layout of an ED always shapes the work practices in particular ways. In the case where the registration clerk is located right next to the triage nurse (as is the case of the two regional EDs, the paper flow between the two will be different compared with cases where there is a physical distance between the triage nurse and registration clerk, as was the case for the pediatric ED). Attention to the contextual contingency of spatial layout can either form re-organizing of work practices for the sake of standardization, or reconfiguration of the embedded model of work in the technical artifact to support the local contingencies. What is essential is that it is acknowledged and negotiated during the design of the technical artifact.

Contextual contingencies might also be related to multiple aspects of the local context such as spatial layout and coordinative artifacts; one example is the data element of ‘weight’. As argued above, weight is essential in pediatric emergency practice. However, in cases of patients below the age of 1, weighing the child requires that the child is undressed before using the highly sensitive scale. Because the triage area in the pediatric ED is close to the front door (and can therefore be cold), and because it takes time to undress a baby, it is decided that the triage nurse never weighs babies during the triage assessments. Instead, the triage nurse leaves the weighing of babies for the bedside nurse. In the paperbased system, this meant that the field of weight would be empty on the paper triage form, although still displayed on the form, prompting the bedside nurse to undress the baby and use the highly sensitive scale in the back-end of the ED, thus documenting the information on the paper form. The work practices surrounding the issue of weight is in this way related to the patient population (pediatric care requirement), the spatial layout (physical location of triage area), and coordinative artifacts (use of paper triage form). In the paper-based system, the issue of weight is solved by having the empty field at the paper form stipulating whether the bedside nurse needs to measure weight or not. After the implementation of the electronic triage template, this would be printed and then used as the coordinative artifact managing the patient trajectories in the ED. However, when printing the electronic triage template, only the fields with content would be printed. This meant that the bedside nurse would not be prompted by the printed form to measure the weight of babies, because without weight information, this field would not be on the printout. In this case, this issue was solved by triage nurses writing ‘0’ in the weight data entry field, prompting the field to be printed on the form. This observation points out that issues of localization and configuration of electronic templates should not only concern negotiations of the electronic user interface, but also include physical artifacts constructed based on electronic input.

## Conclusion

The action research study reported here formed continuous action cycles of diagnosing conflicting design issues (mismatches between the technology and local work practices), identifying possible design solutions (reconfigurations of the electronic template), and evaluating design solutions (negotiating standardization and configuration). The researcher’s role during these design workshops was to articulate the possible clashes between technology and work based on the in-depth ethnographic investigations of the work in the ED, and in this way prompt important negotiations between the practitioners (healthcare practitioners and system developers). The researcher’s role was to be an outsider with much insider knowledge (Forsythe, 1999), since articulation of the tacit local work essential for prompting negotiations requires the distance that only outsiders have and at the same time requires in-depth understanding of what goes on in the ED.

Flexibility refers to the capacity of an IS to adapt, support, and enable organizational practices and change (Gebauer & Lee, 2008). Insufficient flexibility within technology can limit the success of a generic application introduced into multiple contexts by preventing its use in certain circumstances and by making exceptionhandling necessary (Gebauer & Schober, 2006). Designing one single electronic template to be used in multiple contexts embeds the challenge of balancing between standardization – process of striving for one single template – and configuration – the process of customizing the template to support local work practices. How can we develop concepts useful for articulating and identify ing the conflicting perspectives between standardization and reconfiguration embedded within design activities of HIS? Based on our study we suggest the analytical distinction between boundary factors and the contextual contingencies as an approach to handle the conflict through identification, articulation, and negotiation of crucial aspects. Boundary factors are plastic and flexible enough for local interpretation to be transferable between the various contexts. It is possible to negotiate a common definition that captures the richness of boundary factors’ categories and which is flexible enough for local interpretation. Thus, the strategy of accumulative functionality when re-designing the generic electronic template is possible in relation to the boundary factors. Contextual contingencies form the particularities of the organizational context. They are closely related to the particularities of work practices and organizational immalleability, and thus are strong candidates for localization. Contextual contingencies are not logically part of the ‘core’ kernel of generic systems; nevertheless, it is vital that the design activities of standardized electronic templates take the contextual contingencies of situated work practices into account, since the technology will, after the implementation, be an additional artifact shaping the work. Contextual contingencies increase the complexity in solving the standardized design, and strategies such as accumulative functionality are bound to fail in relation to contextual contingencies.

From a standardization perspective, it is convenient to think of all types of data fields in an electronic template as boundary factors with clear definitions – as the ‘core’ part of the application (Keen et al., 1981).
