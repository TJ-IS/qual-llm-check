# DOES RANSOMWARE MAKE INVESTORS “WANNACRY”? ON INVESTORS’ DIVERGENT REACTIONS TO RANSOMWARE HITS AND NEAR MISSES<sup>1</sup>

Sebastian W. Schuetz Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder Boulder, CO, U.S.A., {swschuetz@outlook.com}

Yan Chen Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, Florida International University, Miami, FL, U.S.A. {yachen@fiu.edu}

Jens Forderer Business School, University of Mannheim, Mannheim, GERMANY {jens.foerderer@uni-mannheim.de}

Yusi Ma Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, College of Business, Florida International University, Miami, FL, U.S.A. {yma021@fiu.edu}

In recent years, ransomware has become one of the most dangerous cyber threats, with successful attacks causing severe operational disruptions and staggering damages. Rationally speaking, investors should react negatively to firms’ ransomware disclosures, but this may not always be the case. Based on norm theory, we describe a paradoxical phenomenon wherein investors exhibit negative reactions to ransomware hits (i.e., events that led to operational disruptions) but positive reactions to near misses (i.e., events in which operational disruptions were narrowly avoided). The positive reactions occur due to an outcome bias in which near-miss events—events that are objectively negative but less severe than expected—are viewed positively instead of negatively. We tested these predictions by reporting on an investigation of stock market reactions to disclosures of ransomware hits vs. near misses. To do so, we assembled a comprehensive dataset of ransomware incidents disclosed by U.S. public firms. Using the event study method, we estimated abnormal stock market returns and found evidence in support of our predictions. First, in line with expectations, ransomware hits that led to the expected severe impact resulted in stock price drops of -4.40%. However, near misses, where disruptions were avoided, were rewarded with gains of 2.87%, confirming positive instead of negative reactions. This offers new insights into investors’ biased responses to certain cybersecurity incidents. These positive reactions, however, represent a call for caution because, albeit seemingly favorable, they mask underlying risks.

Keywords: Ransomware, event study, norm theory, near misses, Google Trends, cumulative abnormal returns, operational disruptions

## Introduction

Ransomware refers to malware installed by a criminal organization that encrypts devices and data in order to render them inaccessible until a ransom is paid (Proofpoint, 2022). The menace of ransomware only became fully apparent in 2017, when the “WannaCry” strain affected 230,000 computers in over 150 countries. Since then, the menace has reached “epidemic proportions,” with ransomware attack attempts occurring every few seconds (Morgen, 2019).

To extort lucrative ransoms, ransomware attacks attempt to cause devastating operational disruptions. Consequently, successful attacks often wreak havoc on firms’ primary business functions—such as when ransomware crippled Maersk’s shipping operations, disabled JBS S.A.’s meat processing facilities, or halted Colonial’s gasoline delivery operations. Victims are confronted with the choice of either paying a hefty ransom or enduring financial losses until operations are restored. Moreover, they face a heightened risk of repeated attacks as well as additional downstream costs, including legal fees, heightened investments in cybersecurity, tarnished reputations, and increased insurance premiums (Clancy, 2023).

Against this backdrop, it would be rational to expect that disclosures of ransomware incidents would be met with negative market reactions. Following classic asset pricing theory, stock prices are a function of risk and expected returns, and the disclosure of a ransomware incident should thus indicate substantial idiosyncratic cyberrisk and impaired future returns, leading to reduced asset prices (e.g., Fama & French, 2004; Fu, 2009). This notion finds empirical support in the literature, which has shown negative reactions to data breach disclosures (cf. Ali et al., 2021).

Contrary to these classic expectations of negative market reactions, we posit that an outcome bias may paradoxically result in divergent reactions—both negative and positive—to different types of ransomware events. With ransomware attacks frequently causing operational disaster, we predict that investors, known to be prone to behavioral biases (Hirshleifer, 2015), may exhibit negative reactions to ransomware hits (i.e., incidents in which the expected disaster occurred) but positive reactions to near misses (i.e., incidents in which the expected disaster was avoided). We root our predictions in literature on near-miss bias and norm theory (Kahneman & Miller, 1986), which suggests that positive reactions arise from superficial assessments of event outcomes, specifically through favorable comparisons of near-miss outcomes against their normative counterfactuals—the expected disasters that did not occur (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Gnoni et al., 2022; Tinsley et al., 2012). Therefore, when firms disclose near-miss ransomware incidents, as opposed to ransomware hits, investors make superficial, positive assessments based on the better-thanexpected outcome, leading markets to react positively rather than negatively to the revealed vulnerability and near ransomware disaster.

In this study, we tested these predictions and examined whether ransomware hits and near misses lead to divergent stock market reactions. In doing so, we align with a larger stream of research that has examined the consequences of other cybersecurity events, such as data breaches and cybersecurity investments (e.g., Bose & Leung, 2019; Kwon & Johnson, 2014; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022), as well as the larger stream of work on cybersecurity (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2020; D’Arcy & Basoglu, 2022; Hui et al., 2023). However, to date, empirical examinations of the consequences of ransomware events are still missing despite their prevalence and relevance to stakeholders. Furthermore, while near misses represent an important concept in other disciplines, such as safety management (e.g., Gnoni et al., 2022), the role of near misses in the context of the stock market and cybersecurity incidents remains unexamined.

To test market reactions to ransomware events, we constructed a comprehensive dataset of ransomware events among firms publicly traded in the U.S. by building on the Critical Infrastructure Ransomware Attacks (CIRA) dataset (Rege, 2022) as a baseline and enriching it by identifying ransomware events from several other datasets, news archives, and SEC filings. In total, we identified 165 ransomware events among U.S. public firms. Based on initial disclosure statements, we distinguished between ransomware hits and near misses. Then, after data cleansing, we retained 104 events and conducted an event study to estimate eventinduced stock market reactions (Brown & Warner, 1985).

Across various expected return models, we found consistent evidence supporting our predictions: markets react negatively to ransomware hits and positively to near misses. These findings withstood various robustness checks. Furthermore, to provide additional evidence that positive reactions to near misses are due to investors’ superficial reliance on outcomes rather than systematic analysis, we show that investors devoted less effort to researching near-miss events. Overall, this study offers a novel test of near-miss bias and norm theory in the stock market and documents counterintuitive positive market reactions to the disclosure of near-miss cybersecurity incidents (e.g., Ali et al., 2021; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022). However, our findings call for caution for firms and investors, as positive reactions may obscure the fact that the occurrence of near misses may actually be indicative of future risk.

## Literature Review

## Market Reactions to Ransomware and Other Security Incidents

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has systematically examined market reactions to ransomware attacks, notwithstanding case studies on individual ransomware attacks (e.g., Corbet & Goodell, 2022; Jarjoui et al., 2021). This is punctuated by a systematic literature review of 58 event studies that examined market reactions to security events and failed to find a single study on ransomware (Ali et al., 2021). The paucity of research is probably due to the relatively neoteric prevalence of ransomware. As mentioned above, the first notable outbreak was the WannaCry attack in 2017, followed by a surge of attacks during the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, businesses and policymakers lack research on the financial impact of ransomware attacks. Our research thus represents a timely effort to address this research gap.

Ransomware events differ from data breaches and denial-ofservice (DoS) attacks, two other types of security events that have received considerable research attention (e.g., D’Arcy et al., 2020; Hoehle et al., 2022; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2003; Kwon & Johnson, 2018; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022). Ransomware compromises both the confidentiality and availability of information systems, whereas data breaches affect the confidentiality of information, and DoS attacks impact the availability of information systems. Therefore, ransomware attacks seize access to critical information systems and data and may disrupt business operations, whereas data breaches do not lead to disruptions. Likewise, although DoS attacks can disrupt operations, their duration is typically short (less than 10 minutes) and confined to web-facing services (Hovav & D’Arcy, 2003; Toulas, 2023), whereas the disruptions caused by ransomware attacks can last much longer (days or even weeks) and can impact a wide range of firm-internal systems, including those critical for business operations. Since ransomware attacks pose significant operational risks, it is thus plausible that ransomware events are subject to greater scrutiny by investors and engender stronger reactions. Nevertheless, market reactions to ransomware attacks remain unstudied.

Notably, based on the literature on data breaches, one would expect negative market responses to ransomware attack disclosures. Although estimates vary, a majority of prior studies have reported overall negative market reactions to data breaches (Ali et al., 2021). Likewise, it has been suggested that DoS attacks lead to negative market reactions that are sometimes even more negative than those involving data breaches (e.g., Das et al., 2012; Goldstein et al., 2011; Yayla & Hu, 2011), but others have not found this to be the case (e.g., Hovav & D’Arcy, 2003; Kannan et al., 2007). Overall, however, many estimates of short-term stock market reactions suggest that firms can expect losses of -0.05% to -2% for data breaches and DoS attacks (e.g., Campbell et al., 2003; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Gwebu et al., 2018; Kwon & Johnson, 2015; Martin et al., 2017; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022; Richardson et al., 2019; Yayla & Hu, 2011).

## Reactions to Near Misses

Near misses are “failures that could have happened but did not” (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008, p. 1427, emphasis in original). They are “misses” in that the bad outcome, the disaster, was averted, and “near” in that there was a high possibility of a bad outcome: i.e., it could have been much worse (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Gnoni et al., 2022; Kahneman & Varey, 1990). The interest in near misses in fields such as aviation, healthcare, and construction assumes that near misses and actual disasters share common causes and that studying near misses can help identify these causes as to prevent future disasters (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Feng et al., 2022; Gnoni et al., 2022).

Perhaps ironically, the literature has also shown that learning from near-miss events is hindered by near-miss bias (Dillon et al., 2006; Tinsley et al., 2012). Near-miss bias, a form of outcome bias, stems from the tendency to heuristically evaluate a near-miss event based on the avoided hypothetical consequences rather than critically analyzing its root causes (i.e., whether the event is caused by a systemic problem or a chance event) (Baron & Hershey, 1988; Dillon & Tinsley, 2008). As a result, near-miss events are often mistakenly perceived as successes, despite actually being narrowly avoided disasters (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008).

Notably, despite researchers’ interest in near misses in organizational research, there is a paucity of studies examining investors’ reactions to near-miss events, even though investors’ behavioral biases have received significant attention (see Hirshleifer, 2015 for a review). Similar investor biases due to heuristic comparisons to counterfactual outcomes have been documented. For instance, investors tend to feel regret when they evaluate their investments in reference to counterfactual stocks they could have bought (e.g., Arisoy et al., 2024).

## Theoretical Foundation and Hypotheses

In this study, we investigate investor reactions to ransomware hits and near misses. We consider a ransomware hit to be an event in which a ransomware attack led to lasting operational disruptions (e.g., days or weeks) to an organization’s primary business functions (e.g., sales, production, and fulfillment). Likewise, we consider a near miss to be an event in which an incident occurred, but lasting disruptions were avoided. We expect shareholders to react negatively to hits and positively to near misses. We base our expectations on norm theory, which explains how events are evaluated based on their outcomes and has been used to account for positive reactions to near misses in studies on organizational learning and safety management (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Dillon et al., 2006; Tinsley et al., 2012). We extend and contextualize these explanations to ransomware attacks and stock market reactions.

## Norm Theory

Norm theory (Kahneman & Miller, 1986) posits that exceptional (i.e., unusual or surprising) events invoke counterfactual thinking, meaning that such events are interpreted in relation to their normative alternatives that capture the outcomes that “could, should, or might have occurred, but did not” (Dillon et al., 2006, p. 8; Kahneman, 1992). Normative alternatives, or norms, are “ad hoc representations which reflect the momentary activation of instances retrieved from memory or constructed in imagination” (Kahneman, 1992, p. 306). Thus, events are “not judged in isolation but relative to a reference point” (Dillon et al., 2006, p. 7) and norms serve as this reference point by capturing what would be “normal” or expected (Kahneman, 1992).

The construction of norms occurs naturally in an ad hoc fashion based on backward search for similar experiences stored in memory (Gavanski & Wells, 1989; Kahneman, 1992). Norms thus tend to be biased toward highly available examples (frequent, salient, or recent memories) (Kahneman & Miller, 1986), such as recent ransomware reports in the media in our case. The closer an event resembles its normative alternatives, the easier it is to imagine what could have been different (Dillon et al., 2006; Miller & McFarland, 1986). For instance, investors aware of the ransomware attack on JBS S.A. that led to a processing standstill could easily imagine the consequences of such attacks on other food processing businesses.

Examples of catastrophic ransomware attacks should be readily available to investors. Since 2017, the U.S. media has engaged in intense reporting on ransomware attacks (Aday, 2023). This reporting presumably cuts across industries, as virtually all industries in the U.S. have now been affected (FBI, 2023). As a result, there is now widespread awareness of ransomware among the general population (Haner et al., 2022). Since investors pay close attention to business news, they should also be aware of the ransomware menace (Tetlock, 2007). Furthermore, because sensationalist media reporting tends to focus on severe, negative cases in which firms were hit hard and suffered severely from devastating disruptions, such examples of ransomware hits should be highly accessible to investors and hence inform investors’ norms for typical outcomes.

The use of such norms as a context for the evaluation of abnormal events constitutes heuristic processing and can lead to biased judgments and errors in causal inference (Kahneman & Miller, 1986; Miller & McFarland, 1986). By contrasting the event outcome against the counterfactual outcome, judgments of an abnormal event tend to result in less thorough examination of the event itself, instead heuristically evaluating the event based on a superficial comparison between what happened and what could have happened. However, by judging the event in terms of the disaster that could have been, even objectively negative events can be seen in a positive light (Roese, 1997). For instance, the tourists surviving the 2004 Tsunami in Southeast Asia believed that they were “lucky,” even though, logically speaking, they were the few unlucky ones among the millions visiting that year (Teigen & Jensen, 2010).

Furthermore, the use of norms for counterfactual thinking can lead to errors in causal inference because of a heuristically perceived connection between outcomes and their antecedents (e.g., good outcomes result from good decisions) (Gavanski & Wells, 1989; Roese, 1997), even when this is objectively not the case. Dillon and Tinsley (2008) demonstrate this by showing that participants in an experiment involving students and NASA personnel rated the competence and intelligence of managers of successes and near misses comparably, despite a bad managerial decision almost causing a catastrophe in the near-miss scenario. In both scenarios, however, the ultimate outcome was positive, leading to similarly positive evaluations of managers responsible for successes vs. near misses (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Tinsley et al., 2012). This is, of course, biased, given that the occurrence of a near-miss event indicates a significant underlying problem. Near misses, instead of being judged similar to success, should “be soberly evaluated as a failure” (Dillon et al., 2006, p. 6).

## Market Reactions to Ransomware Event Disclosures

Since hits are “normal” events and near misses are “anomalous” events, their evaluation should lead to differential reactions. Specifically, reactions to ransomware hits would be expected to be negative because the disclosure of severe operational disruptions aligns with norm-based expectations and should thus be judged as a “normal” or expected consequence. Such events are less likely to be judged in contrast to their normative counterfactuals (Kahneman & Miller, 1986), leading to judgments based on normally established rules of inference (Epstein et al., 1992). As per classical asset pricing theory, such judgments deliberatively consider a trade-off between risk and future returns (e.g., Fama & French, 2004; Fu, 2009). As a ransomware hit, logically speaking, indicates that the firm had insufficient capabilities to protect its mission-critical systems, investors may interpret the occurrence of the incident as a sign of poor cyber resilience. More generally, such failures are often considered to be indicative of poor management (Dillon et al., 2006; Tinsley et al., 2012), leading to leadership changes and security investments (Banker & Feng, 2019; Benaroch & Chernobai, 2017; Kwon & Johnson, 2014). As reactionary security investments and leadership changes may take time to address the underlying issues (Kwon & Johnson, 2014), firms face a period of heightened risk. Moreover, the disclosure of a hit would be expected to lead to reduced expectations of future returns, as ransomware attacks that encrypt critical systems lead to direct losses in production output and revenue (Clancy, 2023). In other words, ransomware hits cause firms to bleed money every hour of the attack. Beyond financial losses, they lead to tarnished reputations, eroded customer confidence, and increased insurance premiums. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: Ransomware hits are associated with negative stock market reactions.

In contrast, we expect investor reactions to ransomware near misses to be positive. This is due to the anomaly of the event, which evokes counterfactual thinking and heuristic processing (Dillon & Tinsley, 2005; Kahneman, 1992; Kahneman & Miller, 1986). Against the normative expectations of operational disruptions, near misses do not lead to material consequences and may thus induce surprise and counterfactual thinking. Thus, comparing the near miss to the counterfactual disaster that could have happened, investors will likely feel relieved and evaluate the event positively, despite it being negative in nature (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Dillon et al., 2006). Near misses tend to “be treated as providing too optimistic a picture” (March et al., 1991, p. 9) and are more likely to be interpreted optimistically as evidence of resilience—rather than being more soberly interpreted as evidencing vulnerability (Dillon & Tinsley, 2008). As a result, near misses are often equated with successes (Dillon et al., 2006; Tinsley et al., 2012), which results in less thorough examinations of the underlying causes of the event. Therefore, ransomware near misses may be misinterpreted as a sign of cyber resilience and decreased future cyberrisk since they may be seen as a demonstration of a certain level of effectiveness of existing security measures, as well as preparedness and competence in responding to such events. Hence, being able to dodge the “hit” may lead investors to reprice a firm’s stock given the new information about future cyberrisk, leading to a relief rally. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2: Ransomware near misses are associated with positive stock market reactions.

## Method

## Research Design

To test the hypotheses, we implemented the event study method (Brown & Warner, 1985), which estimates market reactions to events, called abnormal returns (AR). ARs for each firm <sub>??</sub> and day <sub>??</sub> are computed as $A R _ { i , d } = R _ { i , d } - $ $E \big ( R _ { i , d } \big )$ , where $R _ { i , d }$ represents observed returns and $E \big ( R _ { i , d } \big )$ represents the counterfactual returns that would have been expected if the event had not occurred (Sorescu et al., 2017). To capture reactions across a period, ARs are summed up to obtain a cumulative abnormal return (CAR), which represents the total increase or decrease in firm value. The event study method has been used in IS research to estimate stock market reactions (e.g., Benaroch & Chernobai, 2017; Bose & Leung, 2019; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022) and has been extended to study non-stock market reactions, i.e., changes in Google search volume (e.g., Da et al., 2011; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022), which we use in a post hoc analysis.

## Data Collection

We relied on media-based databases, news sources, and SEC filings to assemble a comprehensive sample of relevant events in U.S. public firms. Our starting point was the CIRA v12.7 dataset (Rege, 2022), which is a unique and comprehensive global record of ransomware attacks based on incidents that were publicly disclosed in security or media reports. We then searched other datasets from the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the Identity Theft Resource Center, Information is Beautiful, the Privacy Rights Clearinghouse, the Repository of Industrial Security Incidents, and the Spin.ai ransomware tracker. We further used the news search engines Lexis Uni and Google News, which cover a broad array of news outlets, using the keywords “ransomware” and “attack.” Each news item was screened similarly to the procedure employed for the CIRA dataset (Rege, 2022). Finally, we conducted a full-text search of 8-K and 10-Q/K reports using “ransomware” as a keyword.

In total, we identified 165 events spanning from 2017 to 2023, a period with established disclosure requirements: In 2011, the SEC clarified that firms must disclose material cybersecurity incidents and immaterial incidents if, in combination with other information, they can be deemed material (SEC, 2011). Data breach laws also mandate disclosure when personal information is accessed (National Center of State Legislatures, 2022). While laws are clear on the disclosure requirements of ransomware hits due to their material impact, they are vague on near misses. However, to maintain market trust and avoid potential regulatory noncompliance, firms likely disclose near misses at similar rates. As shown below, we find no systematic difference between firms that disclose hits vs. near misses.

To ensure data accuracy, four researchers independently verified the events, event dates, and further event details through news release searches. Importantly, for each incident, we searched for the date of the earliest news article reporting the incident, which we used as the event date to isolate the effect of the earliest public disclosure of an event.

Event studies assume the absence of confounding events during the event window period (Brown & Warner, 1985); therefore, confounding events were eliminated from the sample. Accordingly, we excluded 28 events for which firms issued 8-K reports that disclosed material events (e.g., earnings and operational results announcements, the departure of officers) as well as 10-Q/K reports filed during the event window. Events with 8-K filings disclosing a ransomware event were retained. To fully isolate the effect of operational disruptions (i.e., hits) from data breaches, we excluded a further 19 events where data was stolen and ransoms were demanded but no systems were encrypted. <sup>2</sup> This led to a sample of 118 events. To obtain stock market data, we manually matched the firm names to CUSIP identifiers in the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) database. For a further 14 events, no sufficient stock market returns could be obtained because firms were not yet listed or had been delisted during the estimation period. Our final sample size is 104, which is well above the minimum detectable sample size (i.e., 26 events per group) necessary to observe an effect of our size, assuming a statistical power of 0.8, according to a power analysis that is not reported for brevity. It is also in line with recommendations for sufficient sample size in event studies (MacKinlay, 1997) and norms for sample size in the literature (see the literature review table in the transparency materials). ## Main Variables

Stock market reactions were measured for each event as CARs. As noted earlier, CARs are estimated by summing up daily ARs, which are the difference between actual returns and expected returns for a given day (Brown & Warner, 1985). To estimate expected returns, we used four commonly used expected return models: the market-adjusted model (MAM), a baseline model in which expected firm returns are equal to the market return; the market model (MM), which relies on the capital asset pricing model to estimate expected returns; the Fama-French three-factor model (FF3), which further considers size and value factors (Fama & French, 1993); and the Fama-French five-factor model (FF5; Fama & French, 2015), which further adds profitability and investment factors. Following prior research, we based our conclusions on the MM, using the other models to assess the robustness of our findings (e.g., Benaroch & Chernobai, 2017; Bose & Leung, 2019; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022). Moreover, following prior research, we relied on a pre-event window of 100 days to calibrate expected returns, using a 50- day gap to the event day (Borah & Tellis, 2014). We also used different event windows. Longer event windows after the event capture information diffusion but risk confounding, while pre-event windows capture information leakage (Sorescu et al., 2017). Ali et al. (2021) reported that the following windows are common: [0, 0], [0, 1], [0, 2], [-1, 0], [-1, 1], [-2, 2]. We applied these windows, with formal tests using a [-2, 2] window to capture both pre-event information leakage and post-event reactions, with consistent results across other windows.

Near miss was measured as a dummy variable and constructed based on a rigorous coding process of ransomware disclosures. We used our definition of hits and near misses as predefined codes and followed the procedure of Nikkhah and Grover (2022) to ensure consistent coding: First, the researchers independently coded a subset of 10 events, followed by a discussion to reach consensus on the qualitative evidence that aligns with our definitions of hits and near misses (see Table 2). Then, all remaining events were coded. Each event was coded by two researchers, which ensured that each event was coded twice and allowed us to calculate interrater agreements. The mean agreement was 86% and Cohen’s kappa was 0.73, indicating excellent agreement (Fleiss et al., 2003). Finally, all disagreements were discussed and resolved, and 100% agreement was achieved. ## Analysis & Results

## Main Evidence

To test H1 and H2, we began by assessing event-induced stock market reactions. Figure 1 displays the mean ARs and CARs four days before and after the events. For hits, we observed a small negative spike in ARs on the event day, with CARs becoming negative. In contrast, for near misses, we observed slightly positive ARs up to two days before the event. This finding indicates information leakage, as has been observed in other event studies (Sorescu et al., 2017).

Table 3 presents the formal tests for H1 and H2. It reports the mean CARs and statistical tests for the four different expected return models. We observed negative mean CARs for ransomware hits across all event windows in all models. For our main model, the MM, we found that ransomware disclosures are associated with a mean CAR of -4.40% for hits and 2.87% for near misses in the [-2, 2] event window, which translate to median market value losses of -\$184.22 million vs. gains of \$66.99 million. Therefore, based on the MM model, we found support for H1 and H2. Notably, except for a few instances, our findings were validated across a wide range of event windows and expected return models, demonstrating the robustness of our conclusions. In addition, all observed differences are significant, supporting the notion of divergent reactions.

## Robustness

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted several falsification tests following prior event studies (e.g., Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Sorescu et al., 2017). First, we tested whether our observed results are an artifact of the firms in the sample. If so, we would expect to observe significant stock price reactions to arbitrary events at different points in time. To test this, we estimated the returns following arbitrary placebo events 45 days and 90 days before the event dates in our sample (e.g., for instance, for a ransomware event occurring on March 1, 2024, a placebo event was tested that occurred 45 days or 90 days prior to that date). The results of both tests showed no significant reactions or differences in mean CAR between the two event types across all models (i.e., MM, MAM, FF3, FF5) and event windows (e.g., [0, 0], [0, 1], …). Panels A and B in Figure 2 plot the ARs for the MM model, which are insignificant. These plots show no patterns that resemble our main results.

Second, we tested whether the reactions were specific to the event dates in the sample by testing the returns using a random sample of stock market firms and the event dates. Across all models and event windows, we found no significant returns or differences. Panel C shows the ARs obtained using the MM model, which are also insignificant.

Third, we assessed whether the results may be spurious due to differences in the estimation window. To do so, we followed prior work (e.g., Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022) and set the estimation window [-150, -50] as our event window, thereby defining a new estimation window equivalent to our main approach (i.e., [-300, -200]). Figure 2, Panel D plots the ARs obtained via the MM model, which arbitrarily move around zero, indicating no clear trend or evidence of a systematic bias. Overall, the placebo tests support that our results are not an artifact of the sampled firms, event dates, or trends or confounding events during the estimation window.

Fourth, we assessed potential omitted variable bias using the impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) procedure (Busenbark et al., 2021). By using the regression coefficient, standard error, sample size, and number of covariates in the model reported in Table 4, Column 1, we estimated the correlation required for an omitted confounder to change our findings. The results show that an omitted variable would have to be correlated at 0.63 to alter the significance of our findings. Correspondingly, the ICTV is $0 . 6 3 \times 0 . 6 3 = 0 . 4 0$ . This correlation is unrealistically large and the threshold is higher than it is in other studies (e.g., in Huang et al., 2018, the threshold is 0.14), suggesting robustness to omitted variables.

## Additional Evidence on Heuristic vs. Deliberative Processing

We argued that positive reactions to ransomware near misses are due to heuristic processing based on a better-thanexpected outcome, while negative reactions are due to deliberative processing, which weighs risks and future returns. Although investors’ processing modes are difficult to observe, examining their alternative consequences can provide further evidence (e.g., Kircher & Foerderer, 2024; Pierce et al., 2015). Specifically, we turned to investors’ information search. As investors often use online searches to research firm-related information (Da et al., 2011), lower search activity in response to near misses should be suggestive of heuristic processing (Dillon & Tinsley, 2005).

To explore this, a measure of information search activity was created via the Search Volume Index (SVI) provided by Google Trends (Da et al., 2011). The SVI captures the relative volume of Google searches for a firm, which can be used to estimate an abnormal SVI (ASVI), defined as the observed SVI for a firm compared to the expected SVI for a firm. To identify the SVI for the 104 events in our main analysis, we identified unique search identifiers for them and retrieved 16,536 SVI data points, with a mean (median) SVI of 20.71 (0) and a standard deviation of 28.04. Following Da et al. (2011), we computed the expected SVI as the median SVI in a pre-event window (100-day length, 50-day gap) consistent with our main analysis.

## Discussion

Our research examines divergent market reactions to ransomware hits and near misses. We argue that ransomware hits are evaluated negatively, leading to negative reactions, whereas near misses are evaluated positively due to near-miss bias, leading to positive reactions. An event study of ransomware events provides evidence to support our hypotheses. Specifically, when ransomware hits disrupted firm operations, stock market prices dropped by -4.40%. In contrast, when ransomware near misses failed to disrupt operations, market reactions were positive, and stock market prices increased by 2.87%. The robustness of these estimates was confirmed through falsification checks and multivariate analysis controlling for firm and event differences. Additional evidence supports near-misses being processed heuristically.

## Theoretical and Empirical Contributions

Our work offers three primary contributions. First, our estimates of stock market reactions contribute to the literature on the consequences of security incidents. In this literature, a substantial stream of work has examined stock market reactions to data breaches and DoS attacks (e.g., Acquisti et al., 2006; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Kwon & Johnson, 2015; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022; Richardson et al., 2019) but not ransomware attacks (see Ali et al., 2021). Our work reveals that ransomware hits—successful attacks that cause operational disruptions of primary business functions—lead to significant and large negative reactions. This is a meaningful finding for two reasons. Prior work has suggested that markets do not distinguish between different types of security incidents (Campbell et al., 2003; e.g., Cavusoglu et al., 2004). However, here, we show that our estimations of ransomware hits are larger than common estimations for other incidents—e.g., reactions of about -0.05% to -2% for data breaches and DoS attacks (e.g., Acquisti et al., 2006; Campbell et al., 2003; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Gwebu et al., 2018; Kwon & Johnson, 2015; Martin et al., 2017; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022; Richardson et al., 2019; Yayla & Hu, 2011). While this comparison is not exactly apples-to-apples due to different research contexts, at a high level, it suggests that markets do indeed distinguish between different types of security incidents, and investor reactions to ransomware hits may be stronger compared to other incidents. Also, the finding that reactions are only negative for hits but not for near misses further informs the debate on the contingencies of market reactions, which has not yet considered the impact on operations (e.g., Cavusoglu et al., 2004; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Gwebu et al., 2018; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022).

Second, we contribute unique arguments and evidence showing that investors can respond overly optimistically and positively to certain types of cybersecurity incidents. Given that the literature has generally assumed that investors react negatively to the disclosure of cybersecurity incidents, implying that they are always seen as unfavorable events (e.g., Ali et al., 2021; Foerderer & Schuetz, 2022; Martin et al., 2017; Nikkhah & Grover, 2022), our study suggests that this is not necessarily the case. In other words, our study contributes to the IS literature by falsifying the conventional assumption of negative reactions. Furthermore, in underscoring the need to consider both heuristic and deliberative processes in order to understand reactions to cybersecurity incidents, our work aligns the IS security literature on event disclosure with the behavioral finance viewpoint that investor behavior is not entirely deliberative (Hirshleifer, 2015). Additionally, our findings introduce the concept of near misses (e.g., Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Feng et al., 2022; Gnoni et al., 2022) and norm theory (Kahneman & Miller, 1986), which have not been previously considered in scholarly work on cybersecurity and market reactions.

Third, our findings break new ground by shedding light on investors’ information search patterns and social media reactions following cybersecurity incidents. As scant attention has been paid to investors’ information search following cyberattacks (c.f., Schlackl et al., 2022), our research uniquely documents an increase in search in the aftermath of security incidents and shows that investors devote effort toward information search—more so for hits than for near misses. We also show that social media reactions are less negative for near misses. This offers new insights into investor behavior regarding cybersecurity incidents.

## Practical Implications

Our work offers several implications for practice. First, our findings indicate that ransomware hits are associated with sizable negative market reactions. This finding can help information security officers justify cybersecurity investments, which are often not a priority for firm leadership (Proudfoot et al., 2023). To better prepare for possible hits, shareholders may also push firms to establish rainy day funds and review insurance policies to ensure that losses due to operational disruptions are covered, considering that many firms only have funds and cyber insurance for data breaches (OECD, 2020). Furthermore, if a ransomware hit occurs, investors and corporate leaders should brace for a considerable market backlash, more than what would be typical for a data breach or a DoS attack. Our study can help firms and investors anticipate and navigate such strong negative reactions. For instance, investors could use our findings to make strategic investment decisions, i.e., by timing market entries to occur after a ransomware hit or profiting from negative reactions through short selling and options trading. Firms should also ensure that their incident response plans comprise strategies for communicating to investors and assuaging their concerns.

Second, our research suggests that managers and investors need to carefully consider their responses to ransomware near misses. Logically speaking, near-miss events signal vulnerabilities in a firm’s security posture, indicating a heightened risk of future incidents. Indeed, several firms in our dataset reported more than one ransomware attack. However, the positive reactions to near misses may discourage thorough recovery and learning efforts, which are crucial to prevent future incidents. Indeed, research has shown that firms frequently fail to learn from near-miss events and may in fact make riskier decisions going forward (e.g., Dillon & Tinsley, 2008; Tinsley et al., 2012). Consequently, firms and investors should not cheer for near misses and positive returns without further scrutiny. Instead, firms should conduct full investigations and cautiously evaluate how to address the revealed vulnerabilities. Likewise, investors should initially view near misses as failures rather than successes and research them thoroughly.

Third, our findings are useful for policymakers. Because our findings show that investors have biased responses to ransomware near misses, i.e., because they focus on the outcomes of the events rather than the causes, policymakers could also require firms’ incident disclosures to include statements that warn investors that the occurrence of an incident suggests vulnerabilities and heightened future risk, which could help investors avoid the pitfalls of superficial conclusions due to near-miss bias. Furthermore, because near misses present important information about underlying vulnerabilities, policymakers could adopt practices from the aviation and healthcare sectors, creating reporting mechanisms for industry-wide learning and prevention.

## Limitations and Future Research

One limitation of this study lies in its exclusive focus on firms publicly traded in the U.S., which represent a major share of U.S. economic activity. Future research could expand our work to cover firms listed in other markets or focus on private firms. A second limitation is the relatively small sample size, as ransomware is still an emergent threat. We thus recommend that future research revisit this issue when additional event data becomes available. A third limitation arises from the observational nature of our research, which prevents the manipulation of variables and full control over potential confounding factors. Consequently, despite our efforts to ensure the comparability of groups, isolate confounders, and demonstrate robustness to omitted variables, the possibility of selection bias remains. Future studies should consider alternative research designs to provide additional evidence for our observed effects.

There are several promising research directions. While our study focuses on the short-term effects of ransomware events, future research could seek to study prolonged market effects. Subsequent investigations may also want to consider additional attack parameters, such as the exploited vulnerabilities, the specific ransomware strain, and ransomware payment, when such data becomes available. Areas for future exploration also include the investigation of other attack types (i.e., data breaches, DDoS) from the hit vs. near-miss perspective to find out if the paradoxical phenomenon of negative reactions to hits and positive reactions to near misses extends to other types of incidents. Finally, future research could take the perspective of attackers and study how they react to near misses and hits. The findings could help organizations develop better strategies to fight ransomware attacks.

## Conclusion

Our investigation sheds light on the divergent reactions of investors to ransomware incidents, revealing a paradoxical phenomenon in their response to near misses compared to hits. While investors react negatively to ransomware hits, leading to a significant drop in stock prices, near misses elicit positive reactions, resulting in stock price gains. Consistent with norm theory predictions, these findings underscore the nuanced nature of market responses to ransomware incidents. However, the positive reactions to near misses highlight the need for caution, as they obscure underlying risks and suggest that leaders and investors might be prone to drawing superficial conclusions from disasters that were narrowly avoided.
