# Phenomenon-based problematization: Coordinating in the digital era

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Coordination Theoretical contributions Gap spotting Problematization Interdependence Metaphors Digital phenomena

## A B S T R A C T

Identifying and revising outdated theoretical assumptions and metaphors is crucial to build new theory about emerging digitally-enabled coordination phenomena. Based on an extensive review of the extant coordination literature, however, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the majority of published manu scripts in top IS and management journals has adopted the well-known and understood (by authors, editors and reviewers alike) strategies of “gap-spotting”, i.e., pointing at gaps in knowledge, and/or “theory-borrowing”, i.e., borrowing unfamiliar theories from a di<sup>f</sup>erent discipline, to construct and legitimize contributions. Hardly any manuscript has systematically used a problematization strategy, i.e. a theory-building strategy that focuses on explicating and revising underlying theoretical assumptions. To improve the explicit and systematic use of pro blematization, we do two things. First, we distinguish between phenomenon-driven and theory driven problematization and hence outline options for how researchers could adopt the former. Second, we explicitly problematize the extant coordination literature. We show that, in contrast to its alternatives, problematization helps coordination researchers identify and address the limits of existing theoretical metaphors, which are dominant in the literature.

## 1. Introduction

IS and organizational researchers are increasingly observing that emerging digital phenomena “question the explanatory power and usefulness of extant theory… [and that theoretical] assumptions are increasingly being challenged” (Nambisan, Lyytinen, Majchrzak, & Song, 2017, p. 223 225). In particular, it appears that the process of coordination, which concerns the alignment of action in the face of interdependence (Bechky, 2003, 2006; Heath & Staudenmayer, 2000; Mintzberg, 1979; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Srikanth & Puranam, 2014), is being radically transformed. New digital phenomena, such as digital platforms that invert the <sup>fi</sup>rm” (Parker, Van Alstyne, & Choudary, 2016) render organizational coordination mechanisms obsolete, while machine learning algorithms and autonomous agents that automate and augment tasks (Rai, Constantinides, & Sarker, 2019) trigger changes in the ways organizational activities are aligned. That new digital phenomena challenge received scholarly wisdom about coordination should be seen as a great opportunity, since the most fruitful, but also the most di<sup>fi</sup>cult avenue of theory development involves examining whether and how “assumptions [of accepted theories] have been… unrealistic” (Whetten, 1989, p. 493). An important issue thus emerges: how to better account for the assumption-challenging aspect of novel digital phenomena in how we conduct research on coordination? This paper aims to address this question by highlighting the value of problematization.

Problematization is a theory-building strategy for systematically identifying and examining potentially problematic or outdated theoretical assumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, 2013, 2014; Rivard, 2014; Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011). Problematization needs to be contrasted to “gap-spotting”, the strategy of identifying gaps in a body of literature (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, 2013). It also di<sup>f</sup>ers from “theory-borrowing”, the strategy of borrowing unfamiliar theories from a di<sup>f</sup>erent discipline (e.g., psychology)(Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Oswick, Fleming, & Hanlon, 2011). However, despite increasing support for problematization over the last decade, our extensive review of the coordination literature <sup>fi</sup>nds that the majority of published manuscripts in top IS and management journals has rarely adopted explicitly this strategy. This poor uptake of problematization especially in what we term phenomenondriven problematization (more about that later) needs to be understood and addressed. Our standpoint is not to say that problematization is better, but rather that it can and should complement existing theory-building strategies in order to take the assumption-challenging aspects seriously as suggested by emerging digitally enabled phenomena.

In this paper, we <sup>fi</sup>rst explore the reluctance to systematically adopt problematization in extant research on coordination, which is suggestive of an underlying lack of “cognitive legitimacy” (Suchman, 1995): compared to its alternatives, i.e. gap-spotting and theory-borrowing, a problematization strategy may not be as well understood, or as comprehensible as these better known strategies. The most widely adopted strategy for constructing opportunities for theoretical contribution to the coordination literature involves pointing at references to theoretical “gaps” and/or making the case for a new, “fresh” theory that is borrowed from an external discipline (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). A “coherent” and “plausible explanation” (Suchman, 1995, p. 582) of what advantages problematization yields in terms of framing opportunities for contribution and building new theory on coordination is needed.

Furthermore, problematization is often con<sup>fl</sup>ated with gap-spotting and theory-borrowing strategies. For example, Locke and Golden-Biddle's (1997) highly cited paper describes problematization as a discursive-symbolic practice, whereby researchers “<sup>fi</sup>rst must represent and organize existing knowledge so as to con<sup>fi</sup>gure a context for contribution that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the consensus of previous work” and “Second... must in a sense turn on themselves, subverting or problematizing the very literatures that provide locations and raisons d'être for the present e<sup>f</sup>orts” (1997, p. 1029). According to Locke and Golden-Biddle, through “the process of problematization, then, a text attempts to signify how much the o<sup>f</sup>ered contribution matters. And, in doing so, it seeks to establish the contribution's importance and relevance to readers (1997, p. 1040). This in<sup>fl</sup>uential view of problematization focuses on consensus in a given literature (or the lack thereof – thus, engaging in a ‘gap-spotting’ process) and on providing “locations and raisons d'être for the present e<sup>f</sup>orts” (thus signifying the value of introducing and potentially borrowing a new theory). While problematization may be combined with gap-spotting and theory-borrowing, it should not be con<sup>fl</sup>ated with these two, equally valuable theory-building strategies. Problematization, we argue, needs to be understood and used in its own right.

To improve the systematic use of problematization in the IS and organizational literature of coordination, we propose a distinction between theory-driven and phenomenon-driven problematization. Problematization is often perceived as a theory-driven enterprise, involving predominantly critical re<sup>fl</sup>ection upon theory and underlying ideological, onto-epistemological and other assumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). Theory-driven problematization may have little or no obvious analytical purchase for researchers studying empirical coordination processes; it may not appear as relevant or even plausible. Yet, we clarify that problematization can also be phenomenon-driven whereby underlying theoretical assumptions can be explicated and scrutinized in conjunction with nove empirical material (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007). This distinction, we posit, renders problematization a more plausible alternative.

Moreover, phenomenon-driven problematization helps us identify and scrutinize the limits of a particularly dominant theoretical metaphor in coordination research: organizations are often faced with complex tasks, which need to be divided across di<sup>f</sup>erent organizational functions and supported by di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies. Through a division of labor across subtasks, the organization creates boundaries and potential gaps; to complete those tasks, gaps need to be bridged for work to be coordinated. We call this the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor . We draw on published studies in coordination research to discuss how researchers have implicitly adopted the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor to construct theoretical contributions. New digitally-enabled coordination phenomena, however, challenge the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor and prompt researchers to re-conceptualize the foundations of coordinative activities. The emergence of new digitally-mediated practices, in particular, the rise of digital platforms recon<sup>fi</sup>gures the scope and scale of coordination and turn extant role and task structures upside down. Coordination appears to be more about emergent alignment at an ecosystem level, rather than tight integration at the level of an overarching task.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we present our review of the coordination literature, examine the uptake of di<sup>f</sup>erent theory-building strategies and o<sup>f</sup>er ways to improve the comprehensibility and uptake of problematization. We <sup>fi</sup>nally discuss broader implications for further research on problematization and digitally-enabled coordination phenomena.

## 2. A review of the coordination literature

IS and management researchers have studied phenomena of coordination to explain or predict the impact of information technology on organizations (Argyres, 1999). IT-enabled coordination phenomena, understood here as involving “the use of computing and communications capabilities to help coordinate… work” (Malone & Crowston, 1994: 88), have been a great source of inspiration for theorizing.

The review presented below examines the role of IT in coordination, and hence its scope di<sup>f</sup>ers from that of previous reviews, which examine how coordination happens in organizations (see Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). At the same time, the paper's scope is broader than previous reviews, which focused principally on “emergent and contextualized coordination processes” in single organizations (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009, p. 472). We investigated how researchers appropriate the concept of IT-enabled coordination to make theoretical contributions irrespective of whether they are interested in emergence and situated work processes. See Appendix 1 for details of our review process and a complete list of all papers reviewed. In what follows, we present the main contributions made by researchers to the IT-enabled coordination literature.

## 2.1. Overview of key contributions

We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four di<sup>f</sup>erent perspectives in coordination research, namely, organizational economics, information processing, group and practice theory, which we brie<sup>fl</sup>y present below.

## 2.1.1. The organizational economics perspective

An organizational economics (OE) perspective focuses on “the mechanisms for coordinating the <sup>fl</sup>ow of materials or services through adjacent steps in the value-added chain” (Malone, Yates, & Benjamin, 1987, p. 485). With its roots in transaction cost economics (TCE) (Coase, 1937; Williamson, 1975, 1979, 1980, 1981) and agency theory (see Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991), the OE perspective seeks to analyze the relative advantages of organizations (or hierarchies) and markets. Organizations and markets are understood as di<sup>f</sup>erent systems of governance, in which alternative coordination mechanisms are utilized. The emphasis is on the economic drivers, which underlie coordination processes.

Using a typology of coordination costs (see Im, Grover, & Teng, 2013), researchers adopting an OE perspective examine the implications of information technology for such costs (e.g., whether IS reduces internal or external costs) and the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of available coordination mechanisms (often called ‘organizational structures’). That is, changes in coordination processes and organizational forms are understood to be triggered by changes in coordination costs (Im et al., 2013). Focusing on macro-level structura changes, researchers conceive of IT investment and use as an exogenous factor that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rm size, diversi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies (Hitt, 1999; Ravichandran, Liu, Han, & Hasan, 2009), market-design (Fan, Stallaert, & Whinston, 2003), inter-organizational governance structures (Argyres, 1999) and relationships (Im & Rai, 2014), as well as outsourcing arrangements (Banker, Kalvenes, & Patterson, 2006). Researchers adopting an OE perspective have relied on a number of constructs, such as contract completeness (Banker et al., 2006), demand uncertainty, industry concentration (Ray, Wu, & Konana, 2009), property rights (Brynjolfsson, 1994), and <sup>fi</sup>rm boundaries (Hitt, 1999) to examine IT-enabled changes in coordination mechanisms and processes.

For example, Malone et al., (1987) drew upon economic theories of organizations to formulate the so-called electronic markets hypothesis:” the adoption of information and communication technologies would over time lead to an expansion in the use of markets to coordinate activities and to a gradual decline of hierarchy-based coordination. A series of subsequent studies empirically tested as well as extended this hypothesis (Brynjolfsson, 1994; Brynjolfsson, Malone, Gurbaxani, & Kambil, 1994; Grover & Ramanlal, 1999; Grover & Saeed, 2007; Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Hitt, 1999; Ray et al., 2009; Wigand, Stein<sup>fi</sup>eld, & Markus, 2005).

## 2.1.2. The information processing perspective

With roots in early organization design and contingency theory (Galbraith, 1973, 1977; Thompson, 1967; Van de Ven, Delbecq, & Koenig, 1976), an information processing (IP) perspective views organizations as systems of collecting, gathering and processing information (Tushman & Nadler, 1978). Coordination is conceived as a key process a<sup>f</sup>ecting the information processing functions of organizational systems. According to Argyres (1999), “information processing approaches focus on the ways in which IT increases the quantity of information—the number of messages—transmitted and received per unit of time. In particular, IT is seen as providing a means of increasing this quantity” (p. 163). From this perspective, new information and communications technologies o<sup>f</sup>er opportunities to transfer objective information messages in more e<sup>fi</sup>cient ways. Not unlike the OE perspective on coordination, the e<sup>f</sup>ect of IT on coordination is illuminated primarily from the prism of pre-existing theoretical frameworks and models, which were developed in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of organization science (see Kraut & Streeter, 1995). A number of researchers combined an OE with an IP perspective to examine how IT a<sup>f</sup>ects coordination (Argyres, 1999; Dewan, Michael, & Min, 1998; Grover & Saeed, 2007; Bapna, Barua, Mani, & Mehra, 2010).

A key novelty o<sup>f</sup>ered by an IP perspective is an elaborate typology of “interdependence.” E<sup>f</sup>ective coordination mechanisms generally ‘match’ the level of interdependence (e.g. pooled, sequential, reciprocal) existing in an information processing system (i.e., an organization). For example, rules and procedures are suitable for pooled interdependencies, sequences are suitable for sequential interdependencies, while mutual adjustments are optimal mechanisms when facing reciprocal interdependence (Gosain, Malhotra, & El Sawy, 2004; Kraut & Streeter, 1995; Legner & Schemm, 2008). From an IP perspective, the impact of new IT on coordination and, by extension, on the functions of an organizational system, is observable at the level of managing given interdependencies.

For instance, new IT, such as advanced project management software, may improve the management of sequential interdependencies on large software development projects. A related, yet distinct stream of studies contributed to the development of Coordination Theory (Crowston, 1997; Keith, Demirkan, & Goul, 2013; Malone et al., 1999; Malone & Crowston, 1994). According to this theory, rapid advances in IT do not simply improve the management of given interdependencies (Gosain et al., 2004; Gosain, Lee, & Kim, 2005), but also o<sup>f</sup>er opportunities to redesign dependencies among activities with the aid of IT (Crowston, 1997; Malone et al., 1999).

## 2.1.3. The group perspective

An increasing number of researchers have developed a group perspective on coordination. A turning point in this line of research was the work by Faraj and Sproull (2000), who pioneered the notion of expertise coordination, which consists of socially shared cognitive processes that develop and evolve in order to meet the demands of task-based skill and knowledge dependencies” (Faraj & Sproull 2000, p. 1556). Expertise coordination becomes necessary for complex non-routine, intellectual tasks, which constitute the de<sup>fi</sup>ning feature of <sup>fl</sup>exible, team-based, and virtual/IT-enabled organizational forms.

The emphasis of a group perspective on coordination has been on the distributed e<sup>f</sup>orts of team members to locate, recognize and synthesize diverse knowledge in novel IT-enabled organizational contexts, such as geographically distributed software development teams (Carmel, Espinosa, & Dubinsky, 2010; Cummings, Espinosa, & Pickering, 2009; Espinosa, Slaughter, Kraut, & Herbsleb, 2007a; Espinosa, Slaughter, Kraut, & Herbsleb, 2007b; Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2007). Inspired by studies in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of social psychology (Stasser, Stewart, & Wittenbaum, 1995), transactive memory systems (Wegner, 1987, 1995), collective mind (Weick & Roberts, 1993), and distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995), coordination is conceptualized as a distributed cognitive process occurring in and through the actual interactions and heedful interrelations among group members; rather than in virtue of intelligent design of interdependence management. A group perspective has been particularly productive in illuminating coordination processes in temporally and geographically distributed teams (Carmel et al., 2010; Cummings et al., 2009; Espinosa et al., 2007a), virtual teams (Kanawattanachai & Yoo, 2007), novel software development project arrangements (e.g., agile programming) (Maruping, Zhang, & Venkatesh, 2009) as well as di<sup>f</sup>erent team-based o<sup>f</sup>shore outsourcing.

## 2.1.4. The practice perspective

Inspired by the “practice turn” in sociology, social theory (Schatzki, 2001; Schatzki, 2005, 2006) and organization studies (Davide Nicolini, 2011; Nicolini, 2012; Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Orlikowski, 2007; Pine & Mazmanian, 2017), a small, but growing number of studies has explored the “di<sup>f</sup>erent ways that coordination is accomplished through speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies” (Beane & Orlikowski, 2015, p. 1554). Practice-based studies have demonstrated that coordination structures and mechanisms are made to work through actions and collective activities; the design of formal means and structures alone is insu<sup>fi</sup>cient for producing coordination.

From a practice-based perspective, IT-enabled changes in coordination and organizing processes manifest in emergent interpersonal interactions. While the unit and focus of analysis might resemble that of a group perspective on coordination, a practice perspective is attentive to the situated, emergent and dynamic aspects of coordinative processes and is non-cognitivist in its assumptions (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011). Its empirical focus is on the ways information technologies create and modify coordination resources, such as, plans and rules, roles, physical location and proximity and enable or disrupt the achievement of coordination in practice (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009; Venters, Oborn, & Barrett, 2014). Importantly, it aims to uncover unintended social consequences of new digitally enabled coordination (Venters et al., 2014). The focus is on illuminating how new digital and socia realities are intertwined to bring about important social-material consequences for coordination (Beane & Orlikowski, 2015).

For example, Kellogg et al. (2006) examined how various information technologies were used on complex website development projects. Actors performing coordination experienced a number of tensions resulting from technology-enabled transparency and visibility of work and heightened synchronization and connectivity. Problematic consequences for project members included: loss of control at work, recon<sup>fi</sup>guration of jurisdictional boundaries (e.g., who is in charge for what), and new norms of constant availability. ## 2.1.5. Summary of literature review

IS and organizational researchers have drawn valuable research implications and pointed out ways to improve coordination processes in the context of outsourcing, multi-sourcing, software development ‘round the clock’ distributed team work and so on. As shown below, their contributions have been constructed by predominantly adopting gap-spotting and theory-borrowing strategies. In what follows, we further unpack these points.

## 2.2. Exploring the construction of theoretical contributions

We examined the uptake of gap-spotting, theory-borrowing and problematization in the corpus of published manuscripts we reviewed. We brie<sup>fl</sup>y explain each of these theory-building strategies.

Through gap-spotting, researchers “mainly try to identify or create gaps in existing literature that need to be <sup>fi</sup>lled” (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 247). The adoption of gap-spotting involves, for instance, identifying competing explanations, searching for overlooked areas, pointing out at the incompleteness of extant theoretical models (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011). Furthermore, new empirical observations are treated as objective evidence – as “a judge or a mirror” (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007, p. 1266) – which plays a key role in the process of gap <sup>fi</sup>lling.

The strategy of theory-borrowing, on the other hand, involves identifying a theory from an external discipline (e.g., highlighting a promising, but ignored perspective) and domesticating this theory in the <sup>fi</sup>eld (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015; Oswick et al., 2011). Theory-borrowing usually relies on a process of analogical reasoning, the use of metaphors and conceptual blending (Oswick et al., 2011; Tsoukas, 2009; Weick, 1996). Moreover, new empirical material is used as an opportunity to demonstrate the relevance of new theory.

Problematization di<sup>f</sup>ers signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly when compared with ‘gap spotting’ and ‘theory borrowing’. Problematization is an “endeavor to know how and to what extent it might be possible to think di<sup>f</sup>erently, instead of what is already known” (Foucault, 1985, p. 9, cited in Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). It involves identifying, articulating, and challenging speci<sup>fi</sup>c underlying theoretical assumptions, for example, ideological, and onto-epistemological commitments. Problematization highlights the fallibility of theoretica knowledge. As Tsoukas and Chia (2011) argued: “Theories are tools for interrogating reality and for deriving their practical imports. They are not irrefutable truth claims. As such, it becomes incumbent on scholars of all stripes and persuasion to open themselves to the possibility of alternative, plausible theoretical viewpoints” (p. 4). This conception of problematization does not refer to a rhetorical turn (cf. Locke & Golden-Biddle, 1997), but pertains to the systematic approach of identifying, scrutinizing and potentially revising underlying, theoretical assumptions in a body of literature (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). In essence, it concerns an epistemic stance that a<sup>f</sup>ords the generation of new insights (Fayard, Gkeredakis, & Levina, 2016).

Our analysis of the literature on coordination shows that most researchers adopt a gap-spotting strategy. The gradual establishment of a specialized community inspired by a distinct perspective (e.g., organizational economics) has provided fertile ground to spot gaps: insofar knowledge accumulates within the boundaries of a specialized community, it becomes easier to delineate what is currently known and, most importantly, not known (i.e. gaps).

A gap-spotting stance has enabled accumulation of knowledge from within a speci<sup>fi</sup>c perspective. Consistent with previous studies (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011), we found that IS researchers enact variations of the gap spotting strategy. For example, (Chua & Yeow, 2010) point to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c empirical gap in the literature in that a speci<sup>fi</sup>c empirical setting had been poorly investigated: “The area of cross-project coordination, where shared goals are tenuous or non-existent, has been under-researched” (p. 838). Beane and Orlikowski (Beane & Orlikowski, 2015), on the other hand, suggest that there is a gap-confusion in the extant literature: “Research investigating the coordination of distributed work with these technologies has produced contradictory <sup>fi</sup>ndings” (p. 1553). We also found that ‘design-focused’ papers employed a gap-spotting strategy, although some of those papers used insights derived from an external discipline to draw design recommendations (e.g., Gosain et al., 2004).

Theory borrowing has also been quite in<sup>fl</sup>uential and has contributed to the emergence of distinct research streams.<sup>1</sup> For example, Faraj and Sproull (2000) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the potential of social psychological theories and distributed cognition theories to illuminate overlooked aspects of coordination activity. The authors insightfully argued that these theories help examine the special case of “expertise interdependence”, as opposed to task interdependencies, on which existing coordination literature had thus far focused (e.g., Malone & Crowston, 1994). The publication of Faraj and Sproul (2000) contributed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly to the establishment of a <sup>fl</sup>ourishing and specialized research community on group coordination. Similarly, other articles, such as Kellogg et al. (2006), explained the potential of practice theory as developed in social science and philosophy to rejuvenate coordination research. These publications largely contributed to the emergence of a practice perspective on coordination.

While gap-spotting and theory-borrowing have been largely prevalent in the literature, we found that problematization has not been explicitly adopted. If we are to take the assumption-challenging aspects of novel digital coordination phenomena seriously, the uptake of problematization needs to be cultivated; in order to complement, rather than replace gap-spotting and theory-borrowing. Moreover, as noted by Okhuysen and Bechky (2009, p. 464), the accumulation of knowledge about coordination processes and practices has created challenges in itself: “the term ‘coordination’ [is] used ubiquitously and loosely to treat a variety of phenomena.” The explicit adoption of problematization can be highly bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in that it helps researchers make the most (in terms of building new theory) of new phenomena of digital coordination and untangle the increasingly complex and “messy theoretical picture” (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009, p. 472), which the coordination literature, as a whole, has painted.

The reluctance to systematically use problematization, we argue, manifests an underlying lack of “cognitive legitimacy” (Suchman, 1995). While legitimacy in general concerns the perceived appropriateness or rightness of a practice, cognitive legitimacy ensues when a practice is made comprehensible, i.e. when “coherent, understandable accounts” of how it is performed and what it a<sup>f</sup>ords exist; such accounts must “mesh… with the experienced reality of the audience's daily life” (Suchman, 1995, p. 582). We posit that the limited, non-explicit use of problematization may be attributed to the lack of comprehensible accounts of how it can be used as a strategy for identifying and constructing theoretical contributions. Proponents of problematization often portray it as a process of critical re<sup>fl</sup>ection upon accepted theories and underlying ideological, onto-epistemological and other assumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). Such accounts, we argue, may not appear as plausible to coordination researchers, whose practice aims to better understand and theorize novel phenomena. The latter may not be able to see the point of critically questioning underlying theoretical assumptions, regardless of the phenomena.

To improve the cognitive legitimacy of problematization, we distinguish between theory-driven (cf. Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) and phenomenon-driven problematization, which creatively leverages new empirical phenomena for theory building purposes. Here, we unpack the latter form of problematization. Through the re<sup>fl</sup>exive and productive “interplay among theory, researcher subjectivity, and empirical material” new theoretical ideas may be developed and old ones be challenged (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007, p. 1266). While gap spotting and theory borrowing rely on new empirical material to validate extant, or search for new, theory, respectively, phenomenon-driven problematization sensitizes researchers to the importance of imagination and abduction to critically open up alternative ways of framing empirical material (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Cornelissen, 2006; Weick, 1989). It essentially constitutes a mode of inquiry through which we entertain hunches when attempting to theorize new phenomena and dig deeper to scrutinize, potentially obsolete, theoretical assumptions. In contrast, the tendency of gap-spotting and theory-borrowing is to favor rigor and newness for its own sake (importing new concepts from other domains). Based on these distinctions, in the following section, we demonstrate the value of phenomenon-driven problematization. First, we identify and explicate one of the underlying root metaphors the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor, which, we argue, remains taken-for-granted and unchallenged in extant coordination literature, across the di<sup>f</sup>erent research streams we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. We show how phenomenondriven problematization could be adopted to identify the limits of that metaphor. ## 3. Demonstrating the value of phenomenon-driven problematization

## 3.1. Explicating the limits of the ‘flipside metaphor’

A phenomenon-driven problematization strategy may be productively adopted to identify and question the limits of “root-metaphor” assumptions (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011). Root metaphor assumptions concern the images, maps and metaphors, upon which researchers inevitably rely on in their theorizing e<sup>f</sup>orts (Morgan, 1986; Weick, 1989), and are often taken for granted (Dreyfus & Taylor, 2015). With regards to the coordination literature, a key root metaphor concerns coordination problems – types of situation where interdependent actions need to be aligned (Schelling, 1980/1960). The prevailing metaphor in the literature is that coordination problems re<sup>fl</sup>ect how an overarching task or problem is broken down; we call it, the “<sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor”. An excerpt from Heath and Staudenmayer (2000), succinctly illustrates this metaphor:

In the simplest version of the coordination problem, an organization divides an overall problem into subtasks and assigns the parts to individuals. We could imagine, for example, an organization that divides the modules of a computer among programmers with similar skill. Organizations undertake this division of labor because individuals have limited information-processing abilities. Eventually, however, the organization must re-integrate the tasks it originally divided. In the end, the modules of a computer program must work together as a single program, so the programmers who develop individual modules must integrate their e<sup>f</sup>orts. Thus, the <sup>fl</sup>ip-side of division of labor is integration. (p.159).

The “<sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor” is implicitly adopted by many studies of coordination. Indeed, even if di<sup>f</sup>erent research streams on coordination (organizational economics. information processing, group and practice perspectives) are underpinned by distinct conceptions of coordination and IT (see Table 1), they seem to share the assumption that coordination problems re<sup>fl</sup>ect the preexisting division of labor. At the <sup>fi</sup>eld level of coordination research, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that this metaphor “unite[s] antagonistic schools, which at one level, often present as di<sup>f</sup>erent and even oppositional but, at a deeper level, share a set of assumptions about their particular <sup>fi</sup>eld” (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 255). As an illustration, we draw on a seminal study, which explicitly adopted a gap-spotting strategy (Bailey. Leonardi, & Chong. 2010). Bailey et al. (2010) investigated technology interdependence in two knowledge work settings: structural engineers in building design and hardware engineers in computer chip design. They (2010) found striking differences in the ways technology interdependence was dealt with in the two settings. In the case of hardware engineers, they reported a propensity to e<sup>fi</sup>ciently manage technology interdependence, which was rooted in (1) the belief that bridging activities automated tasks that were of marginal importance or that involved knowledge that could be codi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and (2) occupational ideas of e<sup>fi</sup>ciency” (p. 723). In contrast, in the case of structural engineers, they concluded that the propensity to avoid dealing with technology interdependence was rooted in the belief among senior engineers that younger engineers' fondness for and reliance on computer analysis prevented them from gaining fundamental design knowledge” (p. 724); hence, “structural engineers purposefully eschewed high coordination because they believed that navigating di<sup>fi</sup>cult interfaces promoted the development of necessary occupational design knowledge” (p. 725).

To theorize these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, Bailey et al. (2010) relied on the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor: “when complex tasks are divided into subtasks to be completed by di<sup>f</sup>erent technologies, the technologies must integrate their component parts to <sup>fi</sup>nish the whole task and hence are interdependent (see Thompson, 1967). The division of work among technologies creates gaps; the completion of work requires integration across these gaps” (2010, p. 725, reference in the original). Like many other coordination studies, by relying on this metaphor, this study made insightful observations as regards the absence or transformation of coordination, despite the presence of high interdependence (cf. Faraj & Sproull, 2000).

A phenomenon-driven problematization strategy would leverage empirical observations di<sup>f</sup>erently. The aim would be to identify the limits and question the taken-for-grantedness of the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor. A number of questions could be generated in this regard. For example, does the fact that high technology interdependence was not managed as expected by extant theories mean that coordination was absent? Since the actors were experiencing a di<sup>f</sup>erent set of imperatives (e.g., meeting “occupational goals”), do we need to rethink the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor? Empirical observations would be used to question the adequacy of <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor

For instance, other empirical studies suggest that a plurality of factors may a<sup>f</sup>ect the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a task. From the point of view of a practicing actor, e.g., a software developer, the task to be coordinated may be multi-faceted and emergently interdependent. Broader ideological goals, for instance, such as the ideal of full transparency, may impinge upon the coordination of software development work and necessitate creative responses (Shaikh & Vaast, 2016). Other empirical studies indicate that actors experience “webs of interdependencies” in their work context (Staudenmayer, 1997). In her in-depth study of two large-scale software development projects, Staudenmayer found that people tend to experience a plurality of interdependencies, which “are socially, culturally, and historically embedded” (1997, p.243). What interdependencies mattered for coordination was not given for practitioners, but shifted and rede<sup>fi</sup>ned the very object of coordination. Staudenmayer herself re<sup>fl</sup>ected on her own assumptions and concluded that a broader, dynamic view of interdependence and coordination would have served her study better. The de<sup>fi</sup>nition of an organization was more complex and emergent than what accepted coordination theories had allowed for. So, she had started problematizing extant coordination literature. From a problematization stance, empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings may expose the limits of the “<sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor”: the idea that an overarching task exhibits ontological stability and thus a priori decomposability may not necessarily illuminate empirical coordination processes.

To transcend the “<sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor,” researchers consider “alternative routes of interpretation and analysis” and “broaden the repertoire of vocabularies and theories that can be mobilized” (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007, p. 1273). In the spirit of problematization, a few studies in the management literature attempted to conceptualize the relation between coordination and interdependence (Bechky, 2006; Gkeredakis, 2014). Bechky (2006) showed that coordination on <sup>fi</sup>lm projects was embedded in a broader “structural context” (Strauss, 1985). She argued that key features of the context, such as understandings of inter-organizational career progression in the <sup>fi</sup>lm industry and the nature of <sup>fi</sup>lm projects as “temporary total institutions” (2006, p. 9), structured actors' experiences of how work needed to be coordinated. In her empirical study, she found that resources for coordination, in particular a “generalized role structure,” enabled actors to <sup>fi</sup>t their actions together in ways that accounted for these structural features of their work context. Bechky noted the importance of occupational in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the content of what the task was about and how coordination was performed. Bechky's analysis helps transcend the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor.

Similarly, Gkeredakis (2014) noted that di<sup>f</sup>erent kinds of interdependencies, which went beyond the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of a particular task or project, a<sup>f</sup>ected the dynamics of coordination. He found that a senior management team on a complex contract award project “was deeply concerned to contribute through its local actions and work outcomes (e.g., bid evaluation, the actual contract award) to broader “objectives of large-scale integration” (2014, p. 1495), such as orchestrating the delivery of a yearlong and highly complex construction project. To analytically unpack how local tasks were made compatible with such broader objectives, he used the concept of conventions (Thévenot, 2001a, 2001b, 2002, 2007). He argued that “conventions equipped [the project team] to fabricate interdependence between speci<sup>fi</sup>c procurement tasks and broader, supra-task, large- scale integration endeavors” (2014, p. 1495, emphasis in original). The concept of conventions helped transcend the limits of the “<sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor” by elucidating the diverse ways of framing and addressing coordination problems and unveiling a richer landscape of interdependent activities than what extant coordination theories embracing the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor could have allowed for. Other conceptual resources and sociological perspectives, such as institutional logics (Thornton, Ocasio, & Lounsbury, 2012) or <sup>fi</sup>elds (Levina & Arriaga, 2014), could also be mobilized to similarly broaden the conceptualization of interdependent action and coordination problems.

In the light of these examples, it could be argued that a practice perspective, which is adopted by the aforementioned papers (Bechky, 2006; Gkeredakis, 2014), accommodates a problematization stance more productively than other perspectives. Yet, this need not be the case. Commitment to a practice perspective does not necessarily entail scrutiny of the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor. And, researchers, who may be interested in di<sup>f</sup>erent aspects of coordination (e.g., economic, information processing), other than how coordination is accomplished in practice (cf. practice perspective), can equally bene<sup>fi</sup>t from examining the potential disadvantages that reliance on the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor may generate.

To conclude, phenomenon-driven problematization leverages empirical evidence concerning coordination phenomena to scrutinize the deep-rooted reliance on the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor. Rather than treating the “absence of coordination” and the “wide gaps” between technologies (Bailey et al. 2010) as a necessary logical conclusion, which is based on the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor, a problematization strategy turns this logic upside down. Through problematizing, as we will discuss in the next section, we are able to recognize that the generative nature of digital technologies (Yoo, Henfridsson, & Lyytinen, 2010) renders the <sup>fl</sup>ip side metaphor obsolete. In essence, by examining, challenging and aiming to transcend the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor , problematization helps develop a more nuanced understanding of digitally-enabled coordination phenomena.

## 3.2. Problematizing digitally-enabled coordination

Problematization o<sup>f</sup>ers opportunities to construct novel research questions and build new theories on digitally-enabled coordination. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the contribution of new digital technologies such as smartphones, arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence and the Internet of Things to coordination processes may be fruitfully problematized. Digital technologies are intentionally incomplete and perpetually in the making in that, they do not “foreclose the range of tasks and operational links… [they] can or might accommodate” (Kallinikos et al. 2013, p. 357). Compared to how other technologies are tied to a product architecture (e.g. how a gear box is tied to the product architecture of a car), digital technologies are product agnostic in that their functionality is less pre-determined and more generative (Yoo et al., 2010).

The incompleteness and agnosticism of digital technologies means that they may no longer “mirror” organizational tasks (Constantinides, Henfridsson, & Parker, 2018) and, therefore, the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor may no longer apply. In a recent paper, Colfer and Baldwin (2016) reviewed 142 empirical studies and found that, in “open collaborative projects” supported by new digital technologies, the “mirroring hypothesis” does not hold true. There is no one-to-one correspondence between technology architecture and organizational structures; technological interdependencies do not correspond to task interdependencies and therefore, coordination of contributions does not necessarily mirror the coordination of software modules.

Although, Colfer and Baldwin (2016) primarily focused on OSS development projects, the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor may be fruitfully challenged in other types of ‘open’ interactions of the kind found on digital platform ecosystems like Google Play Store, the Apple App store and others (Constantinides et al., 2018). These digital platform ecosystems o<sup>f</sup>er opportunities to study coordination among di<sup>f</sup>erent stakeholders (e.g., smartphone users, smartphone manufacturers, service providers, third-party developers, and others) who do not share the same organizational structures nor the same technological architectures. As Tiwana (2013) notes:

“…conventional coordination mechanisms and command-and-control hierarchies that make conventional organizations work are neither scalable to thousands of interdependent ecosystem partners, nor does the platform owner have the legitimate authority to dictate their work as it can in conventional organizations and supply chains.”

Digital platform ecosystems invert organizations from an internal focus on a division of labor across organizational functions to an external focus on ecosystem actors and the value creating interactions between them (Parker et al., 2016). Such ecosystems or “meta-organizations” (Gulati, Puranam, & Tushman, 2012) leverage modular architectures (Yoo et al., 2010), i.e. bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the design of autonomous software modules and sophisticated module interfaces. However, modularity of digital technologies does not necessarily arise from, or re<sup>fl</sup>ect, the breakdown of an overarching task into certain components, which then need to be re-integrated; enabling generative interactions is more important than enabling integration (Henfridsson, Mathiassen, & Svahn, 2014). This is a consequence of a lack of mirroring between organizational structures and technology architecture. When embracing the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor, the absence of structural interdependence and integration would be seen as evidence of coordination de<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Yet, when problematizing unre<sup>fl</sup>ective reliance on that metaphor, we begin to see that coordination problems may no longer re<sup>fl</sup>ect the division of task. Other analytical observations could emerge. For instance, we could argue that there is generative interdependence at the ecosystem level, drawing up a plurality of digital resources (e.g. application programming interfaces (API)) and governance mechanisms (e.g. ratings, recommender systems, etc.). Through problematizing, we can thus become aware of the frame-breaking potential of novel digital phenomena

Consider the following example. A smartphone user downloads an app such as Booking.com on the Google Play Store. A set of Android APIs enable the Booking.com app to interact with other apps such as those provided by di<sup>f</sup>erent airlines, hotels, car rental companies and others and to coordinate the requests of each of these apps as a seamless service for the end user (e.g. search, <sup>fi</sup>lter, select and purchase airline tickets). The APIs define functionalities that are independent of the respective implementations of each of the aforementioned apps, which allows the latter to vary without compromising each other. Moreover, the APIs decouple the apps being used from the digital infrastructures that provide a service and, by extension, the very organizations that own and operate those infrastructures. This allows the service provider (e.g. an airline) to easily switch from, for example, physical data servers to virtua servers that run on the cloud; or from software running on an Oracle-based Java application server (such as credit card processing software) to Node.js software running on Windows Azure. The ability of APIs to allow apps to plug and play with ease is arguably dramatically di<sup>f</sup>erent from the coordinative activity, which was empirically investigated and theorized in previous studies. That is, the APIs do not rely on a pre-existing division of labor or tasks for each of those apps, but rather they provide the building blocks upon which each of those apps can be developed and indeterminably coordinated on the Google Play Store platform.

To problematize and explicate the limits of the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor in the context of digital platform ecosystems does not mean that we should dispense with the concept of interdependence. There are still interdependences between the apps and the service providers that o<sup>f</sup>er them (e.g. an airline and a hotel), but these interdependences appear highly generative and dependent on network e<sup>f</sup>ects. The more end users download and use the apps of these service providers, the more these apps will attract other third parties on the app to o<sup>f</sup>er add-on services, something which will, in turn, increase the number of users downloading the app (Parker et al., 2016). Thus, the more positive network e<sup>f</sup>ects generated through the interactions of di<sup>f</sup>erent ecosystem actors, the more generative the interdependences between them, as more services will bind them together and more APIs will be deployed to coordinate the interaction between the apps. The interdependencies between the ecosystem actors are coordinated through devices, such as ratings and recommender systems, which may lead to either positive or network e<sup>f</sup>ects for the apps being used.

As the above example illustrates, there are di<sup>fi</sup>culties in applying the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor in digital platform ecosystems since there is no pre-determined division of tasks that later need to be integrated. Even if we were to conceptualize the services provided by each app as tasks, we would have to acknowledge that they are constantly recon<sup>fi</sup>gured, coupled with, or decoupled from, other tasks, and even completely removed or replaced with other tasks. This ongoing transformation is never predetermined but rather emerges from the spontaneous, indeterminable interactions among actors in an ecosystem with <sup>fl</sup>uid boundaries.

There are three key di<sup>f</sup>erences between new digital technologies and the information technologies examined in extant literature. First, digital technologies, for example, a mobile app, are intentionally incomplete, which allows them to be malleable to di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks. Second, they are product agnostic meaning that they are not tied to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c product, but rather can become modules of multiple products and even platforms themselves (e.g. Google Maps). Third, their architecture does not mirror any organizational structure or division of labor. This enables them to be of use for di<sup>f</sup>erent tasks on demand. Thus, digital technologies render some of the traditional assumptions held in coordination research revisable, especially around the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor and the ways by which technology enables integration. ## 4. Conclusion and implications

In this paper, we carried out an extensive review of prior coordination studies and found that, while gap-spotting and theoryborrowing have been largely prevalent in the literature, problematization has not been explicitly adopted as a theory-building strategy. We argued that, if we are to take the assumption-challenging aspects of novel digital coordination phenomena seriously, the uptake of problematization needs to be cultivated; in order to complement, rather than replace gap-spotting and theory-borrowing.

We demonstrated the value of problematization by showing how it could help identify the limits of the dominant <sup>fl</sup>ip side metaphor in coordination research. Unlike previous coordination phenomena, new digitally-enabled coordination exhibits a di<sup>f</sup>erent type of interdependence, which does not mirror organizational or technological structures and renders the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor rather obsolete. On digital platform ecosystems, for instance, highly generative interdependences are observed.

It should be acknowledged that problematization has been implicitly used by coordination researchers (Sandberg & Alvesson, 2011). In particular, in our review we found that while most of the published manuscripts did not explicitly adopt problematization to construct opportunities for theoretical contributions, a few did question the relevance of certain assumptions found in the literature. For example, Faraj and Sproull (2000) questioned the adequacy of “administrative coordination mechanisms” to coordinate expertise in emergent situations, while Beane and Orlikowski (2015) highlighted the limits of the notion of a<sup>f</sup>ordance in unpacking the materiality of coordination. To legitimize the questioning of certain theoretical assumptions, these manuscripts engaged in some form of “camou<sup>fl</sup>aged” problematization. They explicitly adopted a gap-spotting and/or theory-borrowing strategy to question the explanatory adequacy of well-established coordination theories

Our paper builds on these e<sup>f</sup>orts by highlighting the value of the explicit adoption of phenomenon-driven problematization. Much more can be gained (in theory-building terms), we argue, through systematically explicating and scrutinizing the taken-for-granted assumptions of accepted coordination theories. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, some assumptions and metaphor – such as the <sup>fl</sup>ipside metaphor – are shared by all perspectives. In other words, explicit problematization may be necessary to shed light on the limits of field-level assumptions, i.e., “broader set of assumptions about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c subject matter that are shared by several di<sup>f</sup>erent schools of thought” (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 255), which novel digital phenomena challenge. Moreover, the explicit use of problematization could go one step further to turn the implicit division of labor among di<sup>f</sup>erent research communities upside down. To leverage the assumption-challenging potential of novel digital phenomena, boundaries across perspectives may need to be revisited.

## 4.1. Research implications

Going forward, future research in coordination may utilize problematization as a complementary theory-building strategy to examine new empirical phenomena, such as autonomous agents, blockchain and the Internet of Things. As noted in our discussion interdependence between tasks is no longer de<sup>fi</sup>ned at the level of organizational structures; new forms of interdependence may no longer be addressed through an organizationally-determined division of labor, but emergently managed by a set of digital coordination resources and governance mechanisms.

In the (not so distant) future, the Internet of Things will become increasingly augmented by machine learning algorithms. Digita devices (e.g. a sensor, a camera, a software application, etc.) may autonomously make decisions, in the absence of pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned rules. Multiple autonomous agents may act and interact seamlessly in the background, without the need of human intervention. A blockchain infrastructure – a generative distributed ledger technology – could also be deployed to support such interactions through learning from the transactions of each agent and smart contracting. For example, autonomous agents nowadays trade energy on regular time intervals on a blockchain platform, without any intervention between building owners and energy generation utility companies.<sup>2</sup> These and other examples are suggestive of how coordination is accomplished in ways that current assumptions may not be able to account for. A problematization strategy is best placed to support theory-building in relation to new digital phenomena, insofar as emphasis is explicitly placed on identifying and scrutinizing deep-seated theoretical assumptions that may be outdated.

## Appendix 1. A review of the IS literature on coordination

Inclusion and exclusion criteria. We relied on ISI Web of Knowledge and included papers published over the last 30 years (1987–2016) in the following journals: MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, Information and Organization, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology. Our selection of papers from these IS journals for the review is consistent with other reviews where all or some of these journals are used to identify representative literature (Baskerville & Myers, 2009; Sidorova, Evangelopoulos, Valacich, & Ramakrishnan, 2008). Our search criteria were ‘TOPIC’ = ‘coordination’ AND ‘PUBLICATION NAME’= (name of journal) .

We also included papers published in <sup>fi</sup>ve top management journals, Organization Science, Management Science, Academy of Management Journal, Academy of Management Review, Administrative Science Quarterly, which studied speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the role of IT and IS in coordination. In keeping a focus on IS, we broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned IS as “a ‘web’ of equipment, techniques, applications and people that creates a social context, including the history of commitments that formed that web, the infrastructure that supports its development and use, and the social relations and processes of its use” (Boland, Lyytinen, & Yoo, 2007, p. 634). Our criteria when searching these journals were ‘TOPIC’ = ‘coordination’ AND ‘TOPIC’=’information technology or information systems or software or virtual’ AND ‘PUBLICATION NAME’= ‘(name of journal)’.

In our search, we excluded articles, for which coordination did not seem to be important theoretically. To determine the importance of coordination for theory development, we examined whether and how the term “coordination” (or variants, e.g., “coordinate”, “coordinating”) was used in the abstract, title, and keywords (using “search” function). For example, the paper (Bandara, Gable, & Rosemann, 2005) included coordination as a keyword (in the ISI web of knowledge), but did not use coordination as an analytic concept and did not use any prior coordination theory. Hence, we excluded it. For the included papers, we examined mainl the theoretical framework and the discussion sections of published papers given our interest in how contributions were constructed.

In the process of studying the articles that met our inclusion criteria, our attention was drawn to the fact that a few other papers had very large numbers of citations; yet, they had not been published in the list of journals we included. These papers were the following: (Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Kraut & Streeter, 1995; Thomas W Malone, 1987; T. W. Malone & Crowston, 1994). Given their salience in the coordination literature, we examined these papers too. The total number of our sample was 209. To organize this sample, we <sup>fi</sup>rst surveyed the various implicit or explicit de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of coordination. Second, being interested in theory-building strategies, we carefully read the selected papers (theoretical framework and discussion) and looked for key statements in which the authors identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and legitimated an opportunity for theoretical contribution.
