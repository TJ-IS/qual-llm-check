# From recreational applications to workplace technologies: an empirical study of cross-context IS continuance in the case of virtual worlds

## Abstract

Although three-dimensional, immersive virtual worlds, such as Active Worlds, Second Life, and Teleplace have been in existence for several years, their organizational use is rather limited. This paper posits, perhaps counter intuitively, that the diffusion of virtual worlds within organizations could be enhanced by their recreational usage. This argument is motivated by the notion developed in this paper that the use of technologies need not remain within a single context, but instead can cross-contexts, for example from recreational to vocational. We term such shift cross-context IS continuance. This paper proposes that workers using virtual worlds for recreational (i.e., hedonic and social) use are suitably positioned to discover those technologies’ workplace applicability, thereby assisting in their diffusion within the organization. Building on the supporting results of an empirical study, this paper recommends that managers consider allowing for ‘playtime’ with virtual worlds as a mechanism for enhancing their adoption and subsequent diffusion in the workplace. From an information systems (IS)-research perspective, this paper makes several important contributions. First, it contributes to the IS continuance literature by arguing for, and providing evidence in support of, the existence of cross-context continuance. To date, this literature stream has examined only one aspect of continuance – for example, within-context. Second, this paper identifies recreational and work as distinct dimensions of technology usage, and hedonic and social usage as sub-dimensions of the former, thereby contributing to the contextualization of this core IS construct. Third, it is one of the early field studies dedicated to the empirical examination of virtual worlds. Journal of Information Technology (2012) 27, 74–86. doi:10.1057/jit.2011.18

## Introduction

V irtual worlds are computer-simulated environments in which individuals can shop, play, chat with friends, and engage in myriad other activities via their avatars, that is, their digital selves in the virtual world. Virtual worlds, such as Active Worlds, ProtoSphere, Second Life, There, and Teleplace, to name but a few, have their roots in the domains of multiplayer online role-playing games and social computing tools (Messinger et al., 2009), but are now also working their way into organizations and the workplace (Ives and Junglas, 2008; Kock, 2008; Overby, 2008; Davis et al., 2009). Large as well as small organizations are starting to take advantage of the capabilities of virtual worlds (e.g., LaBrosse, 2008; Pollitt, 2008), with known examples including IBM’s facilities on Second Life or Sun

Microsystems’ own virtual world within which employees can collaborate on projects, and other companies’ presence in virtual world conferences and activities (e.g., SAP, KPMG, British Petroleum, Chevron, Ernst & Young, and Johnson & Johnson). Recent studies project that virtual worlds will become a dominant platform for business applications over the next decade (e.g., Davis et al., 2009). However, these budding business applications of virtual worlds (see Table 1 for additional examples) appear to be the exception rather than the rule, and the organizational use of these technologies is still in an embryonic stage (Ives and Junglas, 2008; Messinger et al., 2009).

Motivated by the apparent applicability<sup>1</sup> of virtual worlds, this paper seeks to understand the adoption and diffusion of virtual worlds in organizations by closely examining their different usage contexts. Virtual worlds provide a powerful example of a technology that can be used in numerous ways and contexts. For example, a person in a virtual world may spend time talking to friends at a virtual cafe´, attending concerts, and creating two- and three-dimensional art in much the same manner she participates in work meetings, attends professional conferences, collaborates with colleagues, runs event simulations, or prototypes new products. This apparent applicability of virtual worlds makes them likely candidates for being adopted and used in different contexts for the completion of different tasks – a phenomenon we term cross-context IS continuance. To this end, this paper examines whether the recreational usage of virtual worlds, that is, for social and hedonic activities, impacts users’ intentions to use these technologies in the work context as well.

This paper makes several contributions to information systems (IS) research and practice. First, it addresses two important gaps in the literature: (1) the de-contextualization of technology usage (e.g., Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006), and (2) our limited knowledge of post-adoptive behavior (e.g., Jasperson et al., 2005). Integrating these two gaps, this paper examines how post-adoptive behavior can lead to an expansion of usage of virtual worlds across contexts. The IS continuance literature has, thus far, remained within the boundaries of a single usage context over time. Related streams of research, which recognize the malleability of technologies, such as appropriation (Dennis et al., 2001; Fuller and Dennis, 2009) and adaptive structuration (e.g., DeSanctis and Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 2000), also remain within the confines of a single usage context, namely the workplace. Second, this paper further contributes to IS research by identifying hedonic usage and social usage as two distinct sub-dimensions of recreational usage, thereby enhancing our understanding of the complexity of the technology usage construct. Third, this paper makes a contribution to IS practice by identifying a relevant managerial intervention that can enhance the workplace adoption of virtual worlds by leveraging transfer of usage across contexts. Specifically, we argue that allowing for the recreational usage of virtual worlds in the workplace can help workers discover these technologies’ potential of serving as business applications, thereby increasing their diffusion throughout the organization. Finally, we argue that the relevance of this paper extends beyond the focal technology (i.e., virtual worlds) as the trajectory of technological evolution suggests that new technologies will incorporate a rich set of features that will enable their usage across contexts (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1993; Ives and Junglas, 2008; Overby, 2008; Davis et al., 2009).

In what follows, we elaborate on the notion of crosscontext IS continuance as the conceptual foundation for the main thesis of this paper, and identify the conditions under which this phenomenon is more likely to occur. Next, we put forth several hypotheses concerning the cross-context continuance of virtual worlds, as their usage migrates from recreational to vocational. We then present the results of an empirical test of these hypotheses, drawn from a sample of 203 virtual world users. This paper concludes with a discussion of the findings and implications for research and practice.

## Theoretical background

One of the most basic tools, the No. 2 pencil, used by every test taker, illustrates the exceptional freedom of the human mind y You hold the painted wood, write with the lead, and erase with the pink rubber held in place by a metal ring. Four different materials, each with a particular function, all wrapped up into a single tool. And although that tool was made for writing, it can also pin hair up into a bun, bookmark a page or stab an annoying insect. (Hauser, 2009)

Prehistoric evidence informs us that imagining and, subsequently, using artifacts<sup>2</sup> in multiple contexts and for different purposes is a human trait, as the section’s opening quote aptly demonstrates. For millennia, numerous inventions, such as controlled fire, microliths, and the wheel, have been used by humans for different reasons to achieve various goals. For example, our ancestors learned to exploit controlled fire to cook food, regulate temperature, keep enemies and wild animals at bay, manage landscape, and treat metal. The ubiquitous microlith played a variety of roles including weaponry and jewelry. The wheel, originally created and used for pottery, has been used for a variety of reasons including transportation and energy conversion (Torrence, 2008; Bowmen et $a l . ,$ 2009). Although many artifacts are created and used initially for a particular purpose, interactions between humans and artifacts often result in recognition that the latter can provide additional benefits when they are used differently in diverse contexts. Thus, a single artifact that was created for a specific reason and with a primary functionality in mind can provide multiple benefits when it is applied in sundry environments and settings (Torrence, 2008; Hauser, 2009); and technologybased artifacts are no exceptions (Rogers, 2003).

Despite the dramatic changes in our settings since our ancestors learned to create and use tools (approximately 2.5 million years ago) our tendency to explore, imagine, and envision other contexts in which a particular artifact can be used advantageously has remained unchanged. Humans can think in terms of counterfactuals – imagining worlds that have been against those that could be – and leverage this ability to generalize beyond direct experiences in order to come up with new solutions for myriad problems. In the particular context of this paper, consider the following quote from Sun Microsystems’ Project Wonderland<sup>3</sup> blog, which aptly illustrates this point.

There are arenas in World of Warcraft where as many as 200 players from around the world will work as teams at the same time to accomplish defined goals. 3D virtual world immersion, avatar proximity, audio communication, text messaging, and perceptions of presence are all established simultaneously within these massive events that occur in always-on real time. Many times, while playing WoW in Los Angeles, I will be on a team composed of people who are at the same time sitting in Australia, Japan, and New York y If you remove the game aspect of the product, it becomes a coordination tool for teams of individuals brought together from global distances.

This quote exemplifies the human capacity to use a technology-based artifact, such as a virtual world, in one context (e.g., recreationally) and envision an alternate reality in which it is used in a different context and for a different purpose (e.g., for work). What allows humans to draw on existing experiences with tools and be able to generalize to other settings is the capability to take apart composite objects (e.g., tools and ideas) and recombine them into new ones (Hauser, 2009).

A composite object germane to this study is the construct of technology usage (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). Although past IS research has mainly focused on a single technology usage context, technology usage is, in fact, a tripartite composed of a user, a technology, and a task. According to Burton-Jones and Straub, different arrangements of the three components represent contextually different technology usages. For example, the composites User -Technology -Task and User -Technlogy -Task correspond to two distinct instantiations of the technology usage concept (e.g., Task ¼ ‘playing a game’ and Task ¼ ‘coordinating a virtual team’); even though the user and the technology are the same. Thus, usage context can be defined as a particular combination of a user, a technology, and a task.

Building on the above, we contend that the predominant focus of IS research on a single technology usage context, primarily the workplace, while understandable, is too narrow, unnecessarily restrictive, and incongruent with humans’ natural tendencies to generalize to other contexts. We therefore argue for an expansion of the view of technology usage.

## Continued use of virtual worlds across contexts

The term IS continuance refers to behavioral patterns related to the continued use of a technology post-adoption (Limayem et al., 2007). In turn, post-adoption is defined as ‘myriad feature adoption decisions, feature use behaviors, and feature extension behaviors made by an individual user after an IT application has been installed, made accessible to the user, and applied by the user in accomplishing his/ her work activities’ (Jasperson et al., 2005: 531; italics added). Although these and other published works (e.g., Ahuja and Thatcher, 2005) have broadened the notion of technology usage, they also pre-suppose that workplace adoption has already occurred. Hence, in extant IS continuance literature, initial usage and continued usage remain within the same context – primarily, the context of the workplace. Accordingly, the task remains focused on achieving job-oriented goals. In contrast, our discussion of the elements of technology usage coupled with the recognition of the permeability of contextual boundaries affords a broader view of IS continuance by not restricting it to a single setting or context. Consequently, the paper argues that IS continuance can take two forms: within-context and cross-context.

The IS literature has examined the phenomenon of within-context IS continuance extensively (e.g. Karahanna et al., 1999; Bhattacherjee, 2001a, b; Venkatesh et al., 2002; Bhattacherjee and Premkumar, 2004; Kim and Malhotra, 2005; Limayem et al., 2007; Limayem and Cheung, 2008). In contrast, cross-context IS continuance, which refers to the continued use of a technology in different contexts, has yet to be examined. Cross-context continuance appears to be particularly relevant for the malleable technology of virtual worlds and is thus the focus of this paper.

In summary, we explore virtual worlds’ adoption and diffusion in the workplace by contextualizing technology usage and allowing for the possibility of cross-context IS continuance, which we argue is a fundamental ingredient of an expanded view of IS continuance.

## Hypothesis development

We have so far laid the conceptual foundation for the argument that the post-adoptive behavior of users of virtual worlds may extend beyond simple, within-context IS continuance and suggested that such users’ on-going use may transfer to new contexts (e.g., work). We now scrutinize the concept of cross-context IS continuance and focus on its key elements to make a case for the continued use of virtual worlds across contexts, as they transition from recreational platforms to workplace technologies.

## Recreational use of virtual worlds

To date, IS researchers have mainly focused on one type of non-work usage of technology – namely, hedonic usage. Hedonic usage is defined as consumption of a product, or a service, with the intention of deriving pleasure from its consumption (Hirschman and Holbrook, 1982; Holbrook and Hirschman, 1982; Babin et al., 1994). Hence, hedonic consumption of a technology occurs when a user experiences enjoyment from her interaction with the technology. Examples include using a movies website (Van der Heijden, 2004), playing games on a computer (Brown and Venkatesh, 2005), and watching video on demand (Hong and Tam, 2006). We concur that this type of technology usage is an important aspect of the non-work use of virtual worlds, however, since virtual worlds have their roots in the domains of multiplayer online role-playing games well as social computing tools (Eschenbrenner et al., 2008; Dreher et al., 2009; Messinger et al., 2009), it appears that focusing solely on hedonic usage, as the representative aspect of non-work usage, would under-represent the applicability of this technology. In particular, such a view would likely overlook socially oriented activities, where the technology (i.e., a virtual world) is used as a conduit for connecting individuals, such as friends and relatives. Indeed, Davis et al. (2009) argued that one must account for social interactions when studying virtual worlds. Accordingly, we propose social usage as a distinct element of non-work usage and define it as a consumption of a technology as a medium for enabling virtual social gatherings. Unlike the hedonic usage mode of operation, social usage requires the presence of other individuals. Thus, it is not the technology per se from which the individual derives pleasure but rather the other individuals with which she interacts, via the technology. Hence, the task goals are inherently different in the contexts of hedonic and social usage. Specifically, the former aims to obtain pleasure from the interaction with the technology, whereas the latter aims to derive joy from the interaction with other individuals, through the technology.

Given that hedonic usage is not the only non-work activity afforded by virtual worlds – we must account for social usage as well – a new variable is needed to capture the broader non-vocational applicability of the technology. The term Recreation, according to Merriam-Webster dictionary, encompasses single-person, intrinsic, pleasure-seeking activities such as playing computer games, as well as socially oriented activities such as spending time with friends. The term recreation is, therefore, a depiction of non-work activities and is broad enough to accommodate both hedonic usage and social usage of virtual worlds. Accordingly, we propose recreational usage as a new concept for describing and capturing the non-vocational application of the technology – that is, social and hedonic activities.

Since one can use a virtual world hedonically, socially or both, we recognize hedonic usage and social usage as two separate sub-dimensions of recreational usage. This view suggests that we conceptualize recreational usage as a second-order formative construct since each of the first order construct (i.e., social usage and hedonic usage) represents a unique and distinct aspect that contributes to the higher-order construct (Chin, 1998; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer, 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2011).

## Cognitive absorption

Cognitive absorption is a state of mind commonly associated with experimentation mentality and willingness to expand on the meaning of stimuli outside their original context (McCrae and Costa, 1983; Wild et al., 1995). It is indicative of an individual’s readiness to try out new activities, to think unconventionally about objects, and to engage holistically with technology (Trevino and Webster, 1992; Agarwal and Karahanna, 2000; Wakefield and Whitten, 2006; Deng et al., 2010).

Research has found that during recreational activities individuals tend to be creative, imaginative, and inventive vis-a\`-vis the product or service that is at the heart of the activity (Hirschman and Holbrook, 1982; Holbrook and Hirschman, 1982; Mittal, 1987; Gilkey and Kilts, 2007). This suggests that recreational activities are conducive for entering a high state of cognitive absorption, more so than routine utilitarian activities, which tend to characterize work. Thus it appears that to the extent that a person enters a state of high-cognitive absorption while using a virtual world she would take a holistic approach to evaluating the technology, elaborate in its meaning, and exhibit greater readiness to try it out in new contexts. Hence, we foresee recreational usage of virtual worlds (formed by hedonic and social usage) as an activity that is likely to lead the user to enter a state of high-cognitive absorption. Therefore,

Hypothesis 1: High levels of recreational usage of a virtual world will be positively associated with high levels of cognitive absorption.

Next, being in the state of high-cognitive absorption, while recreationally using a virtual world, is expected to be conducive for recognizing the applicability of the technology for the workplace environment. To see this, consider that cognitive absorption is associated with enhanced imagination activity (Shang et al., 2005), which is ‘the act or power of forming a mental image of something not present to the senses or never before wholly perceived in reality (Merriam-Webster Dictionary).’ Thus, when an individual is using a virtual world recreationally, which we is conducive for experiencing heightened levels of cognitive absorption, she is also more likely to imagine other contexts (e.g., work) in which the virtual world can be used advantageously. In the context of this paper, this relationship is conceptualized as a causal path from cognitive absorption to willingness to use virtual worlds for work. Accordingly,

Hypothesis 2: High levels of cognitive absorption, while using a virtual world recreationally, are positively associated with high levels of intentions to use the technology for work in the future.

Finally, in order to account for the possibility that some individuals may have already been exposed to virtual worlds in their work environment, we incorporate current work usage into our model. Including this variable in the model affords an opportunity to measure the net effect of cross-context IS continuance by controlling for the possibility of within-context IS continuance. In line with the traditional IS continuance literature (e.g., Kim and Malhotra, 2005; Hong et al., 2008), we expect that using a virtual world for work today will have a positive impact on intentions to use it for work in the future (within-context continuance).<sup>4</sup> We thus hypothesize that (Figure 1):

## Research methodology

Given the objectives of this study, the population of interest in this study is that of working professionals who are familiar with virtual worlds. Our sampling frame was a LinkedIn group for professionals involved in virtual worlds<sup>5</sup> (the group is called VirtualWorlds). There are about 3000 members in this group. There are no costs associated with joining the group and participation is voluntary. Members represent myriad companies in various industries, according to their public profiles. A personal invitation (Dillman, 1999) to participate in the study was sent via e-mail to 700 randomly sampled group members. In exchange for participation in the study, respondents were offered a US\$10 electronic gift certificate and a report of our findings. Two hundred and ninety-one individuals indicated their willingness to participate in the study. Hence, 291 e-mails with a link to an online survey<sup>6</sup> were sent. Following an initial round and a reminder 233 questionnaires were obtained, representing a 33.3 per cent response rate. After removing questionnaires with a large number of missing values,<sup>7</sup> 203 usable questionnaires (final response rate of 29 per cent) remained and were used for hypothesis testing. Seventy per cent of the respondents were male. The median tenure with current employer was 3–4 years. The median age group was 40–49. Thirty per cent had a business background, 17 per cent had computer science or engineering background, 13 per cent had social sciences background, and 13 per cent has background in humanities. Eighty-eight per cent of the respondents had a bachelor’s or higher degree. Sixty per cent of the respondents held a managerial position.

Although Second Life is the most popular and best known virtual world, there are numerous other virtual worlds that individuals and organizations may use. Accordingly, we did not restrict our attention to a single virtual world but instead asked each respondent to note what his or her virtual world of choice was and to focus on it when completing the questionnaire. Our analysis showed that 70 per cent of the respondents use Second Life and that the remaining 30 per cent were distributed among some of the lesser known worlds such as Prototerra and There.

## Instrument development

Five multi-item scales were used in this study to test the above hypotheses: work usage of a virtual world, social usage of a virtual world, hedonic usage of a virtual world, cognitive absorption, and intentions to use a virtual world for work. To measure social, hedonic, and work usage of virtual worlds we adapted the scales developed by Kettinger and Grover (1997). Kettinger and Grover developed the task usage scale to capture technology-based work-related activities, and its items focus on getting the job done, effectively and efficiently. Accordingly, this scale was deemed an adequate measure of our work usage construct. Kettinger and Grover also developed the social/entertainment scale to capture non-work-related technology-based activities, which suggests that it could adequately be used to operationalize recreational usage as defined in this study. Furthermore, given the scale’s apparent dual role – that is, capturing social as well as entertainment use of technology – it appeared particularly suitable for our model in which recreational usage is conceptualized as a secondorder factor formed by social usage and hedonic usage. However, since Kettinger and Grover did not distinguish between social and entertainment activities, instead treating them as a single factor, it was necessary to separate the items into two sets: those that better indicate social usage and those that fit more closely with hedonic usage of virtual worlds. Also, since the original scales were developed for a technology markedly different from virtual worlds – that is, e-mail – we found it necessary to assess the items’ face validity.<sup>8</sup> As a first step, the three authors of this paper scrutinized the original items suggested by Kettinger and Grover (see Table 2).

Focusing on the usage of virtual worlds in the context of the workplace, we were inclined to remove ‘Keep record of interaction agreement’ from the scale since virtual worlds do not lend themselves naturally to this type of activity. Moving on to the recreational context, we identified the following items as likely indicators of social usage of a virtual world: ‘Keep in touch/maintain relationships,’ ‘Get to know someone,’ and ‘Organize/coordinate social activities/events.’ We were less certain about where to place ‘Participate in entertaining events/conversation’ since it included the words ‘entertainment’ and ‘conversation’ thereby making it a potential indicator of both types of recreational usage. The remaining three items appeared to be more closely aligned with hedonic usage of virtual worlds.

Next, we forwarded the scale items to three IS professors not affiliated with the study and solicited their feedback. The feedback we received generally supported our initial observation; however, the experts suggested that virtual worlds are less likely to be used to ‘Coordinate activities for projects.’ Consequently, we removed this item from the work usage scale. In addition, the experts leaned in the direction of treating ‘Participate in entertaining events/ conversation’ as an indicator of hedonic usage of virtual worlds. In summary, the following six items were used to measure work usage: ‘Give and receive feedback on reports and ideas,’ ‘Brainstorm/generate ideas,’ ‘Seek task information from people I know,’ ‘Carry on negotiations/bargaining,’ ‘Resolve conflict/disagreement’ and ‘Solve problems.’ The items ‘Keep in touch/maintain relationships,’ ‘Get to know someone,’ and ‘Organize/coordinate social activities/events’ were used to operationalize social usage. Finally, ‘Feel up free time,’ ‘Take a break from my work,’ ‘Learn about things/events that interest me,’ and ‘Participate in entertaining events or conversations’ were selected to measure hedonic usage of virtual worlds.

The three usage scales were constructed as formative on the premise that the indicators for each scale are independent of one another (Chin, 1998; Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer, 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2011). For example, it is easy to see that ‘Resolve conflict/disagreement’ and ‘Brainstorm/generate ideas’ can occur separately. Similarly, within the social usage scale, using a virtual world to ‘Organize coordinate social activities/events’ may or may not be accompanied by using it to ‘Keep in touch/maintain relationships.’ In addition, as per our discussion in the ‘Hypothesis development’ section, social usage and hedonic usage of virtual worlds were operationalized as formative, lower-level factors of the higher-level recreational usage factor.

The cognitive absorption construct was developed and validated by Agarwal and Karahanna (2000). Since Agarwal and Karahanna conceptualized absorptive capacity as a higher-order reflective construct, its first-order constructs are interchangeable (Jarvis et al., 2003; Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). Accordingly, our operationalization of the construct relied on one dimension: temporal dissociation. Three items from the Temporal Dissociation scale were selected and adapted to operationalize absorption in our study: ‘Sometimes I lose track of time when I am using the virtual world,’ ‘Most times when I use the virtual world, I end up spending more time than I had planned,’ and ‘Time flies when I am using the virtual world.’

To measure future work use intentions, or continued use, we adopted the scale developed by Agarwal and Prasad (1997). Minor changes were made to the scale to reflect the specific context in which the virtual world would be used. ## Data analysis

In this section, we discuss the validation of the survey instrument, explain how the data were analyzed, and present the key findings.

## Assessment of common method bias

A concern with self-reported data is the possible presence of a systematic error. To reduce the likelihood of common method bias we followed the recommendation made by Salancik and Pfeffer (1977) by designing the questionnaire in such a way that for some respondents the items measuring the dependent variables followed, rather than preceded, the items measuring the independent variables. Furthermore, following the recommendation made by Straub et al. (2004), we intermixed, rather than grouped same-scale items. In order to assess whether common method bias was a concern, the Harmon one-factor test was conducted by entering all independent and dependent variables in an exploratory factor analysis (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The first factor accounted for o50 per cent of the total variance, indicating a lack of a substantial common methods bias.

## Measurement validation

This stage was carried out by observing the statistics associated with the measurement model following confirmatory factor analysis using PLS (SmartPLS v. 2.0.M3). This structural equation modeling technique was chosen for this study due to its ability to handle both reflective and formative scales, which are included in our model.

To assess the scales’ psychometric properties, several tests were conducted. We describe those tests next, beginning with the reflective scales and then discussing the formative scales.

## Reflective scales

Convergent validity. Convergent validity is an assessment of the agreement among measures of the same construct (Bagozzi et al., 1991). Hence, high levels of convergent validity indicate that the items reflect the same latent variable. Two tests were used to assess convergent validity. We first observed the loadings of items, shown in Table 3. Each item loaded high on the intended factor (0.60 or more) and low on the other factor (o0.40), demonstrating the instrument’s convergent validity. Convergent validity was also assessed by observing the square root of the average variance extracted (diagonal elements in Table 4). A minimum level of 0.70 is suggested (Fornell and Larcker, 1981), since it indicates that, on average, the construct accounts for at least 50 per cent of its measures’ variance. Discriminant validity. Discriminant validity indicates that the items measure only the construct for which they were created and not other constructs in the model (Salisbury et al., 2002). ## Findings

The results from the structural model generally support our key arguments for the cross-context IS continuance virtual worlds by empirically demonstrating intentions to shift technology usage, from the recreational to the vocational. Specifically, current recreational usage of virtual worlds (a higher-level factor formed by hedonic and social usage of the technology) had a positive (b ¼ 0.342) and significant (t-value ¼ 8.302) effect on an individual’s absorption with the technology (Hypothesis 1). In turn, an individual that had entered a state of high-cognitive absorption while using a virtual world for recreation also exhibited stronger intentions (b ¼ 0.381; t-value ¼ 10.759) to use a virtual world for work (Hypothesis 2). Finally, in-line with the within-context IS continuance literature, current work usage was found to have a positive (b ¼ 0.305) and significant (t-value ¼ 8.497) effect on the dependent variable (Hypothesis 3). As expected, the impact of recreational usage on the dependent variable was completely mediated by state of cognitive absorption. We elaborate on the findings in the following section.

## Discussion

To get more widespread adoption, both managers and workers need to be confident that the virtual world activity has a clear business value that directly supports their specific work objectives. When we can easily give a quick one-sentence answer to the question ‘How is meeting in Second Life any different that attending a conference call?’ it will be easier to justify in a business environment. (Product Manager)

It’s being seen as ‘too futuristic’ for our industry and not able to generate immediate sales rather than looking at the potential for cost-savings against travel/meeting and training expenses. (Marketer)

The question I struggle to answer is ‘How is second life different from a conference call?’ All I can say is it’s more engaging, interactive, and keeps me focused on task, even when watching streamed video feeds. For collaborative work, it’s way more fun and faster. (Trainer of Customer Service Reps & Technicians)

The above comments, made by survey respondents,<sup>10</sup> highlight the key managerial issue addressed by our study: the adoption and diffusion of virtual worlds within organizations is tightly linked to their workplace applicability. In other words, if organizational members do not recognize the business value of virtual worlds to their work, they would not use them, especially if they supplement rather than supplant existing technologies.

We tackled this issue by conceptualizing a process where using a virtual world for recreational activities (i.e., hedonically and socially) is perceived as conducive for identifying the technology’s applicability for the workplace, in turn leading to enhanced intentions to use it for work in the future. This transformation of usage across contexts (i.e., from the recreational to the vocational) appears to be enabled by entering an absorptive state of mind, in which an individual is more likely to try out new activities, think unconventionally, and expand on the meaning of technologies outside their original contexts. Entering this state, whereby thinking in terms of counterfactuals and generalizing to other contexts is common, is more likely to occur when engaging technologies recreationally, that is, hedonically or socially.

Building on the study’s findings, we propose that recreational usage of, or ‘playtime’ with, virtual worlds can trigger holistic information processing and experimentation mentality, which are conducive for detecting novelty and thinking counterfactually and imaginatively, thereby leading to innovation via discovery of new features and application of features to new contexts. In turn, these discoveries are likely to enhance adoption and diffusion of virtual worlds in terms of speed and coverage since they may unearth multiple benefits in a variety of settings, allowing different potential users to recognize possible fulfillment of needs. Furthermore, this cross-context usage of virtual worlds is likely to lead to faster and stronger institutionalization of the technology, a stage at which organizations reap greater benefits from their IT investments. This is because when multiple user groups find a host of uses for a single innovation (e.g., information technology) we should expect to observe more rapid diffusion within a social system, such as an organization (Backer, 2000). Hence, we propose that organizations consider allowing for ‘playtime’ with virtual worlds in order to facilitate discovery of workplace applicability, at least until a tipping point in adoption and diffusion has been reached.

## Contextualizing technology use

Context was woven into this study by conceptualizing two main usage types (i.e., recreational and work) for the emerging technology of virtual worlds. Technology usage was further contextualized by identifying two sub-dimensions of recreation – namely, hedonic usage and social usage – commensurate with the technology’s origins in multiplayer online role-playing games and social computing tools. We provided empirical evidence supportive of the existence of each usage type in and of itself. Our incorporation of context into this study was especially important given the nature of the technology studied, as virtual worlds contain gaming, socializing, and worksupporting functionalities, and can be used in more than one context simultaneously or over time.

Understanding technology usage in context contributes to IS research in two important ways. First, it assists in reducing the likelihood of errors of inclusion and of exclusion (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006): When the focal technology can be used in multiple contexts, using a single usage construct increases the probability of confounding the measurements. Second, it helps to explicate the nomological net in each substantive usage context.

Recent studies called upon IS researchers to contextualize technology usage, arguing that it is unlikely that a single conceptualization or measure will be able to successfully capture such a complex construct (Burton-Jones and Straub, 2006). Researchers were encouraged to conceptualize, and ultimately measure, contextualized variables, taking into account three important elements: the user, the technology, and the task. In this study, while the user and the technology are the same under the hedonic, social, and work contexts, the tasks are inherently different. Consequently, three distinct technology usage constructs were developed (exemplifying quotes from respondents are provided in brackets): One representing the composite user–VW–socialization (e.g., I use [VW] to visit with online friends), one for the composite user–VW–hedonism (e.g., I use [VW] for fun/entertainment), and one representing the user–VW–work composite (e.g., weekly meetings [in VW] to discuss activities, help get newbies setup, and meet with others remotely). By decomposing technology usage into these three types, this study addressed an important gap in a literature that has, heretofore, primarily treated technology use in a decontextualized manner. As technologies continue to cross contextual boundaries such distinction is likely to remain important.

In summary, the model developed in this paper advances IS continuance research by examining usage across contexts. Overall, this paper provides an important step toward a more accurate representation of the real world, in which our interactions with certain technologies are multifaceted and interrelated.

## Managerial implications

Our next contribution addresses a gap concerning actionable organizational interventions that managers can leverage to enhance technology usage at the workplace (Venkatesh et al., 2008). A key insight we can offer managers stems from the relationship between recreational usage and work use intentions. Specifically, the finding that using a virtual world for recreation has a positive impact on intentions to use it for work – via heightened cognitive absorption – provides managers with an important tool with which they can enhance the technology’s adoption and diffusion at the workplace. An actionable intervention that managers may therefore consider is to allow for ‘playtime’ with virtual worlds in the workplace. Such recreational usage experiences are expected to ultimately impact usage in the workplace.<sup>11</sup> To make ‘playtime’ effective, organizations should reduce perceptions of time pressure and censorship which can negatively affect fun-seeking behavior (Babin et al., 1994). Thus, we recommend a managed implementation of virtual worlds, whereby work mentality and ‘playtime’ co-exist, at least until the innovation is accepted throughout the organization and its use routinized.

Perhaps, if wary of too much ‘playtime’ activity, managers should consider identifying a small group of individuals to serve as virtual worlds ‘evangelists’ and allow them to recreationally use the technology at the workplace in order to help with its diffusion. The extent to which managers choose to leverage cross-context IS continuance via ‘playtime’ should be in accordance with the organization’s culture and work habits. For example, one organization might find it appropriate to broadcast its support for virtual world usage whereas another might find the use of ‘skunk works’ (Rogers, 2003) to be more appropriate. Also, each organization should decide for itself if, and when ‘playtime’ is no longer needed. Note, however, that ‘playtime’ is conducive of creativity<sup>12</sup> and may be of benefit even when virtual worlds have been integrated into organizational routines.

Overall, the important and somewhat counter-intuitive finding of our study, as far as managerial thinking is concerned, is that allowing certain employees to ‘play’ with a virtual world is likely to positively impact future use for work. Such insight represents a shift in IT management that appears to be important for the success of virtual worldsrelated initiatives.

## Limitations and future research

It is conceivable that the study’s participants were early adopters of virtual worlds and as such may not be entirely representative of the population as a whole (in addition, they were recruited through LinkedIn, which is a social technology itself). Early adopters are believed to be technology savvy (Venkatesh and Brown, 2001), hence we caution against generalizations to other populations. In the future, as this technology matures and its use becomes more widespread, other populations of less innovative users may become accessible, allowing researchers to examine the generalizability of the relationships identified in this paper. Another limitation of this study stems from the nature of the data collection process; since data were collected during a single point in time, it is difficult to ascertain causality.

Conceptually, the work presented in this paper can be further developed by scrutinizing the notion of context. Although this paper defines it as a particular combination of a user, a technology, and a task, it is possible to envision context as a distinct dimension rather than as an outcome of a user applying a certain technology for a specific task. Hence, if we conceptualize user as the answer to the who question; technology as the answer to the what question, and task and the answer to the why question; then context may be the answer to the where question.

A promising avenue for future research of virtual worlds is cultural diversity. An important element of this study is the state of absorption. Virtual worlds enable users to imagine themselves in different roles and play out their fantasies. However, behaviors that are socially acceptable or encouraged in one culture may be frowned-upon, or even forbidden in another culture. This suggests that using virtual worlds for recreational (i.e., hedonic and social) activities may not be invariant across different cultures. Hence, the generalizability of the model developed in this study may be augmented in future studies by introducing culture into the model. It appears that companies are already facing this issue and addressing it by introducing guidelines concerning acceptable virtual behavior by their employees.<sup>13</sup>

Work and recreational usage of virtual worlds were measured in the paper by adapting existing scales. Those scales were originally developed for e-mail – a technology vastly simpler than a virtual world. Although several of the original items were deemed adequate for recreational (i.e., hedonic and social) and work usage of virtual worlds, it is possible that additional items would be needed to capture the applicability of virtual worlds.<sup>14</sup>

Identifying all the items purported to form a latent construct is crucial when using formative scales (Petter et al., 2007). In light of that, and informed by a recent Special Research Commentary Series on Advanced Methodological Thinking for Quantitative Research (MIS Quarterly, June 2011), we note that the scales developed in this paper to measure hedonic, social, ad work usage of virtual worlds may more accurately be seen as consisting of causal indicators rather than formative (or composite) indicators (for a discussion, see Bollen, 2011). In the case of causal indicators, and distinct from their composite counterparts, latent variables are not expected to be completely be determined by the indicators (Bollen, 2011). In addition, when using formative scales the model’s generalizability is likely to be somewhat limited in that structural properties (e.g., path coefficients) might be dependent on a specific model (e.g., specified relationships among the variables) and its particular indicators (e.g., number of formative measures) (Bagozzi, 2011).

Much of the extant research on diffusion of innovations has been primarily focused on technologies that are introduced into organizations from the top-down by management. Recently, Parameswaran and Whinston (2007) observed that the adoption of social computing tools often starts at the grassroots, diffusing throughout social systems in a decentralized manner from the bottom up. It could be illuminating to examine to what extent the pattern of diffusion – that is, top-down, bottom-up, or hybrid – affects the breadth and depth of diffusion. Although many emerging technologies can be utilized across contexts, it remains to be seen whether the diffusion pattern – that is, top-down, bottom-up, or hybrid (Rogers, 2003) – impact on the speed with which virtual worlds are being adopted as well as the extent to which they cross contexts.

## Conclusion

This study examined how organizations may enhance the adoption and diffusion of virtual worlds, by taking advantage of cross-context IS continuance. Specifically, we examined how organizations may leverage recreational usage of virtual worlds to help identify their applicability for the workplace. Virtual worlds represent an interesting technology from a research perspective since they not only offer diverse usage contexts (of which we focused on two, namely recreational – that is, social and hedonic – and work activities), but they can also offer different business values to different organizations, depending on the organization’s needs and nature. For this reason we believe that ‘playtime, which is conducive for cross-context IS continuance, is key to the success of these technologies in organizations. Although this study offers several relevant contributions to IS research and practice we believe that additional research is needed to better understand virtual worlds and similar technological innovations.
