## A B S T R A C T

While most research on website has focused on functional tasks, the Internet offers many opportunities for leisure as well as experiential activities. Because of the evolution of developed society toward an experience economy, analyzing the role of technologies in the presence of prior user experiences makes sense. This research identifies variables that play a role and influence online behaviors in a specific experiential environment, namely the high culture museum website. Relying on the literature on experience, we propose a research model tested with two different websites. The results of the free simulation experiment indicates that (1) esthetics is the most important design criteria for experiential interfaces and (2) that website design influences intentions to visit a physical place.

## 1. Introduction

Museums are among the most socially valued cultural institutions in the world, and their attendance has been growing since the end of the 1980s [89]. Indeed, Poulot [89] indicated that in developed countries, one-third of the population frequently visits museums. Through a cross-national comparison, Schuster [96] observed that museum attendance generates the highest participation rates, just after cinema, reading, and sports. The Louvre Museum in Paris, for example, reported 6.1 million visitors in 2000 and 8.8 million visitors in 2011. This increased interest in museums can be explained by the many socioeconomic benefits that they can offer, such as providing new community/social space, improving quality of life for residents, and providing learning resources and support [14]. A study commissioned by the European Union indicates that culture is also closely related to innovation and creativity [57].

Along with this growing interest in high culture institutions, museum websites are grappling with how to improve their website design [9,22,59,74]. A survey conducted by the Institute of

Museum and Library Services [51] revealed that 97% of large to medium-sized American museums while 78% of small museums have a website [51]. Museum professionals initially feared that virtual museums would replace physical museums and discourage people from visiting traditional museums [73]; however, today, most museum experts accept the unique opportunities that websites offer for attracting people to their museums [73]. In 2008, 50% the American museum audience comprised in-person visitors, while 45% comprised people who visited both museums online and in person [50]. Consequently, museums tend to invest increasingly more money in their websites to improve the quality of these virtual interfaces. As an example, within the last decade, <sub>the Louvre has spent more than</sub> s<sub>7 million on its new website [94].</sub> One could thus conjecture that well-designed museum websites will attract Internet visitors and that a ‘‘sticky’’ website [91] may induce visitors to return to it or even induce visitors to regularly visit a physical museum as a future leisure activity. Although welldesigned websites should induce behavior and inspire visits to physical museums, extant research has not documented this phenomenon at any level.

Furthermore, museums are worth studying in Information Systems (IS) research because museums represent a distinctive context in comparison to the business context on which past IS research has mainly focused (e.g., [60,83,106]). In fact, museums belong to the experiential realm characterized by sensations, engagement, and esthetics, among other things [43,46,87]. While most research on websites has focused on functional tasks, the Internet offers many opportunities for leisure as well as experiential activities [25]. As an example, online users regularly visit tourism websites, online gaming websites, sports websites, virtual environments (such as Second Life), and social media platforms. During the first quarter of 2012, the Museum of Modern Art, New York, reached one million fans on Facebook, demonstrating the potential of the Internet to create large communities of visitors and to expand knowledge beyond museum walls. Among the 50 most active museums on social media, The Tate Museum was ranked second in February 2012 with 547,102 followers on Twitter [68].

Orlikowski and Iacono [81] encouraged researchers to study Information Technologies (IT) in different contexts of use and, in doing so, to analyze the differences produced by these new contexts. ‘‘Letting go of a monolithic view of technology implies recognizing that technologies such as the Internet and other distributed applications do not provide the same material and cultural properties in each local time and context of use’’ ([81], p. 32). Indeed, the same technology can be used in different contexts or for different tasks; thus, the context will likely influence the outcomes of the interactions and variables that are considered in a research model. Petter et al. [86] recommended applying the Delone and McLean Success Model to new contexts (p. 239). Similarly, Zhang and Li [110] identified context as one of the four variables that influence key interactions with a system.

The evolution of society toward an experience economy [87] necessitates an analysis of the role of technologies in experiential contexts. Accordingly, in the current research, we identify variables that play a role and influence online behavior in a specific experiential environment, namely, high culture museum websites.

The following research questions guide this study:

1. In an experiential environment, such as museum websites, which design characteristics most satisfy user needs and most contribute to the user experience?

2. Do museum websites encourage physical visits to museums?

Relying on the literature on experience, we propose a research model to be tested with two different websites. This paper is organized as follows. The second section introduces the literature on experience by identifying the characteristics of the experience economy and of user experience. The third section presents hypotheses related to the specific context of museum websites. In addition, this section justifies how website design criteria are linked to the evaluation of experiential settings as mediated by IT. In Section 4, we introduce our methodology. In Section 5, we present the data analysis and main results. In Section 6, we discuss the results and highlight the main contributions of the research. The last section concludes the study by calling for more studies in this socially important context.

## 2. Literature review on experience

Stressing that experience represents the latest stage in modern developed economies, Pine and Gilmore [87] highlighted the role of experience in co-creating value for industrialized societies. As the successor to agrarian, industrial, and service economies, experience captures customer interests at multiple levels:

The company—we’ll call it an experience stager—no longer offers goods or services alone but the resulting experience, rich with sensations, created within the customer. [. . .] experiences are inherently personal. They actually occur within any individual who has been engaged on an emotional, physical, intellectual, or even spiritual level. ([87], p. 30)

Therefore, the main function of experience businesses is to orchestrate events to create memorable and personal experience. Indeed, the factors of demand are driven by ‘‘sensations,’’ a turn of phrase that means that people who are engaged in the experience economy tend to seek and pay for highly sensory activities. Consequently, businesses predicted to perform better in an experience economy are those that can offer a unique experience to their customers, often instantiated as a differentiation strategy.

Individuals can have diverse types of experiences, depending on, for example, their environment, the offering, and their personality. Thus, prior research indicates that experience is best captured as a multidimensional concept [46,47,88]. While their proposed dimensions differ, both Pine and Gilmore [88] and Holbrook [46] identified four dimensions of experience. Relying on level of participation and intensity of connection to the event, Pine and Gilmore [87] obtained four quadrants/types of experience: (a) entertainment, (b) education, (c) escapism, and (d) esthetics. On the basis of a literature review on experience, Holbrook [46] classified the dimensions into ‘‘four Es’’: (a) experience, (b) entertainment, (c) exhibitionism, and (d) evangelizing.

Although interesting in their own right, these two classification schemes do not address the role played by technologies, particularly IT, in experience. With the omnipresence of technologies in both the private and the public spheres, it is critical that we turn our attention to the interaction between IT and experience. The Internet offers many opportunities for leisure and experiential activities. Online users regularly visit tourism, online gaming, and sports websites as well as virtual environments (such as Second Life). Therefore, the evaluation of user experience has become an important field of research for the IS discipline.

User experience (UX) research ‘‘goes beyond the purely cognitive and task-oriented perspective’’ that is generally assumed in research on information systems ([43], p. 92). More precisely, three characteristics of user experience research have been noted [43]: first, it goes beyond the instrumental perspective; second, it encompasses emotions and affect; and third, it deals with experiential issues. For instance, the UX field has paid attention to the very diverse needs addressed by technology, such as play [71], enchantment [76,78], cognitive absorption [25,2], esthetics [41,63,100], and enjoyment [10,104]. Generally in this body of research, emphasis is placed on the emotional, hedonic, or esthetic dimensions of experience [42,43,64]. As such, we can note commonalities between the focus of the UX research stream and the two aforementioned classifications.

Echoing researchers in marketing and management who argue that experience has several facets, Human Computer Interaction (HCI) researchers have also encouraged the use of a multidimensional approach to capture experience [52,75,76]. For instance, McCarthy et al. [76] studied two types of interactive experiences involving enchantment, namely,filmand mobile phone experiences. Their research objective was to identify key ‘‘sensibilities’’ that HCI designers can take into account when designing systems. The presence of the two different contexts of enchantment highlights the importance of having multiple criteria to examine user experience.

The field of user experience has also inspired the creation and development of new IT-based applications, called experiential computing systems [54,55]. As an illustration, Jain and his research group at the Georgia Institute of Technology are focusing on developing applications that acknowledge user experience and that store and process these user insights as traditional data. Experiential computing systems can integrate components, such as sensors, video, or virtual reality, to address user needs from multiple perspectives [54,55].

Although experiential contexts have received greater attention in IT research in the last few years, our knowledge of the variables that play a role and influence online behaviors in specific experiential environments is still limited [43]. Moreover, further scholarly examination of user experience is important, as recent research indicates that user experience with IT influences the continuity of IT use [25]. Consequently, our research focuses on these issues by investigating user experience with museum websites.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses: the case of high culture museum websites

## 3.1. Museums and their missions

As a setting for an intensely experiential environment, museums are a fitting study context. These institutions qualify as experiential settings because they offer their visitors several social and experiential benefits, such as life enrichment, avenues for interactions, enjoyment, and learning experiences [27,30,58].

In the past, museums mainly focused on their collections, and research and exhibitions were their most important activities. Admitting the importance of visitor needs and adding new activities to their collections and research has extended museums’ core mission. Anderson [7] used the term paradigm to denote this core mission. More precisely, Anderson argued that museums have made a ‘‘paradigm shift from collection-driven institutions to visitor-centered museums’’ ([7], p. 1). This paradigm shift was noticed earlier by Vergo ([108], p. 3) who labeled it the ‘‘New Museology’’ in comparison to more traditional views. Led by Desvalle´es [26], the ‘‘New Museology’’ also emerged in France a few years later with a collective reflection and redefinition of museum missions. These researchers encouraged museum experts to put more emphasis on both education and entertainment. The convergence of these two fields is called edutainment [1] and represents a twofold target to attract visitors. Ross [92] identified accessibility and democratization as other characteristics of the ‘‘New Museology’’ stream. In effect, according to Ross [92], institutions following the principles of new museology should make greater efforts to attract a wider public. Consequently, since the early 20th century, role of museum has evolved and has been redefined to enhance the visitor experience [7].

Museum websites are more related to the ‘‘Internet presence websites’’ in the typology of Hoffman et al. [45]. Interestingly, the primary goal of these websites is not sales, even though some museum interfaces do support purchasing activities through online boutiques. By contrast to commercial sites, Internet presence websites, such as museum sites, aim at advertising and providing information to visitors [45]. This advertising is intended to draw more people to the museum, a supposition supported by Lagrosen [61] in his investigation of how the use of the Internet by Swedish museums provides valuable online services to visitors. Indeed, Lagrosen [61] explained that ‘‘it is the visit and the experiences that the visitors have that are the product [of museums]’’ (p. 134). Consequently, encouraging future visits is an important goal of museum websites. Referring to Belanger et al. [8], we can elaborate further goals of museum websites, including life enrichment, knowledge enhancement, online learning, and entertainment.

Based on this logic, our research does not consider purchase intentions; rather, we investigate the behavioral intentions of returning to the website or of visiting a physical museum after visiting a museum website. This research issue is important for two reasons. First, a significant proportion of visitors may use websites as a substitute for going to physical museums. Therefore, websites could have more powerful effects than hypothesized. Second, going to a physical museum clearly involves more effort than returning to a website since it entails physical travel. However, this outcome is interesting because IS research has not investigated the link between websites and intentions to visit physical places. Similarly, few IS researchers have paid attention to the design of museum websites. Accordingly, we posit the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 1 (H1).

Intentions to return to a website will positively influence visits to a museum.

## 3.2. Theory of planned behavior applied to this experiential context

Behavioral intentions have well-established antecedents in the attitudes–behavior literature, and thus, we also assess the influence of usability perceptions on attitudes and, subsequently, the influence of attitudes on intentions. To test these relations, we applied the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) developed by Ajzen [4]. The TPB is an extension of the Theory of Reasoned Action [31], including the additional factor ‘‘perceived behavioral control.’’ This variable captures the notion that individuals do not always completely or rationally control their behavior. The TPB states that ‘‘behavioral beliefs’’ influence ‘‘attitude toward the behavior’’ and that the latter will have an impact on ‘‘intentions.’’ Attitudes are defined as the individual’s positive or negative feelings about performing a behavior. In our research, we evaluated the effect of six behavioral beliefs (perceptions of content, made for the medium, ease of use, emotion, promotion, and esthetics) on attitudes toward the museum website and toward the physical museum. This theory is applicable to our context, as Ajzen and Driver [6] reported that TPB can be used to advance our understanding of leisure activities.

## 3.2.1. Attitudes

Ample evidence suggests that attitudes toward a product or a service influence behavioral intentions toward that product or service. In IS research, many studies have consistently noted the importance of attitudes toward IS in predicting intentions to use the system (e.g., [48,93,97]). Castaneda et al. [16] also showed that attitudes toward a website are a strong predictor of intentions to revisit the website. Thus, we posit the following hypotheses:

H2a. Positive attitudes toward a website will positively influence intentions to return to the website.

H2b. Positive attitudes toward a website will positively influence intentions to visit the physical museum.

## 3.2.2. Subjective norms

To capture the role played by people close to an individual, we also took into consideration the concept of subjective norms, as introduced by Fishbein and Ajzen [31]. Subjective norms can be defined as ‘‘the person’s perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question’’ ([93], p. 302). According to Ajzen [4], subjective norms reflect the perceived opinions of the most respected others (e.g., family members, co-workers, friends). Subjective norms are an important variable to account for, especially in the context of museums. Emphasizing that museum attendance is primarily a social activity, Bourdieu and Darbel [12] highlighted the crucial role played by relatives and other intimates thought to have a disproportionate influence on high culture choices. In a qualitative study, Burton [15] identified six consumer patterns for leisure consumption. One of them is the category of ‘‘peer driven’’ consumers, who rely on others’ decisions to choose their cultural activities (p. 66). As a result, friends, family, or media can influence intentions, particularly for young people who care about their self-image. Moreover, people often go to museums with family or friends since they prefer company while visiting a museum [24]. Therefore, personal relationships can influence a decision to visit a museum or its surrogate (website). Accordingly, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3a. Subjective norms will positively influence intentions to return to the website.

H3b. Subjective norms will positively influence intentions to visit the physical museum.

## 3.2.3. Facilitating conditions

Facilitating conditions can be conceptualized as rival hypotheses or substitutes for perceived behavioral control [66]. In our research, these facilitating conditions consist of time available and prior experience with cultural activities. Research aiming to explain behaviors in high cultural settings also have identified these two elements as influencing behaviors [12,72]. Whereas perceived cost is occasionally listed as a facilitating condition, we have omitted it since multiple studies have indicated that perceived cost weakly explains museum visitors’ behaviors [37,72].

Bourdieu and Darbel [12] found that prior experience is highly relevant to human cultural practices. More precisely, they argued that familiarity with cultural institutions is a prerequisite for repeated museum visits. Martin [72] showed that the primary reason for not visiting museums is insufficient time, a reason that 43% of the respondents from the British public articulated. Therefore, our research aims to demonstrate that these two facilitating conditions, namely, prior experience and availability, influence behavior. We propose the following hypotheses:

H4a. A positive assessment of the facilitating conditions will positively influence intentions to return to the website.

H4b. A positive assessment of the facilitating conditions will positively influence intentions to visit the physical museum.

## 3.3. Evaluation of website design

To assess website design, we relied on the conceptualization of usability developed by Agarwal and Venkatesh [3] and adapted from the Microsoft Usability Guidelines (MUG). This conceptualization uses five categories: (1) content, (2) ease of use, (3) promotion, (4) made for the medium, and (5) emotion. We chose to use this instrument for three principal reasons. First, Treiblmaier ([101], p. 820), noting that ‘‘the same scales are developed over and over again’’ in website design research, encouraged researchers to use existing metrics rather than developing new scales. Second, the five constructs in the MUG have acceptable content validity, as demonstrated by Agarwal and Venkatesh [3]. Third, these metrics also provide several dimensions and subdimensions, which enable a straightforward assessment of different aspects of website usability as well as a good measurement of online users experience. Prior research on the MUG has shown that this set of variables plays a major role in the overall assessment of website design [3,107]. Furthermore, the richness of this scale offers the opportunity to better evaluate the experience offered by a website. The five categories reflect many facets of experience (as shown in Table 1).

Our conceptualization of website usability for museums, however, takes the position that esthetics is missing from the MUG scales. In an experiential environment, such as a museum website, esthetics is very likely to play a role [33,74]. Museums are supposed to be beautiful places; thus, by transference, visitors will also expect their websites to be beautiful [74]. Forbes [33] asserted that museums should provide esthetic experiences to the public to enhance learning. He also argued that art museums are most concerned with esthetic issues; their chief role is to display beauty to visitors. ‘‘To be effective the museum must bring beauty to buildings, their exteriors, their interiors and contents’’ ([33], p. 6). More recently, Bourgeon-Renault et al. [13], using the multimethodological approach of questionnaires, interviews, and observations, identified factors influencing the value ascribed to museums and monuments. They showed that museums that are able to create emotionally and esthetically satisfying visits have higher value for visitors than places where these dimensions were absent. As most museums now have an online presence, they should also render beauty not only in their physical displays but also in their websites. Supporting this view, Lin and Gregor [67] interviewed five museum experts who considered appearance (colors, text, images) to be one of the most important criteria to enhance the enjoyment of learning on museum websites.

Table 1 below further illustrates how the variables used in our research capture the essence of experience. To build this table, we relied on the prior application of Pine and Gilmore’s criteria in IS research (see [77,82]).

The five dimensions of the MUG facilitate the assessment of engagement. ‘‘Engaging experiences have the attention of the user, but, unlike experiences in flow and esthetic theories, do not require users to become so focused that they lose their awareness of physical reality.’’ ([80], p. 940). At first glance, one might single out emotion as the variable that is most closely aligned with experience because of its affective nature, but since engagement can take different forms—emotional, intellectual, and spiritual [88]—cognitive elements, such as content, promotion, and ease of use, can also contribute in major ways to experience. In particular, we identified content has having the potential to contribute to the four dimensions of experience. Indeed, this criterion includes several facets—relevance, media use, depth and breadth, and current/timely information—that all contribute to a richer experience [3]. While informational and textual content will certainly contribute to education, media use is more tightly connected to user engagement and escapism [102]. Made for the medium is a category composed of ‘‘community, personalization, and refinement’’ [3], and it principally relates to offering a unique and personalized service to online users. As such, embedding this feature in a website can offer users the sensation of having a personal experience. Other studies also support the variables included in engagement. For instance, O’Brien and Toms [80] considered esthetics, emotion, and feedback as relevant attributes of engagement, to which we can add perceived ease of use [53].

Escapism occurs when people leave the reality in which they live in a cognitive and emotional way ([44], p. 101). Escapism is partially addressed in this study because we do not have a specific variable reflecting this dimension. However, we can hypothesize that the emotional features of a website, such as a captivating plot, can provide an occasion for escape. Plot refers to the capacity of a website story line to arouse visitor interest. It allows visitors to feel more engaged and concerned with the website content. ‘‘Clear, simple, and compelling stories and themes are very important. They give users an opportunity to relate to the exhibit content, and lend the exhibit a feeling of credibility and authenticity’’ ([98], p. 145). Therefore, a good plot on a website can positively encourage escapism. Similarly, esthetics can convey a sensation of voyage.

As explained previously, our research takes into account esthetics because of its importance in experiential contexts, such as museums. Ease of use, content, and esthetics help to capture this dimension. While ease of use and content reflect the ergonomic quality of the interface, esthetics reflects its hedonic quality [40]. Indeed, Hassenzahl [40] showed that ergonomic quality (EQ) and hedonic quality (HQ) are two distinct dimensions of esthetics. The first refers to the task-oriented quality of a product, and the second refers to the non-task oriented quality of a product, such as originality and innovativeness.

The dimension of education is surely the dimension that is the least addressed by our research since only the content variable measures cognitive aspects. In Pine and Gilmore’s framework, education refers to a learning experience that not only is absorbed as it occurs but also requires active participation.

Having discussed how the criteria retained for website evaluation adequately cover the main dimensions of experience, we next examine the influence of website design on user reactions. Numerous studies have shown that the efficiency of a website design has positive consequences on behaviors and intentions [16,111]. For instance, Flavian et al. [32] studied the reactions of

Internet users and found that highly usable websites are positively related to trust and loyalty. Therefore, the more people perceive a website to be usable, the more they will demonstrate their loyalty by visiting it frequently. Likewise, Schaupp et al. [95] examined the influence of different success measures on intentions to reuse a website. Their results indicated that the design characteristics of a website (namely, information quality, perceived effectiveness, system quality, and social influence) significantly affect user satisfaction with the website. More recently, Zhang et al. [111] showed that highly usable websites positively influence online relationship quality. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H5. A positive assessment of website design will positively influence attitudes.

## 3.4. Contributions of the research model

In sum, our research incorporates recent theoretical developments related to the importance of the experiential realm in predicting future intentions and behaviors. We leverage this literature by including several experiential variables in our research model. Website design is conceptualized as a secondorder factor, which reflect a ‘‘higher level of abstraction [than] first order factors’’ ([18], p. X). Therefore, the measures of website design are formed by all the items of the MUG constructs and esthetics. Our research model relies on all the constructs of the TPB. ## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Research design

Our theoretical model was tested using a free simulation experiment [34], as previously used in the research of Gefen and Straub [35]. In this experimental methodology, the researcher has relatively less control over the manipulated independent variables and the subjects’ approach to the experimental task [34,99]. Furthermore, the experiment is a free simulation because there are no treatment conditions but rather a simulation to which subjects can freely respond. Thus, the values of the independent variables can vary freely with respect to subjects’ interactions with the system. Generally, free simulation experiments are used when the researcher wants to be as close as possible to the real world. For this study, subjects were stimulated to visit two museum websites.

The independent variables that varied freely with their responses were the established usability variables. We also had less control over the subjects since the experimentation occurred outside the laboratory setting.

## 4.1.1. Stimulus websites

Two museum websites were selected for the free simulations. One is the website of the Quai Branly museum, a French museum dedicated to African, American, Asian, and Oceania arts and history. This website was chosen because of its numerous features and esthetic characteristics.

The other interface was the Atlanta History Center, an American Museum dedicated to the national and regional history of Atlanta, Georgia, USA. This website was a locational choice since we wanted the second website to be more or less equivalent to the Quai Branly website (dedicated to art/history) and to be located in Atlanta. As a result, the Atlanta History Center website appeared to be similar to the Quai Branly website in key ways. We checked the quality of these two website designs and concluded that both websites satisfied the condition of reasonably good design (the description of the evaluation can be found in Appendix B).

## 4.2. Sampling procedures

The sample of this study consisted of 230 college students from two different countries. For the US sample, 97 participants were studying in a large southern university. They were offered course credit for visiting the website of the Atlanta History Center and for filling in the Web questionnaire. Most of the participants were enrolled in business and computer information systems courses. The French sample consisted of 133 students at a French university located in Paris. These subjects were invited to visit the Quai Branly website. The French students were also principally enrolled in business and management courses. Student participation was voluntary, and informed consent forms were collected at the beginning of each session. Furthermore, to ensure that the participants would not be reluctant to physically visit the museum because it was too far away, we selected museums that were located in close proximity to the respective universities. The response rate was 51.46% for the American sample and 44.52% for the French sample.

While our results can be generalized to other college students, this sampling frame appears to be relevant for three reasons. First, museums are increasingly targeting young people to broaden their audience and to find new donators [58]. Second, according to a study published by Pew Internet (2014), young people (18- to 29- year-olds) tend to remain the age group that is most likely to go online. In fact, 97% of this age category goes online. Third, this population is in search of highly sensory experiences and thus is more exigent in terms of user experience.

## 4.3. Research instrument and experimental procedures

The data collection technique was a Web questionnaire with a cross-sectional design [99]. This instrument was developed using existing scales. The MUG items came from Agarwal and Venkatesh [3], but we created a second reflective item for ‘‘promotion’’ since Agarwal and Venkatesh [3] had only a single item for this construct and we wanted to ensure measurement reliability. The ‘‘subjective norms’’ constructs and attitudes were adapted from Fishbein and Ajzen [31]. We took special care in selecting the items that reflect attitudes. Indeed, Ajzen explained that an individual’s attitude toward a behavior has two dimensions:

One component is instrumental [bold added for emphasis] in nature, represented by such adjective pairs as valuable–worthless, and harmful–beneficial. The second component has a more experiential quality and is reflected in such scales as pleasant– unpleasant and enjoyable–unenjoyable. ([5], p. 5)

Since Azjen [5] recommended including both components when measuring attitudes, we kept both the instrumental and experiential dimensions to assess attitudes (see Appendix A). The ‘‘intentions’’ constructs were adapted from Pavlou and Gefen [84]. Esthetics items were originally developed by Lavie and Tractinsky [63]. ‘‘Time resources’’ was adapted from Pavlou and Fygenson [83], while ‘‘prior experience with cultural activities,’’ representing the second dimension of facilitating conditions, was adapted from Bourdieu and Darbel [12]. Most of the constructs were measured with seven-point Likert scales with two exceptions: ‘‘prior experience’’ was assessed with a six-point frequency scale (1 = never, 2 = once a year, 3 = three or four times a year, 4 = once a month, 5 = twice a month, 6 = once a week), and attitudes were assessed via semantic differential scales. Detailed information on the constructs is provided in Appendix A. A pretest with 20 subjects was conducted one month before the launch of the study and led to the reformulation and clarification of some questions.

Using the same technique employed by Agarwal and Venkatesh [3], there were two stages to the data collection. The first part aimed to assess the weight of each usability category. The participants distributed a total of 100 points across the five categories of usability plus the additional category of esthetics. Thus, for this trial, the total was divided by six (rather than five as in Agarwal and Venkatesh [3]). This first part of the data collection gave us insight into the relative importance of each usability category for museum website evaluation. The second part of the instrument dealt with the other research variables. It enabled us to test our hypotheses and to answer our research questions. Furthermore, several questions were included to control for subjects’ general interests in museums and specific interests in museums since there are different types of museums (e.g., art, history, and science).

Once the questionnaire had been developed, we published it on a website dedicated to our study. This website also offered complementary information to participants, such as the informed consent form, some instructions on how to participate in the experiment, and hyperlinks to the museum websites.

The participants were invited to engage with the website as if they were a potential visitor of the museum website or the museum itself. To help them while browsing the website, we suggested the following four principal sections that should be of general interest for museum visitors: Practical Information, Collections, Games/Interactive Activities, and Museum History. These suggested sections existed on both websites, which enabled us to replicate the same scenario with both samples. However, the participants were also free to visit any other sections that could be relevant and that were interesting to them. This autonomy is consistent with the free simulation methodology in which researchers have less control over participant interactions.

## 5. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS 12.0, while the other data analyses were performed with SmartPLS 2.0 [90]. There are several reasons for using Partial Least Squares (PLS) rather than other structural equation modeling (SEM) software, such as covariance-based LISREL. First, our research model included a number of formative constructs. Since PLS facilitates the specification of both reflective and formative constructs, it is better suited for directly testing hypotheses without having to alter the model to address underidentification problems. Gefen et al. [36] recommended a minimal sample size of at least 10 times the number of items in the most complex construct when using PLS. Our most complex construct, which is ‘‘prior experience,’’ had five items, so 50 participants was the minimum sample size required for this study. Our pooled sample comprised 230 participants (97 for the USA and 133 for France), which is well above this minimum. Furthermore, although Goodhue et al. [38] tested the statistical power of PLS with different sample sizes and found that PLS does not necessarily work better with a small sample size, our realized N should have been more than sufficient for our purposes. For instance, we paid attention to the loadings when we analyzed our reflective constructs and to the weights when we analyzed our formative constructs. Similarly, validity and reliability were obtained with different statistical techniques.

## 5.1.2. The analysis of the formative constructs

Formative constructs differ from reflective constructs because they are caused by their items rather than reflected by them [11]. Therefore, the items determine the content of the construct. Furthermore, the items represent different dimensions of the formative constructs. For this reason, in the case of low validity, items cannot be deleted without theoretical justification [85]. Very recently, Petter al. [85] highlighted that few IS researchers have correctly identified the nature of their constructs, which can lead to Type I and Type II errors. As a consequence, Petter et al. [85] proposed a detailed guideline for how to validate and use such constructs. We relied on their suggestions and those of Cenfetelli and Basselier [17] to validate our five formative constructs: content, ease of use, made for the medium, emotion, and prior experience with cultural activities. Moreover, for the formative constructs, we needed to analyze the weights, not the loadings. All items significantly correlated with their corresponding construct, which is a sufficient condition for convergent validity [17].

The sum of these tests indicated that the psychometric properties of our instrument were acceptable and that further testing of the structural model was therefore appropriate.

## 5.2. The second-order construct

Chin et al. [20] suggested the use of a hierarchical component model to test second-order factors. This technique, also called Repeated Indicators Approach [69], entails adding all the measures of the lower-order constructs to the second-order construct. The first-order constructs retain their measures, but ‘‘the manifest indicators are repeated to also represent the higher order construct’’ ([109], p. 791). This is also the most common approach used by researchers across various disciplines to validate secondorder constructs [109]. Since we wanted to determine the factors that most influenced the website evaluation, it is important that we can estimate the weight of each usability criterion. Consequently, in SmartPLS, we modeled the first-order constructs (six in total) and their paths to website evaluation (the second-order construct). These paths do not represent hypotheses to be tested but rather represent the contribution of each first-order construct to the second-order construct.

## 5.3. The structural model

The structural model describes the relationships (paths) between the different latent variables [19]. Hence, in this second step, we examined the path coefficients to determine the significance of the hypotheses. Testing the model, we found a substantive percentage of explained variance for our dependent variables. The explained variances for our dependent variables are as follows: ‘‘Intentions to return to the website’’ had an R<sup>2</sup> of 0.560, and ‘‘Intentions to go to the museum’’ had an $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.508. Therefore, our research model explained more than 50% of variance for the two principal outcome variables. ‘‘Attitudes’’ had an $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.373, which is also well within range.

Fig. 2 shows the significant and insignificant paths in our model. As hypothesized, intentions to return to the website positively influenced intentions to return to the museum, and thus, H1 is confirmed $( \beta = 0 . 3 1 7 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 ) .$ . Attitudes influenced intentions to return to the website $( \beta = 0 . 4 3 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but not intentions to visit the physical museum $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 8 , ~ p > 0 . 0 5 )$ . So, H2a is validated, but H2b is not. Facilitating conditions and subjective norms both influenced the two types of intentions, so H3a, H3b, H4a, and H4b are all verified. Moreover, website evaluation positively influenced attitudes toward the website $( \beta = 0 . 6 1 1$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , so H5 is confirmed as well.

Table 5 summarizes the path coefficients and hypothesis support.

Moreover, we tested the influence of two covariates on our outcome variables. Perceived cost of a museum ticket had no significant effect on intentions to return to the museum website $( \beta = 0 . 0 3 6 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ or intentions to visit the museum $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 8 ,$ p > 0.05). Similarly, preference for a type of museum (art, science, or history) did not have a significant effect on intentions to visit the website $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 2 , p > 0 . 0 5 )$ but did influence intentions to visit the museum $( \beta = 0 . 1 7 6 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ .

## 6. Discussion

Whereas attitudes did predict intentions to return to the website, they did not influence intentions to visit the physical museum. Thus, intentions to visit the museum seem to be mediated by intentions to visit the website. Indeed, a direct relationship exists between intentions to return to the website and intentions to visit the museum. This finding suggests that visitors who have a good experience with a museum website and who intend to return to this website also develop a stronger interest in the museum’s collection. Briefly, these online visitors are more likely to visit the museum in person. In contrast, visitors who enjoyed the website (positive attitudes) but who do not plan to return to the website are less likely to make the effort to visit the museum. Therefore, we can asseverate that well-designed websites do indeed induce visitors to return to the website and arouse their interest to visit the museum. The link between the virtual interface and the physical setting is empirically established, as evidenced by the reifying effect of these outcomes. This statement is consistent with recent research that indicates that the Internet has a reinforcing effect on physical visits [39]. In fact, Griffiths and King [39] showed that the more that people use the Internet generally, the more likely they are to visit in-person museums. Our research also shows that the specific use of the Internet with respect to previously visiting the museum’s website is linked to their intention to visit the museum in person.

Subjective norms and facilitating conditions appear to be two additional predictors of intentions to visit the museum. Indeed, our research confirms the argument of sociology of culture and marketing researchers that subjective norms influence high culture practices [12,24]. We corroborate their argument by highlighting museum visits as a social activity. Since many people visit museums to experience a social interaction, the perceptions of intimate others toward museums are essential. It is noteworthy that the role of subjective norms persists even in the online context. Lahire [62] explained that the most important characteristics of individuals between 12 and 25 years old are the constraints surrounding and influencing their lives. In effect, young people often face and are influenced by both school constraints, parental constraints, and peer constraints.

Facilitating conditions—external circumstances that constrain or facilitate individuals’ actions—also play an important role in determining individuals’ intentions to visit museums remotely or in person. Traditionally, facilitating conditions have been captured in IS research as a formative construct. In the Internet environment, researchers have selected measures such as a good understanding of the Internet and inexpensive access to the Internet to assess facilitating conditions [24]; for the wireless Internet, Lu et al. [96] selected help, specialized instructions, training, legal protections, and corporate policies to assess facilitating conditions. We could have selected factors such as the availability of an Internet connection at home or years of experience with Internet technologies. Nonetheless, we used another measure. Since the public that was surveyed in this study corresponds to young people who are quite familiar with the Internet environment and because museums constitute high culture (see endnote 2), we deemed the factors that have been reported in the museum literature as driving or constraining visitor behaviors to be important. Consequently, instead of assessing prior experience with the Internet, we assessed prior experience with cultural activities and time availability (two concepts that were also tested by Bourdieu and Darbel [16]). Similarly, to assess subjective norms, we aimed to determine the role played by relatives with regard to museum attendance rather than relatives’ opinion regarding Internet usage, as Internet usage is very common today, especially among young people.

Our results indicate that both variables—time resources and cultural background/experience—play a significant role in determining individuals’ intentions to visit a museum or to return to a museum website. More precisely, individuals who have more availability (leisure time) will be able to engage in online visits or in-person visits more easily than people who are very busy. Since visiting museums can be considered a leisure activity, it is reasonable to assume that museum visits are not a priority on people’s agendas and that people who have more free time will be able to devote more time to this type of activity. Regarding cultural experience, visitors who regularly engage in cultural activities are more likely to return to a museum website or to visit a museum in person. Cultural ‘‘omnivores’’ generally seek new cultural activities and opportunities to learn [70]. This group of individuals may also compose the 45% of museum visitors who consume culture both online and offline [50]. However, we could challenge the set of activities that are included in our scale (attendance at theaters, opera, concerts, cinema, monuments, and museums), as these activities are not always relevant for assessing cultural activities. More precisely, DiMaggio and Mukhtar [29] found that arts participation is evolving: ‘‘a change is occurring in the composition of artistic cultural capital in response to societal trends toward multiculturalism and greater inclusivity’’ (p. 190). DiMaggio and Mukhtar [29] argued that some traditional activities are in decline, such as ballet, while new activities, such as computer games and Internet, are increasing. Hence, our scale of experience with cultural activities could be enriched by these elements.

In response to our first research question, the results indicate that esthetics is the single most important variable that influences user experience. Consequently, this study confirms findings from prior research highlighting the importance of esthetics for IS evaluation [40,103,104]. In their work, Valacich et al. [103] explained that ‘‘representational delight’’ (p. 86), which is the esthetic characteristic of an interface, should be the most important goal of hedonic websites. Enhancing user experience by providing entertainment and education is a crucial aim for museums [49]. Consequently, museums should try to meet both functional (education) and hedonic (entertainment) needs. By extension, museum technologies should also support these two dimensions of the visitor experience, and for this reason, they fall into the category of hybrid systems [103]. Moreover, following esthetics, ease of use and content, both of which are cognitive aspects, are the most important variables that influence user experience. Our research highlights that both cognitive and emotional criteria play a role in the assessment of an interface.

## 6.1. Theoretical contributions

This research makes several contributions to research and practice, as indicated in Table 6. First, we apply the theoretical dimensions of the MUG to a new context, namely, museum websites. This research thus contributes to the generalizability of the MUG, as encouraged by Agarwal and Venkatesh [3,106], and provides insight into a new domain. Indeed, most research on website usability has focused on business websites, while specific domains in the cultural heritage sector have only been superficially addressed. We believe that museum websites represent a new technology use context that differs from that of commercial websites. Lee and Baskerville [65] explained that generalizability is important in research because it is what demonstrates the usefulness of a theory. For instance, the more that a theory is validated in different contexts, the more likely the theory is useful in explaining a phenomenon. Lee and Baskerville [65] suggested four different types of generalizability. Our research corresponds to the TE type of generalizability, meaning that we generalize from theoretical statements to empirical statements: ‘‘this involves generalizing a theory, confirmed in one setting, to descriptions of other settings’’ (p. 233).

Furthermore, we examined a new variable that has been overlooked in many MUG conceptualizations, namely, the esthetics of websites. Several studies have suggested the inclusion of this variable for evaluating websites, and our research confirms prior conceptual work by showing that esthetics is as important as usability for potential museum users. In this sense, noting that esthetics is a key factor in the high culture setting, our research also contributes to a cumulative body of research by integrating a new concept into the study of website usability.

Second, our research contributes to research on ‘‘user experience,’’ a prominent stream of research in the IS discipline. O’Brien and Toms [80] asserted that ‘‘successful technologies are not just usable; they engage users’’ (p. 938). Our research supports this assertion by showing that an engaging interface influences behaviors, such as intentions to return to a website or intentions to visit a physical setting. Our research model includes several dimensions that reflect experiential aspects.

## 6.2. Managerial contributions

Our research also has practical and managerial implications for museum directors and website designers. First, in applying the MUG to the museum context, we provide an effective way to evaluate whether museum websites meet visitor expectations. According to the Digicult European Report [28], visitors expect cultural institutions to deploy user-friendly applications, produce simple and accessible information and relevant content, show dynamic artifacts, facilitate interactivity, present full-documented collections in engaging ways, and allow the creation of personal collections. It is noteworthy that these expectations can be addressed through the categories of usability and esthetics that are assessed in our study.

Second, we examined whether high usability of a museum website plays a role in attracting new visitors who have never been to the physical museum or existing visitors who have already been to the physical museum. Museum websites seem to be an effective way of attracting novices to museums. This result also supports the idea proposed by Kotler [58] that ‘‘building well-designed websites is another pathway to museum experiences beyond museum walls’’ (p. 422). Indeed, museum websites represent an alternative for people who may be interested in museological content but who are unable or not willing to visit the physical setting. Hence, museum professionals can use their websites to communicate with their audience. Furthermore, our results may encourage museum website designers to improve the usability and appearance of their online interfaces. If museums want to use the Internet effectively as a medium to broaden their audience, they should try to do more than just provide information on their website. Other elements, such as the use of multimedia, emotional content, or an esthetic interface, tend to influence attitudes and behavior, as suggested by the positive influence of the MUG categories on user intentions. However, the role of museum website may be nuanced, since young people also seem to be influenced by the perceptions of intimate others toward museums. As a result, discount prices for two or more visitors and a free pass for an accompanying person may be effective marketing solutions according to this research. Regardless of the specific marketing campaigns implied by our research, it is clear that museum websites can increase museum attendance, especially in the French and American contexts.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

We have identified three principal limitations to this research. First, although we wanted our research design to reflect reality to the greatest extent possible, the free simulation experiment did not involve any manipulation. Therefore, a true experimental design could be implemented to manipulate the characteristics of the website design and to identify the relative importance of each variable in determining user intentions. Second, although the MUG scales are comprehensive, we acknowledge that they do not cover all the dimensions of experience, particularly the education dimension. Future research should consider the learning aspects of experience since this outcome is important in experiential contexts. Third, our investigation focused on a specific type of museum, namely, two museums dedicated to history and civilization. Therefore, the role played by each design characteristic may differ for other categories of museums, such as science or art museum websites. While the esthetic dimension may still be very important for visitors of art museums, this dimension may play a lesser role in scientific websites. Furthermore, visitors generally have a preference for a specific type of museum, which may influence their intentions to revisit a museum’s website or to visit the museum. For instance, a well-designed website specialized in Greek civilization may have more difficulty in attracting and retaining a visitor interested in modern art. Although the test of the control variable was significant only for intentions to visit the museum, we acknowledge that to more fully capture the visitor experience, it may be necessary to examine the interface of different categories of museums and to compare the visitor experience for each category.

## 7. Conclusion

In conclusion, this research attempts to determine the potential of museum websites for attracting visitors either to return to their websites or to visit physical museums. As noted, website design has been principally studied via usability criteria for commercial websites. Therefore, we decided to focus on an experiential environment, and we included several variables that reflect the dimensions of experience. The era of discovery of how websites influence our leisure activities is beginning, and we offer this research as an initial exploration into how the Internet affects daily life and experience. As high culture icons, museums are an appropriate setting to study how the Web grabs the attention of the public, both natives and tourists.

Esthetics appeared as the most important design criteria for museum experiential interfaces. Since an increasing number of museums are developing applications, games, and content for mobile technologies (i.e., mobile phones, tablet PCs, MP3 players), our research findings can be generalized to this context as well. For instance, the Quai Branly museum (analyzed in this research) launched a mobile application that provides visitors with an interactive map of the museum, a calendar of events, videos, suggestions of visits, and postcards to share with relatives. Previous research has shown that design esthetics should be considered in the mobile domain as well since design esthetics predicts loyalty to mobile services [23]. Users will be more likely to reuse a mobile application and to download new versions and releases of the service if they like the esthetics of the application. This association was found in an e-commerce context, so the chances that it applies in an experiential environment are high considering that prior research has already shown the importance of esthetics and hedonic quality in experiential settings.

Museum websites are currently mainly serving as an information source or as a communication channel for potential visitors to find information about physical museums. Thus, the design of the museum websites should aim to facilitate (i.e., reinforce) users intentions to visits the physical sites. One could argue that the role of museum websites differs from that of typical e-commerce websites, which also serve the role of enabling transactions—i.e., purchasing goods and services online. However, most retailers adopt a multichannel strategy by offering their customers multiple transaction channels to generate more revenue. Therefore, online visitors represent potential customers to be attracted to physical stores. Regarding the marketing approach, Chiou et al. [21] notes that ‘‘the typica web visitor is not merely a web user but also a potential customer who needs product information, attractive promotional activities, convenient order process, and after-sales services’’ (p. 286). Website usability has the potential to influence not only online relationship quality [111] but also offline relationships between vendors and their customers. Notably, the alignment between website design and an organization’s strategy is crucial, and our research offers insight into the type of relationship that may exist between online and physical visits. Our findings can be generalized to any context in which establishing and maintaining a link between a website and a physical site represent the strategic goal. For instance, tourism organizations and travel professionals provide substantial content (e.g., videos, pictures, maps) on the Web, but their ultimate goal is to attract more visitors and to arouse their interest for traveling and visiting the setting. In commercial contexts, it is also very important for companies to attract online visitors to the physical store simply because research indicates that customers spend more money in brick-and-mortar stores than online [105]. Furthermore, offline performance (firm reputation, customer loyalty, and satisfaction) tend to affect online performance. In conclusion, having a holistic view is the key to success on the web: the online strategy needs to be aligned with brick-and-mortar strategy and vice versa. Nonetheless, research on multichannel strategies and consumer reactions is still scant [56]. Future research should therefore investigate how to better integrate websites in a global strategy and analyze user expectations regarding multichannel experiences.
