# Scarcity Strategy in Crowdfunding: An Empirical Exploration of Reward Limits

Abstract. Scarcity-based marketing strategies have been widely embraced to communicate the potential unavailability of an item to increase sales. Recently, a similar practice has been prevalently implemented in reward-based crowdfunding in the form of reward limits, whereby campaign creators restrict the number of backers for each reward tier. Whereas most current research has focused on the design strategies of crowdfunding campaigns, less attention has been paid to how campaign design implicates backer-as-consumer needs in reward-based crowdfunding. Drawing on the marketing literature regarding product scarcity and consumer psychology, the current study strives to fill this void by uncovering the effect of reward limits on eventual and concurrent funding performance. Specifically, we performed campaign-level and campaign day–level analyses on a unique data set from a dominant crowdfunding platform. At the campaign level, we determined that setting reward limits at the beginning of a campaign is beneficial, with the number of limited reward tiers exerting an inverted-U-shaped relationship with campaign performance. Further exploration of different forms of reward limits suggests that the scarcity effects largely originate from limited edition and price discount rewards. At the campaign day level, we determined that incorporating new limited edition rewards is helpful for attracting new contributions, whereas depleted price discount rewards will demotivate subsequent contributions. In addition, the goal-directed mechanism in crowdfunding plays an influential role in shaping scarcity effects. Our findings highlight the importance of considering backer-as-consumer needs in campaign design and the dynamics of campaign attribute changes in the fundraising process. The goal-directed mechanism in crowdfunding allows us to add further insights to the marketing literature on product scarcity by considering a noneconomic component of the scarcity effect. Our findings also provide important practical implications for platform operators, campaign creators, and marketers.

## 1. Introduction

Given the challenges faced by entrepreneurs in sourcing financial capital from banks, angel investors, and venture capitalists, many entrepreneurs have recently resorted to online crowdfunding to get their innovative ideas to market (Burtch et al. 2015, Mollick and Nanda 2016, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017). Online crowdfunding, which supports financing an initiative by directly tapping the general public, has garnered substantial attention from both researchers and practitioners. Among the various crowdfunding models (e.g., reward, equity, lending, and donation based), reward-based crowdfunding is a unique fundraising approach that allows entrepreneurs to pitch their product ideas to consumers who may back the campaigns by providing funds in exchange for some nonfinancial returns (Allison et al. 2017, Chan and Parhankangas 2017). With many highly successful campaigns achieving fundraising goals in the millions of dollars (see Pesce 2017), reward-based crowdfunding has become the fundraising mechanism of choice for many aspiring entrepreneurs (Belleflamme et al. 2014, Drover et al. 2017).

In reward-based crowdfunding (henceforth, simply crowdfunding), investors (i.e., backers) behave as typical consumers in that they expect to receive some nonfinancial reward (rather than equity in the venture) in exchange for their monetary contribution to a crowdfunding campaign (Tomczak and Brem 2013, Lin et al. 2014). Considering backers’ traits as consumers, campaign creators instill various marketing strategies when designing their campaigns to more effectively attract backers (Wang et al. 2016). One practice that has become increasingly prevalent is the use of reward limits,<sup>1</sup> which involves campaign creators intentionally constraining the number of backers in reward tiers with the aim of engendering a sense of urgency via scarcity. Reward limits are typically used for rewards that are associated with certain extra benefits; for example, common benefits include access to a unique limited edition (LE) product, price discount (PD) privileges, or early reward (ER) delivery.

However, whether such a practice is effective remains unclear because the existing literature fails to provide a definitive answer regarding whether employing the scarcity strategy ultimately boosts or hurts crowdfunding campaigns. On the one hand, commodity theory posits that “any commodity will be valued to the extent it is unavailable” (Brock 1968, p. 246), which suggests that when scarcity threatens a consumer’s opportunity to obtain a product, he or she will evaluate the product more positively (Amaldoss and Jain 2008) and consequently display productseeking behaviors. Accordingly, reward limits in crowdfunding evoke a sense of urgency among prospective backers to support a campaign. On the other hand, frustration theory suggests that when the obtainment of a product is constrained, the unavailability of the product will result in a frustration response (Oruc 2015). Because reward limits constrain the number of backers that are able to contribute to a campaign with extra benefits as a return, potential backers will likely respond negatively if limited reward tiers become depleted and wil have a lower willingness to contribute to the campaign. Therefore, the overall effects of limited rewards on crowdfunding performance remain enigmatic.

To further complicate this situation, the effectiveness of reward limits may depend on how extensively they are used in a campaign. Because of the multitier design typical of campaign rewards, backers tend to compare reward tiers with limits to those without; therefore, how backers evaluate a scarcity message could be affected by the extent to which this practice is used in a campaign (Wathieu et al. 2004). Furthermore, crowdfunding is characterized by a goaldirected mechanism wherein the obtainment of rewards is contingent on the success of the campaign (Hu et al. 2015). In addition to their role as consumers, backers also assume the role of campaign supporters that evaluate a crowdfunding campaign based on its concurrent performance, with their contributions depending on whether the campaign is successful or not. As a result, the extent of reward limits and the goal-directed mechanism in crowdfunding can further complicate the theoretical tension arising from the scarcity strategy.

To resolve these tensions and provide actionable guidance to crowdfunding campaign creators, the current study empirically explores the role of the scarcity strategy in crowdfunding. Specifically, we aim to examine (1) the overall effectiveness of the scarcity strategy (i.e., setting reward limits) on crowdfunding campaign performance and (2) how to effectively set reward limits to increase campaign performance. Furthermore, because the status of limited reward tiers (i.e., adding new tiers with limits versus having existing tiers depleted) could vary over the course of fundraising, we explore (3) how changes in reward limit status influence subsequent funding performance (i.e., funding speed) and how the crowdfunding mechanism shapes such influences. To uncover the underlying mechanisms for each of these questions, we also consider the effects of different forms of reward limits (i.e., limited edition, price discount, and early access)

We employ a comprehensive data set from Kickstarter.com, one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms. To collect the relevant data we tracked all live campaigns on the platform on a daily basis over seven months. Using this data set, we performed both campaign-level and campaign day–level analyses to answer our research questions.

In the campaign-level analysis, we examine the overall effectiveness and the effective setting of reward limits given the aforementioned theoretical puzzle. We address potential endogeneity concerns by using initial campaign status, implementing propensity score matching (PSM), and the construction of a sample selection model. We note an overall positive effect of incorporating reward limits at the beginning of a campaign on eventual crowdfunding performance. Notably, the proportion/number of limited reward tiers exerts an inverted-U-shaped effect on eventual funding performance. These relationships primarily stem from limited edition and price discount rewards.

In the campaign day–level analysis, we construct a two-way fixed effects panel model to examine the dynamics of reward limit status over the course of fundraising. The results suggest that introducing new limited reward tiers increases subsequent funding performance while having limited reward tiers depleted decreases subsequent funding performance by 3.6% and 5.1% per limited reward tier, respectively. The positive effect is due to newly added limited edition rewards, whereas the negative effect stems from the depletion of price discount rewards. The results also suggest that the goal-directed mechanism serves a moderating role in the dynamics of reward limit status. Taken together, we not only demonstrate the overall positive influence of reward limits but also identify both the positive and negative effects of limited rewards. Our analyses on different forms of reward limits help shed light on the underlying mechanisms of the scarcity strategy effects.

This study contributes to the crowdfunding literature in particular and to the marketing literature on product scarcity more generally. We extend the boundary of the crowdfunding literature by highlighting the importance of considering backer-asconsumer needs in addition to backer-as-supporter goals in crowdfunding campaign design, and by delving into the dynamics of campaign attribute changes in the fundraising process. More generally, our empirical exploration of the effect of reward limits and the role of the goal-directed mechanism enables us to contribute new insights to the product scarcity literature. In crowdfunding, the goal-directed mechanism offers an exemplary context where the scarcity strategy is employed alongside a behavioral/ psychological component. Notably, our investigation of this mechanism indicates a potential role for noneconomic factors (e.g., intention to help a campaign succeed) motivating consumption behavior in shaping the effectiveness of the scarcity strategy. Beyond theory, our findings also have important practical implications for platform operators, campaign creators, and marketers in general.

## 2. Related Literature

## 2.1. Reward-Based Crowdfunding

Reward-based crowdfunding has received much attention among information systems researchers in recent years. Given our present focus on the role of the scarcity strategy in crowdfunding campaigns, the stream of research surrounding the relationship between campaign design and crowdfunding performance is particularly relevant. Existing crowdfunding research has examined multiple aspects of campaign design (Short et al. 2017). The effects of campaign description (Allison et al. 2017, Chan and Parhankangas 2017), videopitch content (Parhankangas and Renko 2017), dynamic goal setting (Li and Jarvenpaa 2016), and reward scheme design (Xiao et al. 2014, 2017; Hu et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2017) have been investigated to date. For instance, Li and Jarvenpaa (2016) found that using dynamic goals in a crowdfunding campaign is associated with higher fundraising performance—especially when the level of community feedback is high. Moreover, with respect to reward tiers, Hu et al. (2015) showed that designing reward tiers with lower product prices may not be attractive to backers, whereas Xiao et al. (2014) showed that campaign creators tend to achieve higher fundraising performance when they set fewer reward tiers and higher maximum backing prices.

Collectively, this stream of research acknowledges the importance of campaign design in crowdfunding. However, there appear to be two gaps in the literature. First, although backers are more like typical consumers than investors in reward-based crowdfunding (Lin et al. 2014, Chan and Parhankangas 2017), research on how creators could design their campaign to fulfill the needs of backers as consumers remains limited. Specifically, because backers expect to receive rewards—typically in some tangible form $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ a preordered product)—in lieu of equity in exchange for their contributions in crowdfunded campaigns, it is imperative for campaign creators to consider backers’ traits as consumers when designing a campaign. In other words, campaign design should consider backers’ consumer needs (e.g., a feeling of exclusivity from possessing a product or having gotten a good deal) to better appeal to backers and subsequently result in better crowdfunding performance. Second, prior studies have largely focused on the influence of campaign attributes on the fundraising outcomes in a static manner, such as the structure of a reward scheme at the conclusion of a campaign. This is potentially because of the limitations of post hoc data collection, which may not allow researchers to observe changes in campaign attributes over the course of fundraising. However, crowdfunding platforms do allow for updates to a campaign, and many creators adjust campaign attributes to boost fundraising outcomes. Such adjustments may result in temporary changes in funding speed, which may not be inferred from final fundraising performance.

Accordingly, the present study contributes to the crowdfunding literature by closing the two aforementioned gaps. Specifically, the use of reward limits is a crucial aspect of campaign reward schemes, whereas reward schemes are the primary channel through which campaign creators convey the returns to supporting a crowdfunding campaign to backers (i.e., backers as consumers; Wang et al. 2016). Exploring the use of reward limits emphasizes the benefits that campaign creators could offer to backers. Furthermore, examining changes to reward limit status offers a dynamic view of the fundraising process.

## 2.2. Scarcity-Based Marketing Strategy

Considering the consumer traits of backers and the marketing strategies adopted by campaign creators, another body of literature related to the present study is research on scarcity-based marketing strategies. Product scarcity refers to an appeal that communicates a potential or certain unavailability of an item in the near future along with the availability of the item in the present (Oruc 2015).<sup>2</sup> Using such a strategy, marketers instigate a sense of scarcity to consumers in various manners. Griskevicius et al. (2009) distinguish between limited opportunity scarcity and distinctiveness scarcity: Limited opportunity conveys a limit on the opportunity to obtain a product, whereas distinctiveness scarcity is based on a special offering that conveys uniqueness and leads a consumer to be distinctive. Others further distinguish between limited quantity and limited time scarcity (Cialdini 2009, Jang et al. 2015)—limited quantity scarcity is based on a constraint on the number of offerings, whereas limited time scarcity concerns a constraint on the duration of offering a product. Overall, we can surmise that scarcity appeals can take on multiple forms (e.g., carrying distinctiveness or not) depending on the design of the product offering that conveys this scarcity information.

Scarcity appeals are frequently used by marketers to induce consumers to perceive a product to be more desirable (Balachander et al. 2009). Regarding the scarcity effect, marketing research notes the merits of introducing scarcity appeals. Based on commodity theory (Brock 1968, p. 246), “any commodity will be valued to the extent it is unavailable.” This implies that consumers tend to prefer products that are less commonly available (Ross et al. 1976, Amaldoss and Jain 2008) and that are more difficult to obtain, wherein the difficulty can be manifested as limits in the quantity or delays in the supply thereof. In consumer psychology, a potential explanation is that consumers tend to perceive a scarce product to be of higher quality than it actually is (Stock and Balachander 2005, Balachander et al. 2009). Owning scarce products cultivates a sense of being chosen (Brock and Mazzocco 2004) or a sense of bandwagon reasoning<sup>3</sup> (Worchel et al. 1975). Overall, the literature recognizes that consumers tend to evaluate a product more positively in the presence of scarcity, as such scarcity is likely to increase the desirability of a product. Conversely, frustration theory proposes that when the obtainment of a product is constrained, its absence will lead to a frustration response. In other words, consumers tend to devalue a product when their opportunity to obtain the product is eliminated; therefore, a product’s offering status changing from available to unavailable is likely to make consumers feel frustrated, which can cause them to evaluate the product negatively (Oruc 2015).

By drawing on insights from the marketing literature on product scarcity, we conceptualize multiple forms of reward limits in crowdfunding and build arguments concerning their effects. Meanwhile, we recognize that reward limits in crowdfunding are characterized by the following distinctive features such that results from prior research (in traditional selling contexts) may not be readily generalizable. First, in crowdfunding, there is still uncertainty with respect to the actual obtainment of the scarce product even if it is available. In traditional selling contexts, even if a product exhibits scarcity (e.g., only a limited number of units are available for purchase), consumers can obtain the product with certainty if they purchase it. However, in crowdfunding, a backer will only be able to obtain the product if she has backed the campaign and the campaign’s fundraising is successful. In other words, apart from the rewards they will receive, backers in crowdfunding must also consider how their contributions affect the success of the campaign (Hu et al. 2015). The reward limits in such a scenario may play a different role compared with product scarcity in traditional purchasing contexts, as backer contributions are not only driven by limited rewards but are also affected by the concurrent performance of a crowdfunding campaign at the time the backer decides to commit and pledge to the campaign.

Second, crowdfunding campaigns typically offer a set of reward tiers, which could each depict different rewards, different versions of the same reward, or the same reward at different prices (Wang et al. 2016) Meanwhile, all of the reward tiers can be observed by potential backers when making decisions, regardless of their status. Such a design using multiple reward tiers affords the comparison of rewards within a crowdfunding campaign, which likely prompts backers to determine their contributions based on a frame of reference (i.e., what rewards have been chosen in the past or might be chosen by other backers in the future). Knowing the information of all of the rewards is likely to influence the evaluation of backers’ own contributions (e.g., whether the act of backing can satisfy their desire for distinctiveness). However, such reference information is not readily available in traditional selling contexts, where consumers are typically only given the choices that are available at the time.

Third, limited rewards in crowdfunding are constrained by both product quantity and the predetermined campaign duration, combining aspects of both limited quantity and limited time scarcity (Cialdini 2009). However, as discussed in the literature, traditional limited products typically carry only one type of scarcity message (Jang et al. 2015). These distinctive features provide the opportunity to extend the product scarcity literature by exploring how scarcity effects work in the context of online crowdfunding.

## 3. Reward Limits in Crowdfunding

In reward-based crowdfunding, campaign creators specify a set of reward tiers that convey the returns backers will receive by supporting the campaign at various pledge levels. Rewards are typically items produced by the campaign itself, such as a comic book, a music album, or a ticket for a theater performance.<sup>4</sup> Each reward tier includes a description of the item, the minimum pledge amount (akin to the item’s purchase price), and the estimated delivery date.

An interesting aspect of reward tiers is that campaign creators can set a limit on the quantity of a particular reward, such that only a finite number of backers can choose the reward tier when contributing to the campaign. When the total number of backers choosing a limited reward reaches its limit, the reward is marked “no longer available” to indicate its sold-out status, resulting in subsequent backers no longer being able to select it. Typically, reward limits can be used to convey different benefits: limited edition, price discount, or early access. A limited edition reward refers to a special version of the deliverable from the campaign that typically includes some additional features that the regular version does not have. A price discount reward offers the item at a lower price (i.e., lower pledge amount required) compared with other regular reward tiers of the same item. Early access rewards offer backers the opportunity to obtain/enjoy the item ahead of time (e.g., before the delivery time of other reward tiers or before the official release of the item). Table 1 presents typical examples of the three forms of reward limits. It is worth noting that the three forms of reward limits are not mutually exclusive (e.g., a limited edition reward could also be obtained at a discounted price).

Another distinctive feature of campaign rewards is that the reward scheme can actually be adjusted over the course of the fundraising campaign. As such, creators may add new or remove existing reward tiers (including the tiers with reward limits) and change the quantity specified in limited reward tiers so that the status of the reward scheme may change dynamically during the fundraising campaign.

## 3.1. The Overall Effect of Reward Limits

Similar to product sellers in the traditional context, campaign creators in crowdfunding convey scarcity messages through multiple forms of reward limits, including limited edition, price discount, and early access rewards. Based on the product scarcity literature, we expect that limited edition rewards satisfy backers’ desire to be exclusive and unique (Snyder and Fromkin 1980, Brown 2001, Balachander and Stock 2009) and/or signal a higher quality of the limited rewards to prospective backers (Stock and Balachander 2005, Amaldoss and Jain 2010). Price discounts and early access rewards provide economic value to backers in terms of cost savings and time benefits (Sheth et al. 1991), and such economic benefits will increase the attractiveness of a reward (Wathieu et al. 2004). Following the aforementioned mechanisms, reward limits may increase backers’ preference for these rewards and subsequently evoke a sense of urgency to support the crowdfunding campaign by selecting the limited rewards. In this regard, using reward limits in a reward scheme would have a positive effect on campaign performance.

However, reward limits may also constrain backing opportunities for incoming backers, which may ultimately hurt crowdfunding performance. As discussed previously, frustration theory suggests that setting reward limits constrains the opportunities available for supporting the campaign with certain benefits, and backers tend to devalue the campaign when the limited reward tiers have been depleted (Oruc 2015). Specifically, backers naturally prefer to obtain a reward with additional value (Hu et al. 2015). The existence of the reward limits increases the salience of the uniqueness (for limited edition rewards) or the economic benefits (for price discount and early access rewards; Wathieu et al. 2004), and backers will typically regard limited rewards as their preferred reward type. Therefore, the depletion of such limited rewards can lead to frustration among backers, which will subsequently reduce their willingness to contribute to the campaign. From this perspective, using reward limits may create only limited positive impacts and may ultimately harm campaign performance if backers who cannot choose limited rewards are less likely to contribute. Based on the two opposing predictions, the overall effect of employing reward limits on campaign performance remains unclear and requires empirical examination.

Regarding the effective setting of reward limits, the multitier design of the reward scheme prompts backers to compare across reward tiers. As discussed previously, backers tend to evaluate their contribution based on a frame of reference (i.e., what rewards have been or might be chosen). Having reward tiers with limits engenders distinctiveness (for limited edition rewards) and economic benefits (for price discount and early access rewards) for backers, especially when they compare reward tiers with limits to those without (Wathieu et al. 2004). Nevertheless, having a large number of limited edition rewards may actually dilute the perception of uniqueness because backers are likely to build their reference set on other rewards providing similar exclusivity, thus resulting in diminishment of the relative uniqueness they could obtain. Similarly, having an excessive number of price discounts or early access rewards could reduce backers’ perception of superior economic benefits, as these limited price discount or early access rewards—rather than other rewards without a price discount or early access—will serve as the reference for comparison. Hence, in terms of the effective setting of reward limits, we contend that the number of limited reward tiers must be properly considered to achieve superior campaign performance (i.e., setting limited reward tiers will be beneficial, though setting too many limited reward tiers may backfire). This suggests an inverted-U-shaped relationship between the number of limited rewards and campaign performance.

## 3.2. The Dynamic Effect of Reward Limit Status

The dynamic nature of fundraising allows campaign creators to adjust the reward scheme of a campaign during the fundraising process. The status of a reward scheme may also change over time when new reward limits are introduced or as incoming backers contribute to a campaign. We suggest that the varying availability of reward limits will exert an effect on the concurrent fundraising performance of a campaign. Our previous arguments suggest that introducing limited reward tiers will satisfy backers’ desire for uniqueness and exclusivity (Amaldoss and Jain 2005, Balachander and Stock 2009) and increase the potential savings for choosing the rewards (Biswas and Blair 1991); however, the depletion of reward limits may evoke frustration (Verhallen and Robben 1995, Oruc 2015). Namely, when new reward limits are introduced to a campaign or when existing reward tiers are no longer available, backers’ evaluations of the campaign could be enhanced or deflated, which would then either encourage or demotivate subsequent contributions, respectively. Therefore, the varying status of reward limits will likely lead to temporary acceleration or deceleration in funding speed.

As a result, from a dynamic perspective, both the positive and negative effects of the scarcity strategy are expected to occur during the course of a fundraising campaign. Specifically, introducing new limited rewards brings additional exclusive values or economic benefits for backers to obtain rewards. Such rewards will make a campaign more desirable and attract additional subsequent contributions. In contrast, when limited rewards become depleted, backers will feel frustrated about being unable to obtain the rewards, thus causing them to set these depleted rewards as a reference in their decision making. By anchoring on these rewards, they will perceive other rewards—and consequently, the project overall—as less attractive and will be less likely to contribute.

Additionally, we argue that the mechanism of crowdfunding likely plays a role in shaping the scarcity effects. Specifically, crowdfunding is char acterized by a goal-directed “all-or-nothing” mechanism wherein the campaign creators are able to collect the funds pledged only when the funds raised surpass the funding goal; consequently, backers are able to collect the rewards for their contributions only when the funding goals are met (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017). Such a crowdfunding mechanism also enables backers to play dual roles as both supporters and consumers, which means that backers will also consider the campaign’s concurrent fundraising performance in their decision making. In this regard, it is likely that backers with a stronger intention to acquire specific rewards will develop a more positive evaluation of a campaign (Hu et al. 2015), and, as a result, they will be more willing to contribute to it (through limited or nonlimited rewards) when it is underperforming. The stronger intention to contribute has the potential to overcome the frustration of observing depleted rewards, as backers would value making the campaign successful and obtaining the reward more than obtaining the limited reward. Hence, we expect that the goal-directed mechanism in crowdfunding will affect backer decisions and thereby moderate the dynamic effects of reward limit status on subsequent contributions. Taken together in considering the dynamic effects of reward limit sta tus, we contend that the varying availability of reward limits and the goal-directed mechanism will factor into concurrent fundraising performance. In partic ular, the negative effect of depleted limited rewards can be compensated by the stronger intention to help from backers when a campaign is underperforming

## 4. The Empirical Study

We adopt an exploratory approach to answer our research questions. Following theoretical discussions, our empirical analyses aim to investigate the effect of reward limits in crowdfunding from three aspects: (1) the overall effectiveness of reward limits on fundraising outcomes, (2) the influence of the number of limited reward tiers on crowdfunding outcomes, and (3) the dynamic effect of reward limit status on concurrent fundraising performance and the role of the goal-directed crowdfunding mechanism in this effect over the course of fundraising. Alongside these aggregate effects, we also examine the three typical forms of reward limits (i.e., limited edition, price discount, and early access rewards) to better understand the possible underlying mechanisms of the scarcity effect.

## 4.1. Data

Our empirical data were retrieved from Kickstarter.com, one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms currently in operation. Similar to other crowdfunding platforms, such as Indiegogo and JD Crowdfunding, Kickstarter campaign creators can set limits on reward tiers when launching their campaigns. They may also make changes before the completion of a campaign. This allows us to investigate both the effectiveness of setting reward limits and the dynamic effects of reward limit status. To capture the changes in reward limit status, we collected snapshots of all live campaigns on Kickstarter every day at 11:00 p.m. (EST) from June 2015 to January 2016. Specifically, we collected all campaign details (e.g., funding goal, duration, description) and information about reward tiers in each campaign (e.g., the minimum pledge amount for each reward tier, the number of backers in each reward tier, whether a reward limit was set, the status for limited rewards). Our data set contains 48,862 campaigns in all 15 categories.<sup>5</sup> We excluded campaigns with incomplete daily information (i.e., left or right censored because of our data collection window) from our analysis. This resulted in a final sample of 31,919 campaigns with complete daily snapshots.

Among all of the campaigns in our sample, onethird of them (33.3%) were successfully funded. Notably, over half of the campaigns (58%) offered at least one limited reward tier, thus suggesting that setting reward limits is indeed a common practice in crowdfunding. Moreover, among the campaigns that set reward limits, nearly all (>95%) implemented them at the start of the campaign. On average, campaigns in our data set have 6.77 reward tiers, of which 2.28 have limits. These observations support the significance of investigating reward limits in crowdfunding and the adequacy of our data set for such analysis. ## 4.2. Campaign-Level Analysis

4.2.1. Model Speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. For the campaign-level analysis, we seek to answer our first two research questions by investigating the aggregate effects of reward limits on crowdfunding outcomes and how different forms of reward limits factor into such effects. Specifically, we first assess the overall impact of incorporating reward limits on campaign performance and then examine the effective setting of reward limits by considering the proper number of limited reward tiers.

4.2.1.1. The Effects of Reward Limits. To assess the effectiveness of incorporating reward limits, we construct a set of campaign-level econometric models with various measures of campaign performance as the dependent variables (DVs). Following prior crowdfunding studies (Mollick 2014, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Parhankangas and Renko 2017), we adopt the variables Success (whether the funding goal was reached), Pledged (amount of funds received in USD), and Backers (number of backers supporting the campaign) at the end of campaign to measure campaign performance. Apart from these commonly used measures for campaign performance, we also include DaysToSuccess (the number of days to reach the funding goal if a campaign was successfully funded, or total campaign duration if a campaign was not successfully funded). Because crowdfunding campaigns can continue to receive contributions from backers even after the funding goal has been reached, campaign creators who achieve their funding goals earlier will have more room to set higher-level goals (Li and Jarvenpaa 2016). Earlier goal attainment also reduces the uncertainty of contributions from potential backers. Thus, this measure not only captures overall campaign performance, but also provides important implications for understanding the fundraising process and momentum therein.

In this set of analyses, our key independent variable is HasLimit, an indicator variable coded as 1 if a campaign has at least one limited reward tier in its reward scheme and 0 otherwise. To account for other confounding factors related to both the decision to incorporate reward limits and fundraising outcomes, we incorporate a series of control variables including Goal (the campaign’s funding goal in USD), Image-Count (the number of images in the campaign’s story), HasVideo (whether a video is provided), Campaign-Duration (the prespecified duration of the campaign in days), NumReward (the total number of reward tiers), AvgRewardPrice (the average price in USD across all reward tiers), NumCreated (the number of past campaigns launched by the creator), and NumBacked (the number of past campaigns backed by the creator; Yang and Hahn 2017). We also include category, year, and month as fixed effects in all models.

We apply different specifications in our analyses to estimate the scarcity effects depending on the nature of the dependent variable. A logit model is used for Success, whereas ordinary least squares (OLS) is applied for log-transformed Pledged and Backers. Given its nature of “time to event,” we employ the proportional hazards model for DaysToSuccess<sup>6</sup> (Helsen and Schmittlein 1993). We treat the event of becoming successful (i.e., achieving the funding goal, which is operationalized by the number of days a campaign takes to achieve a funding amount that is equal to or greater than the predetermined funding goal) as a “failure” event in survival analysis and estimate a survival model with right-censored duration (because unsuccessful campaigns do not experience the failure event over the window).

Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Strategy. Although we control for a series of campaign-level variables to account for confounding factors, the models may be subject to potential endogeneity issues such as reverse causality (e.g., creators may change or add limited rewards during the fundraising process) and unobserved decision making related to reward limits. In addition to the regression analyses, we implement two approaches for identification to establish the causal effect of incorporating reward limits.

First, in the regression models, we construct all of the independent variables using the snapshot of the first day of each campaign. This helps us rule out the reverse effects caused by possible changes in reward limits in the fundraising process. Second, we apply PSM (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983, Heckman et al. 1998) to construct a control group of campaigns that did not incorporate reward limits but are similar to the campaigns in the treatment group that have reward limits (Rubin 2005). The matching approach enables us to capture the potential intention of incorporating reward limits and control potential confounding factors in a more rigorous manner. Specifically, we regard HasLimit as a treatment and estimate the propensity score with a probit model by including a series of factors that affect not only the treatment but also the campaign outcomes (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). Empirically, we include all control variables used in the regression models to estimate the propensity score and select campaigns for inclusion in the control group using a caliper (0.01). In addition to PSM, we further consider the matching of covariates (instead of relying solely on the propensity score) and employ Mahalanobis distance matching, which uses all of the key covariates to match and select the control group (Rubin and Thomas 2000) to assess the robustness of the results.<sup>7</sup> All four dependent variables<sup>8</sup> in the regression analysis are used to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT; Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008).

4.2.1.2. Effective Reward Limit Setting. To investigate the most effective methods of setting reward limits, we explore how limited reward tiers should be offered in a reward scheme. We consider the variable Limit-Proportion, which captures the proportion of limited reward tiers across all reward tiers in a campaign. As discussed in our theoretical arguments, although limited rewards could encourage more contributions through the scarcity effect, overuse of limited rewards likely reduces the perception of uniqueness and economic benefits. Therefore, to allow for nonlinearity, we include both the linear and quadratic terms of Limit-Proportion in the regression models. Because the model is specified based on the decision to include reward limits to examine how to effectively set limited rewards, certain sample selection issues may exist (i.e., campaigns with reward limits could be substantively different from those without). To account for potential sample selection bias, we build a two-stage selection model in our estimation (Heckman 1979):

$$
\begin{array}{r} \operatorname * {P r} (H a s L i m i t = 1) = \Phi (Z \gamma + u), \\ O u t c o m e = \Omega (X \beta + \lambda + \varepsilon). \end{array}
$$

In the two-stage model, the first-stage equation estimates a probit model with a set of additional identification variables, whereas the second-stage equation incorporates the correction term (λ) derived from the first stage estimation to account for selection bias. To meet the exclusion restriction condition (Wooldridge 2010), in addition to all covariates in the previous models, we introduce three additional variables in the selection equation that may affect the decision to incorporate reward limits to a campaign without affecting the crowdfunding outcomes: AvgDelivery (average estimated duration, in months, to deliver the rewards), DaysToLaunch (difference in days between the launch date and the creation date of a campaign), and OtherLimit (number of campaigns launched within one week before the focal campaign and having incorporated reward limits). These variables will likely affect the campaign creator’s decision to use reward limits but should not have direct effects on the crowdfunding outcomes.<sup>9</sup> Their explanatory power in the probit model will reduce the potential collinearity between the correction term and the independent variables in the second stage while helping to better capture the selection process (Certo et al. 2016).

In the second stage, we incorporate the correction term for selection bias with the reward limit proportion terms as well as all control variables and dummies. Furthermore, the selection model specification enables us to account for an additional factor related to limited rewards: the price of the limited rewards. We construct the variable LimitRewardPrice, which is operationalized as the average price of limited reward tiers in a reward scheme in the secondstage equation.

To estimate the selection model, we derive different estimation approaches depending on the nature of the dependent variables and model specification in the second stage. For the nonlinear model cases (i.e., Success and DaysToSuccess as dependent variables), we specify the probit model and proportional hazards model with Weibull distribution, re spectively, for the second-stage equation and adopt the full-information maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) approach. We follow Van de Ven and Van Praag (1981) and Boehmke et al. (2006) to derive the likelihood function for the probit and duration models with sample selection and then estimate the two-stage equations simultaneously with maximum likelihood estimation. The right censoring in the duration model is also incorporated in the likelihood function. For the linear model cases (i.e., Pledged and Backers as dependent variables), we directly estimate the Heckman selection model with the inclusion of the inverse Mills ratio in the second stage (i.e., the Heckman two-step approach).

4.2.1.3. Different Forms of Reward Limits. As noted previously, three typical forms of reward limits are commonly used: limited edition, price discount, and early access. Our earlier theoretical discussion suggests that the three forms of reward limits may induce different mechanisms of the scarcity effect in crowdfunding. To compare the mechanisms and forms of reward limits, we explore how different forms of reward limits play a role in the effectiveness of reward limits and the effective setting of reward limits.

By analyzing the reward descriptions of limited rewards, we are able to classify reward limits into the three typical forms. Specifically, we first identify a set of keywords that are commonly used for each form of reward limit.<sup>10</sup> These keywords are then matched in the descriptions of the limited rewards to classify them into the three forms. To validate the keywordbased classification procedure, we compare it with manual classification using two independent coders. The validation suggests that our keyword-based approach could accurately classify limited rewards into the three typical forms (96.1% agreement between coders and the keyword-based approach, with Cohen’s κ = 0.94, on average). Table 4 presents the keyword examples used to identify each form of reward limit (the details of the classification validation are presented in Online Appendix A).

As shown in Table 4, the most common form of reward limit is the limited edition reward (62.3%), followed by price discount (27.1%), and then early access (15.1%). Apart from the three major forms, there is a small set of limited rewards (<10%) that do not fit any of the aforementioned forms. Our manual scrutiny of the unclassified reward limits suggests that they typically represent limits that are constrained by supply (e.g., becoming a member of the development team, getting a product used by campaign creators, or becoming a business collaborator). This confirms that the three forms of reward limits are the most typical ones used by campaign creators and that most of the reward limits are strategically manipulated rather than naturally constrained.

With these three forms of reward limits identified, we extend our campaign-level analysis by incorporating variables with different forms of reward limits. To assess the effectiveness of various forms of reward limits, we create binary indicators for the three forms of reward limits to capture whether a specific form is used in the reward scheme (i.e., HasLE, HasPD, and HasEA). To assess the effective setting of reward limits, we construct the linear and quadratic terms for each form in the second-stage model (i.e., ProportionLE, ProportionPD, and ProportionEA). Although there is only a small proportion of reward limits outside of the three typical forms, we nonetheless include a control variable for other forms of reward limits to account for their potential effects. Because of the exclusion of non-English campaigns, the sample for examining different forms of reward limits comprises 31,480 campaigns.

## 4.3. Campaign Day–Level Analysis

The previous campaign-level analyses focused on the overall effects of setting reward limits at the beginning of a campaign on eventual crowdfunding performance. Considering the dynamic nature of the reward scheme and crowdfunding process, we now explore the dynamics of limited reward tiers to obtain further insights by decomposing the potential positive and negative effects of reward limits. Similarly, to reveal additional nuances of the dynamic scarcity effects, we also examine how the status of different forms of reward limits affect subsequent funding performance and how the goal-directed mechanism of crowdfunding plays a role therein.

4.3.1. Model Speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. To answer the third research question, we adopt a dynamic perspective for crowdfunding campaigns by constructing a campaign day–level model to estimate the influence of the current status of limited rewards on subsequent daily funding performance. Such a model utilizes the panel structure of our data and captures the dynamic process of fundraising. Specifically, we adopt a twoway fixed effects model at campaign and day levels to jointly account for campaign-level heterogeneity as well as temporal trends or shocks over the fundraising process. The baseline econometric models are specified as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \times N u m L i m i t R e w a r d _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {2} \times N u m L i m i t R e w a r d G o n e _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {3} \times \ln (C u m R a t i o _ {i t - 1}) + \beta_ {4} \times D a y R a t i o _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + C o n t r o l _ {i t - 1} + \varepsilon_ {i} + \eta_ {t} + u _ {i t} \end{array}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{r l} C o n t r o l _ {i t - 1} & = \beta_ {5} \times I m a g e C o u n t _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {6} \times H a s V i d e o _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {7} \times \ln (D e s c r i p t i o n L e n g t h _ {i t - 1}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {8} \times U p d a t e _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {9} \times P r o j e c t W e L o v e _ {i t - 1} \end{array}
$$

and $Y _ { i t } = \{ \ln ( P l e d g e d _ { i t } ) , \ln ( B a c k e r s _ { i t } ) \}$

In the panel structure, we consider both the amount of contributions $( P l e d g e d _ { i t } )$ and the number of contributors $( B a c k e r s _ { i t } )$ to campaign i on day t as the dependent variables to measure the speed of fundraising. In terms of the key independent variables, we use the total number of limited reward tiers $( N u m L i m i t R e w a r d _ { i t - 1 } )$ and the number of depleted limited reward tiers $( N u m L i m i t R e w a r d G o n e _ { i t - 1 } ,$ , for limited reward tiers that are marked as no longer available) for campaign i at the end of day $t - 1$ to capture the status of the limited reward tiers. This specification allows us to examine the impact of reward limit status on subsequent contributions and avoid simultaneity issues.

Additionally, to account for confounding factors related to the status of limited reward tiers, we incorporate the cumulative funding percentage $( C u m R a t i o _ { i t - 1 } ^ { - } )$ to control for current funding performance by day $t - 1$ (Burtch et al. 2013) and cumulative fundraising progress $( D a y R a t i o _ { i t - 1 } )$ to capture the percentage of fundraising duration by day $t - 1$ (Hong et al. 2018). We also include a set of control variables that vary over time, such as the number of images $( I m a g e C o u n t _ { i t - 1 } ) .$ whether there is a video $( H a s V i d e o _ { i t - 1 } )$ , the length of the campaign description $( D e s c r i p t i o n L e n g t h _ { i t - 1 } ) .$ whether a campaign update was posted $( U p d a t e _ { i t - 1 } ) .$ anda “Projects $\bar { \mathrm { W e L o v e ^ { \prime \prime } } }$ indicator<sup>11</sup> $( P r o j e c t W e L o v e _ { i t - 1 } )$ Other factors that are invariant across time $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ funding goal and duration) are accounted for by the campaign-level fixed effects $\left( \varepsilon _ { i } \right)$ . Moreover, the time trends of contributions are controlled by $\eta _ { t } .$ . Fixed effects at the campaign level are incorporated in the estimations via within-group transformation, whereas time trends or time fixed effects are implemented using time dummies $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ dummies that capture each day of the fundraising duration).

To further examine the dynamics of different forms of reward limits, we follow the same approach as in our campaign-level analysis by classifying each limited reward into the three forms (limited edition, price discount, or early access; other if it does not fall into any of these categories). Then, we construct the key independent variables in a similar manner: the number of limited reward tiers and the number of depleted limited reward tiers for each form of reward limit. We also include a control variable for the other form of reward limit. Furthermore, to understand how the goal-directed mechanism in crowdfunding plays an influential role in shaping the scarcity effects, we conduct interaction analyses for the key independent variables of reward limit status. We use a dummy variable (AboveGoal) that captures whether the campaign has achieved its funding goal at the end of day t <sup>−</sup> 1 and interact this dummy variable with the limited reward variables. The variables in the interaction analysis are mean centered to ease the interpretability of the coefficients.

However, the results for NumLimitRewardGone suggest that funding speed is negatively affected by the number of depleted limited reward tiers. When more limited reward tiers are depleted during the campaign (i.e., subsequent backers can no longer contribute to the campaign with these reward tiers), the campaign tends to receive fewer contributions on the following day. In other words, limited reward tiers that are no longer available likely become a barrier for potential contributors and demotivate their contributions, even though they could still contribute to the campaign by choosing other reward tiers.

Specifically, all else being equal, one more depleted limited reward would decrease subsequent contributions by 5.1%, thus implying a significant loss for campaigns. These results shed light on the positive and negative aspects of reward limits in the dynamic fundraising process. Given that campaigns could eventually raise approximately \$10,000 on average, one new limited reward tier could attract \$360 more in contributions, whereas one depleted limited reward tier could reduce contributions by \$510. Because campaigns generally incorporate multiple limited reward tiers in a reward scheme, the gain from new limited rewards and the loss from depleted limited rewards could amount to a significant proportion of the total contributions received.

Models 20 and 21 display the dynamic effects of different forms of reward limits. We further observe that the positive effect of introducing new limited reward tiers arises from the limited edition rewards (a 4.8% increase in contributions with one new limited edition reward), whereas the negative effect of depleted limited reward tiers comes from the price discount rewards (a 6.5% decrease in contributions with one depleted price discount reward). This result suggests that when campaign creators add a new limited reward tier, backers tend to be more responsive to the limited edition compared with the price discount reward. Introducing new limited edition rewards increases the distinctiveness of choosing the campaign and subsequently leads to better funding performance on the following day (Amaldoss and Jain 2010). However, adding a new limited reward tier with price discounts may not increase the desirability of the campaign, as backers tend to be sensitive to the existence or availability of price discounts rather than the number of discounted reward tiers. This is also likely due to the homogeneity of economic value backers could receive from price discount rewards; that is, all economic benefits from price discounts are associated with monetary benefits, whereas special features of limited edition rewards could be heterogeneous (Sheth et al. 1991).

When a limited reward tier becomes depleted, the reverse seems to be the case (i.e., backers tend to be less receptive to the depletion of price discount compared with limited edition rewards). This implies that backers are more sensitive to the disappearance of economic benefits than the loss of distinctiveness from a reward. Because backers tend to make comparisons among multiple reward tiers when they make a decision on whether to back a campaign, they will anchor their decision on the depleted limited rewards (Simonson and Drolet 2004). These limited rewards may contribute to the formation of reference prices (Urbany et al. 1988, Anderson and Simester 2008), such that when the limited rewards with lower prices become unavailable, backers are less likely to contribute because of the higher amount that they would need to pledge compared with the reference prices to receive similar rewards.

Models 22 and 23 present the results of the interaction analysis, where the AboveGoal indicator is introduced and interacts with focal variables related to the status of reward limits. In particular, the goaldirected mechanism shapes the negative effects of depleted limited reward tiers; that is, the negative effect of NumLimitRewardGone is moderated by the status of achieving the funding goal. Generally, the negative effect of depleted limited rewards is salient only when a campaign has received sufficient funds to be successfully funded and is not significant when a campaign has yet to reach its funding goal. This result suggests that even though backers prefer to obtain a limited edition reward or a lower price, they attach a higher value to as-yet-unsuccessful campaigns (Hu et al. 2015). In other words, they would like to support a campaign that has not been successfully funded even though the limited reward tiers are depleted because both obtaining the rewards and helping the campaign’s success are factored into their contribution decisions.12

In summary, our campaign day–level analyses answer the third research question by examining the dynamic effects of reward limits over the course of the fundraising campaign and the role of the goaldirected mechanism in shaping such effects. We estimated the dynamic effects of reward limits using changes in the availability status of limited reward tiers and unveiled both the positive and negative effects they exert. Specifically, we found a positive effect of adding new limited reward tiers and a negative effect of having more limited reward tiers depleted on subsequent funding performance. The analysis involving multiple forms of reward limit suggests that the positive effect primarily stems from limited edition rewards, whereas the negative effect mainly arises from the depletion of price discount rewards. We also examined the moderating role of the concurrent funding performance of a campaign relative to its funding goal. Because of backers’ dual roles as both supporters and consumers, on average, they tend to respond negatively to the depletion of limited rewards only after the funding goal has been achieved.

## 4.4. Robustness Checks

Our campaign-level and campaign day–level analyses have provided insights into our core research questions.

Nonetheless, concerns remain regarding the robustness of these results and the validity of model specifications. To address these concerns, we conduct several robustness checks to provide stronger support for our findings.

4.4.1. Robustness of Matching. We first examine the robustness of matching in our campaign-level analysis. One important aspect of matching is to ensure the quality of matching so that the treated and matched samples are similar after matching. Although Table C1 in Online Appendix C shows significant improvement in standardized biases after PSM and Mahalanobis matching, some of the t-tests comparing covariates across the treatment and control groups remain statistically significant, although the differences are small in magnitude. To address this concern, we consider three alternative matching analyses to further enhance the quality of the matching analysis. Specifically, we use a smaller caliper for PSM, adopt the trimming method to exclude observations with low propensity score density, and enforce a stricter caliper in the Mahalanobis matching. Although these alternative approaches exclude a great number of campaigns because of the stricter matching criteria— which may undermine the sample representativeness and generalizability of our results—the results continue to support the positive effects of incorporating reward limits (see Online Appendix C, Tables C2 and C3).

Another concern in our matching analysis and campaign-level analysis relates to the validity of treating reward limits as a binary variable and examining the effects of the number of limited rewards separately. It is possible that reward limits could be directly multilevel and endogenously decided at each level. This suggests a potential “continuous” treatment effect of reward limits instead of a binary treatment effect. To obtain further insights, we adopt the generalized propensity score approach (Hirano and Imbens 2004, Imai and van Dyk 2004) to estimate a “dose– response” function for different levels of limited rewards (Guardabascio and Ventura 2014). We plot the conditional expectation of the outcome variables at each treatment level of reward limits, with the results suggesting an overall inverted-U-shaped continuous treatment effect that is consistent with our campaignlevel analysis regarding the effectiveness of reward limits and the effective number of limited rewards (see Online Appendix C, Figure C1).

4.4.2. Alternative Measures. We also examine whether our key measures are robust to alternative operationalizations. Although we measured our core variables in a suitable manner, alternative measures of limited rewards may also be employed. In our campaign-level analysis, we can measure reward limits by using the total quota for backers in the limited reward tiers instead of the proportion of the number of limited reward tiers. In a similar vein, in our campaign day–level analysis, the status of reward limits can be alternatively operationalized by the number of backers allowed in the limited reward tiers (i.e., the total backer quota of limited reward tiers and the total number of backers that have chosen the limited reward tiers). The results for both alterna tive operationalizations are consistent with our main analysis (see Online Appendix D, Tables D1 and D2).

Furthermore, the pricing effects we control for in the campaign-level analysis primarily capture the influence of actual price (LimitRewardPrice). Although the results show the negative effects of Limit RewardPrice, this variable may be confounded with the value of the regular product. To further make sense of controlling the value of limited reward tiers, we alternatively consider the relative position of limited reward tiers as a control variable. Because reward tiers are listed in order of increasing price in crowdfunding campaigns, the position or order of a reward tier is highly correlated with its price. Specifically, we operationalize this variable as the average position of limited reward tiers standardized by the total number of reward tiers (to avoid the high correlation between overall positions and the total number of limited reward tiers). Notably, our results for the main variables (i.e., the original and quadratic terms of limited rewards) remain consistent.

4.4.3. Alternative Panel Model Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Although we have estimated the two-way fixed effects panel model in our campaign day–level analysis, there may still be concerns regarding the model specifications. First, we consider an alternative specification that includes the lagged dependent variable in the model because the key independent variables may be cor related with the lagged fundraising performance variables. We first check a model with lagged dependent variables and confirm the results are not affected by the inclusion of lagged DVs. To address the issue of endogeneity due to the inclusion of a lagged DV, we employ dynamic panel model estimation with the system generalized method of moments (system GMM) estimator (Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998). This estimation instruments the lagged DV with the higher-order lags of the dependent variable. Our results with various lagged structures as instruments in the dynamic panel model are shown to be consistent with our main results (see Online Appendix E, Table E1).

Another concern regarding our panel model estimation arises from potential endogeneity because the status of reward limits may depend on time-varying fundraising outcomes. Although our panel model controls for the cumulative funding percentage, the cumulative fundraising progress, and time (day) fixed effects, we further examine whether our estimations are robust to time-variant confounders and simultaneity. Specifically, we employ the heteroskedasticity-based instrumental variable approach of Lewbel (2012) by constructing orthogonal instruments from exogenous covariates. Notably, the results are consistent with our main estimations (see Online Appendix E, Table E2).

Finally, we address a remaining concern related to model specification by focusing on the number of nonlimited rewards at the end of day t <sup>−</sup> 1. It is possible that the status of limited reward tiers is correlated with the status of nonlimited reward tiers, whereas nonlimited rewards will have an effect on contributions on day t. However, we find that the correlation between limited and nonlimited reward tiers is extremely low $( \rho < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Additionally, our estimations with the number of nonlimited reward tiers at the end of day t <sup>−</sup> 1 as a control variable yield consistent results, thus ruling out this alternative explanation.

## 5. Discussion

Our econometric analyses investigate the effectiveness and dynamics of reward limits in crowdfunding. Notably, several major findings emerge from our results. First, it is beneficial to set reward limits at the beginning of a crowdfunding campaign, and such benefits mainly stem from limited edition and price discount rewards. Second, there is an inverted-Ushaped relationship between the proportion of limited reward tiers and eventual fundraising performance, and this relationship also largely applies to limited edition and price discount rewards. Third, adding a new limited reward tier is beneficial, though a limited reward tier becoming depleted is detrimental to subsequent daily funding performance. The positive effect is due to the addition of limited edition rewards, whereas the detrimental effect is due to the depletion of price discount rewards. Fourth, the scarcity effect is not homogenous across different stages of the funding process. The negative effect of depleted reward tiers is salient only when the concurrent performance has surpassed the funding goal.

The marketing literature suggests that introducing scarcity appeals could result in higher sales because it increases the attractiveness and desirability of a product (Amaldoss and Jain 2008); however, limiting product availability can also prevent consumers from purchasing a product, which subsequently decreases sales (Stock and Balachander 2005). Although tensions exist surrounding the overall effectiveness of the scarcity strategy in crowdfunding (e.g., employing reward limits), our analyses present evidence of an overall positive and economically significant effect of reward limits on campaign performance. Using reward limits in the form of limited edition reward and price discounts at the beginning of a campaign increases crowdfunding performance. This finding implies that using such a practice in reward scheme design could create exclusivity and uniqueness for prospective backers and provide economic benefits to acquiring the rewards—both of which increase the attractiveness of crowdfunding campaigns (Wathieu et al. 2004, Balachander and Stock 2009).

Notwithstanding the overall positive effect of re ward limits, our empirical exploration yields evidence for both positive and negative aspects of incorporating limited rewards. Notably, the findings are twofold. First, at the campaign level, the results suggest that the scarcity strategy should not be overused, as only a moderate level of limited reward tiers is beneficial. Too few limited reward tiers wil make it too easy to reach a sold-out status, which wil subsequently exclude backers because they cannot obtain additional value (i.e., exclusivity from limited edition rewards and economic benefits from price discount rewards) from supporting the campaign (Hu et al. 2015). However, if there are too many limited rewards offered, then the perceived value of limited re wards is diminished. Specifically, too many limited edition rewards decrease the exclusivity of the rewards because nothing is truly exclusive if almost everything is “exclusive” (Amaldoss and Jain 2008, 2010). Furthermore, too many price discount rewards reduce the perceived savings from acquiring the rewards because backers may use other discounted rewards to form the reference price for their own contribution when making a comparison across multiple reward tiers (Biswas and Blair 1991). Our analyses indicate that a moderate number of limited reward tiers is helpful and that it is not always beneficial to incorporate more limited rewards.

Second, during the fundraising process at the campaign day level, we find a positive effect of adding new limited reward tiers (3.6% per tier) and a negative effect of existing limited reward tiers becoming depleted (5.1% per tier) on subsequent funding speed. Adding limited edition rewards contributes to the positive effect, which implies that backers value the special products offered by campaign creators, and they, like consumers, prefer to obtain special consumption value from their purchase (or backing) activities. Having new limited edition rewards increases the desirability of a campaign and restricts the number of (other) backers that would access the same rewards (Brown 2001, Stock and Balachander 2005). The depletion of price discount rewards is associated with a negative effect, which implies that backers are more sensitive to the loss of economic benefits. Specifically, the pledge amount of unavailable reward tiers may induce the formation of backers’ reference prices (Anderson and Simester 2008). Notably, backers are likely to interpret the current pledge amount (of available reward tiers) as price unfairness when the discounted price of the depleted limited rewards acts as a reference, which subsequently reduces their likelihood of contribution.

We also find that the goal-directed mechanism shapes the scarcity effect such that the negative effect of depleted limited reward tiers in the fundraising process only emerges when a campaign is overperforming. This finding implies that crowdfunding backers have a different evaluation function for their contributions when compared with general consumers and their purchasing decisions (Hu et al. 2015). Specifically, backers also consider the success of a crowdfunding campaign in their contribution apart from the actual rewards they could obtain. Overall, our analyses at both the campaign and campaign day levels offer meaningful insights into the positive and negative effects of the scarcity strategy. These findings enable us to move beyond the dominant positive effects of the scarcity strategy discussed in the marketing literature and provide nuanced implications that the inappropriate use of such scarcity may result in unfavorable outcomes.

Furthermore, because reward limits can be manifested as different forms in crowdfunding, our analyses on the various forms of reward limits not only add nuances to the heterogeneous effects of the scarcity strategy, but also shed light on the underlying mechanisms of such effects. First, the effect of limited edition rewards suggests that the scarcity strategy also provides noneconomic benefits apart from the economic value in terms of monetary or time savings. In particular, limited edition rewards convey special value to backers and induce a sense of exclusivity (Thompson et al. 2006). Moreover, obtaining limited edition rewards satisfies the backers’ desire for distinctiveness; however, this feeling of uniqueness will decrease if campaign creators offer special rewards to too many individuals (Amaldoss and Jain 2010).

Second, regarding price discount rewards, in addition to the economic incentives conveyed (i.e., monetary savings), the consumer psychology literature proposes a reference price explanation wherein consumers tend to interpret prices charged for other similar items as a reference from which they can judge fairness (Anderson and Simester 2008). Similarly, in the context of crowdfunding, the salient accessibility of the prices of other limited price discount reward tiers in the reward scheme will induce the formation of reference prices for backers. If there is a great proportion of depleted rewards that offer discounted prices, then backers will anchor their reference price with the prices of the depleted ones (with lower prices) and consequently interpret the amount they would have to pledge (for other similar rewards) as unfair, which will subsequently decrease their intention to contribute. This is likely why we observe a negative effect for depleted price discount rewards in the campaign day–level analysis. If the scarcity effect merely occurred in an economic manner for the price discount rewards, the negative effect of depleted price discount rewards would not be significant. Taken together, the conceptualization and findings of this study substantiate that reward limits occur in both economic and noneconomic (psychological) manners.<sup>13</sup>

Finally, because scarcity appeals are used by campaign creators to attract backer contributions, this study has some bearing on the economics literature on behavioral intervention, especially regarding the external incentives on producing desired behaviors in humans. A general theme of this literature is that external incentives could help provoke expected effects on behavior (e.g., attendance and enrollment, academic performance, and lifestyle habits; Fryer 2011, Gneezy et al. 2011). From this perspective, setting reward limits could also be viewed as an incentive to evoke the urgency of backing a campaign. Nevertheless, the incentives discussed in the economics literature are largely economics oriented (e.g., financial incentives, extrinsic rewards, or punishment). In accordance with our previous arguments, we posit that the use of reward limits offers both economic and psychological incentives. Specifically, it provides financial incentives in the form of price discounts as well as the psychological and emotional incentives manifested as limited edition rewards. Therefore, investigating reward limits in crowdfunding enables us to complement the existing literature on external incentives by offering a fine-grained view of the types of incentives that could be offered to encourage desired behaviors. Our findings further demonstrate that humans respond differently to different types of incentives. For example, when an incentive becomes unavailable, humans tend to react negatively to economic incentives by reducing the desired behavior (i.e., backing a campaign), whereas this would not be the case for psychological incentives. Our findings imply that humans are more sensitive to economic loss than to emotional or psychological loss in the design of incentives.

## 6. Conclusion

The present study examines reward limits in crowdfunding. With a unique data set that captures the daily dynamics of crowdfunding campaigns over seven months, our empirical exploration offers important insights into the positive and negative effects of reward limits, in spite of the overall positive effect. Our examination on multiple forms of reward limits further unveils the underlying mechanisms from both economic and psychological perspectives.

## 6.1. Theoretical Contributions

Our study advances knowledge in several areas. First, we contribute to the growing literature on digital business strategy in general and crowdfunding campaign design strategy in particular by highlighting how crowdfunding creators could manage and design their campaigns to satisfy backer-as-consumer needs in addition to backer-as-supporter goals. Notably, backers play dual roles as supporters and consumers in rewardbased crowdfunding (Lin et al. 2014, Chan and Parhankangas 2017). On the consumer side, they expect to obtain rewards in exchange for their contributions to crowdfunding campaigns. Our findings suggest that it is imperative for campaign creators to instill backers’ consumption needs in designing rewards for their contributions. The examination of reward limits in campaign reward schemes reveals that backers, like consumers, desire not only economic benefits but also exclusivity and uniqueness when contributing to a campaign (Brock and Mazzocco 2004, Balachander and Stock 2009). The different forms of reward limits further shed light on backer-asconsumer needs in multiple dimensions. Limited edition rewards satisfy the desire to be distinctive and unique, whereas price discounts provide the economic benefits of obtaining a reward.

We also enrich the crowdfunding literature by taking a dynamic view of the fundraising process. The general theme of prior research has largely been to understand the effect of campaign attributes on eventual crowdfunding performance (Short et al. 2017), which neglects the possibility that campaign creators could adjust campaign attributes over the course of fundraising, and that such adjustment would affect subsequent funding performance. Accordingly, we complement the existing literature by examining how changes in reward scheme status (specifically in reward limit status) affect subsequent funding performance. Although the overall effect of reward limits is positive, our empirical evidence suggests that there are both positive and negative effects over the course of fundraising. Hence, the present study emphasizes the importance of understanding campaign design from a dynamic perspective by delving into the fundraising process to facilitate a better understanding of what is effective in this process and what contributes to overall funding performance.

Third, echoing our discussions on the marketing literature, we argue that crowdfunding is characterized by distinctive features that make the findings of previous studies not readily generalizable. Our empirical exploration of the effect of reward limits enables us to provide new insights in the product scarcity literature. In particular, through the goaldirected mechanism of crowdfunding, our findings reveal that backers may consider the outcome of a crowdfunding campaign in addition to the direct values they could obtain from the rewards (especially in the presence of time constraint of a campaign), unlike prior studies of scarcity effect that mostly assume consumers only evaluate what they can obtain from the consumption. Broadly speaking, the use of scarcity strategy is becoming increasingly prevalent in certain situations (e.g., when the availability of products is goal-directed) or on products with special attributes (e.g., an innovative product that satisfies curiosity). Our findings indicate a potential role for noneconomic factors in motivating consumption behavior and shaping the effectiveness of the scarcity strategy.

Furthermore, the structure of the reward scheme allows backers to compare across multiple reward tiers without substantial information processing efforts. We find that backers tend to base their decisions (i.e., to contribute or not) on such comparisons— especially regarding price discount rewards—because overusing price discount rewards at the beginning of a campaign and the depletion of price discount reward tiers over the course of fundraising have been shown to have negative effects. This finding also implies that backers are more sensitive to economic losses compared with economic gains (Tversky and Kahneman 1992, Schindler 1998, Anderson and Simester 2008). Notably, these findings enrich the literature on discount location and discount presentation (e.g., Kim and Kramer 2006, Biswas et al. 2013) and demonstrate that the design of price presentation should not neglect its effect on consumer perceptions in addition to its prominent role in highlighting economic benefits.

## 6.2. Practical Implications

Our study yields several implications for practitioners regarding crowdfunding as well as traditional selling contexts. First, one of the major objectives of crowdfunding platform operators is to bring creative ideas to reality. To promote the success of crowdfunding campaigns, platform operators could regularly organize communication forums to facilitate information sharing with campaign creators on how to effectively design campaigns. Based on the findings of this study and the important economic influences of using reward limits, they could inform campaign creators of the prominent role of reward limits in crowdfunding success while also reminding creators of the potential negative effects of using reward limits in certain ways.

Additionally, platform operators could leverage the psychological effects of reward limits suggested by our results. Given backers’ desire for exclusivity in limited edition rewards and their frustration upon encountering depleted price discount rewards, we suggest that platform operators introduce new features that amplify distinctiveness and interfere with the formation of reference prices. For limited edition rewards, platform operators could add features that increase the recognition and visibility of those distinctive backers (e.g., social media sharing of choosing limited editions and special identification on the campaign or personal profile page). For price discount rewards, platforms may selectively feature certain rewards to shift backers’ attention from depleted discounts to featured ones or hide the prices of depleted price discount rewards so that the psychological biases from reference prices are less likely to take effect. In so doing, platform operators can keep backers active and satisfied to ensure the sustainable development of the platform.

Third, the findings of our study offer immediate guidance for campaign creators on effectively setting reward limits. Before the launch of a campaign, campaign creators should set a moderate number of limited reward tiers in the form of limited editions and price discounts to evoke a sense of urgency. Creators should also note that only the financial dimension of economic benefits is positively perceived by backers, as the time dimension conveyed by early access rewards is shown to be ineffective. Moreover, campaign creators must carefully keep track of the status of limited reward tiers in the fundraising process. Given backers’ preference for exclusivity and negative response to the discontinuation of discount opportunities, campaign creators could introduce new limited edition rewards or increase the limit in price discount rewards to generate upward fundraising momentum. By doing so, they can boost fundraising performance and avoid significant losses due to depleted limited rewards. Moreover, we suggest that they monitor concurrent funding performance (i.e., successfully funded or not) to adjust reward limits, because backers react differently to reward limits, especially when a campaign is overperforming.

More generally, company marketers could adopt multiple forms of scarcity appeals when implementing the scarcity strategy, with each form satisfying different consumer needs. For example, a limited edition could be advertised to consumers in need of special values from consumption. Moreover, limited quantity price discounts could be exposed to consumers expecting economic benefits. In addition, similar to the special characteristics of crowdfunding, marketers could design additional constraints along with the scarcity appeals, such as a targeted goal for the scarcity appeals to become active or a limited time message. Our findings imply that these extra constraints could further urge certain consumers to purchase products. We also suggest that such constraints are particularly useful for innovative products, such as new products in crowdfunding. Because consumers are typically less informed in these scenarios (Courty and Nasiry 2016), they are more likely to rely on peripheral information to make a purchase decision.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

Although our study provides many useful results, we note its limitations as well as relevant directions for future research. One limitation is that we could not identify backers, so we could not observe their individual decision making. In this study, our main focus is on the implementation of the scarcity strategy and firm-level performance (crowdfunding campaign performance, in our context). A fruitful direction for future research would be to investigate campaign design strategies and the choice behavior of individual backers using backer information to verify the perceptions and decision mechanisms regarding reward limits.

Second, we focus on the goal-directed mechanism (i.e., whether the funding goal has been achieved) in the current study. Notably, the management and psychology literature also suggests a goal-gradient mechanism (i.e., the distance or proximity to funding goal; Locke and Latham 2002). Such a mechanism in crowdfunding dynamics is worthy of further investigation. Taken together, we suggest that future researchers explore the nuances and mechanisms related to funding goals in order to understand how they interact with the dynamics of reward limits.

Another limitation stems from the potential constraints in our context. Because campaign creators set reward limits in a specific way to highlight the potential value of limited rewards (e.g., exclusivity or economic benefits), it is possible that if reward limits are designed without strong signals of such benefits (e.g., the benefits of limited rewards are negligible), our findings may not be applicable. Therefore, our causal inferences and findings may be restricted by the scope of data. Researchers are encouraged to discern the boundary conditions of our findings.

Finally, our analyses are based on data from a single crowdfunding platform, which raises concerns regarding the external validity of our findings. However, focusing on one platform enables us to obtain indepth insights at a fine-grained daily level. Whereas we are investigating the scarcity appeals of one platform, this practice has also been widely employed by other similar reward-based crowdfunding platforms such as Indiegogo and JD Crowdfunding. We suggest that our findings should be generalizable to other crowdfunding platforms and expect future research to validate our findings across multiple platforms.
