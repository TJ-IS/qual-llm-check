# Intermediaries in inter-organisational networks: building a theory of electronic marketplace performance

Philip O’Reilly<sup>1</sup> and Patrick Finnegan<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Accounting, Finance and Information Systems, University College Cork, Ireland; <sup>2</sup>The Australian School of Business, University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia.

Correspondence: Philip O’Reilly, Department of Accounting, Finance and Information Systems, University College Cork, Cork, Ireland. Tel: + 353 21 4903833: Fax: + 353 21 4271566

## Abstract

Intermediaries such as e-Bay and Amazon arise when market system knowledge is dispersed amongst participants (e.g., buyers and suppliers), and generate revenue by providing value-added services to participants in addition to creating and managing the digital infrastructure. Consequently, such intermediaries (which we call electronic marketplaces) play a vital role in facilitating exchanges in networks characterised by disparate knowledge and are essential network orchestrators in peer-to-peer markets and intellectual property exchanges. However, there is a high failure rate associated with electronic marketplaces leading to questions as to the long-term sustainability of emerging inter-organisational networks characterised by dispersed knowledge. This paper draws on research in a number of disciplines as well as a study of eight electronic marketplaces to build a theory of electronic marketplace performance. In doing so, we identify key performance measures for electronic marketplaces as well as the strategic, structural and contextual factors that impact performance. We identify how these factors can be observed, and illustrate how the fit between strategic and contextual factors affects performance. We present our theory as hypotheses and provide the empirical indicators for the constituent constructs. European Journal of Information Systems (2010) 19, 462–480.

Keywords: electronic marketplace; performance; model

## Introduction

In examining inter-organisational systems (IOS) and networks many information systems researchers have based their research on the economic theories of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1975, 1981, 1991, 1999); they thus predominately view the market system governance, within which the IOS operates, as either a hierarchy or market (although hybrid governance has also been studied). This is particularly evident in Malone et al.’s (1987) seminal work on electronic hierarchies and markets; referred to as the electronic markets hypothesis. Building on the economic theories of Coase (1937) and Williamson (1975, 1981) and given the ability of Information technology (IT) to reduce co-ordination costs, Malone et al. (1987) predicted an increased utilisation of electronic markets at the expense of electronic hierarchies. Researchers such as Clemons & Row (1992), Bakos & Brynjolfsson (1993) and Hess & Kemerer (1994) have criticised the theory, stating that it ignores key aspects of interorganisational relationships, including how organisations manage risk and the fundamental nature of buyer/seller relationships. Furthermore, there has been limited empirical evidence confirming this hypothesis.

Significantly, rather than the emergence of electronic markets proposed by Malone et al. (1987), researchers such as Bakos (1991), Lee & Clark (1996) and Soh et al. (2006) noted the increased number of third-party market makers which co-ordinate inter-organisational activities. Such intermediaries are not only evident in industry markets for physical products (e.g., that the Dutch Flower auctions, and Amazon), but also in markets for intellectual property (IP) and innovation capacity (e.g., Yet2, NineSigma and Innocentive) and in consumer-to-consumer environments (e.g., eBay). For the purpose of this study, and in line with the work of Bailey & Bakos (1997), who emphasise the role of intermediaries in electronic markets for aggregating, matching suppliers and customers, providing trust, and providing inter-organisational market information, we classify such intermediaries as electronic marketplaces. This concept of electronic intermediaries is empirically supported by the work of Kambil & Van Heck (1998) and Kaplan & Sawhney (2000).

We posit that the emergence of third-party intermediaries may be explained by the work of Hayek (1945) on the emergence of intermediaries in the market system. Hayek argued that one of the main issues for parties conducting economic activity is access to market knowledge. Such knowledge does not exist in a concentrated or integrated form but as ‘dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all separate individuals possess’ Hayek (1945, p. 77). Hayek believed that one of the key considerations for firms was the process for obtaining and aggregating such knowledge (e.g., potential suppliers, potential buyers, price, availability etc.); a process that could be undertaken (as a value-added service) by third-party merchants (intermediaries). This consideration of market knowledge is not reflected in the majority of IOS research, which sees product characteristics, asset specificity and structural embeddedness (cf. Granovetter, 1992; Jones et al. 1997) as the key determinants of the appropriateness of autonomous or co-ordinated inter-firm adaptation that characterise electronic markets and hierarchies. Building on the work of Soh & Markus (2002) and Dai & Kauffman (2002), we define an electronic marketplace as:

an organisational intermediary that electronically provides value added communication, brokerage and integration services to buyers and sellers of direct and/or indirect products and/or services in specific horizontal or vertical markets by supporting basic market functions, meeting management needs for information and process support, and/or operating the required IS/IT infrastructure.

The growing interest in electronic marketplaces as business-to-business intermediaries in the research literature has been accompanied by an acknowledgement that the failure rate of such intermediaries is high (cf. Grieger, 2003: Soh et al., 2006: Fairchild et al., 2007: Son &

Benbasat, 2007). This is supported by evidence from emarketservices (www.emarketservices.com), which in January 2010 revealed the existence of 643 electronic marketplaces; representing a decrease from 1500 electronic marketplaces in 2000. Indeed, while researchers acknowledge that electronic marketplace performance is an issue worthy of research (cf. Corsten & Hofstetter, 2001; Soh et al., 2006), there is a paucity of research on electronic marketplaces, particularly how such marketplaces can improve their performance. Furthermore, in the context of understanding electronic marketplaces, Grieger (2003) argues that ‘having accepted the challenge to create a synchronised supply chain that can compete in the future e-economy, what concrete capabilities must then be mastered? y which competencies must a business model, using the Internet medium, manage amidst a competition regarding ‘‘supply chain versus supply chain’’?’ (p. 280).

In addressing issues surrounding electronic marketplaces, extant research is constrained by a number of issues. First, the definitional problems surrounding electronic marketplaces mean that researchers may not be examining the same phenomena. Second, a common understanding of electronic marketplace performance (including a definition) is not evident in the literature. Indeed, terms such as electronic marketplace success (Dai & Kauffman, 2002), and converging stakeholder satisfaction (Ribbers et al., 2002), which are inferred to reflect electronic marketplace performance, are not consistently defined or explained. Third, while some studies (e.g., Kambil & Van Heck, 1998; Dai & Kauffman, 2002) report that particular factors impact upon electronic marketplace performance, these claims are not supported with transparent performance measures.

The objective of this study is to develop an electronic marketplace performance model. Four research questions were formulated:

(i) How do marketplaces measure their performance?

(ii) What factors impact upon marketplace performance?

(iii) How do these factors impact upon marketplace performance?

(iv) Is the fit between factors useful in explaining electronic marketplace performance?

In order to achieve our objective, an analysis of extant research is undertaken to build relevant theory by delineating constructs and the relationships between these constructs in the form of theoretical propositions. This is followed by a discussion of the research process. We then use an analysis of eight electronic marketplaces to test our propositions, determine empirical indicators and propose hypotheses. Finally, we conclude by discussing the implications of our work for research and practice.

## Theoretical grounding

In this section we use extant literature on (1) electronic marketplaces; (2) organisational theory; (3) management;

and (4) accounting in order to conceptualise the phenomenon and to serve as a guide for data-gathering – an approach recommended by Forrester (1961) and Wheeler (2002). Specifically, we utilise the buildingblocks of theory development proposed by Dubin (1969) and Whetten (1989) to delineate constructs as well as the relationships between these constructs in the form of theoretical propositions.

In keeping with the conceptualisation of electronic marketplaces as organisational intermediaries, we build upon research on organisational performance evident by the work of McNair et al. (1990), Kaplan & Norton (1996), Otley (1999), Norreklit (1999) and Brealey & Myers (2004) to define electronic marketplace performance as ‘the efficiency of the marketplace in performing its tasks and meeting its objectives, while continuing to innovate, grow, and expand’. We now proceed by theorising how the alignment of particular characteristics of electronic marketplaces may lead to improved performance.

The concept of strategic alignment has been used by a number of researchers seeking to understand organisational performance (cf. Scott Morton, 1991; Doty et al., 1993; Venkatraman et al., 1993). The concept of strategic alignment has also been applied to inter-organisational settings, revealing that the performance of a dyadic IOS is dependent on the alignment between information processing needs and information processing capabilities (Bensaou & Venkatraman, 1992, 1995). In illustrating the importance of alignment, Bensaou & Venkatraman (1995) emphasise that the dimensions of information processing needs by themselves do not predict performance; they found high performing as well as low performing configurations operating under low information processing needs and, on the other hand, low performing as well as high performing configurations operating under high information processing needs. This reinforces their view that the alignment between information processing needs and capabilities is more important than either dimension alone. In the electronic marketplace literature, Soh et al. (2006) utilised the concept of strategic alignment to illustrate that the alignment between an electronic marketplace’s value proposition and the value activities needed to deliver the promised value to the target market is essential for superior performance. While the work of Soh et al. (2006) focuses on just two characteristics of electronic marketplaces, it illustrates the potential of a fuller consideration of the alignment between characteristics.

As shown in Table 1, our review of the electronic marketplace literature revealed seven factors which are believed to affect marketplace performance. However, as also shown in Table 1, the applicability of these factors is open to question due to the acknowledged limitations of particular studies. They nevertheless illustrate that strategic and contextual factors (cf. Doty et al., 1993) affect the performance of electronic marketplaces. However, we found no evidence in extant research that structural factors affect electronic marketplace performance.

Strategic factors have for decades been considered central to successful business performance, with factors such as market share, product quality, order of market entry and capital intensity considered vital (Buzzell & Gale, 1987; Buzzell, 2004). Critics of this perspective have argued that unobservable factors such as access to scarce resources, corporate culture and management skill are as important and that failure to control for such factors, both biases and exaggerates the effects of strategic factors on performance (cf. Jacobson, 1990).

Following Mintzberg (1979a) we view strategic factors as ‘the set of behaviors by which the organisation establishes, for a time, its place in the environment (p. 73). Using this view, we can identify three aspects from extant electronic marketplace literature that impact performance; market process design, IT and ownership. Market process design refers to how a marketplace offers products which support one or more of the communication, brokerage and integration functions (Kambil & Van Heck, 1998; Dai & Kauffman, 2002). IT refers to the IT products offered by the marketplace to its participants (Bakos, 1991; Soh & Markus, 2002). Ownership refers to the parties who control the marketplace and determine who can utilise it (Kambil & Van Heck, 1998; Grieger, 2003; Byungjoon et al., 2007). We therefore present the first proposition:

## Proposition 1: Electronic Marketplace performance is affected by strategic factors.

Our view of contextual factors is derived from the work of Sinha & Van de Ven (2005), and refers to the specific environmental context, setting or situation confronting the organisation. Using this view, we can identify four aspects from the electronic marketplace literature that impact performance; marketplace power, trading culture, trust, and buying into the vision. Marketplace power refers to the alternative(s) to the marketplace available to market participants and their switching costs in utilising these alternatives (Bakos, 1991; Choudhary et al., 1998; Kambil & Van Heck, 1998). Trading culture refers to the amount of social interaction among participants and its role in how products are procured (Kambil & Van Heck, 1998; Son & Benbasat, 2007). Trust refers to the level of perceived risk from a market participant’s perspective attached to the marketplace (Ba et al., 1999; Smith et al., 2000; Ba & Pavlou, 2002). Finally, buying into the vision refers to market participants’ understanding of the potential of the marketplace concept for them (Lennstrand et al., 2001). We therefore present the second proposition:

## Proposition 2: Electronic Marketplace performance is affected by contextual factors.

While it is evident that the market performance of any commercial venture is affected by the success of its products and services in that market, the overall success or failure of the venture is also determined by the effectiveness of the venture in organising its resources and transforming inputs into sellable products and services (Nickell, 1996; Meijaard et al., 2005). Considerations of organisational structure have largely been based on the work of Child (1972), Pugh & Hickson (1976) and Mintzberg (1979b), with the complexity of the structure, the locus of authority in decision making and the use of formal procedures for coordination considered important (Meijaard et al., 2005). While extant work reveals that structure impacts upon performance (Geeraerts, 1984; Meijaard et al., 2005), specific structural arrangements are seen to be less relevant than the fit between structural arrangements and contextual factors (Doty et al., 1993; Meijaard et al., 2005).

As electronic marketplaces are organisational entities, it could be deemed that performance would be affected by structure. However, our analysis of extant research on electronic marketplaces did not reveal evidence of any structural factors deemed to affect performance. This may in part be due to the limitations of such research as discussed earlier in the paper. Nevertheless, for the purpose of the study we present our third proposition as follows:

## Proposition 3: Electronic Marketplace performance is not affected by structural factors.

We develop our final proposition by building on the work of Scott Morton (1991), Doty et al. (1993) and Venkatraman et al. (1993), which indicates that performance is affected by strategic alignment or ‘fit’. In line with such work, ‘fit’ is operationally defined as the compatibility of one factor with another. In particular, Doty et al. (1993, p. 1196) argue that ‘fit’ or alignment between ‘patterns of relevant contextual, structural and strategic factors’ leads to superior performance and misalignment results in performance erosion. Given the identification of strategic and contextual factors that affect performance in extant literature, and absence of evidence that structural factors affect performance, we conclude by presenting our final proposition:

Proposition 4: Electronic Marketplace performance is affected by the fit between strategic and contextual factors.

## Research method

Epistemologically, we position our work as post-positivist in that we seek to ‘approximate reality’ (Guba, 1990) using methods that emphasise the verification of existing knowledge and the discovery of new knowledge (Denzin & Lincoln, 2000). We adopt a case study approach, as case studies can be used to capture the reality and richness of organisational behaviour in detail, and are thus suitable for exploratory research (cf. Galliers, 1992; Darke et al., 1998). Our method is consistent with the case study approach of Benbasat et al. (1987) and Yin (1994) in that we study electronic marketplaces in their natural setting, employing multiple data collection methods to gather information from a few entities, without employing experimental control or manipulation. We, thus, follow in the tradition of Eisenhardt (1989) and Madill et al. (2000) by seeking to reveal pre-existing, relatively stable and objectively extant phenomena and the relationships among them.

Eight electronic marketplaces (Table 2) were selected for this study using the directory of electronic marketplaces provided by emarketservices (www.emarketservices .com). Case selection was purposeful on the basis of performance. In order to facilitate replicability, a number of cases were chosen in similar product areas and sectors. Five of the marketplaces studied; BTTransact, IBX, Eutilia, Nordpool and Proceedo were rated at the time of this study by emarketservices as being among the top 20 marketplaces worldwide. Of the top 20, five of these marketplaces were selected based on variations in their performance over time as well as willingness to participate. These two criteria were important as we needed to be able to study variations in the dependent variable (performance) as well as having sufficient access to suitable interviewees and documents (including competitively sensitive data) to be able to explain the changes in such performance. The other electronic marketplaces studied, Globalcoal, Dealcotton and Comdaq, were selected to add diversity, but also exhibited variations in performance and were willing to provide the researchers with appropriate access to data. We thus adopt Pettigrew’s (1989) philosophy that such cases may provide insights which the other electronic marketplaces may not, and are useful in building theory. Data gathering took place over a 20-month period using semi-structured interviews and document analysis.

Semi-structured interviews were held with senior management and other personnel responsible for policy formulation. In total, 31 people were interviewed with over 100 hours of interviews taking place (see Table 3). Each interview began by asking broad questions about the electronic marketplace, before proceeding to ask more specific questions about electronic marketplace performance. A combination of focussed and openended questions were used. The focused questions were designed to elicit information related to the factors identified by extant research (Table 1) as well as the relationships between them (Propositions 1–4). Acknowledging the limitations of extant research, open-ended questions were used to ensure that a complete understanding of the factors affecting the performance of each marketplace was attained. This approach is in line with that advocated by Bouchard (1976) and Trauth & O’Connor (1991), and provided the researchers with the flexibility to re-focus during the interview. In addition, the researchers had access to extensive documentation regarding electronic marketplace performance (see Table 3).

The data were analysed using open, axial and selective coding techniques (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Urquhart, 2001), as exemplified by the research of Orlikowski (1993) and Urquhart (1997). This approach, is consistent with a post-positivist epistemology (cf. Charmaz, 2000), and facilitates the development of substantive theory and can be utilised in conjunction with existing theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). This approach necessitates the researchers to be immersed in the data (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) and to draw on existing theoretical knowledge (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Urquhart, 1997). It thus encourages the researcher to be flexible and creative (Sarker et al., 2000) while imposing systematic coding procedures (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). The first step (open coding) involved the data being examined ‘line by line to ascertain the main ideas. These were then grouped by meaningful headings (informed by the constructs in Table 1) to reveal categories and sub-categories/properties. The next step (axial coding) is the process of determining hypotheses about the relationships between a category and its subcategories, for example, conditions, context, action/interaction strategies and consequences. The focus then turned to the data to assess the validity of these hypothesised relationships. Relational and variational sampling (cf. Strauss & Corbin, 1990) was used to select data for this analysis. This process continued in an iterative manner and resulted in the modification of categories and relationships. Finally, selective coding was undertaken to identify the relationships between categories (constructs) using hypothesised conditions, context, strategies and consequences. Discriminate sampling (cf. Strauss & Corbin, 1990) was used to select data to examine strong and weak connections between categories. Furthermore, venting (cf. Goetz & LeCompte, 1984) was used as results and interpretations were formally discussed with marketplace representatives.

Our analysis provides empirical support for Proposition 1 and we thus conclude that strategic factors affect electronic marketplace performance. While the analysis does not find evidence that IT directly affects performance, the other two factors evident from extant research (marketplace process design and ownership) are evident in seven distinct strategic factors affecting performance. Specifically, marketplace process design is evident as bias, revenue model, product offering and personnel, while the strategic aspects of ownership are evident as investor objectives, investor commitment and governance efficiency. ## The impact of contextual factors on performance

Our analysis provides empirical support for Proposition 2 and we thus conclude that contextual factors affect electronic marketplace performance. Our analysis revealed that five contextual factors affected performance in the marketplaces studied; trading norms, competition, market conditions, maturity of the marketplace concept and participant characteristics. The manner in which these factors manifest themselves is shown as empirical indicators in Table 7, while how they impact performance in the marketplaces studied is discussed below.

Trading norms impact upon the performance of electronic marketplaces, especially those that operate in commodities. For these marketplaces, the difficulty is that participants have been trading commodities via bilateral contracts for decades. As a result of such norms, many participants are unwilling to migrate to marketplaces to procure goods. This is reflected in many instances in lower than anticipated buyer and supplier numbers, traded volumes and transaction numbers.

Competition impacts upon the performance of all the electronic marketplaces included in this study. This is reflected in buyer and supplier numbers, transaction numbers, volumes traded and tender success rates of electronic marketplaces. Competition for electronic marketplaces comes in two guises: (1) electronic marketplaces; and (2) other forms of competition. Marketplaces which facilitate the trade of indirect goods face intense competition from each other. Competition among electronic marketplaces (IBX, Proceedo) in the Nordic is very intense as they operate in a market characterised by low market penetration levels. For example, only seven public tenders seeking e-procurement solutions were published in the Nordic markets in 2003. In 2004, the sales cycle in this market according to Proceedo’s Vice President was approximately ‘15 months long from tender through to a decision to adopt’. This is why both Proceedo and IBX assess their tender success rate. For those electronic marketplaces which operate in the commodities market, bilateral contracts represent the largest share of the market. This negatively impacts upon the number of buyers and suppliers adopting the marketplace and volumes.

Market conditions impact upon the marketplaces operating in commodities markets (Comdaq, Dealcotton, Globalcoal and Nordpool) in terms of the number of buyers and suppliers (specifically speculators), transactions and volumes traded. Market conditions are affected by unanticipated market shocks and production conditions. Unanticipated market shocks affect the number and types of traders utilising electronic marketplaces. The study revealed that market shocks have resulted in an increase in commodity prices and consequently a reduction in the number of speculators operating on marketplaces financial markets, as well as a reduction in volumes traded on both physical and financial markets. Production conditions, and levels of demand/supply for the product being traded, have also impacted upon volumes traded. The number of buyers/suppliers, together with volumes traded, in turn impact upon revenues earned by electronic marketplaces.

The greater the level of maturity of the marketplace concept in the marketplaces target sector, the better the marketplace performed. For example, for Proceedo, the maturity of the electronic marketplace concept among private sector organisations in the indirect goods market (in its primary market, Sweden) greatly impacted upon Proceedo’s adoption levels (buyer and supplier numbers). Even by 2001, after three years operating in the market, Proceedo’s Vice President argued that the market had not as yet reached a level of maturity whereby the business community understood the concept of an electronic marketplace and what it delivered. Proceedo management argued that one of the reasons for the lack of awareness of the role and functions of electronic marketplaces relates back to the late 1990s. At this juncture, there was a large number of venture capitalist backed entities on the market calling themselves different things such as emarkets, electronic marketplaces and service providers. Proceedo’s Vice President stated that ‘there was a lot of confusion among market participants of what exactly an electronic marketplace was’. Proceedo’s Vice President argued that this lack of acceptance and understanding of the electronic marketplace concept among private sector organisations in Sweden made it very difficult to establish and define a market for electronic marketplaces and accordingly ‘greatly inhibited our performance, especially in trying to get buyers to sign up’.

## The impact of fit on performance

Analysis revealed that the concept of fit (operationally defined as the compatibility of one factor with another) is useful in helping to further explain electronic marketplace performance, and we find support for Proposition 4 that the fit between strategic and contextual factors affects performance. Specifically the fit was evident as the fit between:

1. Product Offering (strategic) and Trading Norms (contextual);

2. Revenue Model (strategic) and Maturity of the Marketplace Concept (contextual); and

3. Investor Objectives (strategic) and Maturity of the Marketplace Concept (contextual).

Proactive actions by marketplaces to improve the fit between their product offering and trading norms have resulted in improvements in performance. Analysis revealed that providing functionality that does not take into account trading norms in their marketplace’s market sector has a negative impact upon electronic marketplace performance. For example, both Dealcotton and Globalcoal designed auction-based trading systems, with the objective of bringing transparency to trading in the cotton and coal markets, respectively. Yet in both these sectors, the trading norms are dominated by the use of bilateral contracts and trade brokers to procure goods. Many of the large suppliers and buyers were reluctant to commit volumes to marketplaces as they believed that transparency impacted upon the price they received/ paid. Therefore, the transparency which both these marketplaces provided, impaired their performance, reflected in volumes, the number of buyers and suppliers adopting the marketplace and profitability. As a result, Dealcotton decided in 2002 to abandon the auction mechanism and develop a workflow management solution for mill-to-merchant trading. This solution could be configured to handle a contract negotiation workflow including: original offer, counter bid, counter offer, counterparty acceptance, counterparty rejection, finalised contract, PDF contract document, logistics and insurance. The cotton industry supply chain has traditionally been notorious for the amount of paper generated and the lack of standardisation in relation to transactions. By utilising generic templates of the workflow management solution, Dealcotton hoped to improve standardisation in the supply chain and reduce transaction costs for participants. According to management, the introduction of the workflow management solution was one of the key reasons for the improvement in volumes and increased buyer/supplier adoption on Dealcotton post-2002.

Analysis also revealed that the fit between the revenue model and the maturity of the marketplace concept impacted performance. All the electronic marketplaces studied found that choosing an appropriate revenue model and pricing strategy was critical to performance. The revenue models adopted by these marketplaces have evolved over time, predominately as a result of the degree of maturity of the electronic marketplace concept, and in an effort to improve performance. For example, Dealcotton initially adopted a transaction-based model but due to the immaturity of the electronic marketplace concept, management switched to a subscription-based model in late 2002. Dealcotton management reported that this change increased acceptance among buyers and suppliers. As a result of this, management found it much easier to attract new buyers and suppliers to the marketplace.

Analysis also reveals that the fit between investor’s objectives and the maturity of the marketplace concept, impacts upon electronic marketplace performance. Management in Comdaq, Dealcotton, Eutilia and Proceedo agreed that when their marketplaces were set up, electronic marketplaces were an emerging phenomenon with marketplace designers having few successful marketplaces in their respective industries to observe. The owners didn’t have input into designing their electronic marketplace’s value proposition; nor was their motivation for investing in the electronic marketplace explored by marketplace designers. Since their inception, the investor structure changed in Dealcotton, Eutilia, Proceedo and IBX. These changes in investor structure can be partially attributed to the maturity of the electronic marketplace concept. The immaturity of the electronic marketplace concept in the utilities sector is reflected in certain investors’ motivations for joining Eutilia: these involve either the hope of financial gain or the fear of being left out. However, it was the opinion of management at Eutilia that in certain instances these investors didn’t fully understand the role which Eutilia played in the sector. Eutilia’s performance suffered as a result. This brought about a change in ownership, whereby in 2003 there was a reduction in the number of investors from the original 11 to 6. The six remaining members were all committed to utilising Eutilia for procurement purposes. As a result, Eutilia’s performance improved as illustrated in an improved number of suppliers (2542 in 2003 to 4500 in 2004) and revenues (an increase of 30% between 2003 and 2004) earned by Eutilia. This contrasts with the IBX case. Both Ericsson and SEB (the initial investors) accepted and understood IBX’s role in the marketplace. IBX’s Communications Manager revealed that the belief within these organisations that electronic marketplaces represented an ideal mechanism for procuring goods meant that they, together with the existing investors, were happy for Novo Nordisk to become involved as an investor in 2003. This design helped to contribute to the increase in the number of buyers and sellers utilising IBX in 2003 and 2004.

## Conclusion

This paper explored how the performance of electronic marketplaces is impacted by strategic, contextual and structural factors, and presented a theory of electronic marketplace performance (see Figure 1). We utilised a theory building process consistent with the work of Dubin (1969), Whetten (1989) and Wheeler (2002). First, we began with an empirically observable phenomenon that couldn’t be adequately reconciled using extant research/theories. Second, we conceptualised the phenomenon and laid the basis for data gathering by using extant literature in several fields to (i) bound the theory; and (ii) delineate constructs and relationships in the form of propositions. Finally, we used a multiple case study research design to test our propositions, derive empirical indicators for our constructs and specify hypotheses.

The performance of electronic marketplaces is of scientific and practical interest for a number of reasons. First, electronic marketplaces facilitate exchanges with a greater variety of suppliers and customers than hierarchical or direct market relationships, and thus may have a profound impact on the competitive positioning and performance of organisations in a variety of business sectors. Second, electronic marketplaces are particularly effective as market system intermediaries when knowledge is dispersed among network participants. Electronic marketplaces, thus, directly address key challenges in the exchange of products and services in a wide variety of emerging physical and digital business networks such as peer-to-peer markets and IP exchanges. Third, the high failure rate of electronic marketplaces indicates the need to understand how the performance of market system intermediaries can be improved.

Overall, electronic marketplaces, as market system intermediaries, are essential network orchestrators when knowledge is dispersed among participants in an interorganisational network. Thus, while the use of digital networks to mediate inter-organisational relationships may continue to be dominated by traditional market and hierarchical distribution networks, the phenomenal growth of market system intermediaries such e-Bay and Amazon (in mediating physical markets) and Innocentive and NineSigma (in IP markets) indicates that value-added services provided by an intermediary are likely to be as important as the digital infrastructure in enabling the dynamic and smart inter-firm relationships envisaged by IOS researchers and practitioners.

## Implications for practice

We have demonstrated that the performance of electronic marketplaces can be assessed by examining three financial and seven customer-oriented measures. In addition, we have identified 13 strategic, contextual and structural factors that affect performance and have shown how each of these affect various financial and customer measures.

Following on this, we see two specific, actionable implications of the current study. First, the wide variety of measures used by electronic marketplaces to assess their performance may be unnecessary. Of the 26 measures that were used by the eight marketplaces studied here, only 10 proved significant in assessing the performance of the marketplaces. Second, those who operate electronic marketplaces should pay particular attention to the 13 factors identified by the study as affecting performance. By demonstrating how improving the fit between strategic and contextual factors results in better performance, we provide marketplace managers with insights on how they can improve their marketplaces performance. Therefore, the performance of electronic marketplaces may be improved by ensuring a good fit between strategic and contextual factors.

## Implications for research

The theory building process utilised in the study proved to be highly effective for leveraging extant research from several disciplinary perspectives to help develop new insights on inter-organisational networks. This study has implications for researchers whose focus is on designing marketplaces and electronic market business models. It provides conclusive evidence that in order to optimise marketplace performance, a marketplace’s strategic factors must be aligned with contextual factors.

This study extends our understanding of electronic marketplace performance by illustrating the specific performance indicators which marketplace factors impact. In examining the factors that impact upon performance, previous research (e.g., Choudhary et al., 1998; Kambil & Van Heck, 1998) reports that the competition facing an electronic marketplace impacts upon performance. We confirm this and illustrate that competition impacts upon electronic marketplace performance in terms of the number of buyers and suppliers adopting the marketplace, number of transactions and volumes traded. Trading culture has been previously reported (cf. Kambil & Van Heck, 1998; Son & Benbasat, 2007) as impacting upon electronic marketplace performance. We build upon the concept of trading culture (focused on social interaction) to a broader set of trading norms and illustrate that trading norms impact upon electronic marketplace performance as reflected in the number of buyers and suppliers, the number of transactions and volumes traded through electronic marketplaces. Previous research (e.g., Lennstrand et al., 2001; Son & Benbasat, 2007) found the success of an electronic marketplace to be related to buyers buying into the vision of an electronic marketplace. This study extends this concept by illustrating that the level of acceptance and understanding (maturity) of the electronic marketplace concept among market participant’s impacts upon marketplace performance in terms of the number of buyers and suppliers adopting the marketplace and transaction numbers.

Previous research (Lennstrand et al., 2001; Grieger, 2003; Byungjoon et al., 2007) utilised the concept of electronic marketplace ownership to categorise electronic marketplaces, and illustrated that ownership impacts upon entry and promoted content. This study has revealed three distinct aspects of ownership of electronic marketplaces; investor structure and objectives, investor commitment and governance efficiency. We conclude that investor objectives affect both investor commitment and governance efficiency, which in turn impact the performance of electronic marketplaces as reflected in volumes, buyer and supplier numbers, transactions and revenues.

Analysis of the existing electronic marketplace research revealed that the expertise and knowledge of electronic marketplace personnel was not identified as being important in relation to marketplace performance. However, our study illustrates the importance of electronic marketplace personnel, and particularly how their expertise, knowledge and contacts impact upon marketplace performance, reflected in the number of buyers and suppliers adopting the marketplace and a marketplace’s revenues.

The area of electronic marketplace performance now requires further research. In particular, we call for research to test the applicability of the performance model to other marketplaces. The further step in this research process would be the operationalisation of the refined research model to help electronic marketplace managers improve their marketplace’s performance. This would require an action research approach and would necessitate a researcher being involved in the decisionmaking process in order to bring about controlled change of specified variables. However, such research may be difficult to conduct due to practical limitations; with management in many marketplaces perhaps unwilling to grant such access due to their need to control their marketplace.

Finally, there is a need for further research on market systems intermediaries. In particular, there is a need to fully explore the role played by intermediaries in overcoming the limitations of hierarchical and market relationships by aggregating market knowledge, and the systems that facilitate this role. Also, there is a need to examine market system intermediaries in emerging contexts such as peer-to-peer exchanges (e.g., eBay) and IP markets (e.g., Innocentive).
