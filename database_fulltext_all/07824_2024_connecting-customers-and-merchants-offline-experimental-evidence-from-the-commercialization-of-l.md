# CONNECTING CUSTOMERS AND MERCHANTS OFFLINE: EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FROM THE COMMERCIALIZATION OF LAST-MILE STATIONS AT ALIBABA<sup>1</sup>

Brian Rongqing Han Department of Business Administration, Gies College of Business, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Champaign, IL, U.S.A. {brianhan@illinois.edu}

Tianshu Sun Cheung Kong Graduate School of Business, Beijing, CHINA {tianshusun@ckgsb.edu.cn}

Leon Yang Chu Cheung Kong Graduate School of Business, Beijing, CHINA {yzhu@ckgsb.edu.cn}

Lixia Wu Department of Artificial Intelligence, Cainiao Smart Logistics Network Ltd., Zhejiang, CHINA {wallace.wulx@cainiao.com}

Many e-commerce platforms have established extensive networks of stations as their last-mile logistics infrastructure. This study investigates how this last-mile infrastructure may serve as an offline platform to connect customers and merchants in the physical world by leveraging walk-in traffic (organic interaction) and prompting interested customers through online intervention (induced interaction). Using free sample distribution as an example, we designed two large-scale studies in collaboration with Alibaba—an observational study across 1,032 stations and a randomized field experiment among 189,019 customers— to examine the causal effects of organic and induced interactions on customers’ subsequent online purchases from the focal brands, respectively. We found that induced interaction drives significantly more online sales than organic interaction. Under induced interaction, the online intervention effectively increases the number of free samples distributed. Nevertheless, the more significant increase in online sales for induced claimers is not simply due to more free samples distributed but because the induced customers are more interested and more likely to purchase. We identified this phenomenon as a screening mechanism that facilitates an advantageous selection of customers claiming the samples. Customers willing to pay the additional travel cost to claim samples are more likely to purchase the focal brands afterward. Finally, we developed a customized targeting framework using a generalized random forest model to enhance the effectiveness of induced interaction at the last-mile stations. Our study raises a key insight that the “foot-in-the-door” traffic in an omnichannel environment can be fundamentally different depending on whether the offline customers are driven from the online channel.

Keywords: Offline platform, online-offline, last-mile, randomized field experiment, induced interaction, generalized random forest

## Introduction

Major e-commerce platforms such as Amazon, Alibaba, and JD.com have invested in an extended network of offline infrastructure at the last mile to meet their logistics needs. For example, Cainiao Network—Alibaba’s logistics subsidiary— has established over 30,000 last-mile stations across China. Amazon has also deployed hub lockers in over 900 cities in the U.S. (Amazon, 2022). These stations are embedded within neighborhoods and make it easy for customers to pick up or drop off packages for shipping at their convenience, which has significantly relieved the burden of last-mile logistics.

Beyond its crucial logistics functions, the last-mile infrastructure may also serve as an offline intermediary for commercial activities. For instance, the platform can distribute physical promotional items such as free samples from its online merchants/brands<sup>2</sup> to the last-mile stations. Walk-in customers can then claim free samples, i.e., through organic interaction. The offline-to-online literature (Avery et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2016; Zhou et al., 2019) has shown whether and how offline retail stores drive online sales, for example, through store engagement (Kumar et al., 2019). Similarly, the last-mile stations can also function as an offline infrastructure to connect online merchants with customers, which may increase online sales.

Alternatively, platforms can leverage mobile notifications to nudge nearby customers visit the stations. Some interested customers might be induced from the online channel to claim free samples offline, i.e., through induced interaction. The mobile targeting literature has shown the effectiveness of mobile ads in influencing customers’ offline behavior, especially when the stores are nearby (Hui et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2013; Fong et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017). Therefore, the induced interaction may also increase offline traffic and, consequently, online sales.

We thus ask: Are these two types of interaction different from each other? If not, how should the platform target different customers? This paper posits that induced interaction is an innovative way of combining the unique advantages of online and offline channels in a complete cycle (Figure 1). It uses online intervention (as an online touchpoint) to inform nearby customers (based on the offline environment) about free sample promotions at last-mile stations (i.e., offline infrastructure). Interested customers can then make purchases at the online channel (i.e., online conversion). Previous studies have not integrated and investigated the cross-channel effects in a complete cycle. Therefore, it is important to investigate organic and induced interaction and compare their effectiveness in driving online sales. Using free sample distribution at last-mile stations as an example, we seek to provide empirical evidence on whether and why organic interaction and induced interaction drive online sales differently. The insights gained from this study can help us to understand the value of offline infrastructure and how to manage online intervention to influence offline behavior in the omnichannel world (Wiesel et al., 2011; Bijmolt et al., 2021).

Although both types of offline interactions are expected to increase online sales, a key observation suggests that they differ from each other in a nontrivial way. Take the free sample distribution as an example. Since organic sample claimers (customers who claim free samples regardless of online notification) are exposed to events at the stations, it is easy for them to claim free samples. In contrast, induced claimers (customers who claim free samples if and only if targeted by online notification) are informed through their online app when they are away from the stations. Therefore, induced claimers must decide to incur additional travel costs. The fact that the induced claimers must self-select to claim free samples suggests that they may have different (observed or unobserved) characteristics, on average. Such self-selection behavior could either increase or not affect online sales of the focal brand. The induced claimers are either attracted by the “freebie” or are genuinely interested in the product and have a stronger intention to experience the focal brand (advantageous self-selection). If, for instance, advantageous self-selection outweighs “freebie” behavior, online notifications would further highlight the value of the last-mile stations. These stations could then be seen as serving as a screening device to specifically attract customers who are more interested and thus more likely to make purchases after claiming the sample (Figure 1).

We are interested in two research questions: First, is organic interaction at the logistic infrastructure effective in driving online sales? Second, compared to organic traffic at last-mile stations, would induced traffic be more effective in driving online sales, and why? In close collaboration with Alibaba— one of the world’s largest e-commerce platforms—and several major cosmetics brands, we conducted two large-scale studies (one observational study and one randomized field experimental study). Since every customer had to scan a QR code to claim a free sample, we were able to obtain detailed customer-level data on offline station visits and online purchases. We conducted an observational study to estimate the causal effect of organic interaction on the online sales of focal brands and a randomized field experiment to estimate the causal effect of induced interaction, investigating the difference between the two interactions.

In the first study of organic interaction, we investigated eight free sample distribution campaigns by different brands between March and June 2018. Customers claimed a total of 139,204 free samples across 1,032 last-mile stations. Since we were unable to control for customers’ organic walk-in behavior, we could not randomize customers’ organic sample claiming behavior. Therefore, we had to rely on the observational study to estimate the causal effect for organic claimers. Specifically, we tailored a two-stage matching procedure coupled with a difference-in-differences (DID) analysis. The results suggest that organic interaction slightly increases customers’ online purchases from the focal brand. For example, purchase probability, i.e., purchase conversion rate, over the next five weeks increased by 0.05%. We also adopted alternative matching methods such as coarsened exact matching and verified the robustness of our results. We show that the organic traffic at last-mile stations is not as valuable as that in offline retail stores, possibly because of a lack of interest/purpose in customer visits. In other words, the insights from the offline-to-online literature in the retailing context cannot be naturally extended to our case. A customized solution would be needed to further increase the effectiveness of offline promotions in increasing online sales.

In the second study of induced interaction, we designed a large-scale randomized field experiment with a significant brand across 40 stations in August 2018. We identified all customers (189,019) in the vicinity of these stations and randomly notified 80% of them on their mobile Taobao apps about the free sample distribution. The random assignment created an exogenous increase in customers’ awareness of the event, which resulted in 14% more sample claims. Based on the local average treatment effect (LATE) framework (Imbens & Angrist, 1994; Angrist et al., 1996), we estimated the conversion rate for induced claimers at 3.8%, much larger than that for organic claimers. We conducted various robustness checks on the underlying assumption of the LATE framework, which yielded consistent results.

Moreover, we again used a randomized field experiment to identify the underlying mechanism. Based on the LATE framework, we propose a way to systematically test the existence of induced claimers’ self-selection behavior, finding that the advantageous self-selection mechanism outweighs “freebie” behavior (Figure 1). In other words, we found that induced customers from the online channel are more valuable than organic offline visitors and are more likely to purchase after claiming free samples. This provides an essential insight into one key role of this last-mile infrastructure. That is, when an online notification is introduced to boost offline traffic, the physical infrastructure can serve as a screening device and attract a subgroup of more interested customers to engage with the focal brands (i.e., the online-to-offline-to-online effect). Furthermore, the advantageous self-selection cannot be explained by customers’ offline characteristics (such as selfpickup, parcel shipping, or location) or online behavior (such as visits, item views, or purchases). This suggests that customers from the online channel self-selected to claim free samples offline based on unobserved preferences for the focal brand.

Our results provide important insight for brands seeking to promote physical products through free sampling, which incurs actual costs related to providing the products (in contrast to digital free samples). Instead of arbitrarily distributing free samples to customers, our findings indicate that it is more effective to induce customers through online intervention and impose some costs on them for claiming free samples. Doing so can help limit the number of free samples distributed and, consequently, the associated costs while also encouraging a healthy amount of advantageous selection to improve the effectiveness of free sampling.

Finally, the complete cycle in Figure 1 generates rich data on the complete customer journey. While the platform cannot control customers’ organic visits (or the number of free samples given away), it can control which customers are informed by the online notification. Such flexibility allows the platform to target particular segments of customers online based on their omnichannel behavior in a complete cycle, thereby maximizing its effectiveness. Although we were unable to identify individual induced claimers, we were able to estimate the LATE of each customer conditional on a customer being an induced claimer. We further developed a practical targeting framework based on a state-of-the-art machine learning technique—generalized random forest for instrumental variable regressions—to illustrate how the platform can use targeting to induce the “right” customers and maximize returns for a focal brand. Our key idea is that the “right” customers should correspond to customers who are (1) likely to be induced online to claim samples and 2) likely to make a purchase afterward. We found that both customers’ offline and online characteristics are important in predicting the heterogeneous causal effect. Given the predicted return for each customer, the platform can strike a balance between cost (the total number of samples distributed) and returns (the predicted total number of purchases). In this way, the last-mile infrastructure can be seen as an offline platform for connecting customers and merchants. As today’s online marketplace is becoming increasingly saturated, <sup>3</sup> this new commercial channel presents an opportunity for e-commerce platforms to help merchants reach customers offline.

Before proceeding, we summarize our key managerial insights to facilitate data-driven decision-making for the offline platform:

The effect of organic interaction on online sales at the last-mile stations is not economically significant. The offline-to-online effect, in this case, cannot be taken for granted, and a customized solution is needed.

By introducing online intervention, the platform can effectively induce customers through an advantageous self-selection mechanism, which can, in turn, generate a significant increase in customers’ online engagement with the focal brand. The last-mile infrastructure essentially serves as a screening device due to the additional travel cost, thereby specifically attracting interested customers.

Using online data, the platform can use targeting to induce a particular set of customers by considering both customers’ sample claim decisions and purchase decisions in order to further maximize the effectiveness of induced interaction.

## Literature Review

There is a long line of research studying online and offline channels in various contexts. Our work mainly contributes by integrating all features of the online and offline channels in a complete cycle.

Specifically, the online-offline literature has studied the interplay among four unique features. The offline channel consists of physical infrastructure (e.g., stores, pickup stations) and environment (e.g., social environment, physical location). The online channel serves as a touchpoint (e.g., platforms, online ads, mobile targeting) to connect people and is an important medium to enable customer conversion (e.g., online stores). We can then categorize previous studies into several streams based on how these features are discussed across the online and offline channels (Table 1).

The first stream of literature examines the interaction between offline stores and online stores, focusing on the differences between the online and offline channels regarding, for example, product quality (Overby & Jap 2009), information provision (Gao & Su, 2016), or demand elasticity (Avery et al., 2012).

The second stream of literature examines the directional impact of the offline channel on the online channel, particularly online stores. These studies provide a comprehensive understanding of how offline stores complement or substitute for online stores (Soysal et al., 2019), which may depend on the popularity of the brand (Wang & Goldfarb, 2017), product assortment, pricing (Tang et al., 2016), or customer segments (Kumar et al., 2019).<sup>4</sup> One crucial phenomenon discussed is “showrooming,” where consumers visit a physical store to experience or inspect a product and, if they like it, then buy it from an online seller, often at a lower price (Bell et al., 2015; Gao & Su, 2016; Bell et al., 2017; Mehra et al., 2017; Kuksov & Liao, 2018). Others have studied the offline environment and discussed how e-commerce interacts with offline retail regarding cross-channel competition (Brynjolfsson et al., 2009), pricing (Overby & Forman, 2015), market efficiency (Subramanian & Overby, 2017; Nault & Rahman, 2019), or online word-of-mouth (Sun et al., in press). Note that papers from the first and second literature streams focus on, at most, two key features of the online and offline channels.

The third stream of literature focuses on the online-to-offline effect and can integrate more than two features of online and offline channels. For example, there is a rich literature leveraging the entry of platforms (e.g., Airbnb, Craiglist) to examine the economic and societal impact of the online marketplace on offline activities (Chan & Ghose, 2013; Seamans & Zhu, 2013; Greenwood & Agarwal, 2015; Greenwood & Wattal, 2017; Zhang & Li, 2017; Alyakoob & Rahman, 2019). These studies examine the interplay between the online platform (as a touchpoint to connect) and offline local activities (i.e., environment). Others have studied how online interventions such as online ads (Dinner et al., 2013; Lewis & Reiley, 2014), informational websites (Pauwels et al., 2011), or email (Zhou et al., 2018) affect offline sales <sup>5</sup> . More recent studies have shown that mobile targeting is particularly effective in influencing customers’ offline behavior (Andrews et al., 2016; Ghose et al., 2013, 2019), especially when stores are nearby (Hui et al., 2013; Fang et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2013; Fong et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017). Such studies have also incorporated offline environmental elements to investigate the online-to-offline effect in retailing.

As shown in Table 1, the induced interaction in our study is novel in two ways. First, the previous literature has not been able to integrate all the identified advantages/features of the online and offline channels. In contrast, we use mobile notification (as an online touchpoint) to inform customers about promotions at last-mile stations (i.e., offline infrastructure) nearby (based on the offline environment). If they like the product, they can make purchases on the online channel (i.e., online conversion). Such integration creates a unique type of online induced customer that has not been identified in previous literature. Therefore, we creatively combine all unique features, which fully brings out the advantages of channel integration in omnichannel retailing. Second, existing studies have examined the cross-channel effect in one direction, either online to offline or offline to online. In our study, the cross-channel effect is recovered in a complete cycle. That is, the platform can initiate online targeting, observe offline behavioral changes, and collect data on online purchasing behavior for each customer. This allows e-commerce platforms to record a complete customer journey, which can help develop more personalized solutions. By clearly distinguishing and comparing induced customers with organic walk-in traffic, we found that the induced customers are more likely to purchase afterward due to additional travel costs. This shows that leveraging the offline location as a screening device to induce self-selection—only those interested in the free samples (induced customers) would accept the travel costs and visit the offline infrastructure. As a result, induced customers are generally more interested and valuable to brands. We expect that our proposed self-selection mechanism and the framework for testing such an effect also apply to the general context of omnichannel retailing (Bijmolt et al., 2021).

Finally, we contribute to the literature on customer acquisition and free sampling. Previous literature has investigated various approaches to acquiring new customers, depending on the direct or indirect channel of communication (Steffes et al., 2011; Datta et al., 2015), referral program (Schmitt et al., 2011), or pricing promotion (Lewis 2006; Iyengar et al., 2011). One effective pricing promotion is free sampling. The marketing literature on free sampling has mainly focused on how the acquisition is formed from the consumers’ perspective, focusing, for example, on their value generation process (Marks & Kamins, 1988; Heiman et al., 2001), churning behavior (Bawa & Shoemaker, 2004), or response to the promotion (Lammers, 1991; Datta et al., 2015). The information systems literature has mostly focused on the distribution of free information products online (Chellappa & Shivendu, 2005; Cheng & Liu, 2012; Lee & Tan, 2013; Niculescu & Wu, 2014). More recently, Lin et al. (2019) studied the rating bias effect of distributing physical products online from the perspective of the e-commerce platform. However, much less attention has been paid to the design of information and logistics for the free-sampling of physical products from the firm’s perspective. We contribute to the free-sampling literature by exploring a new channel for distributing physical free samples offline and examining how customers should be informed about promotional campaigns.

Our results suggest that online intervention combined with offline distribution (through the last-mile stations) offers a unique advantage because the additional travel costs associated with offline sampling help to reduce “freebie” behavior.

## Causal Effect of Organic Interaction

As one of the world’s largest e-commerce platforms, Alibaba has built a large network of more than 30,000 last-mile stations (as of 2019) across China to accommodate the delivery and collection of tens of millions of packages daily. Leveraging the logistics infrastructure, we worked closely with the platform and experimented with its online merchants on a series of commercial activities with a focus on free sample distribution. In a typical free sample distribution, a merchant provides a batch of (10,000-100,000) free samples for offline distribution across a number of last-mile stations. The station owners place the free samples on the shelves together with a QR code banner. Any walk-in customer can claim one free sample by scanning the QR code using their Taobao app. Each sample claim was linked to the customer’s Taobao online user ID. (Please also see Figure A1 in Appendix A for pictures of free sample distribution at a station.<sup>6</sup>) Each campaign might last for a few weeks. On average, one station can distribute a few hundred free samples over two weeks. This section first investigates the causal effect of organic sample claims on the focal brands’ online sales.

## Experiment Setting and Data

In our study, organic visitors’ exposure to the free samples was, to some extent, exogenously determined. Specifically, all campaigns were conducted at the stations without any online advertising. Thus, sample claimers did not have prior knowledge about the specific campaigns taking place when coming to those stations (and when making purchases online prior to that). <sup>7</sup> Such exogenous assignment assisted in our identification of the causal effect. Nevertheless, a direct comparison between sample claimers and average Alibaba customers who did not claim free samples may be subject to bias. For example, sample claimers were more likely to be frequent station visitors (who were picking up packages more often), and they were more likely to be active online in general. Consequently, compared to the average customer, they were more likely to engage the merchant online after claiming free samples, regardless of the actual impact of sample claims. Thus, it is crucial to consider these differences in customers characteristics and past behavior (both offline and online) to rule out confounding explanations.

Combining multiple approaches documented in previous observational studies (Avery et al., 2012; Bell et al., 2015; Kumar et al., 2019), we applied a series of econometric techniques (exact matching, propensity score matching, and difference-in-differences) to account for such bias at a customer level. To achieve this, we constructed a rich dataset to include a proper control group and capture possible confounding factors. First, we obtained all sample claims from March 2018 to June 2018, including eight brands from different stations. Because a typical station distributes a few hundred free samples in two weeks, we focused on the stations that distributed at least 20 free samples. <sup>8</sup> The resulting data consisted of 210,934 sample claims across 1,032 stations located in 20 cities and 15 provinces in China. Second, we included all customers who did not claim free samples but had visited one of the 1,032 stations within three months before the campaigns began, amounting to a total of 3,874,389 customers. In total, our raw data included over 4.08 million customers. Third, we appended a granular set of data, including customer characteristics, detailed online behaviors (purchases and item views), and a complete record of offline logistics services (self-pickup and parcel shipping) for each customer. Last, we constructed a series of outcome measures and built a proper control group through matching based on this large and rich dataset. To facilitate a multiperiod difference-in-differences (DID) analysis, we chose the customer-week as the unit of analysis. We then defined four outcome variables to measure the impact on the focal brands.

## Outcome Variables:

Brand purchase or not: a binary variable indicating whether a customer makes a purchase from the focal brand’s online store during a given week

Brand spending (log): a continuous variable measuring a customer’s total spending from the focal brand’s online store during a given week

Brand view or not: a binary variable indicating whether a customer visits the focal brand’s online store during a given week

• # of brand item views (log): a continuous variable measuring the total number of items viewed by a customer at the focal brand’s online store during a given week

All outcome variables were aggregated to a customer-week panel structure from 2018/02/05 to 2018/08/26 (24 weeks in total). The time span of our data sample ensured that we had observations for at least five weeks before and five weeks after the sample claim week for all customers.

## Two-Stage Matching

Recall that the DID analysis is built on the parallel trend assumption, which asserts that the difference between the treatment and control groups is constant over time in the absence of treatment. Leveraging a rich dataset, we combined exact matching and propensity score matching (Sun et al., 2016) to construct a control group such that its pre-treatment behavior and customer characteristics were identical to the treatment group to the extent possible.

We provide a detailed discussion of our two-stage matching procedure in Appendix B and summarize our overall idea here. In the first stage, we exactly matched a set of binary variables, including customers’ purchase and item-view behavior at both the focal brand and category levels. We also exactly matched customers’ self-pickup and parcel shipping patterns. Consequently, the pre-treatment trends of the two groups are the same regarding these binary variables. Nevertheless, exact matching cannot guarantee the pre-treatment trends parallel regarding the corresponding numeric variables. For example, the binary outcome “self-pickup or not” does not necessarily characterize how many packages were picked up, which may prevent the two trends regarding the number of self-pickups from being parallel. Moreover, the time-invariant characteristics (such as demographics) of the two groups may also be different. Thus, we adopted propensity score matching to further balance the treatment and control group over a larger number of features in the second stage. In this way, the matched groups not only had similar tendencies in their behavior but also comparable time-invariant characteristics, strengthening the validity of our identification.

After matching, we were able to find the best single match for a total of 139,204 sample claims distributed across 1,032 stations. We summarize the organic sample claims by each brand in Table 2. Importantly, the matched treatment and control groups now have the same past purchase, browsing, and station usage patterns (through first-stage exact matching) and indistinguishable propensity scores based on a wide range of characteristics such as demographics, past spending, and location (through second-stage propensity score matching).

To further quantify the causal relationship, we next present the DID analysis.

## Main Results on Organic Sample Claims

Following the literature (e.g., Manchanda et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2016), we specify our linear panel data model as below. Our main estimating equation is:

$$
\begin{array}{r} O u t c o m e _ {i t} = \alpha + \xi \cdot S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i} + \beta \\ \cdot S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i} \cdot A f t e r _ {i t} \\ + v _ {i} + \theta \cdot A f t e r _ {i t} + u _ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The variable Outcome<sub>it</sub> denotes the particular outcome of customer i at week t. The treatment group dummy SampleClaimer<sub>i</sub> is a binary variable indicating whether customer i claimed a sample during our study period (i.e., treatment status). <sup>9</sup> $A f t e r _ { i t }$ is a binary variable that indicates the posttreatment period for each treated user and the matched counterpart. <sup>10</sup> This variable accounts for potential temporal factors that may simultaneously influence the treated and control customers. In addition, $v _ { i }$ is the customer-specific fixed effect, and $u _ { t }$ is the week-specific fixed effect. Note that given the panel structure of our dataset, we utilized variation in the outcomes for each customer over time to control for pretreatment heterogeneity at a granular customer level. There was no need to include other time-invariant control variables. Moreover, the week-specific fixed effect controlled for any temporal outcome differences across individuals. Finally, we were interested in the estimated value of the parameter β (i.e., the DID estimate), which represents the average treatment effect on the treated, i.e., the average effect of organic sample claims. In our results below, we cluster the standard errors of $\dot { \epsilon } _ { i t }$ at each matched treatment and control pair to allow for heteroscedasticity.

We present our main results in Table 3. The DID estimates regarding all four outcomes are positive and statistically significant. However, the economic impact of organic sample claims is limited. For instance, it increases brand purchase probability by 0.01% (Column 1) and the number of item views by 0.24% (Column 4) each week, on average. In addition to our main DID model, we also carried out a series of robustness checks in Appendix C (for the parallel trend assumption), Appendix D (for the magnitude of the estimates), and Appendix E (using coarsened exact matching). Overall, our first study on organic interaction suggests that only leveraging the organic traffic at the last-mile stations for commercial activities could result in low returns. This finding is different from previous literature (Heiman et al., 2001; Marks & Kamins, 1988; Bawa & Shoemaker, 2004) studying the effect of free sampling at brick-and-mortar retailers. One of the possible explanations is that, in our case, organic customers do not visit stations to shop, i.e., for the focal brands. For example, less than 1% of the customers were existing customers who had made a purchase during the previous year. Converting random walk-in customers (who visit the stations for logistics purposes) to customers making purchases through sampling may not be as effective as it is in retail stores. More importantly, by allowing any walk-in customers to claim free samples, the platform cannot selectively target specific customers, which motivates alternative approaches to improve the effectiveness of offline interaction. Next, we investigate the causal effect of induced interaction in which customers are driven from the online channel.

## Causal Effect of Induced Interaction

In this section, we designed a large-scale field experiment to investigate (1) whether the platform can leverage online notification to causally induce more sample claims, and (2) the causal effect of such induced interaction, respectively.

## Experiment Setting and Data

We collaborated with a major domestic brand and launched a three-week free sample distribution across 40 stations in four major cities<sup>11</sup> in August 2018. We first identified all eligible Alibaba users in the vicinity of the stations (409,704 users in total) and randomly assigned 80% of them to the treatment group, which received an online ad in the Taobao mobile app about the free sample distribution at the nearby station. We then excluded customers who did not visit the particular page where we placed the ads (for both the treatment group and the control group). This resulted in a sample size reduction from 409,704 to 189,019. All the remaining customers were exposed to the particular page in the Taobao app. The treated customers were exposed to the online ad about free sample distribution, while the control customers were exposed to other ads unrelated to our campaign, which served as control ads (Lewis & Rao, 2015; Johnson et al., 2016b, 2017). Since the treatment assignment was completely randomized in our original data sample, we expected the assignment to remain randomized, conditional on page exposure.

For Table 4, we checked the balance of various pre-treatment covariates and confirmed the randomization. In this way, the ad created an exogenous variation of awareness (of the free sample distribution) among a group of customers who had a similar tendency to visit the app in general, which may further boost sample claims.

Finally, we defined the post-treatment period as five weeks after the free sample distribution ended. For each customer, we computed the outcome variables defined above in the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction section and aggregated them to a five-week post-treatment window. Thus, the only difference was that each outcome variable in this section covered five weeks, whereas the outcome variables in the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction section covered one week (because of the customer-week panel structure).

## Identification Strategy

We now leverage the randomized online notification to identify the causal impact of induced interaction, which can be broken down into two stages corresponding to the two causal effects mentioned earlier. In the first stage, we estimated the following linear model (Duflo et al., 2007).

$$
S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \cdot O n l i n e A d s _ {i} + u _ {i},\tag{2}
$$

where SampleClaimer<sub>i</sub> is a binary variable indicating whether customer i claimed a free sample and OnlineAds<sub>i</sub> is a dummy variable indicating whether customer i was notified with the mobile ads. The coefficient $\alpha _ { 1 }$ represents the causal effect of online notification on sample claims. To estimate the causal effect of induced sample claims, we first used the local average treatment effect (LATE) framework to formally define the induced claimers (Angrist et al., 1996). There are three types of customers: organic claimer, no-claimer, and induced claimer. The organic claimers are those who would always claim samples regardless of the online intervention.

(Recall that the organic claimers are also the central focus of the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction section.) The noclaimers are those who would never claim samples. The induced claimers are those who would claim free samples if and only if notified. These three types of customers (organic claimer, no-claimer, and induced claimer) correspond to the always-taker, never-taker, and complier in the LATE framework (Imbens & Angrist, 1994; Angrist et al., 1996). We are particularly interested in the causal effect for the induced claimers, i.e., the causal effect of induced sample claims.

Following the LATE literature, we carry out a two-stage least square (2SLS) estimation using OnlineAds<sub>i</sub> as an instrumental variable (IV) for SampleClaimer<sub>i</sub>

$$
O u t c o m e s _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i},\tag{3}
$$

where SampleClaimer<sub>i</sub> is the predicted value from Equation (2).

Given the exogenous treatment, the 2SLS estimation is built on two assumptions. First, the inclusion criterion requires that the IV (online notification) correlates with sample claims. This assumption holds if the first-stage causal effect is significant, which was shown to be the case, as discussed in the following results. Second, the exclusion restriction asserts that the online notification should not directly affect the final outcomes (e.g., online purchases). Our experiment meets this requirement for two reasons. First, the mobile ads did not provide a direct link to the focal merchant’s online store. Thus, the ads could not have directly affected customers’ online purchases or item views. <sup>12</sup> Second, the stations were not informed of the online intervention; thus, the control and treatment groups could not have received an additional notification or advertising apart from the online notification. Using the LATE framework, we investigated the LATE coefficient $\beta _ { 1 }$ , which represents the causal effect of sample claims for the induced claimers only (Imbens & Angrist, 1994; Angrist et al., 1996).

Nevertheless, it is not clear why induced interaction is more effective than organic interaction in increasing short-term sales for the focal brand. Leveraging our field experiment, we next investigate the underlying mechanism.

## Mechanism Underlying the Effectiveness of Induced Interaction: Self-Selection

Based on our discussion in the Introduction, the fundamental difference between the induced and organic interactions is that their sample-claiming processes were rather different. While organic claimers were exposed to the events at the stations, the induced claimers were informed through their mobile phones when they were away from the stations. Both had to scan the QR code at the station to claim free samples. If they liked the product, they could purchase it afterward at the focal brand’s online store. Therefore, the only difference was where they were informed about the free sample promotion.

Previous literature has shown that a coupon requiring shoppers to travel farther from their planned path resulted in a substantial increase in unplanned spending over a coupon for an unplanned category near their planned path (Hui et al., 2013). Here, organic claimers do not need to travel to claim free samples (after they are informed about the event), and there is no unplanned path. Induced claimers have to pay the travel costs associated with a previously unplanned path in order to claim a free sample. In our experiment, about 13.1% of the claimers in the treatment group claimed free samples without receiving any logistics services (i.e., self-pickup or parcel shipping) within a one-hour window. Thus, it is reasonable to conjecture that induced claimers may be a completely different group of customers from organic claimers, perhaps with higher purchasing power or interests.

Motivated by this particular observation, we propose a key mechanism—self-selection—as a plausible explanation for the behavioral difference between organic and induced claimers. Self-selection has its origin in information economics (Stiglitz, 1975) and asserts that individuals (e.g., job applicants, customers) may choose specific arrangements (e.g., labor contract, technology adoption) based on their observed characteristics or unobserved preferences (see Rothschild & Stiglitz, 1978, and De Meza & Webb, 2001, for references to a typical example in the insurance market). In our case, the fact that induced claimers must undertake additional trips to claim free samples indicates that they may have a stronger preference for the samples and may have significantly different (observed or unobserved) characteristics compared with organic claimers.

Moreover, such induced claims could either advantageously affect or have no effect on whether customers will purchase from the focal brand in the future. The induced claimers are either attracted only by the “freebie” or they are genuinely interested in the product and have a stronger intention to sample it (advantageous self-selection). Our findings in the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction and the Causal Effect of Induced Interaction sections above show that induced interaction is more effective in increasing customers’ purchases from the focal brand. Thus, it signals that advantageous self-selection outweighs “freebie” behavior. However, since these two studies are carried out for different last-mile stations in different time periods, it is not necessarily a fair comparison. Using the randomized field experiment, we next provide empirical tests to systematically investigate whether the observed or unobserved characteristics between induced and organic claimers drive the different outcomes and whether this is advantageous.

Recall that there are three<sup>14</sup> types of customers: organic claimer, no-claimer, and induced claimer. Based on the definition of these customer types, we can partition our study population into four groups (Table 7) based on their treatment status (whether they were notified with mobile ads) and first-stage decision (whether they claimed a sample).

In Table 7, we identify all the claimers in the control group (Group 3) as organic claimers because they claimed free samples even without being targeted. Furthermore, the claimers in the treatment group (Group 4) may consist of both induced claimers and organic claimers because both would claim free samples if targeted. <sup>15</sup> Although it is impossible to identify whether a claimer is individually organic or induced (Imbens & Angrist, 1994; Angrist et al., 1996), this partition is sufficient for us to identify the systematic difference between organic and induced claimers.

## Self-Selection Based on Observed Characteristics

To test self-selection based on observed characteristics, we first verified whether organic claimers and induced claimers were different in their observed characteristics and then examined whether such observed differences explained the outcomes. By directly comparing the pre-treatment covariates of Group 3 and Group 4 (Table 8), we found that the claimers in Group 4 had significantly more annual purchases on the platform and were less active at the stations. As the difference was only caused by the additionally induced claimers in Group 4, the induced claimers were generally more active online and less active offline, compared with the organic claimers. We then carried out a subsample analysis by partitioning our study population based on the median value of customers’ past platform purchases in Table 9. We observed that only “high spenders” were induced to claim samples in the first stage and that they were also associated with a significant increase in sales from the focal brand. This evidence suggests that the customers’ self-selection based on observed characteristics (past purchases) is likely to affect the outcomes in a positive way, i.e., advantageous self-selection.

## Targeting to Induce the “Right” Customers

So far, we have shown that the platform can systematically leverage the stations as a physical hub to induce self-selection and screen customers with strong preferences. However, such an advantageous self-selection mechanism cannot be explained by the observed characteristics (Table 10 and Table 11). It creates an additional challenge when the platform seeks to selectively target certain customers because not all customers being targeted (based on observed characteristics) will comply and claim samples offline. In this section, we take advantage of a state-of-the-art machine learning technique that can help estimate LATE at the individual level. Although we cannot identify individual induced claimers, we can estimate the LATE of each individual, conditional on a customer being an induced claimer. Below, we illustrate how the platform can leverage big data accumulated online to further enhance the effectiveness of offline interaction for a focal merchant and also to provide free samples to those yielding the greatest benefit.

Practically speaking, online ads often incur significant opportunity costs, given limited space in mobile apps. Physical free samples are also costly from the brand’s point of view. Therefore, it is important for platforms to target the “right” customers with the highest LATE. High-value customers should have to a higher likelihood of purchasing after claiming samples and a higher likelihood of claiming samples. Thus, we adopted a generalized random forest algorithm (Athey et al., 2019), taking into account both the first-stage decision and second-stage outcomes to accommodate such targeting. The algorithm was specifically designed to estimate the heterogeneous causal effect of induced sample claims (LATE) through instrumental variable regression. Please refer to Appendix H for an overview of the algorithm.

We trained a forest (with 4,000 trees) on our study population with the binary outcome variable “brand purchase or not” to predict the conversion of purchase behavior from the focal brand. We used all the covariates defined in Table 4, including customers’ online and offline characteristics. For example, we used the number of monthly pickup and parcel shipping instances to capture customers’ offline activities and used annual platform orders to capture customers’ overall engagement with the online channel. We expected both online and offline characteristics to be associated with the heterogeneity of LATE. We randomly split 70% of the data for training the generalized random forest. The other 30% of the data was used to predict the heterogenous LATE. We adopted the honest splitting rule, where the algorithm uses half of the training data for splitting and the other half for estimating the performance metric (mean squared error of the causal effect). We fine-tuned several important parameters that affect the growing behavior of each tree and the balance of splitting through 10-fold cross-validation. The parameters that governed the growth of each tree included the fraction of samples used by each tree, the minimum number of samples in each node, and the number of variables considered during each split. The parameters that affected the balance of splitting are the maximum imbalance of splits (measured by the percentage size of child nodes of parent nodes) and an imbalance penalty parameter controlling the extent to which imbalances are penalized.

![](/api/attachments/NQN775D4/fulltext/images/c82d6983243d131c4b42802fd82171efd73d43a88e6d04a0461e6a67593bdbfe.jpg)  
Figure 3. Top 5 Variables Explaining the Heterogeneous LATE

Before presenting the results, we adopted a heuristic approach suggested by Athey et al. (2019) to assess whether the algorithm can successfully detect the heterogeneous effect. Specifically, we grouped individuals into high and low LATE groups based on the predicted effect on the test dataset. The high LATE group comprised those whose predicted effect was larger than the median. The low LATE groups comprised those whose predicted effect was lower than the median. We then estimated the low and high groups’ average LATE (with standard deviation). Let $( \hat { \tau } ^ { h } , \hat { \tau } ^ { l } )$ and $( \hat { \sigma } ^ { h } , \bar { \hat { \sigma } } ^ { l } )$ be the average estimated effect and standard deviation of the two groups. We can then compute the 95% confidence interval for the difference in the average effects:

$$
[ \hat {\tau} ^ {h} - \hat {\tau} ^ {l} - 1. 9 6 * \sqrt {(\hat {\sigma} ^ {h}) ^ {2} + (\hat {\sigma} ^ {l}) ^ {2}}, \hat {\tau} ^ {h} - \hat {\tau} ^ {l} + 1. 9 6 * \sqrt {(\hat {\sigma} ^ {h}) ^ {2} + (\hat {\sigma} ^ {l}) ^ {2}} ].
$$

The result is [0.0018, 0.0500], which does not cover zero. In other words, the generalized random forest was able to estimate significantly different heterogeneous effects. The difference in the average effects between the two groups ranged from 0.00018 to 0.0500 with 95% confidence level. For a typical random forest-based method, we obtained the importance of each variable (in explaining the variations of LATE) by counting the number of times a variable was used for splitting. We present the top five most important variables in Figure 3. The most important variable is station parcel shipping, as one of the key offline characteristics, while the second most important variable is monthly spending in the category, which measures customers’ interests. Our findings suggest that these variables are the most useful in explaining the heterogeneity of LATE across different customers.

Finally, we plotted the distribution of the predicted LATE on the test dataset in Figure 4. Table 6 shows that the average LATE is estimated to be around 3.8%. The machine learning algorithm effectively identified those who had higher LATE from those with lower LATE. For example, there was a relatively small proportion of customers with LATE estimates larger than 4%. This means that if they had been induced to claim free samples, the free samples would have increased their purchase probabilities from the focal brand by more than 4%. Based on our results, by optimizing the subpopulation to target with online ads, the platform can effectively strike a balance between cost (the total number of samples distributed) and return (the predicted total number of purchases).

## Conclusion and Discussion

In summary, we investigate a new way of connecting customers and merchants in a complete online-offline cycle. Our results introduce a key insight that the “foot-in-thedoor” traffic in an omnichannel environment can be fundamentally different depending on whether the offline customers are driven from the online channel. We show that offline infrastructure helps facilitate an advantageous selfselection mechanism when customers are induced by online intervention. Our study highlights the value of offline infrastructure and the potential for firms to leverage online data to manage offline activities as a platform. We expect that this phenomenon can be generalized to other cases in the omnichannel retailing setting as well (see, for example, Zhang et al., 2019).

Our analyses of the induced claimers also provide important practical insights. Based on Table 7, the size of induced claimers is about 14% of the organic claimers. As shown by the LATE estimation, a small proportion of interested customers can generate significant returns associated with free sampling. That is, for every 10 free samples claimed by walk-in customers, there will be one induced claimer interested in the focal brand who will be more likely to make a purchase. Such an observation suggests a few actionable insights to improve the returns of free sampling. For example, platforms could use a stronger communication method to advertise offline promotions, such as text messages, social media, or messages through the app. This can increase customers’ awareness of the promotional events and increase the proportion of induced claimers (compared with organic claimers), eventually improving the returns of free sampling. Alternatively, the platform could decrease the proportion of organic claimers by hiding free samples in stations (as a “secret menu”). In this way, only customers who receive the ad will know about and be able to claim free samples, which can effectively prevent freebie behavior. Importantly, the above-mentioned approaches do not require the use of user information for personalization, which may be preferable for privacy concerns.

Establishing local logistics infrastructure is a fast-growing trend for e-commerce platforms seeking to relieve the burden of last-mile delivery. At the same time, it is equally important to develop a smart logistics network in the offline world and generate additional value from the spared capacity of the logistics infrastructure, according to Jack Ma—Founder of Alibaba (Shijia, 2018). Overall, our proposed idea of an “offline platform” is exciting and opens up new opportunities for e-commerce platforms such as Amazon, JD, and Alibaba, who manage their own logistics network to leverage the lastmile infrastructure as a new intermediary for various offline commercial activities. Beyond practice, we believe that our study could lead to several directions for future research.

First, we focus on interventions (e.g., free sample distribution) that are purposefully created to benefit the platform’s online merchants. Nonetheless, with proper design, offline interaction may also benefit last-mile logistics itself. For example, the platform could distribute coupons to motivate customers to return products to the stations, which could dramatically reduce logistics costs for e-commerce platforms (Lee & Whang, 2001). We expect this to be effective because, even in the case of our particular intervention (i.e., offline sample claims are designed to benefit online merchants rather than increase station usage), we also documented a significant offline spillover. We carried out additional analyses to assess how organic sample claims affect the usage of last-mile stations in Appendix F. Interestingly, we found that the weekly number of packages picked up and shipped from the stations increased by 2.39% (p < 0.01) and 0.6% (p < 0.01), respectively. Thus, it is vital for future research to explore the best intervention (e.g., offline distribution or online distribution) to improve last-mile logistics efficiency. Generally speaking, in today’s omnichannel world (Brynjolfsson et al., 2013; Bell et al., 2014), we believe that such connection and integration of the online channel and the offline infrastructure are extremely promising for improving the logistics process itself.

Second, our study mainly focuses on how to target induced interaction using random assignment at the customer level. However, the platform can also selectively target particular stations for organic interaction. Future research could conduct field experiments at the station level and collect a rich set of station characteristics to address this question. We hope that our experiment and proposed targeting method can provide a framework to address the optimal selection of last-mile stations. Future research could also examine how platforms could combine customer-level and station-level data to maximize the effectiveness of offline interactions. An important question is how the customer-level findings could be integrated with station-level data, such as geographic location, to facilitate station selection in a data-driven manner.

Finally, the Targeting to Induce the “Right” Customers section illustrates our targeting approach in a retrospective way based on a well-executed field experiment. The study population in a single free sample distribution event was used for both the estimation and evaluation of the targeting methods. However, in practice, the platform often has to rely on historical data for decision-making. For example, the merchants in past free sample distribution events might be different from the those participating in a new campaign. Or we may not have the luxury of carrying out randomized experiments every time a new intervention is proposed (e.g., induced interaction for in-station sales). As a result, new campaigns may impose new challenges, forcing the platform to rely on limited historical data or experiments to facilitate targeting decisions. It is also imperative for platforms to incorporate uncertainty into their decision-making (Gupta et al., 2019; Kallus, 2017). Nevertheless, our study takes the first step and demonstrates the value of data-driven decisionmaking for offline platforms.

## Two-Stage Matching from the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction Section

In this section, we describe our matching procedure in detail and provide evidence of its effectiveness. To account for the imbalance of treatment and control groups regarding multiple dimensions, we first define three sets of main matching variables in Table B1 to capture the direct impact on the focal brands, online spillover at the category level, and offline spillover regarding utilization of station services, respectively. The direct impact is the same as our main outcome variables. To quantify the online spillover, we computed customers’ total spending in the same product category. To quantify the offline spillover, we measured customers’ usage of logistics services at the station (i.e., self-pickup and parcel shipping) based on the number of packages. Similar to our definition of outcome variables, we used both binary and numeric variables to characterize each measure. For instance, “Self-pickup or not” is a binary variable indicating whether a customer has picked up any packages at the station, whereas “# of self-pickup” corresponds to the number of packages picked up.

## Appendix C

Parallel Trend and Long-Term Dynamics from the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction Section

Besides our main model, we employed an alternative specification for two purposes. First, the specification allowed us to directly test whether the main effects appeared before the treatment (sample claim) week as a key robustness check.<sup>17</sup> Second, we leveraged the new specification to inspect the long-term dynamics of the causal effect of organic sample claims. For each customer, we first included data for exactly 5 weeks before and 10 weeks after sample claim week (16 weeks in total) and constructed new panel data. The new panel data allowed us to estimate the leads/lags model

$$
\begin{array}{r l} {O u t c o m e s _ {i t} = \alpha + \sum_ {\tau = - 5} ^ {- 1} \beta_ {\tau}. S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i}. L a g _ {t + \tau} + \sum_ {\tau = 1} ^ {4} \beta_ {\tau}. S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i} + L e a d _ {t + \tau}} \\ {+ \beta_ {5}. S a m p l e C l a i m e r _ {i}. A f t e r _ {i t + 4} + v _ {i} + u _ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t}} \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where Lead and Lag are the corresponding leads and lags of the time dummy variables indicating week t. This specification effectively break down the DID estimates by week. The coefficients $\beta _ { - 5 } \dots \beta _ { - }$ <sub>1</sub>measure the difference in a particular outcome between the control and treatment groups for each week prior to sample claim week, respectively. (The sample claim week serves as the baseline week.) If the parallel trend assumption holds, we would not expect them to be statistically significant from zero (Autor, 2003). Moreover, this specification also estimates the long-term effects over ten weeks. Specifically, we were also interested in the coefficients $\beta _ { 1 } \ldots \beta _ { 5 } .$ . The coefficients $\beta _ { 1 } \ldots \beta _ { 4 }$ measure the short-term effects from one week to four weeks after the sample claim week, respectively. The coefficient $\beta _ { 5 }$ measures the longterm effects after the fourth week, i.e. from the fifth week to the tenth week after sample claim week. By examining the pattern of thes coefficients, we can explore the long-term dynamics of the causal effect.

We present estimation results in Table C1. Recall that, according to the design of our matching procedure, the pretreatment trends regarding the binary outcomes must be the same. We further plot the estimates for the numeric outcomes in Figure C1. All the estimated lag coefficients are close to zero, which further assures that the treatment and control groups have parallel trends before the treatment. As such, the parallel trend assumption is justified in our case, which provides the validity of our approach.

Moreover, the graph presents the dynamics of causal effects in a revealing way. Specifically, the focal brand purchase (brand spending) is increased only in the short term during the first three weeks after sample claims but stays relatively flat in the long run. Furthermore, th effect of organic interaction on the number of item views remains significant over the long run.

## Coarsened Exact Matching from the Causal Effect of Organic Interaction Section

## Exclusion Restriction

Our identification of the LATE in the Causal Effect of Induced Interaction section relies on the assumption that the treatment (online ads) does not directly affect sales with the focal brands, i.e., the exclusion restriction assumption. We believe that the assumption is likely to hold in our setting. One of the main reasons is that the particular ad about the free sample promotion did not provide a link that directs to the online stores of the focal brands. For the online ads to affect sales directly, customers would need to search for the focal brand on their own and then make a purchase. With this in mind, we provide three ways to test the exclusion restriction assumption in Table G2.

First, we acquired additional data on the sales of the focal brands at indirect sales channels on the C2C platform.<sup>18</sup> If the online ads directly increased the sales of the focal brand, we would also expect increases at the indirect channel because many individual sellers on the C2C platform of Alibaba would carry products from the focal brands. When customers search and shop for the focal brand, these items from the C2C platform will appear along with the brands’ flagship online stores on the B2C platform. We report the ITT and LATE estimates in Columns 1 and 2 of Table G2. We found that there was no significant increase in sales of these related products.

Second, we carried out a subsample analysis in Column 3 of Table G2. If the online ads directly increased the sales of the focal brand, we would expect that the treated customers who have not claimed free samples have more brand spending, compared with control customers who have not claimed free samples. So, we can test whether this is the case by regressing the outcome variable (brand spending) on the treatment status (OnlineAds) for those customers who have not claimed samples. As we can see, there is no significant effect. It suggests that among customers who have not claimed samples, the online ads do not drive more treated customers to purchase from the focal brand than control customers.

Third, it is reasonable to believe that the effect of online ads (if existing) will appear sooner, compared with the effect of free sampling promotion. In our main result (Table 6), we computed the outcome variables, skipping one week after the campaign ends. In Column 4 of Table G2, we recomputed our outcome variable skipping two weeks after the campaign. We found the LATE estimated effect (0.175) is positive and significant. The magnitude of the estimate is also close to our main results in Table 6 (0.208). It suggests that the estimated effect of induced sample claims was not likely driven by the online ads.

Besides these empirical tests, we also note that we did not find a significant increase in item views in our main results (Table 6). This suggests that the sales increases were likely driven by the customers’ experience with the physical free sample. Overall, our results suggest that the exclusion restriction is likely to hold and does not affect our findings.

## Interference From Other Channels

As described in the Causal Effect of Induced Interaction section, the stations were not informed of the online intervention, so the control and treatment groups could not have received an additional notification or advertising apart from the online notification. Thus, we specifically designed our experiment to prevent interference from affecting the treatment group and control group differently.

The only possible interference could happen between the interaction of the two groups. For example, the treated customers might inform customers in the control group about the free sample promotion after seeing the online ads. Nevertheless, such interference can only happen if the customers have personal connections with each other. Although our experiment was carefully designed, we acknowledge the limitation that we were not able to track whether such an interference happened offline. Moreover, we also note that the interference may not have affected our LATE estimation significantly. The LATE estimate of a randomized control experiment can be considered the ratio of the intention-to-treat (ITT) estimate and the first-stage estimate (Angrist et al., 1996). If, for example, some treated customers informed the control customers about the free sample promotion, it would potentially cause more customers in the control group to claim samples, and these customers would then essentially be induced claimers. So, our first-stage regression may underestimate the true effect of online notification. At the same time, the ITT estimate (as a direct comparison of the outcome between the treatment group and control group) may also underestimate the true ITT effect. As a result, the interference may equally affect both the numerator and denominator of the LATE estimate.

## Causal Forest and Generalized Random Forest

Causal forest (Wager & Athey, 2018) and generalized random forest (Athey et al., 2019) are two state-of-the-art machine learning methods for estimating heterogeneous causal effects. Both methods build on the seminal paper (Athey & Imbens, 2016) that proposes to use treebased models (Breiman, 2001) to partition the data into subpopulations that differ in the magnitude of their treatment effects. We provide an overview of the methods below.

Let (Xi, Yi, Zi) denote the features, outcome, and randomized treatment status of a sample i. Here, Zi corresponds to whether an online ad is used to notify the customers. A single causal tree (Athey & Imbens, 2016) partitions the feature space into a set of leaves L and estimates the causal effect for feature x using

$$
\hat {\tau} (x) = \frac {1}{| \{i : Z _ {i} = 1 , X _ {i} \in L \} |} \sum_ {\{i: Z _ {i} = 1, X _ {i} \in L \}} Y _ {i} - \frac {1}{| \{i : Z _ {i} = 0 , X _ {i} \in L \} |} \sum_ {\{i: Z _ {i} = 0, X _ {i} \in L \}} Y _ {i}
$$

Athey and Imbens (2016) also propose an honest splitting rule in the decision tree where one subset of the data is used for splitting, and another is used only for estimation. This is because a causal tree optimizes splitting based on the mean squared error (MSE) of the causal effect, which is unknown by its nature. An independent subset of the data is needed to estimate the MSE without interference from the splitting rules. Causal forest (Wager & Athey, 2018) generates B number of such trees, each of which produces a set of estimates. It then aggregates the predictions by averaging them. We can use the causal forest to estimate the heterogeneity in the ITT effect. It allows us to estimate the heterogeneous causal effect of the online notification on customer purchases from the focal brand. The causal forest has recently attracted increasing attention from the IS literature (Luo et al., 2019).

The generalized random forest (GRF) (Athey et al., 2019) further extends the original random forest (Breiman, 2001) into a flexible method for estimating any quantity identified via local moment conditions. Therefore, causal tree and causal forest are special cases of GRF. One of the key applications of GRF is the estimation of heterogeneous treatment effects with instrumental variables. It is helpful when a compliance issue is present. In our case, not all customers (Zi = 1) who received the online ads claimed free samples. Let Wi {0, 1} denote whether a customer claims a free sample. Our average LATE estimate can be expressed as Cov[Yi,Zi]/Cov[Wi,Zi]. The generalized random forest can estimate the conditional LATE:

$$
\tau (x) = C o v [ Y _ {i}, Z _ {i} | X _ {i} = x ] / C o v [ W _ {i}, Z _ {i} | X _ {i} = x ],
$$

based on moment conditions derived from the IV assumption.

Overall, the main difference between GRF and classic random forests is how the quality of a split is determined. A classic random forest seeks to measure the quality of splits based on information gain (for classification problems) or prediction error (for regression problems). GRF, on the other hand, seeks to maximize the difference in treatment effect estimates between the child nodes. We adopted machine learning methods to estimate the heterogeneous causal effect, which provides important managerial implications for targeting.
