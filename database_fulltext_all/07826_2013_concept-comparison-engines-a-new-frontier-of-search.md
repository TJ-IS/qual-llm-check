## a b s t r a c t

In a traditional search engine interaction scenario, a user begins with a certain concept and <sup>fi</sup>nds documents that are similar to their concept. However, the user may wish to compare alternatives and a search capability should compare concepts and present the best alternatives. This task can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult without proper decision aids. We propose a concept comparison engine as a decision support tool that may be used to compare attributes of different alternatives and aid in making an informed selection. We describe an architecture and an interaction scenario and implement a prototype. We propose a number of evaluation metrics for measuring the viability of different terms for the purpose of comparing concepts. In scripted experiments, orderings for candidate terms from the prototype are compared to gold standard ranking lists from structured external sources. Our results indicate that a Rankor analysis may be promising as a measure of the differentiating power of candidate terms a user might choose to support concept comparison.

## 1. Introduction

The decision making process often involves de<sup>fi</sup>ning the problem, gathering the data, building a model, generating alternatives, and selecting a good alternative [24]. However, with the overwhelming volume of free-form textual information available on the internet, it is extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to process the avalanche of results from a single search, let alone compare alternatives. Research on the use of decision support systems for preferential choice tasks has found that users seek to minimize their overall expenditure of effort, even if this negatively impacts decision quality [49]. Users frequently stop early before a comprehensive search and analysis is complete [9]. Furthermore, users quickly become overwhelmed by the number of available alternatives, unless the large number of alternatives can be easily accessed by attribute values [28].

Modern search engines are great at giving relevant information on one topic, but they are not properly designed for comparing among alternatives. For example, while Google and Yahoo are excellent for <sup>fi</sup>nding relevant documents on the state of Virginia, they are not particularly suited for comparing Virginia to Alaska. Current search engines give the user some information on the target item: for instance, links to the state's home page, tourism pages, and wiki pages. However, often in human decision making, the merit of a solution can be understood only when it is compared against other alternatives. For example, while someone may say that Virginia is a great place to do business, the question remains, compared to what? Also, on what basis are you comparing the states—population, climate, transportation, natural reserves like coal and oil? In this paper, we propose a framework, which attempts to shift the search engine decision support task from solely presentation of relevant information on a single topic to providing a decision aid for comparison of multiple topics.

Consider MountaineerGear, a (<sup>fi</sup>ctitious) manufacturer of mountain climbing gear, looking to expand its network of direct-to-consumer outlet stores. MountaineerGear uses a traditional search engine to <sup>fi</sup>nd some subjective information from popular journalistic sources (e.g. articles on “the best places to go mountain climbing”). However, this journalistic information is imperfect: it may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by editorial biases (pandering to paying advertisers), or may simply be hamstrung by each journalist's limited expertise. After some research, MountaineerGear determines that quantitative information about mountains and mountain climbing is available from governmental and commercial data sources. For example, government data providers like the United States Geological Survey can provide the locations of the 100 highest peaks in the country, but this tells MountaineerGear where mountains are, not where people like to go to climb mountains. Commercial data providers can provide the locations of all competitor stores that are listed under the industry classi<sup>fi</sup>cation NAICS category 33999 (“Climbing and Rappelling Gear”). This would show where some mountain-climbing-related stores are currently operating, but not where are the good future locations. For an in-depth comparison MountaineerGear might consider using a search engine to gather documents from each candidate location. But, each candidate location search uncovers dozens of documents. MountaineerGear must synthesize the information from the document collection for each candidate location, and then attempt to compare locations against each other. The process is arduous and likely to be biased by eventual search fatigue. Inadequate review of locations could lead to high losses or high opportunity costs. A concept comparison engine (CCE) would allow MountaineerGear to compare candidate locations. We propose that an automated comparison of locations, showing the extent to which web pages in each location refer to “mountain climbing,” could provide a rapid and informative “heat-map” of mountain climbing enthusiasts. Such a comparison is outside the gamut of traditional decision support tools, but would be useful, and complementary to the above data sources.

In Table 1, we suggest a number of sample scenarios where a CCE may be helpful. In each case, the prospective user and their goal are speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. We describe this as a two-step process. In Step 1, we describe what alternatives the user would compare (i.e. the concept list), and what attributes the user could use to compare these alternatives, using the CCE to analyze both structured (Step 2a) and unstructured (Step 2b) data sources. For instance, in the MountaineerGear example (Example 1 in Table 1), the marketing manager uploads a list of US cities (available from the United States Geological Survey) and compares them by both population (Step 2a), and the relative term frequency for “mountain climbing” in each city (Step 2b). In Example 2 in Table 1, a high school graduate may wish to <sup>fi</sup>nd an affordable college with a strong business ethics program. The graduate would upload a list of AACSB accredited schools to the CCE (Step 1) which may be part of a spreadsheet from the AACSB which has tuition fees for each college (Step 2a). The CCE would then gather a document collection for each college on the AACSB list, and allow the graduate to <sup>fi</sup>nd the term frequency for “business ethics” for each candidate college (Step 2b). The mash-up of structured tuition fee data (Step 2a) and unstructured program content information (Step 2b) helps the graduate compare colleges. Finally, in Example 6 in Table 1, a College Career Services Director for University X may be looking to build recruitment relationships with the best companies. The director may use a CCE to choose companies within a 150 mile radius that have the high revenues or pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, and have alumni amongst their listed employees (i.e. employees that specify “University X” on their web or social media pages). More detailed information about the CCE implementation, including a full worked example (Example 4 in Table 1) listing concepts to compare, evaluation criteria, data collection from structured and unstructured sources, and comparing the alternatives, is provided in the Online Supplement to this paper.

The CCE is intended to provide a layer of additional functionality on top of traditional search, to reduce total expenditure of effort in preferential choice tasks and improve decision quality [49]. The CCE is also intended to allow alternatives described in unstructured text to be more easily accessed by attribute, potentially improving perceived usefulness of the DSS [28]. In short, the CCE is aimed at easing the process of not just gathering information (as in traditional search), but also of comparing alternatives.

In light of the need for mechanisms to automatically compare concepts that are described in both free-form text documents and in tabular data sources, we make a number of contributions. Firstly, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne a model for concept comparison from text documents, and describe an architecture and implementation for this new type of DSS. Secondly, we propose a number of CCE evaluation metrics, and evaluate the applications and limitations of the CCE through various experiments.

Section 2 brie<sup>fl</sup>y describes the related work in the information retrieval and information extraction <sup>fi</sup>elds. Section 3 illustrates the proposed CCE model. Section 4 describes and illustrates an interactive decision support session where the user works with the CCE on a “choice-between-alternatives” decision making task. Section 5 shows the general architecture of a CCE and Section 6 remarks on our speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementation. Section 7 speci<sup>fi</sup>es metrics for determining the plausibility and validity of the comparisons produced by the CCE. Section 8 reports the results of a large number of experiments with the CCE system and explains the implications of this experience for how CCE's should be built, con<sup>fi</sup>gured, and used.

## 2. Related work

Information retrieval (IR) on the web, commonly known as “web search,” is the process of gathering documents relevant to a particular concept, and ranking them by relevance [50]. The output is a ranked list of search results (pointers to relevant documents). A search on “Boston Population,” for instance, may yield millions of documents relevant to Boston and its population. In information extraction (IE) [13,14,19,29], relevant facts are extracted from available documents, to <sup>fi</sup>ll in the blanks in a user's knowledge. For example, the query “Boston population” might result in the fact “Boston population is 589,141.” We distinguish concept comparison (CC) as a value-added feature on top of retrieval and extraction. Comparing Boston and Philadelphia, by population, for instance, requires <sup>fi</sup>rst retrieval of relevant documents (discussing the population of both cities), and then extraction of the pertinent facts, before comparison can be operationalized. The state of the art in information retrieval and information extraction has advanced rapidly in recent years. Commercial and academic search engines, which once only listed matching documents ranked by relevancy, are now able to classify or cluster results by category, show number of hits by category, supply domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collections, compute aggregate statistics on document collections, and extract structured data from text documents. We review these features in the following subsections.

## 2.1. Search engines using document classification or clustering

Automated document classi<sup>fi</sup>cation involves the automated assignment of documents to categories, and has been well-studied over the past half-century [2,7,11,27,31]. Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation may be guided by a training set of manually tagged documents [30,40,42,45], or may be entirely machine-directed [16,38]. Categorization may be universal, or personalized to the user [18,51]. Most often, the document classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is used to disambiguate search results. Search results are partitioned by topic to allow the user of the web search engine to rapidly locate a pertinent document in the user's intended subject area, thereby reducing information overload [17,23,54]. For example, documents containing “Chicago” may be separated into the categories “Movies” and “Places,” to allow users to quickly <sup>fi</sup>nd documents about the movie Chicago, or the place Chicago.

Document clustering is also often used to give the user rapid insights into the content of a document collection. For instance, a user of yippy.com, a popular document clustering engine, may observe that documents about <sup>fi</sup>shing are clustered into those that discuss “<sup>fi</sup>shing lures,” “<sup>fi</sup>shing rods,” “freshwater <sup>fi</sup>shing,” and other clusters. Document classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is therefore useful for both <sup>fi</sup>nding documents and understanding (exploring) document collections [33]. However, while document classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is helpful for <sup>fi</sup>nding documents in different categories, it is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for comparing items of a similar type, as facts (attributes) for each concept still need to be extracted. Faceted search engines, described next, provide an interface for exploring items by both class and attribute.

## 2.2. Faceted search engines

Faceted search engines [25,43] allow the user to rapidly see how many search results are in each category, and drill-down to view items in each category. Search results may be grouped into numerical bands (e.g. “handbags b\$100,” “handbags \$100–\$500,” “handbags > \$500”) or into textual categories (e.g. handbags by brand name, handbags by color). With faceted search, objects are <sup>fi</sup>rst catalogued by annotating them with attribute values or classi<sup>fi</sup>cations, to facilitate navigation through or exploration of the catalogue. Annotations may be manually assigned or automatically assigned using information extraction techniques. Objects in the catalogue may be documents, web pages, products, books, artworks, recipes, images, people, houses, or other items [25].

Faceted search techniques are frequently used in product catalogues (e.g. Amazon.com; Zillow.com) or comparison shopping engines (CSEs) – e.g. Google.com/products, pricegrabber.com, [55] – for browsing, <sup>fi</sup>ltering, and sorting products by brand, price, and other attributes, typically using a left-hand navigation tree for this purpose. The catalogue of items to compare is provided by the system, not de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the user. The items listed in a CSE are typically restricted to commercial products with a limited set of well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned attributes like price, merchant, brand, and user ratings. For services, such as <sup>fl</sup>ights or hotel stays, a variety of CSEs also exists (e.g., Travelocity.com, Hotels.com). Comparing non-product concepts, like cities, businesses, or people – all of which have unusual attributes – is not possible with a CSE. Consider the example given in the Online Supplement to this paper, of the marketing manager of a coal mining company attempting to get market insights into relative demand for a new coal mining technology in different locations. The marketing manager cannot simply feed “Mead, West Virginia” and “Los Angeles, California” into Google Products because non-product concepts are not catalogued in CSEs. The Online Supplement describes how the marketing manager can instead use a CCE to complete the comparison. Domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c catalogues (e.g. hoovers.com) specialize in comparing a certain type of entity (e.g. comparing corporations), but are unable to compare noncatalogues items (e.g. cities or products). Concept comparison may therefore be viewed as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant generalization of the narrower <sup>fi</sup>elds of Comparison Shopping and domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c catalogues.

## 2.3. Concept-specific search engines

Two modern search portal creation techniques are helpful for creat ing concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collections. With vertical search engines [15], the user builds a domain-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collection by specifying a seed URL and (optionally) keywords that must, or must not, appear in the document collection. For example, a user wanting documents about Chicago may gather all documents from the seed URL choosechicago.com (the of<sup>fi</sup>cial visitors' guide for Chicago). Or they may search Google for “Chicago [AND] Illinois [BUT NOT] movie” which would hopefully gather documents for the place, Chicago, rather than the movie, Chicago. With a focused search process [12], the user provides an exemplary document about a concept (e.g. exemplary document describing the place “Chicago, IL”), and the search engine collects similar documents. Both vertical and focused search are helpful for users wishing to explore a single concept, since users can rapidly assemble a document (or snippet) collection speci<sup>fi</sup>cally relevant to that concept. However, comparison of multiple concepts using these techniques is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, since multiple separate collections would need to be gathered, and then manually analyzed.

## 2.4. Aggregate analysis of document collections

Aggregate analysis of multiple document collections has been tackled by various authors. Qualitative data analysis [41] involves the collection of text documents, followed by coding, and visualization. For example, word frequency analysis may be used to analyze user comments on new movie releases, for the purposes of ranking movies by expected popularity. Alternatively, aggregate statistics on, for instance, word frequency for documents associated with different items in a museum archive, can give insights into the content of the archive [21]. Aggregate statistics for categorized documents, such as inbound emails, can be computed in order to determine correlations [47,48]. For example, an analyst could determine whether particular product complaints are associated with a particular reseller. Itemization of alternatives to compare, followed by the gathering of both raw and aggregated facts about those items (from both textual and tabular data sources), for the purpose of comparative analysis of the items, requires a framework for further research and analysis.

## 2.5. Extracting structured data from text

Much previous work has been done on extracting structured information from text, sometimes referred to as information extraction [13,14,19,29]. For example, XML templates can be populated with facts extracted from documents, and a hybrid query language based on XQuery and SQL has been suggested to allow the hybrid fact-set to be queried [3]. The information extracted from text is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the existing database schema. The intention is to facilitate the framing of structured queries on a combination of textual and structured data.

Markup languages, often XML-based, are frequently used to annotate text and allow the extraction of structured data from such text. For example, structured data can be easily extracted from documents annotated with XBRL [53], a business and <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting markup language. This data can then be used for performing comparisons. In deed, new rules from the United States Securities and Exchange

Commission (SEC) require large corporate <sup>fi</sup>lers to provide their data in XBRL format on the web [46]. The SEC speci<sup>fi</sup>cally cites improved comparability as a rationale for this requirement. However, the SEC mandate applies only to United States public companies and foreign private issuers, and therefore covers only a small subset of global corporations. Furthermore, the mandate covers only a limited portion of corporate data: primarily <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements prepared in accordance with United States Generally Accepted Accounting Practice (GAAP).

Ontologies provide a way to describe concepts and their properties and relationships, and can be expressed using semantic web [5] markup languages. Popular semantic markup languages include Resource Description Framework (RDF) [w3c.org/rdf], Web Ontology Language (OWL) [w3c.org/owl], and, more recently, lightweight “rich snippet” markup notations (such as microformats, microdata, and RDFa). For example, the hProduct microformat and GoodRelations RDFa format, both supported by Google, allow product attributes, such as brand, category, and price, to be tagged within web pages. RDF, OWL, “rich snippet” markup notations, and other XML-based knowledge representation languages allow the expression of ontologies and semantics, and improve our ability to extract structured data from annotated text. While their usage is growing fast, only a small percentage of web pages are currently tagged with semantic markup: Yahoo estimates only 3.6% of 12 billion web pages sampled in October 2010 contain any microformat or RDF markup [35]. A more general concept comparison method, not dependent on the existence of semantic tags, is therefore needed. We propose a simple theoretical model of concept comparison engines next.

## 3. Theoretical model

Traditional search engines assist with the ranking of documents and based on this ranking, the documents resulting from the search are displayed to the user. In contrast, a CCE assists with the comparison of concepts. For our model, we make the following de<sup>fi</sup>nitions. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a concept as an item, of a certain type, for which we would like information, in order to compare to other items of the same, or a similar type. The information about a concept is held in its attributes. Attributes for a concept can be inferred from an appropriately-gathered document (or snippet) collection for that concept. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a document collection as a set of one or more documents which possess explicit or implicit information on a concept. Information about the concept may be explicit, but distributed across one or more documents in the document collection. Alternatively, information about the concept may be implicit, and need to be aggregated from multiple documents.

Let C denote a concept i, described in multiple documents. Let D be a document collection describing C . Let X and Y be the values of attribute X and attribute Y, for concept i. The value of each attribute can be inferred from the contents of an appropriately gathered document collection for that concept, where $\operatorname { F } _ { \mathrm { X } }$ is a function that de<sup>fi</sup>nes the mapping from the document collection to the attribute value, for attribute X such that ${ \Chi } _ { i } = { \sf F } _ { \mathrm { X } } ( { \sf D } _ { i } )$ . Similarly, for attribute $\mathrm { Y } , \mathrm { Y } _ { i } { = } \mathrm { F _ { Y } } ( \mathrm { D } _ { i } )$ ). A concept can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a vector of its attributes: $\mathsf C _ { i } = ( X _ { i } , \mathsf Y _ { i } , \ldots )$ . Similarly, a second concept, j, can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as $\mathsf C _ { j } = ( X _ { j } , \mathsf Y _ { j } , \ldots )$ . When viewing only two attributes, concepts can be plotted on a Cartesian plane, and more attributes can be imagined on higher dimensional polytopes.

## 4. Description of an interactive, concept-comparison, decision support session

A concept comparison engine is an interactive decision support system. Fig. 1 illustrates a user interaction session. For each concept that the user wishes to compare, the search space that results from the massive amount of available documents makes comparison dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult, potentially causing information overload. However, many of these documents are irrelevant and the more the search space contains irrelevant documents, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it becomes to do an effective comparison. So, interactively, the decision aid allows the user to specify some measure of likelihood that the results satisfy the criteria. As shown in $\mathrm { F i g . }$ 1, we operationalize this concept by having the decision aid ask the user to specify a desired con<sup>fi</sup>dence level that determines the likelihood that the results have a high chance of being relevant. This process narrows the document search space to fewer, pertinent documents. As illustrated in Fig. 1, documents $\mathsf { D } _ { 1 }$ and $\mathsf { D } _ { 2 }$ remain after the user has chosen a narrow con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval, but document ${ \sf D } _ { 3 }$ is eliminated.

The CCE extracts attributes for each concept, from the document search space. Here, four concepts $\{ \mathsf C _ { 1 } , \mathsf C _ { 2 } , \mathsf C _ { 3 } , \mathsf C _ { 4 } \}$ are being compared across two attributes (dimensions X and Y) which the user has chosen. After the con<sup>fi</sup>dence level is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed and the search space is reduced, the decision aid will eliminate the concepts that are inferior based on the values of the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed attributes. For example, assuming that higher values of each attribute are better, a user who wants to compare concepts based on two attributes can disregard all that are dominated by the solutions that are on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient frontier [34]. In Fig. 1, the items (concepts) on the ef<sup>fi</sup>cient frontier deserve serious consideration while others are sub-optimal and can be eliminated, further reducing the search space, and making the selection of the best alternative easier. In the scatter plot in Fig. 1, item ${ \sf C } _ { 2 }$ dominates item $\mathrm { C } _ { 3 } \mathsf { a s C } _ { 2 }$ has a higher value for Y, and identical value for X. The CCE can therefore eliminate the dominated solution, $\mathsf { C } _ { 3 } ,$ from further consideration, leaving only the items $\{ \mathsf { C } _ { 1 } , \mathsf { C } _ { 2 } , \mathsf { C } _ { 4 } \}$ under consideration. The user considers the merits of each item, on a variety of comparison bases (e.g., attributes $\mathrm { X , Y , Z , \ldots ) }$ and chooses $\mathsf C _ { 1 }$ as their favored alternative.

This user session with a CCE demonstrates interesting differences from a traditional search engine interaction process. Notably, a search engine narrows a search space of documents. In comparison, the CCE begins with a search space of documents, but proceeds to extract concepts and attributes, allowing the user to compare concepts, not documents, and select the preferred alternative from a more manageable search space. Various <sup>fi</sup>lters imposed by the user and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency considerations interactively minimize the search space. As an example, a user looking to compare two locations may start searching through documents on the web using a traditional search engine, like Google. The automated decision support in a conventional search process ends with a set of documents describing each location. In contrast, with a CCE, decision support continues by extracting concepts and attributes, and the result is a tabular or graphical comparison of the two locations, rather than merely a set of documents. The user then compares the items on a variety of comparison bases, and chooses their preferred alternative.

## 5.1. Project configuration

The user begins with project con<sup>fi</sup>guration. During this process, the user de<sup>fi</sup>nes the set of concepts they wish to compare. Concepts should generally be of a single type, or at least share some similar attributes. For example, the user may wish to compare companies, cities, people, or other types of concepts. The user may supply their own shortlist of items (i.e., concepts), or may obtain a list of alternatives to compare from an external data source (e.g., list of companies, list of cities, list of people). Optionally, the user may use a hierarchical tree-view, or an imported classi<sup>fi</sup>cation scheme, to group items by category (e.g., public vs. private companies), to facilitate later analysis.

## 5.2. Spiders

After the user provides the list of concepts, the system responds by triggering spiders to collect topic-speci<sup>fi</sup>c documents pertaining to each concept. A search engine factory eases the creation of multiple document collections. With the search engine factory, the user speci<sup>fi</sup>es wildcards and parameters, or uses custom scripts, to rapidly create multiple separate collections of concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c documents. For example, the user may request the ten most relevant documents for all cities in all states. The DSS would interact with the user using a graphical user interface to start a search engine factory which would repeatedly execute the following query, with appropriate values substituted for CITY and STATE each time the search query is executed:

$$
\text { url } = \text { http }: / / \text { www.google.com } / \# q = [ \text { CITY } ] \% 2 C + [ \text { STATE } ], \text { docs } = 1 0
$$

This will cause the search to iterate through all cities, and create a restricted document collection for each speci<sup>fi</sup>c city, by obtaining only the 10 most relevant search result documents for that city, from Google's search results. This simple technique effectively creates multiple concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document repositories—one for each unique city (similar to undertaking focused crawling for each city, but using a query expression, rather than an exemplary document, for each city). As we report later, the experimental data suggest that while it is not the optimal, the technique we use is satisfactory for obtaining documents relating to each city; the city name quali<sup>fi</sup>ed with state makes it suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently clear to Google that the user is requesting, for instance, documents describing the location “Chicago, Illinois,” rather than irrelevant documents describing other types of concepts with similar names, such as the movie “Chicago.”

## 5.3. Attribute skimmers

In the architecture proposed in Fig. 2, the attribute skimmer component processes each concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collection and associates values with attributes for each concept. The attribute skimmer parses the concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collections and uses information extraction techniques to extract numeric, categorical, and aggregate information. In what follows, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y describe the technical foundations of the architecture via an example.

For numeric attributes, a numeric attribute extractor extracts units (e.g. “177”) and, if possible, units of measure (e.g. “meters above sea level”) that describe the dimension (“altitude”). This attribute (i.e. dimension) must be associated with the concept itself (e.g., Chicago, Illinois) rather than an unrelated concept mentioned in the document. Ideally, the attribute value extracted should be very relevant (i.e. speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the particular concept itself), recent (i.e. not dated or expired), and authoritative (i.e. from a credible source). Numeric attribute extraction requires good named entity recognition (<sup>fi</sup>nding the right concept), good attribute matching (<sup>fi</sup>nding the right attribute, for the right concept) and value extraction (<sup>fi</sup>nding the right value for the attribute). This information extraction process is an extremely hard challenge, and has been the subject of ongoing research by many authors for a number of years. Approaches vary from simple (shallow) regular expression matching, template (frame) matching, and pattern matching to complex (deep) logical and linguistic rules. See [4,13,14,36,37,52] for a discussion of state-of-the-art information extraction methods.

Fig. 3 provides a sample output from the TextRunner system [4] for numerical attribute value extraction. The query we executed here requested the “population” attribute of “Boston.” Notice that the population <sup>fi</sup>gures extracted are widely divergent: in Fig. 3, the <sup>fi</sup>gures of 7,000 and 18,320 are the Boston populations in the years 1700 and 1790 respectively (from two different documents about Boston's history), 559,034 is from the (outdated) 2005 US Census, and the <sup>fi</sup>gure of 580,000 is from a (non-authoritative) 2007 blog post on city-data.com discussing Boston. The <sup>fi</sup>gure of 4,624 at the base of the results is for the incorrect entity (New Boston is not the same as Boston). The accuracy of this attribute extractor is reduced as the recency, authority, and relevance of the snippets reviewed was not taken into account. While TextRunner appears to be a viable attribute value extractor, con<sup>fl</sup>icting value resolution features on top of TextRunner are clearly necessary in order for it to be useful for concept comparison. Given that numerical attribute extractors, like TextRunner, are imperfect, the user may be concerned as to how many documents the extractor needs to go through before a secure value can be displayed to the user and used in the ranking. We propose two resolution methods for dealing with con<sup>fl</sup>icting values:

1. (Automated) Quantitative resolution: In quantitative resolution, multiple values are extracted, in the order in which they are provided by the search engine (i.e. ranked by relevance). A cumulative mean, standard deviation, and con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval (95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence lower- and upper-bound) is calculated as each successive attribute value is extracted. The user can instruct the CCE to accept attribute values as soon as the con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently tight (i.e. some numerical convergence has been achieved). Fig. 4 provides a stylized example. In Fig. 4, population <sup>fi</sup>gures are being extracted for the search term “Boston, Massachusetts” from an unnamed search engine. The <sup>fi</sup>rst ten search results are for concept A (the Boston, MA city area; population around 617,594 according to the most recent 2010 US Census), the next ten search results are for concept B (the Boston, MA Metropolitan Statistical Area, including Boston city, Cambridge, Quincy, and surrounding areas; population around 4,552,402), and the <sup>fi</sup>nal ten search results are for concept C (the town of Boston, Georgia; population around 1,315). Notice that the con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals are tight, and continue to tighten for concept A (<sup>fi</sup>rst 10 results), but widen as new concepts (concepts B and C) are confused with the initial results, as relevance decreases (i.e. as total number of search results increase). The CCE user can con<sup>fi</sup>gure the CCE to discard results outside of the con<sup>fi</sup>- dence intervals, or if the con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval itself is too wide.

2. (Manual) Qualitative resolution: Statistical con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals are not the only method for resolving numerical con<sup>fl</sup>icts. Presented with two con<sup>fl</sup>icting attribute values, from two sources, an end user may wish to compare the authority of the sources (e.g. US Census bureau vs. Wikipedia), recency (time) of the sources (yesterday vs. 10 years ago), relevance of the sources (Boston, MA city vs. the town of Boston, GA), and other factors. Fortunately, Google's PageRank algorithm [8], takes these, and other, qualitative factors into account, meaning the automated quantitative resolution shown above should account for most con<sup>fl</sup>icts, and the CCE user should not frequently need to resort to manual (qualitative) resolution.

For nominal (categorical) attributes, automated categorical attribute extraction employs common natural-language-processing tools from the information extraction literature, such as noun phrase extractors, semantic extractors, and named-entity recognizers, to associate true/false Boolean values with each categorical attribute found. The system guides this search so that direct or indirect associations between the concept and the attribute are captured. The system then consolidates the results to reduce duplicate mentions of the same category under different names, which would otherwise make later reporting dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. For example, “<sup>fi</sup>shing” and its synonym, “angling,” could be consolidated into “<sup>fi</sup>shing.” Stemmers can also be used: “Mountain” and its plural, “mountains,” could be consolidated into “mountain”; “coal mine” and its derivative “coal mining,” can be consolidated into “coal mine.” Word sense disambiguation (WSD) techniques [1,26,44] can be used to separate distinct senses of the same word. Naturally, none of these processes is perfect, but all are, at the very least, helpful decision aids for the user to interactively and iteratively mine for insights in the data for comparing concepts. For text that has been marked-up with a semantic mark-up language, such as XBRL [53] or RDFa, structured data can readily be extracted.

Aggregate attribute extraction is used to compute aggregates (e.g. term frequencies) from the concept-speci<sup>fi</sup>c document collection, which may be used as surrogate values for attributes. For example, frequency of occurrence of the topic “mountain” in the document collection for a city may be used as a (partially reliable) surrogate measure of the prevalence of mountains in that city.

The attribute skimmer creates a structured database (attribute store) which stores the concepts and their attributes, attribute values, attribute sources, and meta-data annotations. Meta-data annotations may, for example, capture user judgments as to the reliability of the attribute or attribute value, the usefulness of the attribute, or access permissions of other users to view the attribute.

## 5.4. Masher

Mashing has recently emerged as a popular analysis technique, involving mixing data from multiple proprietary (internal) and public (external) data sources, often using hosted visualization services, like Google Maps [10]. Mashing is highly useful in our context, for combining data from public text documents with public or private data from tabular sources like databases or textual comma-separated-value (CSV) <sup>fi</sup>les. A masher component is used to import values of attributes for each concept from external tabular data sources. The masher appends these columns to the attributes derived for the concepts from unstructured text. The attribute joiner component of the masher, for instance, allows the user to import the population for each city, from a tabular data source provided by the United States Census Bureau, and append the population data onto data on “coal mining” in that city which was gleaned from textual documents. Because attributes and their values may come from diverse sources, and the dependability of attributes may vary, an attribute selector allows the user to select authoritative sources. A sorter and <sup>fi</sup>lter allows the rapid comparison of concepts by attribute value.

## 5.5. Visualization

Visualization can be undertaken using traditional OLAP/PivotChart analyses [20]. For example, the user can create a line chart showing cities by population and estimated prevalence of coal mines. Population data may be acquired from structured sources, whereas the estimated prevalence of coal mines is acquired from free-form text sources. For example, one could use the frequency of occurrence of the topic “coal mine” in the document collection for the city as a plausible surrogate for the prevalence of coal mining in that city.

## 5.6. Annotation

A groupware DSS feature can allow users of the CCE to engage in an interactive and collaborative process of annotation, sharing their observations and commentaries with others [22]. For example, a product development engineer using the CCE system may observe that more populated towns in West Virginia are unsuitable locations for their product, due to local legislation prohibiting its use. In an interactive and collaborative group problem-solving process, the engineer annotates the locations as “unsuitable,” to assist the marketing manager in excluding this location from further consideration in their comparative analysis of target locations.

## 5.7. Scheduler and archiver

Finally, a scheduler allows the user to automate the data gathering and result noti<sup>fi</sup>cation process. For example, a user interested in tracking city populations over time, could schedule continuous, iterative spidering and monthly results noti<sup>fi</sup>cation. The archiver maintains historic records of past document collections, and historic attribute values, allowing the incremental computation of cumulative results. For example “population growth from 2000-2010” or “population in 2010” can be archived once they are calculated since such values do not change. Hence, over time, the CCE will develop a repository of facts that can be used as a basis of conducting comparisons in the future.

## 5.8. Summary

The entire concept comparison engine is heavily dependent on both information retrieval (IR) and information extraction (IE) techniques as its underpinnings. As the CCE architecture shows, IR and IE are foundational tiers for the construction of the third tier, concept comparison (CC).

## 6. Implementation

Our CCE implementation is an instance of the architecture depicted in the previous section. Components shown in grey in Fig. 2 are beyond the scope of our prototype CCE implementation.

Notably, in our CCE implementation, we restricted the scope of the attribute value extraction component (see §5.3) solely to aggregate attributes, and did not implement numeric or categorical attribute value extraction routines. Our reason for limiting our unstructured data analysis solely to aggregate concept statistics (i.e. term frequencies across the document set for the given concept) is that aggregates are more robust than point estimates. Our experiments are therefore not reliant on attribute matching and value extraction technology which is still a hot-bed of active research [4,13,14,36,37,52]. We obtained all numeric attribute values (e.g. for city population) in our experiments from authoritative structured data sources (viz. spreadsheets from the United States Census Bureau).

The scheduler and archiver component (see Section 5.7), which is required for continuous, industrial application of the CCE, is not implemented in our prototype, as our research experiments are conducted at a single point in time.

## 7. Metrics: Comparison strength heuristic and comparison performance measures

As previously mentioned, we restricted our experiments to aggregate attributes (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, term frequencies) for each concept, as these are more robust than point estimates (such as numeric values directly extracted from text). We now de<sup>fi</sup>ne two types of metrics that are useful for assessing the quality of concept comparisons that use aggregate attributes (term frequencies): (1) a comparison strength heuristic is any metric that provides some information as to the plausibility of the candidate comparison term for making comparisons between the chosen concepts; (2) a comparison performance measure is any metric that describes how valid the comparison is: that is, does the comparison made from text correlate with other comparisons made using reliable, independent source data?

## 7.1. Comparison strength heuristics

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst challenge is to determine whether a candidate comparison term is plausible for the purposes of comparing concepts. The presumption is that some terms will be helpful for comparing, and others not. It is well known that certain words occur with high frequency across any large English corpus [32]. These words are known as “stop words” or “noise words.” For example, A. Kilgarriff's English word frequency list, built using the British National Corpus, found the most common words in English to be “the,” “of,” “and,” “a,” “in,” and “to.” E.W. Dolch's list of high frequency words is similar. Clearly, uniformly high-frequency words do not provide much comparison value, as they occur with roughly equal frequency in different documents, and therefore are not helpful for comparing concepts.

The notion of relative term frequency (e.g. hits per 1,000 words) is of fundamental importance in the information retrieval literature [50]. The practice of <sup>fi</sup>nding unusual words or terms to distinguish documents is commonplace—see Luhn's Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words [32], or Amazon.com's Statistically Improbably Phrases (SIPs). While Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words are helpful for <sup>fi</sup>nding a unique document in a collection, they cannot, alone, rank concepts, since the most Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words appear only in a single document in the collection. Further, the most Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words selected by the information retrieval system may not be the criteria by which the user wishes to rank concepts. For example, “rural” may be the most significant word for “Mead, West Virginia,” but the user might wish to rank this location by “coal mining” or other less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words.

We therefore de<sup>fi</sup>ne the concept of a Rankor term. A Rankor term is a user-selected term (word, phrase, or collection of words) whose frequency is of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient variability in different document sets, to suggest (but not guarantee) the possibility of meaningful ranking of those document sets by that term. That is, a Rankor term is a viable (plausible) term for the purposes of comparison. There are numerous ways to determine how much the term frequency for a particular search term varies amongst items being compared:

Standard deviation: The standard deviation (σ) of a term's frequency provides a measure of the variability of the term frequency across multiple document sets. However, σ can be distorted, since different terms have different average frequencies across multiple documents. A term with a low average frequency and low σ may have relatively more variation than a term with a high average frequency and high σ. Coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of variation (CoV): The CoV is the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean (i.e. σ/μ). CoV provides a normalized measure of dispersion. Taking the CoV of a term's term frequency across different document sets gives some indication as to whether the term's frequency variation is relatively large. A high CoV indicates the term frequency variations for the term, between document sets, are relatively high. A low CoV (close to zero) indicates the term has relatively stable frequency across document sets. However mean term frequencies close to zero are commonplace, and the CoV is highly sensitive to small changes in near-zero means, so the usefulness of the CoV metric is limited.

Rankor score: We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a novel heuristic, which we term, the Rankor score. To calculate the Rankor score for a candidate comparison term, start with the term frequency in each document set. Then, for each pair of document sets, compute the Z-score using a standard difference of proportions test, to determine if term frequency is significantly different in two different document sets. Note that, since language is not random, this Z-score is not a true statistic, but rather a heuristic. We use the following algorithm. Sort the document sets by term frequency for the term. Compare the document set with the highest term frequency with the document set with the lowest term frequency: if the Z-score is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, then the Rankor score is 0 (zero). If there is statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance, proceed with comparing the next-highest-frequency with next-lowest frequency, until no statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance is reached, or until you meet in the middle of the document sets. If you meet in the middle, then the term is a 100% Rankor (Rankor score=1). Otherwise, the term is only a partial Rankor. e.g. a term would be only a 50% Rankor if it only allows you to rank the upper quartile (by term frequency) of document sets above the lower quartile (by term frequency) of document sets. The Rankor score is convenient as all terms can be given a score from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates there was no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in term frequency across any document sets, and 1 indicates that the term frequency was always signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different between the document sets compared.

Number of unique ranks (NUR): If the Rankor score is zero, no further investigation of the term is needed, since the term frequency is relatively similar even in the document set with the highest term frequency versus the document set with the lowest term frequency. However, for high Rankor score values, an additional metric, the number of unique ranks (NUR) is helpful. To compute the NUR, compare the term frequency in the document set with highest term frequency, to the document set with second highest term frequency, using a standard difference of proportions Z-score test. If there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference, compare the highest frequency to the 3rd highest frequency. If there was a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the highest frequency and the 2nd highest frequency, proceed to compare the 2nd highest frequency to the 3rd highest frequency, and so on. Each time the frequencies are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, increment NUR. Low NUR (close to zero) indicates all frequencies were similar. High NUR (relative to the total number of document sets being compared) indicates most of the term frequencies were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different.

Overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dence (ORC): High Z-scores (>1.96) for each difference in proportion test indicate that the concepts could be con<sup>fi</sup>dently ranked (i.e. distinguished based on the comparison metric). Low Z-scores (b1.96) indicate the ranking distinction is less con<sup>fi</sup>dent. (1.96 is the threshold Z-score for a two-tailed test with 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence.) ORC is computed as the average Z-score and standard deviation of the Z-score across all difference-of-proportion calculations. Average Z-scores greater than 1.96 indicate, on average, the concepts compared could be con<sup>fi</sup>dently distinguished, using term frequency, from each other using a two-tailed test with 95% con<sup>fi</sup>- dence. The overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dence can be computed in two ways:

2. Overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dence (successive ranks): Here, the Z-scores from the NUR calculations are used, to determine how signi<sup>fi</sup>cant the difference is between rank 1 and rank 2, rank 2 and rank 3, and so on.

In summary, a good Rankor term has Rankor score close to 1 and number of unique ranks (NUR) close to the number of items being compared. Rankor scores close to zero (NUR close to 1) suggests that the term is a non-Rankor: that is, it is highly unlikely to be useful for ranking (comparing) the items. Stop words (e.g. “the,” “and”) are generally non-Rankor terms, as they occur with uniform frequency across multiple documents: the term frequency is statistically indistinguishable across the document sets. On the other side of the spectrum, Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant words and SIPs (Statistically Improbably Phrases), which are highly useful for information retrieval, are also non-Rankor terms – i.e. unsuitable for concept comparison – as they occur in too few documents to allow comparison.

## 7.2. Comparison performance measures

In order to judge a user's success or failure in obtaining useful decision support from a CCE, evaluation metrics need to be de<sup>fi</sup>ned to assess the performance (validity) of the comparison. In information retrieval (IR), the performance of a user of an IR system can be evaluated by their precision and recall [50]. Precision is usually de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the percentage of documents retrieved that are relevant, and recall is the percentage of available relevant documents that are actually found. In information extraction (IE), accuracy and coverage are often used for measuring the effectiveness of IE systems [29]. Accuracy is usually de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the percentage of facts that are correct, and coverage is the percentage of available facts that are extracted. For concept comparison (CC), we propose that the performance of users interacting with these systems can be measured against a benchmark de<sup>fi</sup>ned by perfect manual analysis of the data set. The ranking of concepts generated by the user using the CCE for a particular comparison basis can be compared against the benchmark, using a statistical correlation measure. The correlation becomes a measure of CCE performance compared to perfect manual analysis. Given that perfect manual analysis is not always feasible, if the CCE performance is “good enough” then the automation efficiency has significant merit.

For example, consider a sample document collection containing documents and facts about multiple companies and miscellaneous unrelated concepts and a user comparing a shortlist of companies using “revenue” as a comparison basis. What is the correlation between the user's rank of companies in the user's shortlist by revenue, using the CCE, versus the rank of companies by revenue that a perfectly diligent and rational user would obtain by manually reading and synthesizing the documents in the collection? For extremely large, comprehensive data sets, where manual reading and synthesizing is impractical, external high-quality data, from an authoritative expert organization may be used as a benchmark. For instance, when ranking public companies by revenue using a CCE (from documents on the internet) it would be appropriate to measure the CCE user's ranking against a ranking of these companies, by revenue, from the United States Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) as a benchmark. A high correlation between the two rankings would indicate that the CCE is able to produce a valid ranking.

A variety of extant statistical rank correlation metrics is available, including Pearson ρ [39], Spearman's rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, and Kendall's tau rank correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (Kendall's τ). As these are broadly comparable, for simplicity, we have used Pearson ρ as our chosen performance metric for experimental evaluation in the following section.

Table 2 summarizes the different performance metrics that are appropriate for different types of decision tasks: information retrieval vs. information extraction vs. concept comparison. Clearly, comparison performance is dependent, amongst other factors, on the performance of the CCE's information retrieval measures such as precision and recall for <sup>fi</sup>nding documents, and the performance of the CCE's information extraction components such as accuracy and coverage of fact extraction. Weakness in either of these foundational components would obviously diminish the user's success in concept comparison.

## 8. Experiments

We have proposed a simple theoretical model, software architecture, and illustrative implementation for a CCE. Next, we conduct a set of experiments using paid human research assistants as users working with the CCE. Research assistants were assigned to perform scripted steps to gather documents, construct gold standard ranking lists, and evaluate terms in order to judge the performance of the algorithms and evaluation metrics. We assessed Rankor scores of various concept/term pairs as they compare to other potential measures of differentiation. We comment on the conditions that seem to enhance the meaningfulness of comparisons, and we discuss the implications we consequently observe, for how CCEs should be developed and how users should con<sup>fi</sup>gure and use CCEs.

## 8.1. Methodology

To enhance robustness by minimizing the sensitivity to point estimates and to narrow the scope of our evaluation, we chose to evaluate only aggregate attributes (term frequencies) gleaned from text, rather than numerical or categorical values obtained by the attribute skimmer. In order to assess the quality of location-comparisons produced by our DSS, we needed independent external data, to validate or repudiate the DSS's rankings. We found independent external data containing location rankings from two convenient sources: (1) commercial data provider: InfoUSA provided us with rankings of locations, by franchise outlet prevalence, for each of a number of franchise industries; (2) government data providers: various government data sources (listed later) provided us with rankings of locations by natural resources (e.g. oil, coal, gold) or natural phenomena (e.g. rain, wind).

Seven paid research assistants worked as a team to collect data for our experiments. These assistants were employed to assist with data gathering, rather than to study their behavior, given that their task was very speci<sup>fi</sup>c and deterministic. The assistants were undergraduates of junior or senior standing, majoring in business or engineering. Each assistant was trained in the application of a deterministic protocol for data acquisition and analysis. To eliminate variation, compliance with the protocol was assessed both on initial studies (formative evaluation) and when all studies were completed (summative evaluation). In cases of non-compliance, studies were re-executed until compliance was achieved.

To ensure all participants used a common data set, we imported a list of United States place names, taken from the United States General Services Administration Geographic Locator Codes (US GSA GLC) Geographic Names Service, into the prototype CCE. The CCE's document collection was then populated with the ten most relevant documents for each place, from Yahoo. We used city name+“, ”+state name (e.g. “Philadelphia, Pennsylvania”) as a search term in each case, as we found this tended to result in highly relevant documents for locations of all sizes. A total of 27,547 individual places were investigated, and we collected the ten most relevant documents for each (275,470 documents). This common data set was shared by all research assistants.

Each research assistant was then assigned to investigate a subset of industries. For each assigned industry, the user gathered industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c search terms for the industry, using a keyword selection protocol. For each keyword, the user executed a word count routine using the CCE to determine the total hits for the keyword, for each known location. Hits for each location were then aggregated by geographic state. Relative term frequency (hits per thousand words) was computed for each state. We then made use of public, structured data sources for each state to validate or repudiate the ranking generated by the research assistants using the CCE. In all cases we used the Pearson correlation (Pearson's ρ) to determine whether the computer-assisted ranking of states produced by the CCE user was correlated with the ranking we independently obtained for those states from an alternative structured, quantitative data source that was viewed as authoritative, and hence provided a meaningful benchmark for performance.

## 8.1.1. Commercial data provider

We sought data from commercial providers on industry strength, by location. We began by selecting the top 100 American franchise corporations from Entrepreneur Magazine's Franchise 500 listing for the year 2008. As some of the franchises were in the same industry, we selected only the largest franchise from each of the 37 unique industries found.

For each industry, users visited the website for the largest franchise corporation in that industry, viewed the home-page source code, and selected up to 4 META tag keywords listed by the company, which described the company's main product or products. In the rare event that META keyword tags were not available or appropriate, the user was allowed to choose alternative keywords. We then ran each keyword against the above-described document collections (documents for each United States location from the US GSA GLC), aggregated the hits for each keyword by state, and ranked the states from states with the most hits per 1,000 words for that keyword down to those states with the least. We divided raw hit count for the state by number of total words in the documents for that state, to reduce population biases which result from some states having more locations, and hence more documents and words, than other states.

8.1.1.1. Comparison strength heuristics. For each comparison, we found the term frequency (hits for term divided by total words for the state) of the search term, and found the standard deviation (σ) of the term frequency, coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of variation (CoV) of the term frequency, Rankor score, number of unique ranks (NURs), and overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dences. These metrics gave us an idea of the likely usefulness (plausibility) of the candidate term for comparing the states.

8.1.1.2. Comparison performance measures. To determine whether the user's ranking correlated with independent data about the popularity of each product in each state, we visited a commercial data provider, InfoUSA. For each corporation, we submitted the name of the corporation to InfoUSA, and obtained a count of the number of outlets operated by the corporation in each state. To reduce population biases and attempt to ascertain relative demand for each product in each state, we divided the number of outlets in the state by the population of that state from the US Census Bureau (year 2000 US Census), and then ranked the states by number of outlets per-capita for each corporation. Finally, we compared the ranking of states the users obtained with the assistance of the CCE (relative term frequency for the product keyword in each state) to the ranking of the states by number of outlets per-capita operated by the corporation in each state (from InfoUSA).

## 8.1.2. Government data providers

We consulted various government bureaus in search of structured data comparing locations by different natural resources or phenomena. We began the process by group brainstorming and searching to compile a short-list of natural resources and natural phenomena for which structured government data for all states would exist. We compiled a short-list of terms describing these natural resources and phenomena: e.g. “rain,” “wind,” “oil,” and “mountains.”

8.1.2.1. Comparison strength heuristics. We computed σ, CoV, Rankor score, NUR, and overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dences, for all candidate comparison terms, to determine whether the term was likely to be useful for comparing the states.

8.1.2.2. Comparison performance measures. For each phenomenon, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a government data source that provided authoritative state-speci<sup>fi</sup>c metrics for that phenomenon. Users then used the CCE system to rank each state by the relative prevalence of key terms describing that phenomenon. We then compared these rankings with rankings established by the government data sources. For example, the user determined the relative prevalence of the term “coal” in each state, using the CCE, and then compared their ranking of states by prevalence of the term “coal” to the of<sup>fi</sup>cial government data source on coal availability in that state. The following are the government data sources we gathered for each natural phenomenon:

Wind energy: We obtained the most recent data from Department of Energy (DoE), Energy Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and Renewable Energy, on current installed wind power capacity, in Megawatts, per state. We also obtained data from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) annual average wind resource estimates, in Megawatts, in the contiguous United States.

## 8.2.2. Government data provider

For the natural resources/phenomena, we computed the correlation, using Pearson's $\rho ,$ between the state ranking using the government data versus the CCE user's ranking of states by search term frequency (hits for the search term per 1,000 words) for that phenomenon. The Pearson's ρ is shown in column 4 of Table 4. For instance, for mountains, we saw a strong (0.65) correlation between the ranking of states by USGS Elevation Data and the ranking of states by term frequency for the term “mountain climbing.” As shown in Table 4, for natural phenomena, we regularly saw statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant correlations between the ranking of the states by relative term frequency of the search term using the CCE, and the ranking of the states using government data. Given the large number of correlations run, the Bonferonni correction [6] was applied to determine whether each result, when considered alone, was statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. After dividing the desired statistical con<sup>fi</sup>dence level (p=0.1) by the number of experiments (11), the actual p-values obtained are, in most cases, suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently low to conclude that the correlation is still signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

To determine whether the set of correlations, when taken together, is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, a chi-squared test can be performed. The Bonferonni adjustment is notoriously conservative and lacking in power so a chi-squared test here is con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory. For instance, at a 90% con<sup>fi</sup>- dence level it is likely that 10% of studies performed would, by chance, indicate correlations. Of the 11 natural-phenomena industries investigated, 9 produced statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive correlations (correlating the CCE ranking against the external governmental rankings). Though a small sample, the p-value for this χ test (9 actual correlations obtained in 11 studies compared to 1.1 expected correlations in 11 studies) is substantially less than 0.01, indicating statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance.

We conclude that these keywords, relating to natural phenomena in various states, were effective for ranking the states, as the rankings they produced for the states matched well with independent, authoritative government data

## 8.2.3. Summary

The results in Tables 3 and 4 show that, as expected, keywords with a low Rankor score (i.e. relatively uniform term frequency across states) seldom produced reliable rankings. In contrast, keywords with a high Rankor score often (but not always) produced reliable rankings. The NUR is (roughly) proportional to the Rankor score: a high Rankor score is associated with a high NUR, and a low Rankor score is associated with a low NUR. σ and the CoV did not provide consistent information about whether the term would be useful for ranking the states—as explained earlier, the magnitude of these metrics is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult to interpret. The Rankor score is therefore a helpful (but not guaranteed) metric for determining whether a term may be useful for comparison. The most helpful aspect of the Rankor score is that terms with a low (close to zero) Rankor score can be rapidly eliminated as possible comparison bases, since these terms almost certainly have no comparison value.

It is worthwhile to note that the overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dences for highest vs. lowest rank are high (i.e. >1.96), indicating very strongly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences, whereas, for successive ranks, the overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dence is lower. The insight here is that our concept comparison technique (based on aggregate text statistics) is sensitive to wide difference (highest vs. lowest ranked) between concepts but not to smaller differences (successive rankings). The CCE is able to distinguish dramatic differences, but not trivial differences. While the inability to consistently distinguish successive ranks is a limitation of the CCE, we believe that the CCE's ability to con<sup>fi</sup>dently distinguish top vs. bottom items is valuable, since decisions matter most when we are comparing things that are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, and matter least when concepts are very similar. In addition, the experiments can avoid using aggregated data to detect small variations and become more sensitive to small variations, but of course this comes at the cost of robustness of the results.

Note that, in our experiments, aggregate operators were used for each concept: for each concept, we computed the overall term frequency for the attribute of interest, rather than attempting to extract speci<sup>fi</sup>c attribute values. Since we did not use attribute value extraction for the experiments, and instead looked at term frequencies, we are looking at averages, and single data points have relatively little in<sup>fl</sup>uence. The overall ranking con<sup>fi</sup>dences are indicators of the con<sup>fi</sup> dence of our rankings.

For CCE analyses that extract speci<sup>fi</sup>c attribute values (rather than aggregate term frequencies) for each concept, the attribute values (which are extracted from different sources) may have some variance. Thus, for CCE analyses that use attribute value extraction, the CCE should also report the width of the con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals for each attribute of each concept. Furthermore, the CCE can report the average (and standard deviation) of the width of the con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals for that attribute for all concepts, so that users can determine how precise the ranking by that attribute is likely to be. Tight (narrow) con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals – which assure greater con<sup>fi</sup>dence – are preferable to broad (wide) intervals. In addition, when the search reaches stable states and is a candidate to converge, the variance will go down indicating that additional documents are not likely to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect the aggregate values captured by the averages and minimized variance.

## 8.3. Observations and recommendations

The statistical results from the experimental data indicate that Rankor analysis is promising as a measure of the differentiating power of candidate terms a user might choose to support concept comparison. Our statistical results are, however, insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient to demonstrate that this differentiating power will be more generally useful in facilitating better comparisons created by users. Further research is necessary to determine whether this can be extrapolated.

Analysis of the successful cases and comparing them to the unsuccessful cases reveals a number of conditions or factors that affect the accuracy of the comparison. Firstly, we found that population was a confounding factor. We also found that the quality of the results aggregated from textual documents is also industry sensitive, and one should not over generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the CCE. It is important that the CCE comparisons therefore be viewed as exploratory, guiding the comparison as the user interacts with the CCE though the DSS interface. The user should engage in further investigative exercises to con<sup>fi</sup>rm or deny the preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The concept comparison system does not provide a <sup>fi</sup>nal, de<sup>fi</sup>nitive comparison; it provides a rapid, initial (i.e., tentative) assessment of attributes that would have been dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to <sup>fi</sup>nd, measure, and compare through manual analysis, synthesis, or summarization of thousands or millions of documents on the web.

As is evident from the experiments, aggregate results may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by unexpected underlying phenomena, such as population factors in the case of franchise industries. While normalizing the data (e.g., dividing by population) may be attempted, our experiments show that such adjustments may still be insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient. Results should therefore be viewed cautiously, and can become meaningful only after the user interacts with the DSS to guide the comparison using proper Rankor terms. The user must weigh the potential costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a fast, risky, comparative assessment, against a lengthier, more reliable investigation. In that spirit, CCE usefulness at this embryonic stage of development should be viewed as a pre-processing technique to <sup>fi</sup>nd potential relationships that can guide effective comparison of concepts. We hope to conduct further research in this important area of concept comparison, because the merit of concepts can only be measured with respect to others, rather than in isolation, lending importance to concept comparison engines. The interactive nature of it also points to the strong potential it has in DSS discipline.

## 9. Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we have presented a simple theoretical model of a CCE, a proposed architecture, evaluation metrics, and sample user experiences. We suggest that the massive amount of data available online, which is constantly increasing, requires more than merely search engines but value added services on simple search such as ability to provide comparison among concepts. This is what we refer to as a new frontier of search which we describe as concept comparison engines. Search engines are good for <sup>fi</sup>nding, whereas CCEs could be especially helpful for comparing. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we viewed a CCE as a component of a decision support system for comparing items that are described both in documents and in structured content. Including a CCE as a component of a DSS is feasible and useful given the advances in the information retrieval and information extraction <sup>fi</sup>elds and the utility they have in operationalizing comparison engines. In our experiments, research assistants provided (through a controlled process with some judgment) terms of interest to be used by an algorithm which ranked/ordered documents. For the subset of studies involving natural resources or phenomena, this ordering was, in many cases, statistically correlated with gold-standard rankings gathered by the research assistants from independent and authoritative structured data sources. The experimental results reveal that Rankor analysis appears promising as a measure of the differentiating power of candidate terms a user might choose to support concept comparison.

To be used effectively, a DSS should minimize user effort for preferential choice tasks [49]. While the effort devoted to understanding and using a CCE is greater than that of a traditional search engine, the CCE reduces the processing effort associated with obtaining information in the choice matrix described earlier. Despite the experimental and conceptual merits of the CCE we reported in this study, quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation of total effort reduction and assessment of comparative decision quality with and without the aid of a CCE needs to be further investigated. For users to continue to use a DSS they should be able to perceive the usefulness of the DSS for preferential choice tasks; for example, the CCE should enable users to compare alternatives by attribute [28]. While it was beyond the scope of the current study, future research should assess user beliefs and attitudes when using the CCE so that future generations of CCE can be more effective and tailored for human usability.

Improving the usability and performance of CCEs is critical to the successful implementation of such decision aids. Benchmarking performance of alternative CCE implementations would be a potential area of future research. For example, to facilitate rank correlation metrics of CCE performance, a benchmark could be created with “model answers” showing the correct concept rankings for various concept shortlists and comparison bases. Such a benchmark can be produced by diligent manual reading and synthesis of the document collections. In the absence of such a benchmark collection, researchers can fall back on, as we did, comparative data from independent external sources. Naturally, advances in IR and IE techniques will positively bene<sup>fi</sup>t CCE research. Comparisons produced by CCEs could be vastly enriched if the CCE also used ontologies and semantic markup languages; this could be a fruitful avenue of future research. It is hoped that the model, architecture, and performance measures de<sup>fi</sup>ned here will provide a foundation for further development and evaluation of CCE theory, implementation, and application.
