## Abstract

The stock market reactions to information technology (IT)-related events have often been used as proxies to the value or cost of these events in the information systems literature. In this paper, we study the stock market reactions to information-security-related events using the event analysis methodology with consideration of the effects of a number of contingency factors, including business type, industry, type of breach, event year, and length of event window. We found that pure e-commerce firms experienced higher negative market reactions than traditional bricks-and-mortar firms in the event of security breach. We also found that denial of service attacks had higher negative impact than other types of security breaches. Finally, security events occurred in recent years were found to have less significant impact than those occurred earlier, suggesting that investors may have become less sensitive to the security events. Most interestingly, our analyses showed that the magnitude and longevity of security breaches vary with time across sub-samples. This raises some serious questions regarding the validity of analyzing only short-term stock market reactions as an indicator of the cost of security breaches, and in general, an indicator of the value of IT-related events. The implications of these results are discussed and potential future research directions are proposed.

## Introduction

O <sup>ver</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>last</sup> <sup>two</sup> <sup>decades,</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>rapid</sup> <sup>development</sup> <sup>and</sup>broad deployment of information technologies (IT), broad deployment of information technologies (IT), especially the Internet-enabled innovations and applications, have brought businesses and consumers ever closer and enabled businesses, large and small, to become players in the global economy. Unfortunately, the high level of connectivity has also created unprecedented opportunities for a dark side of the technological world to emerge. Advances in computer security technology and management often could not keep up with those in computing in general (Whitman, 2004). As a result, computer viruses, spyware, cyber attacks, and computer system security breaches are almost daily occurrences. In various studies, computing executives ranked these types of deliberate security attacks as the most important threats to information security (Whitman, 2004) while information security and privacy issues are consistently among the top three IT management concerns of organizations (Luftman et al., 2006).

The number of computer-related attacks and the severity of these attacks have increased dramatically in the last two decades. For example, in the 10-year period of 1993–2003, the number of security incidents reported to CERT increased from 1334 to 137,529 (CERT, 2004a). According to the 2008 CSI Computer Crime and Security Survey, of the 250 respondents, 14% stated that they experienced 5 to 10 security incidents, 13% stated that they experienced 10 or more security incidents in 2008, where 26% stated they do not know exactly how many security incidents they experienced (Richardson, 2008). One of the reasons for this increase is that advanced computer programming languages, application development tools, and Web technologies have enabled almost anyone with moderate computer skills to create and distribute computer viruses and launch attacks on remote computer systems. Moreover, cyber attack tools are getting more powerful and easier to use with graphical interfaces, and attackers are getting more organized and prepared with these tools (Denning, 2000; CERT, 2003a).

It is estimated that security attacks have resulted in financial losses to businesses amounting to billions of dollars worldwide (Niccolai, 2000; Lemos, 2003; Gordon et al., 2004; ZDNet, 2004; Cavusoglu et al., 2005). On average, the cost of a security breach increased more than double from \$168,000 in 2006 to \$345,000 in 2007 and leveled at around \$350,000 in 2008 (Richardson, 2008). Thus, it is not surprising that information security has become a central issue of IT management and academic research in recent years. A significant number of studies have been published in an attempt to understand how virus attacks and security breaches succeed and what organizations can do to reduce or mitigate the risk. One of the important aspects of information security research is to assess the financial impact of security breaches on firms. Among the many different approaches, a stream of research has attempted to quantify the financial impact of virus attacks and security breaches on firms based on the stock market reactions (e.g., Campbell et al., 2003; Ettredge and Richardson, 2003; Garg et al., 2003; Hovav and D’Arcy, 2003; Cavusoglu et al., 2004b; Hovav and D’Arcy, 2004). Interestingly, even though there is a strong theoretical basis for hypothesizing a negative market reaction to the news of virus attacks and security breaches, these studies so far have produced mixed results. The short time periods and relatively small samples used in these studies have also left some important questions unanswered. If security events do have negative impacts on firm values, are such impacts temporary or long lasting? Are all firms being treated equally by the market in terms of the negative impacts? Do the types of security events matter in terms of the severity of the impact on market values?

In this study, we examined the impact of security breaches on the stock prices of the target firms using the event analysis methodology. We focused on firm-specific security breaches such as hackers altering a company’s website or steal confidential customer data. Security breaches that affect several firms at the same time such as computer virus outbreaks were not included in the sample. Our study differs from prior event studies in a number of significant ways, thus extending the information systems (IS) literature on the economics of information security and the application of event study methodology. First, we evaluated our findings for 5-day and 10-day event windows in addition to the commonly used 1-day (i.e., [-1, 1]) event window. Adding this time dimension to the analyses could provide a deeper understanding of how the longevity and magnitude of security breaches change across different contingency factors such as business types, industry membership, and security breach types. Understanding the longevity of the impact of security breach events is an important objective of this study. This is because if the negative impact of an event lasts only for a couple of days, then it is irrelevant to information system management.

However, if the event has a long lasting effect, then the shareholder value is at stake and it should become a concern for IT managers and top executives. Second, the change of sensitivity of the market to security breach events over time was analyzed. Recognizing the escalation of security concerns among customers and the increasing legal actions by individuals, businesses, and government entities, we believe that analyzing the market reactions to recent vs earlier events could shed some light on the evolving market sentiment about information security. Third, grouping of security breach types was improved over previous studies, which mostly focused on denial of service (DoS) attacks. In this study, we created a new grouping schema to evaluate the security breaches according to their impact on the firm’s ability to conduct its business and sustain its competitiveness. Finally, the relatively larger sample size in our study afforded us to extend previous studies by analyzing the effect of industry membership on the impact of security breach events on the market value of firms.

The rest of the paper is arranged as follows. In the next section, we review the literature regarding the impact of security incidents on the stock values of firms and develop our research hypotheses. This is followed by the discussion of the research method and the data collection process. Afterwards, we present the results of event analysis and discuss their implications. And finally, we present our main conclusions and discuss the limitations of this study and future research of this subject.

## Research background and hypothesis development

## The challenge of determining cost of security incidents The challenge of determining cost of security incidents

The growing threats of malicious attacks and loss of privacy and confidentiality in the highly connected global economy have forced companies to move information security onto the center stage of their corporate IT management (Dutta and McCrohan, 2002; Gordon et al., 2004). In fact, 90% of the global organizations surveyed by Ernst & Young (2003) said that information security is of high importance for achieving their overall business objectives. In general, the business drives of information security are ensuring business continuity (Nash, 2008) and protecting reputation and brand name (Ernst & Young, 2008). Although companies are continuously increasing their budget for information security to strengthen their networks and secure their websites, insufficient budget is still one of the biggest obstacles to information security management (Briney, 2001; Ernst & Young, 2003). According to a global security survey conducted in 2003, on average, companies designate only 6% of their total IT budget to information security management (Deloitte Touche Tohmatsu, 2003). This trend unfortunately continues today. In the 2008 CSI Computer Crime and Security Survey, half of the 522 respondents stated that they allocated 5% or less of their IT budget for security (Richardson, 2008). In 2007, the median security budget was around \$20,000 (CSO, 2007). Similar industry surveys revealed more troublesome issues. Ernst & Young (2008) survey showed that only 50% of the companies are planning to increase their security budget. However, this increase, especially for companies in North America, is around 5% (Nash, 2008).

Overall, the industry reports show that, over the past decade, organizations have given only the minimum attention to information security investments. The main obstacle in getting sufficient funding for information security initiatives seems to be the difficulty in justifying the return on security investment (Cavusoglu et al., 2004a). This is because, as preventive technologies, the best effect of information security investments is ‘nothing bad happened.’ Thus, ascertaining the true cost of potential security incidents, including virus attacks and security breaches from both internal and external sources, is necessary for effective information security investment decisions (PricewaterhouseCoopers, 2004) as well as for developing an effective security management strategy and related policies (Hovav and D’Arcy, 2003). However, cost assessments of such incidents are often ignored by many companies. According to the 2004 e-Crime Watch Survey, 32% of the respondents did not even track monetary losses due to electronic or related crimes (CERT, 2004b). The difficulty of quantifying cost-benefit structures of information security investments is likely one of the main reasons for such ignorance. The main causes of this difficulty are the lack of accurate metrics and effective tools for economic and financial analysis (Mercuri, 2003). What exacerbates the complexity of such analyses is the fact that security breaches could result in both tangible and intangible costs (D’Amico, 2000), as shown in Table 1.

Although there are abundant media and trade reports about the estimated tangible cost of computer virus attacks and security breaches, most of these figures are estimates whose accuracies and reliabilities are rarely empirically validated. However, gathering data for information security research has significant roadblocks. The first is in the detection of a security incident itself: it is estimated that only one tenth of computer attacks are detected (Bosworth and Kabay, 2002). The second is in the reporting of the detected attacks. Forty-eight percent of the respondents of the 2004 CSI/FBI Computer Crime and Security Survey admitted that they did not report all cases of their computer intrusion incidents; only 20% reported security incidents to law enforcement agencies and 16% of them reported breaches to their own legal councils (Gordon et al., 2004). Another roadblock is the estimation of intangible cost of security incidents. It is difficult, if feasible at all, to determine how much customer trust or loyalty has been lost due to a security breach in which private information has been stolen, such as the widely publicized incidents of ChoicePoint (O’Harrow, 2005), MasterCard (Evers, 2005), and Kerzner International (Reuters, 2006). In addition, the subsequent legal and regulatory costs to the focal companies are almost impossible to estimate ex ante.

## The event analysis methodology

Given these difficulties in estimating the true cost of various security breaches, it is wise to search for alternative indicators and proxies to the direct costs. Recently, a series of studies were published by researchers using the event study methodology to estimate the impact of security incidents on the stock values of target firms as a proxy for the potential cost of such incidents. The basic theory behind event study is that since the stock price of a firm is supposed to reflect the true value of that firm determined by all relevant public information about the firm at the time, the changes in the stock price due to a specific incident should measure the financial impact of that incident more effectively than measures based on accounting numbers which are subject to manipulation (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997).

The event study methodology has many applications in accounting, finance, and management fields, covering different research streams including mergers and acquisitions, earnings, stock splits, brand leveraging, celebrity endorsements, strategic alliances, and various investment decisions. In the IS literature, event study methodology has become a valuable tool for studying the impact of IT on the value of firms. One of the main research streams examined the impact of IT investments on the market value of firms (Dos Santos et al., 1993; Im et al., 2001; Chatterjee et al., 2002; Dehning et al., 2003). Other studies that utilized the event study methodology examined the impact of e-commerce announcements (Subramani and Walden, 2001), newly created CIO position announcements (Chatterjee et al., 2001), and web traffic announcements (Benbunan-Fich and Fich, 2004).

As information security becomes an increasingly important part of IT management in recent years, event analysis methodology has been used to analyze the impact of security-related events on the market value of firms. Table 2 presents the significant studies that utilized this methodology to examine the effect of security breaches. Unlike the positive IT events that often produce positive abnormal returns, security breach events are usually considered as indications of poor management of technology and business processes, and therefore have negative impact on the market value of the focal firms. Interestingly, however, the results of empirical studies on this subject so far have been mixed and inconclusive.

Campbell et al. (2003) are among the first who used event analysis methodology to investigate the stock market reactions to the news of security breaches in specific firms. After examining the stock prices of 43 publicly traded firms, the authors found that there was no statistical evidence to support the negative stock market reaction hypothesis when all firms were considered together. But the study found a highly significant negative market reaction to the security breaches that involved unauthorized access to confidential information. However, the small sample size (only 11 incidents related to confidential data) limits the generalizability of the findings.

Hovav and D’Arcy (2003) followed a similar methodology but investigated only DoS attacks. They found no evidence to support the negative stock market reaction hypothesis, but with only a small number of incidents (20) being analyzed. In a later study that involved a much larger sample size (186) of data on virus attacks, Hovav and D’Arcy (2004) once again found no evidence to support the negative stock market reaction hypothesis. However, the findings of this study may be limited because a virus attack often affects a large number of firms simultaneously which may render event analysis less effective. For instance, the Melissa virus affected at least more than 300 organizations in 1999 (CERT, 2003b); and in 2007, the Storm Worm affected an estimated 1 to 10 million computers worldwide (Wikipedia, 2009). It is likely that a portion of those affected computers and sites belongs to publicly traded companies, leading to the assumption that no single company will be selectively punished by investors in the stock markets $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq) for such incidents. Since the event study methodology defines the abnormal return as the difference between the market’s return and the company’s return, event analysis for computer viruses would be ineffective and even invalid. This is the main reason for excluding virus outbreaks from our analysis.

In another study, Garg et al. (2003) analyzed 22 security breaches that occurred between 1996 and 2002. Their results showed that companies that experienced security breaches suffered from negative abnormal market returns. A similar but more comprehensive study was done by Cavusoglu et al. (2004b). Although their study is more recent and has a relatively larger sample size (66) than many prior studies, the security incidents in the sample were from the same time period (between 1996 and 2001). The authors found significant negative abnormal returns in the full sample in a two-day event window. They also found that the negative abnormal returns were larger for Internet firms and small firms (in terms of capital, assets and etc.) compared to traditional firms and large firms, respectively.

However, contrary to Garg et al. (2003), Cavusoglu et al. (2004b) concluded that investors’ reactions were not different across security breach types.

## Research hypotheses development

It is clear from the literature review that, while the findings of the event studies about positive IT events are quite consistent, the findings about the impact of security events on the market value of firms are inconclusive. We submit that several methodological and data-related issues may have undermined the reliability of the results and contributed to the mixed findings. While studies on the positive IT events usually have clearly defined events and large samples, the studies on the security breach events suffer from small samples and events that often impact a number of firms instead of just one. Small sample size is perhaps the most significant shortcomings of these studies since the degrees of kurtosis and skewness are higher for small sample sizes (Brown and Warner, 1985). Daily abnormal returns tend to be highly non-normal when sample size is less than 50 firms (Brown and Warner, 1985). As a result, in small samples the statistical power of the event study methodology diminishes. However, as the sample size gets larger, the mean abnormal return tends to converge to normality (Brown and Warner, 1985). The study by Hovav and D’Arcy (2004) does have a larger sample size, but the security incidents investigated were virus attacks which may not be suitable for event analysis as previously stated. However, the shortcomings related to sample size issue and the nature of security events should not diminish the validity of event analysis methodology in analyzing the impact of security breach events on the market value of the focal firms if they can be properly addressed.

The main argument behind the expected negative returns of a firm’s stock value is based on the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) (Fama, 1970) on the valuation of the firm. According to the EMH, investors consider all available information and valuate a firm as $V _ { t } ,$ where $V _ { t }$ represents the discounted value of expected future cash flow of the firm at time (t). When there is new information in the market, investors update their valuations accordingly. Thus, at time (t þ 1), the value of the firm would be $\bar { V _ { t + 1 } } .$ . The difference between $V _ { t }$ and $V _ { t + 1 } , \Delta V ,$ , represents the change in the value of the firm due to the new information. If the new information is considered as positive by the investors, DV would be expected to be positive, and vice versa.

Therefore, one can argue that the investigation of stock market reaction to event announcements is a reflection of examination of the perceptions of investors regarding the continuity and success of the firm in the future. When the arguments of EMH are extended to the examination of security breach announcements, it is only logical to expect that after the announcement, DV would be negative since investors would consider the security breach as a potential threat to future cash flows of the focal firm. It is conceivable that because firm-specific security breaches can cause significant tangible and intangible costs to a focal firm, as summarized in Table 1, investors could perceive such costs as having an adverse effect on the firm’s ability to produce profit in the short term. Furthermore, firm-specific security breaches could also be viewed to have a long-term negative effect on the stock price of the firm because of the perceived inability of the firm to protect its confidential customer information, trade secrets, and other information assets which are increasingly valuable and vital to the firm in the digital economy. Thus, we posit that:

Hypothesis 1: A firm-specific security breach will have a significantly negative impact on the stock value of the firm when the news of such breach is made public.

The stock market reaction to the news of a firm-specific security incident is a result of the perceived cost associated with the security breach and the perceived vulnerability of the firm’s business to similar attacks in the future. If a firm’s business model is entirely based on the Internet (e.g., pure e-commerce firms), such perceived cost and vulnerability would be much higher than in those firms that do not or only partially rely on the Internet (e.g., traditional bricks-and-mortar firms where e-commerce is only complementary to their traditional marketing and sales channels). The rationale behind this is that a security breach such as a DoS attack can cripple a pure e-commerce firm’s sales for a considerable amount of time. For instance, DoS attacks in 2000 that hit many of the well-known e-commerce websites including Yahoo! and eBay cost around \$100 million in revenue loss (Niccolai, 2000). Moreover, such an attack not only results in lost revenue, but also decreases customer’s confidence on the firm and may result in loss of future transactions.

Furthermore, one of the significant barriers of e-commerce is customer’s perception regarding the safety of online transactions. Owing to the distant and impersonal nature of e-commerce, customers perceive various risks including economic, personal, and privacy risks (Pavlou, 2003). For instance, customers fear that their credit card and personal information will not be safe or Web retailers will behave in opportunistic manners. They may also suspect that their online transactions can be intercepted by hackers and they may lose confidential data. From this perspective, when e-commerce firms lose confidential data as a result of security breaches, a customer’s confidence towards online shopping at these sites severely declines. On the other hand, the issues of lost revenue and negative security perceptions regarding online shopping do not affect traditional bricks-and-mortar firms as much since their revenues do not heavily rely on the Internet. Consequently, e-commerce firms inherit more information-security-related risk than traditional firms. Previous studies that utilized event analysis (e.g., Hovav and D’Arcy, 2003; Cavusoglu et al., 2004b) have also reported greater negative abnormal returns for pure e-commerce companies compared to conventional companies. This leads to our second research hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2: A firm-specific security breach will have a stronger negative impact on the stock value of pure e-commerce firms than that of traditional bricks-andmortar firms when the news of such breach is made public.

Security breaches can cause customers, users, and investors to question the effectiveness and reliability of software and hardware products such as operating systems, virus protection programs, intrusion detection programs, firewalls, and routers used in the target firms. However, in addition to the deficiencies in such products, security breaches also occur as a result of poor security management. Organizations that fail to implement proper security policies and adequate software patch management practices or that use inadequate and legacy technologies are more likely to experience security breaches. It is logical to conjecture that customers and investors perceive that firms in the information technology industry, such as those that design and manufacture computer and networking products and computer software, should manage security technologies and protect their information assets better than non-technology firms which primarily use the hightech products in their business processes. For instance, regarding the security breach at Guidance Software, provider of computer forensics software, one of the firm’s customers has stated that ‘you’d think Guidance would be the last company this kind of thing would happen to’ (Krebs, 2005).

Therefore, when a firm in the computer technology industry fails to protect its own computer systems and data, investors might have a much stronger negative reaction to the security incident than in the case of a non-technology firm. According to the EMH, investors have rational expectations and they update their expectations according to new information. Thus, it is likely that a firm whose core business is information technology would lose more credibility and confidence of investors when it suffers from computer security breaches. In addition, information technology firms, by the nature of their business, rely more heavily on information technology for their survival than non-technology firms do, which exacerbates the negative perceptions. Thus:

Hypothesis 3: A firm-specific security breach will have a stronger negative impact on the stock value of technology firms than that of non-technology firms when the news of such breach is made public.

Companies may experience different types of security breaches including DoS attacks, theft of trade secrets (i.e., new product designs and source codes), theft of customer information (i.e., credit card numbers and social security numbers), theft of employee data, and website alterations. Table 3 presents brief descriptions of different types of security breaches. Each type of security breach may result in different consequences to the target companies. Website alterations and DoS attacks might have strong negative impact on pure e-commerce companies since they depend on their online presence, and therefore the consequences of the attacks are much more visible. However, these types of attacks rarely result in loss of confidential information or other valuable information assets, thus may be less detrimental to the bricks-and-mortar companies. On the other hand, security breaches, such as unauthorized access to customer data, trade secrets, and employee data, are often categorized as breach of confidential information and may result in both intangible and tangible costs to all companies. These security breaches should have similar negative effects across firms regardless of their business types.

However, previous studies found mixed results concerning the impact of different security breach types, which is probably a result of inconsistent patterns of grouping of these breaches. For example, Campbell et al. (2003) grouped security breaches into confidential and nonconfidential, and kept thefts of employee data, company data, and customer data together in one group as confidential, whereas Garg et al. (2003) classified security breaches into four groups: theft of credit card information, DoS, website defacement, and theft of customer information. Yet Cavusoglu et al. (2004b) used another scheme by dividing security breaches into two categories: DoS attacks and all others. We believe that if the different types of security breaches are grouped according to their impact on the firm’s ability to conduct its business and sustain its competitiveness, then a clearer pattern could emerge. Thus:

Hypothesis 4: Security breaches that impact a firm’s ability to conduct business and sustain competitiveness will have a stronger negative impact on the stock value of the firm than other type of breaches when the news of such breaches is made public.

Although the Internet has been used for commercial purposes since mid-1990s, many services that exist today weren’t available in the early days. More and more online services such as shopping, banking, bill payment, and financial tools require users to reveal their personal information including their social security numbers, addresses, birth dates, and credit card numbers. The data gathered from online transactions grew considerably as the number of online users and companies offering online services increased. In addition to customer data, company databases store employee data, business transactions, and other valuable firm-specific information. As the amount of information stored in these databases increases, companies are being exposed to even higher level of security risk. Recently, series of security breaches targeted large customer databases and the amount of data that has been compromised was astonishing, often involving tens of millions of customers. Although in most cases companies are not required to announce security breaches to the public, these recent developments motivated consumer advocates to push the federal government and state legislatures to take initiatives to protect consumer privacy (Gaynor, 2005). For instance, the State of California has passed a bill that requires companies to notify customers when their sensitive information has been compromised. Twenty other states have followed California and passed similar bills and also credit-freeze bills that allow customers to freeze their credit reports (Gaynor, 2005; Krim, 2005). In another data breach incident, the California Department of Managed Health Care fined the firm that lost confidential customer data (Rosencrance, 2005). These recent developments indicate that customers are becoming more aware of the security risk and less tolerant of mismanagement or negligence of their private information by firms. Thus:

Hypothesis 5: Firm-specific security breaches occurred in recent years will have a stronger negative impact on the stock value of the firms than those occurred in the earlier years.

Another important factor in the event analysis methodology is the length of the event window selected. Although there is no standard window size for event analysis, most studies, including most of the event studies in the IS literature, have chosen the one-day window ([-1, 1]). It is arguable that short window sizes may be appropriate for financial-related studies in which the focus is often shortterm trading opportunities in the stock market where trade transactions are executed in seconds or minutes after an event becomes public. However, their validity in evaluating the cost of security breaches to firms is debatable at best. On one hand, certain impacts of the security events could be short lived since the security holes are usually patched up and the affected systems and data are restored to their pre-event state quickly. On the other hand, other impacts could be long-lasting, such as loss of customer confidence and loyalty. The bad publicity could lead to lost revenue and increased cost, and legal liabilities could linger for years.

There are several potential factors that may affect the longevity of the impacts of security breaches. One factor is the type of security breach. For instance, if a hacker accesses a company’s web servers and makes alterations to the webpages, the company under attack can quickly delete or overwrite the files uploaded by the hacker, patch up the security holes, and solve the problem. However, a stolen hard disk full of customer private information, or the millions of customer records downloaded from a breached database cannot be solved as easily, and therefore, the effect may last much longer. Another factor is industry membership. Investors may react more adversely to security breaches to companies in the technology industry considering that the core competence of such companies is technology and therefore they should be held more responsible for security breaches. Similar to this point of view, the negative reaction of investors may be held over longer time periods for security breaches to pure e-business companies such as eBay and Amazon.com since the only revenue stream for these firms is over the Internet. Therefore, it may take longer to persuade investors about the well being of pure e-business companies experienced security breaches as compared to that of traditional companies. From these perspectives, it is reasonable to expect that the longevity of the security breach impact would be different depending on these contingency factors. Thus, we posit that:

Hypothesis 6: The longevity of the impact of security breaches on the stock value of a firm depends on the types of business, breach, and industry of the firm.

In the following section, we test these six research hypotheses using firm-specific security breach incident data and discuss our findings.

## Data and research methodology

## Sample data selection

To identify firm-specific security breach announcements, we used Internet search engines including Google and Yahoo! in addition to the Lexis-Nexis business news database. The terms used for the electronic searches included ‘cyber attack,’ ‘information security breach, ‘hacker,’ ‘computer crime,’ and ‘DoS attack.’ To be included in the sample, the firm identified in the news or announcement of a security breach had to be listed on one of the public stock exchanges (i.e., NYSE, AMEX, or NASDAQ). The firm also needed to be listed during the estimation period, which usually fell in the range of [-130, þ 10] from the event date. With these requirements, a total of 130 firmspecific security breaches were identified between 1994 and 2006. In the second stage of the data collection, we analyzed possible confounding effects including earnings and dividend announcements, strategic alliances and mergers, and CEO retirements during the 10-day window after the event date and one day before the event date. As a result, a small number of events were dropped from the sample for various event windows. For example, 7, 14, and 26 events were dropped from the original sample for the [-1, 1], [-1, 5], and [-1, 10] window analyses, respectively. Table 4 presents general characteristics of the firms in the sample included in the [-1, 1] window analyses. The sample consists of entirely large firms with average market capitalization of \$69 billion. While almost 45% of the firms are in the business services sector, the sample firms cover a wide range of industries. Table 5 provides more details about the types of security breaches that were included in the data sample.

## Estimation method

This study utilizes event study methodology to examine the impact of security breach announcements. This methodology uses financial market data to ‘measure the impact of a specific event on the value of a firm’ (MacKinlay, 1997). Event-based studies are preferred over accounting databased studies when the reliability of accounting data are questionable due to the flexibility of managers in choosing accounting procedures (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997) or when accounting data are difficult to collect as in the case of assessing the cost of security breaches. Moreover, event studies are relatively easier since the only required data are the daily stock prices of the sample firms. These prices reflect the value of a particular firm more accurately since stock prices are relatively free of insider manipulation and reflect all of the relevant information known about the firm under the EMH (Fama, 1970). As reviewed before, a stream of IS research has adopted this methodology for analyzing the value of IT-related events to the focal firms. For these reasons, we chose the event study methodology to analyze the effect of security breaches on stock prices of companies. This effect can be used as a proxy for assessing the actual financial damages of these breaches to the firms or the value of IT security investment that could prevent such breaches.

The standard approach in event studies is to first compute stock return on or around the date of the event under consideration by applying a market model based on the capital asset pricing model (CAPM):

$$
R _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

where $R _ { i t }$ is the return in the stock price of the ith firm on day t; $R _ { m t }$ is the total market return on day $t ; \alpha _ { i }$ and $\beta _ { i }$ are the intercept and the slope of the market model for firm $i ,$ respectively; $\boldsymbol { \varepsilon } _ { i t }$ is the disturbance term.

Following the convention of event studies, we used the equal-weighted NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq index return as the market return in our model and set the estimation period to 120 days, starting at t ¼ -130 days and ending at t ¼ -10 days, where t ¼ 0 day represents the event date. However, it is important to make a clarification on the security event date. From the investors’ perspective, the meaningful event date for a firm-specific security breach is the day when such breach becomes public knowledge, which is not necessarily the date when the breach actually occurred. For example, for a DoS attack or website alteration incident, the breach occurrence date and the public knowledge date are usually the same, even if the companies choose to make their announcement in the following days. On the other hand, the event date for a less publicly noticeable breach such as an unauthorized access of confidential database resulting in stolen private information or trade secrets is the date the breach incident becomes public knowledge through media announcement or via business news wire. In this study, we have carefully chosen the appropriate event date for each security breach incident based on the type of the breach and the context of the announcement.

With the daily stock return estimated for each firm over the window of [-130, -10] using the CAPM model, the next step is to compute the abnormal returns, AR, for firm i at and around the event dates t ¼ -1, t ¼ 0 and t ¼ 1, 2, 3 y using

$$
A R _ {r i t} = R _ {i t} - (\alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t})\tag{2}
$$

The abnormal return is the difference between the actual and the expected normal return of the stock over the event period. Cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) of firm i on this event are then calculated over the event period using

$$
C A R _ {i} = \sum_ {t _ {1}} ^ {t _ {2}} A R _ {i t},\tag{3}
$$

where $t _ { 1 }$ and $t _ { 2 }$ represent the start and end date of the time period in which abnormal returns are cumulated.

For all N firms in the sample, the average CAR can be calculated using;

$$
C A R = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} C A R _ {i}\tag{4}
$$

If the calculated CARs are significantly different from zero, then an abnormal return due to the event is declared, indicating that there is a greater possibility than mere chance that the change(s) of the stock price(s) of the firm(s) during this time period is(are) caused by the underlying event(s).

## Results and discussions

## Analysis of the full sample

Using the daily stock prices of the firms, the market parameters of equation (1) were estimated for each sample firm and then ARs and CARs were calculated using equations (2)–(4). The results for the full sample are presented in Table 6. Before analyzing the results, it is important to note that the sample size varies across event windows. This is because in longer event windows we were forced to drop additional firms due to the occurrence of confounding events in some firms. For instance, if an earnings announcement occurred in the seventh day after the event date (t ¼ 0), the data point is included in the 1-day [-1, 1] and 5-day [-1, 5] analyses, but dropped from the 10-day [-1, 10] analyses.

For the event window [-1, 1], the mean CAR is -0.0092 for all firms. This figure indicates that between the day before the announcement of the security breach and the day after the announcement, the portfolio of the firms in the sample experienced an average 0.9% decrease in their stock prices compared to their market valuations based on the CAPM model. This figure is weakly significant at Po0.10 level. Compared to same event window in other studies that investigated the effect of security breach announcements, the magnitude of the effect of the announcements in our study is considerably smaller (e.g., Campbell et al. (2003) reported -0.0188).

We further analyzed this sample with [-1, 5] window to investigate the longevity of the negative effects of the announcements. The mean CAR for [-1, 5] window is -0.0161. This figure is greater than the previous results for [-1, 1] window and also significant at Po0.05 level, suggesting that a delayed reaction to the news by some investors may have occurred. Thus, a longer time frame is also analyzed to verify that these effects are not just for the short terms. The mean CAR for [-1, 10] window is -0.0152. This longer event window is slightly smaller than the results for [-1, 5] and weakly significant at Po0.10 level. This result could be interpreted as the market valuation of the firms is indeed recovering from the initial shocks during the 10-day period. The findings show that there is a statistically significant negative stock market reaction to the announcements or news of firm-specific security breaches, and that this negative effect varies with the length of the event window.

## Analysis of effect by industry type

We then proceeded to test hypothesis 3 by dividing the full sample into two sub-samples: firms in the SIC code group 737x (information technology firms) and firms in other SIC code groups. Table 8 presents the results from event analyses based on the industry types. With technology firms, the mean CAR for the [-1, 1] is negative, suggesting that there is a drop in market valuation; however, this figure is not statistically significant. On the other hand, for the [-1, 5] window, the mean CAR is -0.0275, indicating an average drop of over 2.8% in market valuation, and it is statistically significant at Po0.05 level. Further investigation of this sub-sample for the [-1, 10] event window revealed that the mean CAR value is not only negative and significant at Po0.05 level, but also much greater than the shorter event windows.

The analyses of the non-technology sub-sample indicate that the mean CARs for [-1, 1] and [-1, 5] windows are negative, and they are smaller than the mean CAR values from the technology sub-sample. Comparison of two industry sub-samples further revealed an interesting phenomenon. For technology firms, the initial negative effect of security breaches increases as the event window gets longer. For instance, the [-1, 10] mean CAR value is more than five times bigger than the [-1, 1] mean CAR. On the other hand, for non-technology firms, the initial negative impact increases slightly, and dissipates almost completely 10 days after the event date. This observation shows the effect of time on the contingency factor (i.e., industry type), lending further support for hypothesis 6. The t-tests analyses also revealed that the difference between these two sub-samples is statistically significant for the [-1, 10] event window (see Appendix B).

Since the sample size of the technology sub-sample is less than 50, we conducted non-parametric analyses to confirm our findings. The results of the non-parametric tests are consistent with our previous findings for the technology sub-sample (see Appendix A). Thus, we can conclude that hypothesis 3 is supported by the empirical evidence. That is, the negative reaction of the stock market to firm-specific security breach announcements or news is much stronger to firms in the technology industry than to firms in other industries.

## Analysis of effect by security breach types

In order to analyze the effects of security breach types, the full sample was divided into three sub-samples. The first sub-sample consisted of DoS attacks, the second consisted of unauthorized access to customer data, and the last consisted of all other types of breaches such as unauthorized access to company information, company network and employee data, and website alteration. This grouping was based on the impact of these breaches on the target firm’s ability to conduct business and sustain competitiveness, as previously discussed.

The market reactions to DoS attacks are considerably stronger than to all other types (Table 9). The mean CAR for the [-1, 1] window is -0.0207 and statistically significant at Po0.05 level. For the [-1, 5] window, the mean CAR value is considerably larger, and it indicates an average drop of over 4.9% in market valuation during the event window. The results also show the longevity of the negative effects of these attacks. For the [-1, 10] window, the mean CAR is -0.0677, significant at Po0.05 level, and it is greater than that of the [-1, 5] window and more than three times that of the [-1, 1] window. These results contradict the findings of previous studies, especially Hovav and D’Arcy (2003) who concluded that overall the market does not penalize companies that experience DoS attacks. Possible reasons for this contradiction may be the differences in sample size and event periods. Their data set consists of 20 events that occurred between 1998 and 2002, whereas the data set in this study consists of 123 events between 1994 and 2006.

The results from the remaining sub-samples are unanticipated (see Table 9). For the unauthorized access to customer data incidents, although the abnormal returns are negative, none of them are statistically significant for the event windows. One main reason could be the fact that in most cases, companies inform their customers about such an incident well before they make a public announcement. For instance, banks usually send replacement credit cards to their customers and make the announcement after finishing the distribution of the new cards. Therefore, the negative effect of these security breaches may be diluted over a longer period of time, preventing us from capturing it in our event study.

## Analysis of effect by event year

In order to investigate the effect of event year, the full sample was divided into two sub-samples: events between 1994 and 2000, and events between 2001 and 2006. The cut-off at year 2000 was selected mainly because it splits the sample in the middle of the time range. It also approximates our recency argument in the hypothesis development. For the 1994–2000 sub-sample the mean CAR is -0.0271 for the [-1, 1] window, significant at

Po0.05 level. Furthermore, the mean CAR for the [-1, 5] window is -0.0392, significant at Po0.05 level, indicating a noticeable increase in the negative impact of the events. However, after analyzing the [-1, 10] window, we see that this increase vanished and the abnormal returns dissipated. The summary of these results is presented in Table 10. The results from non-parametric analyses to this sub-sample are consistent with these findings (see Appendix A).

On the other hand, the results from the 2001–2006 subsample were not as expected since none of the windows were statistically significant. Table 10 shows that the mean CARs in this sub-sample are very small, indicating that the security breach announcements made between 2001 and 2006 didn’t have any substantial negative impact on firms’ market value. Although we have argued that the security breach announcements would have a different effect on the sub-samples, the results indicate that the difference was not in the hypothesized direction. That is, the security breaches occurred before year 2001 had stronger negative impact than those occurred in and after year 2001. This difference is also statistically significant at Po0.01 level for [-1, 1] and [-1, 5] windows and weakly significant at [-1, 10] window (see Appendix B).

Further investigation is required to fully understand these results. One explanation could be the increasing availability of information on the Internet. This may cause security breach news to become public via multiple channels on the Internet well before the announcement in the mainstream media such as newspapers or business wire in more recent years than in more remote years, thus diminishing the effect of the announcement for recent security breaches. Another explanation could be the decreased investor sensitivity as a result of the increasingly frequent headlines of security breaches in the media in more recent years. The sample in this study includes only publicly traded companies. When security incidents experienced by universities, government agencies, and privately owned companies are considered together, the number of incidents would be considerably higher in 2000s than the number in 1990s. This may lead to the perception by investors that security breaches are almost inevitable in the global information age, and therefore they react less aggressively than they did in the past.

## Analysis of event longevity

In order to test hypothesis 6, the market reactions to the security breaches were examined using different event window lengths. The [-1, 1] event window was used to analyze the initial short-term reactions of the market to firm-specific security breach announcements and the longevity of these reactions was investigated using the [-1, 5] and [-1, 10] event windows. With the exception of e-commerce firms, in every sub-sample and in the full sample, as shown in Tables 6-10 and Figure 1, the [-1, 5] windows have noticeably greater negative abnormal returns than the [-1, 1] windows. However, analyzing the [-1, 10] window revealed that the longevity of the negative effects differs noticeably across the sub-samples. For instance, in some sub-samples (e.g., events after 2000, other type of attacks, and traditional firms), there is no statistically significant change between the mean CARs in 5-day and 10-day event periods. And in other sub-samples (e.g., DoS attacks, technology firms), the severity of negative abnormal returns is actually greatest in the 10-day window. These findings support our hypothesis 6. Contrary to the common belief that the effect of security breaches is only short lived, our findings indicate that the longevity as well as the magnitude of the impact of the security breach announcements differ depending on several contingency factors including business, industry, and security breach types.

## Cross-sectional analyses

In the last stage of our analyses, we conducted crosssectional analyses to explain the abnormal returns using a linear regression model as suggested in the literature (McWilliams and Siegel, 1997). Regression data are screened for outliers and multicollinearity using Mahalanobis distance and variance inflation factor, respectively. In addition, linearity, normality, and homoscedasticity are evaluated using residual plots. We used CARs as the dependent variable, and included business type, industry type, security breach type, and event year as explanatory variables. The explanatory variables are coded using dummy variables. Given that there were three groups for security breach types, we omitted ‘other types of attacks’ in our analysis. We also included firm size and systematic and unsystematic firm risk into the model as control variables.

Firm size can be a potential determinant of abnormal returns considering that larger firms may have higher volatility and therefore may have bigger abnormal returns compared to smaller firms. We used the log of market capitalization as a proxy to firm size. Systematic risk also controls for firms’ stock volatility in relation to the market volatility. Correlation between firm’s return and composite market return over the trailing 12 months is used to capture systematic risk. Unsystematic risk, on the other hand, controls for any non-market-related risk inherited to firms, such as operational risk. Therefore, we used standard deviation of firm’s return on assets (ROA) indicator over the trailing eight quarters.

The results of regression analyses show that the models explain considerable amount of variance in cumulative abnormal returns (Table 11). Analyses of $R ^ { 2 }$ change show moderate increase when the adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of the model with only control variables compared to that of the full model (DR<sup>2</sup> 0.09, 0.06, 0.06 for event windows [-1, 1], [-1, 5] and [-1, 10], respectively). Moreover, the result from the [-1, 1] window shows that business type and year of security incident have statistically significant effects on the cumulative abnormal returns of the firms. More specifically, the abnormal returns are greater for pure e-commerce compared to traditional firms, and security breach announcements before year 2000 have greater negative effects compared to announcements made after year 2000. The analysis of [-1, 5] windows shows similar effects of the year of announcement. In addition, this analysis also shows that DoS attacks have negative effect on the stock price of firms. However, its effect is statistically weak. The [-1, 10] event window shows similar effects of DoS attacks.

These results are largely consistent with our previous findings (with the exception of hypothesis 3 where t-test results show that the abnormal returns of firms in technology sector are statistically different than that of firms in other sectors for the [-1, 10] event window), and they also underline the important differences between the shorter and longer time windows. For instance, the effect of security breach type can be seen in the longer time periods, whereas the effect of business type is significant only in the shorter time periods. Furthermore, the results indicate that firms with smaller market capitalization are hit more severely than those with larger market capitalization, yet the significance of the market capitalization diminishes in the longer time periods.

Table 12 summarizes the findings of the study. As it can be seen, the majority of our research hypotheses are supported, though some are only weakly supported in statistical sense. The notable exception is the effect of recency. Contrary to what we hypothesized, the security events in the more recent years (after 2000) had no statistically significant impact on the firms’ stock market valuation while the security events in the more remote years (before 2000) had statistically significant negative impact on the focal firms’ stock market valuation.

## Conclusion

In this study we investigated the stock market reactions to firm-specific security breach incidents. Our results showed that there were indeed significantly negative stock market reactions to firm-specific security breaches. However, further analyses revealed that the degree of this negative effect was subject to a number of contingencies, including business types, security breach types, year of the security incidents, and the time frame of analysis. E-commerce firms suffered more from the security breach announcements compared to traditional bricks-and-mortar firms. DoS attacks generated more severe negative reactions than other types of security breaches. In addition, contrary to what we hypothesized, events in more recent years had less negative impact than those in more distant years. Finally, both the magnitude and the longevity of negative effects differ across sub-samples and the negative impact of security events may last longer than previously expected.

We believe that these findings have some major theoretical and practical implications. While some of the findings are consistent with previous studies, our most significant finding is that the magnitude of negative effects of security breaches changes over time and the magnitude and direction of this change vary across sub-samples of various contingencies. Theoretically, this finding emphasizes the importance of time dimension and suggests that, without incorporating time into the analysis, the value and validity of using stock market reaction as an indicator of the cost of security breaches, or of the value of positive IT-related events, would be significantly limited. For instance, when the event windows were expanded to 10- days, for some sub-samples the negative abnormal returns diminished, while for other sub-samples the negative abnormal returns didn’t decrease or even increased. If the negative market reaction is only temporary and quickly disappears within days, then the stock value loss due to security breaches may not be used to justify the amount of security investment, as it has been suggested in the literature. On the other hand, as demonstrated in this study, the difference between the sub-samples can become significant in 5-day or 10-day period as compared to 1-day period; thus if longer term windows are not analyzed, the results may not reflect the true effects. A similar inference can be made for the impacts of positive IT events such as IT investments and IT innovations. We submit that the value of using event analysis as a proxy for either the cost of security breach or the value of IT investment hinges on the longevity of the abnormal return caused by the event.

Practically, our findings suggest that management should pay special attention to DoS attacks because even these attacks seize in short time, their negative impact on firms’ market value escalates with time. The results indicate that even 10 days after DoS attacks, investors were still negative towards the attacked firms, reflected by the approximately 7% decrease in market valuation over the 10-day period. Moreover, the management of technology firms must pay extra attention to security breaches since the negative effects of the events increase with time regardless of the type of the attack. On the other hand, our results suggest that bricks-and-mortar companies should not be overly concerned with stock market reactions to the security events. Rather, their focus should be on preventing the real losses from stolen customer credit card numbers or trade secrets which may eventually hurt the companies in terms of their revenues and competitiveness in the marketplace.

This study also differs from previous ones in many important aspects. One of them is the inclusion of the time dimension. Most studies in the literature have only analyzed the [-1, 1] event window. In this study, we especially focused on investigating the effect of time and our findings confirmed the importance of such analyses. Another important difference is our data sample. The most recent data set in the previous studies included the events up to the year 2002. Considering the increasing number of security breaches in recent years, we believe that including the events up to the year 2006 has provided a more complete and revived understanding of the effects of security events. Moreover, increasing the time span of our analyses resulted in a larger sample, which is important in getting reliable statistics and drawing inferences from the analyses.

We must also note the limitations of this study. Like other event studies, the challenge is to identify the correct event date from public media sources. It is possible in some cases that the public disclosure date of a security event is days, even weeks, after the actual occurrence, and during that period of time the information about the event might have leaked out to the privileged groups and individuals, resulting in diluted market reactions. Second, our analyses only reflect the negative effects of security breaches on the market value of the firms in relatively narrow time frames. Other effects such as declined sales and revenues in the future due to decreased customer loyalty cannot be captured with this methodology since it would involve much longer time windows (weeks or months) which inevitably would be confounded by other events. Third, we did not use multiple coders to classify each security breach announcement and provide inter-coder reliability statistics. The classification of all event announcements was done by one of the authors due to the fact that there was little difficulty in the process. Nevertheless, this method could result in biased or inaccurate grouping of the announcements into the relevant categories. Finally, despite our effort to create a larger sample, only 123 usable data points were collected from public sources. This sample is still relatively small when sub-samples are analyzed. Future studies could focus on creating a larger sample of firms. With a larger sample, further analyses at the industry or market segment level can be conducted with more refined processes.

Nevertheless, our findings raise some provocative questions for future research. Perhaps the most important question is: if the magnitude of stock market loss due to security breach varies with time, is it still a valid approach to use the loss of short-term market value to justify investment in information security? Using the same reasoning, if the abnormal market returns due to the announcement of positive IT events, such as implementation of ERP or creation of a new CIO position, varies with time, is it still a valid approach to use such short-terms abnormal return as an indicator of the value of such IT investment or management strategies? So far, none of the event studies in the IS literature has explored the long range windows (i.e., 430 days), which have been fiercely debated and contested in the finance literature (Barber and Lyon, 1997; Fama, 1998; Lyon et al., 1999). We believe future research could shed significant light on these questions by using longer event windows and reexamining the established conclusions in the IS literature. Another venue for future research is examining the risk effects of security breach announcements. The inclusion of systematic and unsystematic firm risk into the market model has been recently proposed in the IS literature (Dewan and Ren, 2007). Since security breaches have a potential to harm firms’ current and future cash flow, consideration of risk effects can improve the estimation of market valuation. Lastly, although we have conducted post-hoc analyses of interaction effects, with the benefit of a larger sample size, future research can conduct more thorough investigation of these interactions between contingencies.
